# The eight senses: recognising and managing hypersensitivity and hyposensitivity

## What this covers

This topic opens the book's section on managing sensory needs. It explains what a sensory profile is, introduces eight senses rather than five, defines hypersensitivity and hyposensitivity [msg #71, p. 68], and then gives four tables organised by sense: examples of hypersensitivity [msg #72, p. 69], ways to manage it [msg #73, p. 70], examples of hyposensitivity [msg #74, p. 71] and ways to manage it [msg #75, p. 72]. According to the book, neurodivergent people are more likely to have a different sensory processing system, and looking at hyper- and hyposensitivity preferences is a way to understand one's own sensory differences [msg #71, p. 68].

## Core ideas

- **Everyone has a sensory profile; neurodivergent people often have a different one.** Having "our own unique sensory processing system and needs" is a natural human experience, but neurodivergent individuals are "often more likely to have a different sensory processing system" [msg #71, p. 68].
- **There are eight senses, not five.** Five external senses plus three internal ones — vestibular, proprioception and interoception — all affect sensory needs and differences [msg #71, p. 68].
- **Sensory needs are unique and changeable.** Needs can shift as the environment changes and as capacity shifts; preferences "can change daily, weekly or depending on different things" [msg #71, p. 68].
- **Hyper and hypo are not either/or.** "Everyone can experience both hyper and hyposensitivity with one, some, or many of the senses" — a person may be over-responsive in one sense and under-responsive in another [msg #71, p. 68].
- **Hypersensitive people avoid; hyposensitive people seek.** Hypersensitive individuals tend to avoid certain input or need frequent breaks from it; hyposensitive individuals tend to seek out and need higher levels of input and stimulation [msg #71, p. 68].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### The eight senses
The sensory processing system "takes in information we receive from all our senses and processes them so we can respond accordingly" [msg #71, p. 68].
- **External senses (5):** sight, touch, smell, taste and sound — "the five senses we know" [msg #71, p. 68].
- **Internal senses (3):** [msg #71, p. 68]
  1. **Proprioception** — body awareness and spatial awareness [msg #71, p. 68].
  2. **Interoception** — internal body awareness, such as hunger cues [msg #71, p. 68].
  3. **Vestibular** — related to balance; the book's example: you might experience motion sickness "because your vestibular system is over-sensitive!" [msg #71, p. 68]

### Hypersensitivity vs hyposensitivity
- **Hypersensitivity** is an over-responsiveness to sensory input that "can cause sensory overload, distress, discomfort and pain". Hypersensitive individuals tend to avoid certain stimuli or sensory input, or need frequent breaks from sensory input [msg #71, p. 68].
- **Hyposensitivity** is an under-responsiveness to sensory input: individuals "often struggle to register low levels of sensory input or may receive less information from the senses". Hyposensitive individuals tend to seek out and need higher levels of sensory input and stimulation [msg #71, p. 68].

### Examples of hypersensitivity (over-responsiveness), by sense
- **Visual:** sensitive to light, especially when sleeping; details are easier to focus on; lights appear too bright [msg #72, p. 69].
- **Smell:** sensitive to smells; perfumes and shampoos are overpowering; refusal to eat certain foods [msg #72, p. 69].
- **Sound:** sensitive to background noises; multiple sounds are overwhelming; startled by loud noises [msg #72, p. 69].
- **Taste:** certain textures cause discomfort and distress; a predictable diet and safe foods; flavours are overwhelming [msg #72, p. 69].
- **Proprioception:** prefers to sit down or remain grounded; often leaning; difficulties with fine motor skills [msg #72, p. 69].
- **Touch:** hair brushing can cause discomfort; textures on skin are irritating; difficulty with wet textures [msg #72, p. 69].
- **Vestibular:** often gets car and motion sickness; avoids swings, ladders and merry-go-rounds; loses balance easily [msg #72, p. 69].
- **Interoception:** easily overwhelmed by internal sensations; heightened sensitivity to physiological cues, which "can cause anxiety or discomfort" [msg #72, p. 69].

### Managing hypersensitivity, by sense
The strategies reduce or soften the input that overwhelms each sense [msg #73, p. 70]:
- **Visual:** reduce fluorescent lighting; utilise lamps; use sunglasses; reduce clutter and visual distractions [msg #73, p. 70].
- **Smell:** use fragrance-free products; ask for events or parties to be perfume-free; keep rooms ventilated [msg #73, p. 70].
- **Sound:** use noise-cancelling headphones; try out earplugs; have a quiet area away from background noises [msg #73, p. 70].
- **Taste:** eat your preferred food; "don't force yourself to eat foods you don't want"; use chewellery (chew jewellery) [msg #73, p. 70].
- **Proprioception:** reduce items with buttons/laces; use different seating like bean bags; use grippy tools for items like pens or keys [msg #73, p. 70].
- **Touch:** remove tags from clothing; use seamless socks and items; ask for firmer pressure over lighter pressure [msg #73, p. 70].
- **Vestibular:** reduce car ride length; sit in the front to reduce motion sickness; take regular breaks from movement activities [msg #73, p. 70].
- **Interoception:** use visual prompts and communication scripts; take frequent self-care breaks; use regulation strategies [msg #73, p. 70].

### Examples of hyposensitivity (under-responsiveness), by sense
- **Visual:** poor depth perception; trouble locating an item; difficulty identifying differences in pictures, words and similar [msg #74, p. 71].
- **Smell:** difficulty interpreting smells; enjoys foods with strong smells; uses smell to engage with objects or people [msg #74, p. 71].
- **Sound:** attracted to loud spaces; difficulty localising a sound; often turns music or the TV up louder to register it [msg #74, p. 71].
- **Taste:** likes foods with intense flavours; has a predictable diet; "big on tactile stimming"; putting items in mouth [msg #74, p. 71]. (!) UNCLEAR: "big on tactile stimming" is listed in the taste block in the extracted text; it may belong to the touch column.
- **Proprioception:** difficulty navigating a room; crashes into furniture; prefers tight clothing and heavy blankets; trouble balancing [msg #74, p. 71].
- **Touch:** high pain threshold; likes tight clothing and weighted blankets; requires tight hugs "to register the pressure" [msg #74, p. 71].
- **Vestibular:** seeks all forms of movement, like swinging; frequent rocking back and forth; always seeking stimuli [msg #74, p. 71].
- **Interoception:** difficulty sensing when hungry or thirsty; requires more input to sense; trouble interpreting what we're feeling [msg #74, p. 71].

### Managing hyposensitivity, by sense
The strategies make input easier to register or supply more of it [msg #75, p. 72]:
- **Visual:** have items on open shelves so they are easier to find; have multiple forms of lighting; reduce clutter [msg #75, p. 72].
- **Smell:** have scented items on hand; "CLEARLY label foods and items" [msg #75, p. 72]. (!) UNCLEAR: the labelling item sits in the smell block of the extracted text; it may belong to the visual column.
- **Sound:** provide visual cues and instructions; ask for instructions to be broken down; listen to music for sensory input [msg #75, p. 72].
- **Taste:** wear chew jewellery; keep a list of safe or same foods; have crunchy snacks on hand [msg #75, p. 72].
- **Proprioception:** keep a room clear of furniture; use input like weighted blankets; use grip pens or weighted pens [msg #75, p. 72].
- **Touch:** provide lots of fidget items; use weighted blankets; ask friends/partners for tighter hugs and pressure [msg #75, p. 72].
- **Vestibular:** get a chair swing, exercise ball or mini trampoline at home; introduce a sensory diet; take frequent movement breaks [msg #75, p. 72].
- **Interoception:** regular check-ins/breaks; alternative descriptions; accessible hydration options; simple, quick snacks [msg #75, p. 72].

## Detailed insights

### Recurring tools across the tables
Some tools appear on both sides, used differently: chewellery/chew jewellery is a taste strategy for hypersensitivity and for hyposensitivity [msg #73, #75, pp. 70, 72]; pressure appears as "ask for firmer pressure over lighter pressure" for touch hypersensitivity and as weighted blankets and "tighter hugs and pressure" for hyposensitivity [msg #73, #75, pp. 70, 72]; and a predictable diet appears as an example under taste in both the hyper and the hypo tables [msg #72, #74, pp. 69, 71]. Reducing clutter is listed for visual hypersensitivity (as a visual distraction) and for visual hyposensitivity (to make items easier to find) [msg #73, #75, pp. 70, 72].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- Sensory preferences are not fixed: they shift with environment and capacity and can change daily or weekly, so a profile is a snapshot rather than a permanent label [msg #71, p. 68].
