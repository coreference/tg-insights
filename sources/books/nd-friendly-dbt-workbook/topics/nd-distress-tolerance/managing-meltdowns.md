# Managing meltdowns: signs, aftercare, prevention, Meltdown Mapping and a tip sheet for loved ones

## What this covers

The distress-tolerance section's part on meltdowns, which the author added to DBT to make it neurodivergent friendly: what meltdowns are and what causes them, how they can look, why compassion and aftercare matter, a set of prevention tips, and two worksheets — one to map your own meltdowns and one to tell loved ones how to support you. According to the author, mapping meltdowns, triggers and warning signs "can help us better respond to and manage our meltdowns as well as find ways to prevent or minimise them" [msg #43, p. 40].

## Core ideas

- **A meltdown is involuntary.** It is a response that can occur in a time of crisis or distress in which "we often lose control of our behaviour and responses" [msg #43, p. 40].
- **It signals a surpassed limit.** Meltdowns often happen when we become dysregulated or overstimulated by stress, sensory input, our emotions and other factors, because "our body and brain is communicating to us that we have reached (and surpassed) a limit" [msg #43, p. 40].
- **It is never just one trigger.** Others may see a meltdown follow a single event, but meltdowns "are always due to ongoing combination of factors that lead up to the big show" [msg #43, p. 40].
- **Meltdowns are not tantrums.** The author says this explicitly [msg #43, p. 40].
- **Not intentional, so meet it with compassion.** Meltdowns can lead to behaviours that may cause hurt, but those behaviours are not intentional "because we aren't in control" — which is why it's important to hold compassion for yourself when a meltdown happens [msg #43, p. 40].
- **Aftercare is essential.** Meltdowns are "super tiring and emotionally draining", so taking care of yourself afterwards is really important [msg #43, p. 40].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Signs of a meltdown
Meltdowns can look like (the complete list) [msg #43, p. 40]:
1. shouting and screaming [msg #43, p. 40]
2. throwing things [msg #43, p. 40]
3. crying or loss of verbal words [msg #43, p. 40]
4. self-injury like hitting your head [msg #43, p. 40]
5. rocking back and forth [msg #43, p. 40]
6. shutting down [msg #43, p. 40]
7. inability to communicate [msg #43, p. 40]
8. pacing or escaping [msg #43, p. 40]
9. hyperventilating [msg #43, p. 40]
10. physically curling inwards [msg #43, p. 40]

### Meltdown prevention tips
The book's full set of fifteen tips (printed as a scattered poster; each tip reconstructed as one item) [msg #44, p. 41]:
1. **Listen to your body.** [msg #44, p. 41]
2. **Create a routine that works for you.** [msg #44, p. 41]
3. **Make a meltdown plan for loved ones to use** — the Meltdown Tip Sheet below is the book's tool for this. [msg #44, p. 41]
4. **Learn your triggers + create a plan** — the Meltdown Mapping worksheet below is the book's tool for this. [msg #44, p. 41]
5. **Create a sensory toolbox with your favourite sensory items.** [msg #44, p. 41]
6. **Regular sensory breaks.** [msg #44, p. 41]
7. **Identify early warning signs.** [msg #44, p. 41]
8. **Allow yourself space to unmask.** [msg #44, p. 41]
9. **Use AAC or written communication.** [msg #44, p. 41]
10. **Have an exit strategy for situations.** [msg #44, p. 41]
11. **Give yourself time to stim.** [msg #44, p. 41]
12. **Utilise noise cancelling headphones and sunglasses.** [msg #44, p. 41]
13. **Plan for events and outings in advance.** [msg #44, p. 41]
14. **Reduce triggers like sensory input asap.** [msg #44, p. 41]

### Meltdown Mapping worksheet
Purpose: to map out your meltdowns, triggers and warning signs so you can better respond to and manage meltdowns and find ways to prevent or minimise them [msg #43, p. 40]. The worksheet has five boxes to fill in [msg #45, p. 42]:
1. **Meltdown triggers** — what sets off or builds up to your meltdowns (remembering that meltdowns come from a combination of factors) [msg #43, #45, pp. 40, 42].
2. **Early warning signs** — the signs that a meltdown is approaching [msg #45, p. 42].
3. **Coping strategies that can be put in place** — what you can do in response [msg #45, p. 42].
4. **Your needs or boundaries during a meltdown** — what you need, and what others should respect, while a meltdown is happening [msg #45, p. 42].
5. **Meltdown recovery ideas** — aftercare ideas for once the meltdown has passed [msg #45, p. 42].

### Meltdown Tip Sheet for loved ones and family
Instruction: "FILL THIS IN FOR LOVED ONES AND FAMILY" — a sheet you complete and give to the people around you so they know how to support you [msg #46, p. 43]. The five prompts:
1. **"EARLY WARNING SIGNS TO PAY ATTENTION TO:"** — the signs they should watch for [msg #46, p. 43].
2. **"PLEASE DO THESE THINGS WHEN YOU NOTICE THE WARNING SIGNS:"** — what they should do as a meltdown approaches [msg #46, p. 43].
3. **"THINGS THAT AREN'T HELPFUL DURING A MELTDOWN:"** — what they should avoid [msg #46, p. 43].
4. **"THESE ARE MY PREFERRED COMMUNICATION METHODS:"** — how to communicate with you (relevant since a meltdown can involve loss of verbal words or inability to communicate) [msg #43, #46, pp. 40, 43].
5. **"THINGS TO DO AFTER A MELTDOWN:"** — how they can help with aftercare [msg #46, p. 43].

## Detailed insights

### What meltdown aftercare feels like, in the author's experience
The author describes their own state after a meltdown: they often feel like they have an "emotional headache", have been drained of energy, and "just need to lie in the dark". This illustrates why recovery time and self-care after a meltdown are important [msg #43, p. 40].

## Notable quotes

> "Remember, meltdowns are not tantrums." — Sonny Jane Wise [msg #43, p. 40]

> "This is why it's important to hold compassion for yourself when a meltdown happens." — Sonny Jane Wise [msg #43, p. 40]

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions
