# Distress tolerance basics: the STOP skill and a personal crisis plan

## What this covers

This topic gathers the book's foundation for distress tolerance: its definition and neurodivergent framing, the situations in which the skills are useful, the STOP skill, the personal crisis plan worksheet from the Everyday Well-being section, and the reflection questions that close the Distress Tolerance section [msg #13, #39, #53, pp. 10, 36, 50]. According to the author, these skills matter because neurodivergent people are "likely to experience high levels of stress living in a neurotypical society" [msg #39, p. 36].

## Core ideas

- **Definition.** Distress tolerance is about managing stress and distress to keep it at a manageable level — a level we can cope with [msg #39, p. 36].
- **Why neurodivergent people need it.** Living in a neurotypical society tends to bring high levels of stress; distress commonly arises during sensory overload, Rejection Sensitivity from RSD, difficulty regulating emotions, or being close to a meltdown [msg #39, p. 36].
- **Listen first.** The skills help manage and tolerate distress, but "distress tolerance should always come from a place of listening to what we need and responding accordingly" — they are not about pushing through regardless of needs [msg #39, p. 36].
- **Prepare before the crisis.** During a crisis or high stress we may have a harder time remembering important and basic things, so a plan written in advance acts as a reminder [msg #13, p. 10].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### When to use distress tolerance skills
The book lists five situations in which distress tolerance skills can be used — when you are [msg #39, p. 36]:
1. overwhelmed by stressors like multiple tasks;
2. feeling the urge to act on negative emotions;
3. feeling overwhelmed and you can't think clearly;
4. experiencing emotional or sensory overload;
5. close to a meltdown.

### STOP skill
**Purpose:** when you are feeling overwhelmed or distressed, remember STOP "so you can proceed mindfully and intentionally" rather than reacting [msg #39, p. 36]. The four letters, in order:
1. **S — Stop, pause, and don't react.** The first move is to halt the automatic reaction — not acting on the urge in the moment [msg #39, p. 36].
2. **T — Take a step back, walk away and take a deep breath.** Create physical and mental distance from the situation and use a deep breath to settle [msg #39, p. 36].
3. **O — Observe: notice your surroundings and how you feel.** Take in both what is around you (relevant to sensory triggers) and your internal state [msg #39, p. 36].
4. **P — Proceed mindfully: what can you do?** Having paused and observed, choose a deliberate next action by asking what you can do [msg #39, p. 36].
(!) INCOMPLETE: the book gives each step as a one-line instruction only; it does not add examples or pitfalls for the individual steps [msg #39, p. 36].

### Personal crisis plan (worksheet)
**Purpose:** a plan "ready to go" that you can use as a reminder whenever you have reached a point of distress, because in a crisis or times of high stress it can be harder to remember the important and basic things [msg #13, p. 10]. **How to do it:** fill in each section in advance, when calm; then consult it when distressed. The worksheet's sections, each to be completed by you [msg #13, p. 10]:
1. **"I know I'm triggered or in need of support when:"** — your personal signs that you are triggered or need support.
2. **"Three ways I can distract myself are:"** — three specific distractions you can turn to.
3. **"Safe people I can call or message:"** — with fields for each person's **Name** and **Contact details**, so you do not have to look them up in the moment.
4. **"Coping tools or skills that help are:"** — the tools or skills you know work for you.

### Distress tolerance summary (reflection)
**Purpose:** the closing page of the Distress Tolerance section, for personalising what you have learned [msg #53, p. 50]. Answer each question in writing:
1. "Which distress tolerance skills are most helpful for you?"
2. "What are some signs that you need to use these skills?"
3. "Can you identify any barriers or accommodations needed in using these skills?"
4. "What reminders do you need to use your distress tolerance skills?"
These questions push you to pick your own skills, recognise your early warning signs, anticipate obstacles and needed accommodations, and plan prompts to actually use the skills [msg #53, p. 50].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The author frames distress tolerance as responsive to needs, not as enduring distress for its own sake [msg #39, p. 36].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the crisis plan and summary pages are blank fill-in worksheets; the book gives no worked example answers [msg #13, #53, pp. 10, 50].
