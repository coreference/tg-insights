# The Window of Tolerance and emotional dysregulation in neurodivergent people

## What this covers

This topic opens the book's Emotional Regulation section. It introduces the Window of Tolerance (a concept the author credits to Dr. Dan Siegel) with three states — hyperarousal, the zone of tolerance and hypoarousal — and the complete list of signs the book gives for each, then offers a worksheet for mapping your own window. It defines emotional regulation and emotional dysregulation, lists the factors that make regulation harder (especially for neurodivergent people) and the ways dysregulation can look. According to the author, it matters because neurodivergent people often feel emotions easily and intensely, and DBT emotional regulation skills can shift you back into the zone where you function best [msg #62, #64, pp. 59, 61].

## Core ideas

- **There is an optimal zone for each person.** The Window of Tolerance is the "perfect window" between being overstimulated/overwhelmed and being understimulated/underwhelmed; inside it you can feel different emotions and still stay regulated, think clearly and get done what you need to do. Everyone's window is unique [msg #62, p. 59].
- **Two ways out of the window.** Emotions that "get too much" push you into hyperarousal; things getting you down or feeling numb push you into hypoarousal. Outside the window "things start happening" — anxiety, a fast heart, racing thoughts on one side; dissociating, numbness, tiredness on the other [msg #62, p. 59].
- **Neurodivergent people feel more, both ways.** The author says neurodivergent people often feel emotions "more easily and more intensely" — which can be "a beautiful thing" (intense joy and excitement over small things) but also means the unpleasant emotions are felt intensely too [msg #62, p. 59].
- **Regulation keeps you in the window.** Emotional regulation is important precisely because it keeps us within the optimal zone, the Window of Tolerance; dysregulation is its absence [msg #64, p. 61].
- **Regulation is hard and is a skill.** Eight listed factors (see *Factors that impact the ability to regulate emotions*) make it harder; DBT skills focus on regulation, and like every skill they take practice and finding what works for you [msg #64, p. 61].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Window of Tolerance (Dr. Dan Siegel, as adopted in the book)
Purpose: a way to understand our emotions by locating ourselves in one of three states — too much (hyperarousal), just right (zone of tolerance) or too little (hypoarousal). The author notes the concept "was coined by Dr. Dan Siegel and it's since been adopted to understand our emotions" [msg #62, p. 59].

1. **Hyperarousal (above the window)** — what happens when emotions get too much. The book's full list of signs [msg #62, p. 59]:
   - flight or fight response
   - anxiety and panic
   - fear
   - racing thoughts
   - inability to keep still
   - reactive emotions
   - shaking
   - aggression
   - defensiveness
2. **Zone of tolerance (inside the window)** — the regulated state where you are neither overwhelmed nor underwhelmed; "Here you can think clearly, process information and do what you need to do, so it's the ideal state to be in." Notably, being in the zone does not mean having no emotions: the list includes feeling all the emotions. The book's full list of signs [msg #62, p. 59]:
   - feeling safe
   - feeling calm
   - thinking clearly
   - self-soothing
   - regulating your emotions
   - insight and awareness
   - being present in the moment
   - feeling all the emotions
3. **Hypoarousal (below the window)** — what happens when things are getting you down or you feel numb. The book's full list of signs (the book spells it "disassociation" [sic]) [msg #62, p. 59]:
   - disassociation
   - unable to think
   - intrusive thoughts
   - no energy
   - depression
   - numb
   - withdrawn
   - disconnected from self
   - shutting down
   - fatigue

(!) The three lists are printed on the page as a diagram (hyperarousal above, zone of tolerance in the middle, hypoarousal below); the text extraction preserves only the headings and items, not the visual layout [msg #62, p. 59].

### Your Window of Tolerance worksheet
Purpose: because everyone's zone is unique, the author suggests it "can be helpful to understand what it looks like when we're in our zone and what it looks like when we're outside our zone." Since emotions also influence our thoughts, physical sensations and our actions or urges, "there's often a lot going on and a lot of signs to notice" — so the worksheet asks you to collect your own signs across all of these channels [msg #63, p. 60].

How to do it — the worksheet has three boxes, each with the same prompt, "Write down the signs or things you notice..." [msg #63, p. 60]:
1. **Hyperarousal** — write down the signs or things you notice when you are above your window (your own version of the list above: thoughts, body sensations, actions/urges) [msg #63, p. 60].
2. **Zone of tolerance** — write down the signs or things you notice when you are inside your window and regulated [msg #63, p. 60].
3. **Hypoarousal** — write down the signs or things you notice when you are below your window [msg #63, p. 60].

The book's general lists in *Window of Tolerance* above serve as examples of what can go in each box [msg #62, #63, pp. 59–60].

### Factors that impact the ability to regulate emotions
The author stresses that "Emotional regulation isn't easy at all" and lists factors that impact our ability to regulate. Two are explained in the text: for neurodivergent people, emotional sensitivity and heightened emotions make regulation harder; and if you did not have access to a safe environment or to caregivers who were emotionally regulated, you might have trouble regulating "because you didn't have an opportunity to learn" (this is what "unlearned skills" refers to). The full list [msg #64, p. 61]:
1. executive function
2. heightened emotions
3. biological factors
4. unlearned skills
5. sensory processing
6. masking
7. alexithymia
8. environment

(!) INCOMPLETE: apart from heightened emotions and unlearned skills (lack of a safe environment or regulated caregivers), the book lists these factors without explaining how each one affects regulation [msg #64, p. 61].

### Signs of emotional dysregulation
Definition: "If emotional regulation is the process of regulating and controlling our emotions, emotional dysregulation is the inability to regulate or control emotions, including our emotional experiences, actions and responses." The book says it can look like [msg #64, p. 61]:
1. impulsiveness
2. intense emotions
3. acting on urges
4. avoidance or attempts at numbing
5. poor problem-solving
6. fear of abandonment
7. extreme emotional responses
8. poor attempts at coping

## Detailed insights

### What emotional regulation covers
The book defines emotional regulation as "basically the ability to control and manage your emotions as well as your reactions to your emotions." It spans three aspects: **when** you have emotions, **how you experience** them, and **how you express or act on** them. So regulation is not only about suppressing feelings — it includes the experience and the response [msg #64, p. 61].

### Why neurodivergent people benefit especially
Emotional regulation skills are helpful for everyone, the author writes, but "especially for us neurodivergent individuals" because they often experience big emotions and have difficulties with executive function and with regulating emotions. This is where DBT skills "come in handy": they focus on emotional regulation and "can help shift you back to that zone of tolerance where you function best." The author adds that, "Just like every kind of skill, it takes practice and figuring out what works best for you" — i.e. no single skill is prescribed; you experiment [msg #64, p. 61].
