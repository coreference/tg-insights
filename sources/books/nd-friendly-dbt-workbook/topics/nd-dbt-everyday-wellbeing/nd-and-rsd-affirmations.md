# Affirmations for neurodivergent people and for rejection sensitive dysphoria (RSD)

## What this covers

Part of the workbook's "Everyday Well-being" section: two ready-made lists of affirmations — one for neurodivergent people in general, one for Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria (RSD) — plus a page for writing your own. The author's rationale: being neurodivergent in a neurotypical world means often hearing and internalising negative views, beliefs and stereotypes, and RSD actively reinforces messages that "we aren't good enough or no-one likes us"; the affirmations are meant to bring comfort and to combat those messages [msg #15, #16, pp. 12–13].

## Core ideas

- **Internalised negativity comes from the mismatch with a neurotypical world.** Living as a neurodivergent person in a neurotypical world exposes you to negative views, beliefs and stereotypes that you can come to internalise; neurodivergent-friendly affirmations are offered as a source of comfort against them [msg #15, p. 12].
- **RSD is an intense, message-reinforcing reaction to rejection and criticism.** It is common in neurodivergent people, especially ADHDers, and the intensity of the emotional response is what drives home beliefs of not being good enough or not being liked — hence affirmations aimed specifically at those messages [msg #16, p. 13].
- **Your own words count.** The reader is invited to write their own affirmations, return to them when needed, and even share them with someone who can say them aloud in hard moments [msg #17, p. 14].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Affirmations for neurodivergent folks
Purpose: to counter internalised negative views, beliefs and stereotypes absorbed from living in a neurotypical world; the author hopes they "will bring you some comfort" [msg #15, p. 12]. The full list (six affirmations, verbatim):
1. "It's okay if most strategies and tools that were designed for and by neurotypical people don't work for me." — permission for standard (neurotypical) tools to fail without that being a personal failing [msg #15, p. 12].
2. "My brain works exactly the way it was designed to, and I deserve to find ways to support my brain." — reframes the neurodivergent brain as working correctly and deserving support, not correction [msg #15, p. 12].
3. "I will not hide my identity and neurodivergent traits just because they make people uncomfortable." — counters masking for others' comfort [msg #15, p. 12].
4. "I can forget to close cabinets, brush my teeth, and reply to messages and still be worthy." — separates worth from everyday executive-function slips (cabinets, teeth, messages) [msg #15, p. 12].
5. "I do not have to hold myself to neurotypical standards, rules, or expectations." — rejects neurotypical norms as the yardstick [msg #15, p. 12].
6. "My sensory differences are real and valid, and they deserve to be accommodated." — validates sensory needs and the right to accommodation [msg #15, p. 12].

(The first affirmation is printed run into the introductory paragraph on the page; it reads as the first item of the list.) [msg #15, p. 12]

### Affirmations for Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria
Purpose: RSD "causes an intense emotional response that reinforces messages to us that we aren't good enough or no-one likes us"; these affirmations are included "to combat these messages" [msg #16, p. 13]. The full list (six affirmations, verbatim):
1. "My interests are valid, and I am allowed to be excited and passionate about them even if other people aren't." — protects enthusiasm from others' lack of interest [msg #16, p. 13].
2. "People disagreeing with me does not mean my lived experience or my opinion are invalid." — separates disagreement from invalidation [msg #16, p. 13].
3. "I am allowed to ask for reassurance and I am worthy of people's time and attention." — legitimises seeking reassurance [msg #16, p. 13].
4. "I am allowed to ask questions and require certainty in order to feel safety and control." — legitimises needing certainty to feel safe and in control [msg #16, p. 13].
5. "Some people won't like me no matter what and that is not a reflection of how likeable I am." — decouples individual dislike from one's likeability [msg #16, p. 13].
6. "I decide what thoughts to reject and let pass." — reclaims agency over which thoughts to accept [msg #16, p. 13].

### Write your own affirmations exercise
What it is: a page for writing and adding your own affirmations [msg #17, p. 14]. How to use it, per the author:
1. **Write** your own affirmations on the page (the page is open space after the prompt) [msg #17, p. 14].
2. **Come back to the list** "when you need to remind yourself or whenever you might need comfort" [msg #17, p. 14].
3. **Optionally share it**: "Maybe you can even share this list with a partner or a friend so they can say these things to you in times of need" — so that a trusted person can voice the affirmations to you when you're struggling [msg #17, p. 14].

## Detailed insights

### What RSD is, according to the author
The author defines Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria as "a sensitivity to rejection and criticism that is a common experience for neurodivergent individuals, especially ADHDers". The mechanism they describe: the emotional response to rejection or criticism is intense, and that intensity reinforces negative messages about the self — that one isn't good enough, or that no-one likes them. The RSD affirmations target exactly those messages (validity of interests and opinions, worthiness of attention, likeability, and control over thoughts) [msg #16, p. 13].
