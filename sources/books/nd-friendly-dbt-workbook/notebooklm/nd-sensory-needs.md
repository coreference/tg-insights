## The eight senses: recognising and managing hypersensitivity and hyposensitivity

### What this covers

This topic opens the book's section on managing sensory needs. It explains what a sensory profile is, introduces eight senses rather than five, defines hypersensitivity and hyposensitivity [#71, p. 68], and then gives four tables organised by sense: examples of hypersensitivity [#72, p. 69], ways to manage it [#73, p. 70], examples of hyposensitivity [#74, p. 71] and ways to manage it [#75, p. 72]. According to the book, neurodivergent people are more likely to have a different sensory processing system, and looking at hyper- and hyposensitivity preferences is a way to understand one's own sensory differences [#71, p. 68].

### Core ideas

- Everyone has a sensory profile; neurodivergent people often have a different one. Having "our own unique sensory processing system and needs" is a natural human experience, but neurodivergent individuals are "often more likely to have a different sensory processing system" [#71, p. 68].
- There are eight senses, not five. Five external senses plus three internal ones — vestibular, proprioception and interoception — all affect sensory needs and differences [#71, p. 68].
- Sensory needs are unique and changeable. Needs can shift as the environment changes and as capacity shifts; preferences "can change daily, weekly or depending on different things" [#71, p. 68].
- Hyper and hypo are not either/or. "Everyone can experience both hyper and hyposensitivity with one, some, or many of the senses" — a person may be over-responsive in one sense and under-responsive in another [#71, p. 68].
- Hypersensitive people avoid; hyposensitive people seek. Hypersensitive individuals tend to avoid certain input or need frequent breaks from it; hyposensitive individuals tend to seek out and need higher levels of input and stimulation [#71, p. 68].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### The eight senses
The sensory processing system "takes in information we receive from all our senses and processes them so we can respond accordingly" [#71, p. 68].
- External senses (5): sight, touch, smell, taste and sound — "the five senses we know" [#71, p. 68].
- Internal senses (3): [#71, p. 68]
  1. Proprioception — body awareness and spatial awareness [#71, p. 68].
  2. Interoception — internal body awareness, such as hunger cues [#71, p. 68].
  3. Vestibular — related to balance; the book's example: you might experience motion sickness "because your vestibular system is over-sensitive!" [#71, p. 68]

#### Hypersensitivity vs hyposensitivity
- Hypersensitivity is an over-responsiveness to sensory input that "can cause sensory overload, distress, discomfort and pain". Hypersensitive individuals tend to avoid certain stimuli or sensory input, or need frequent breaks from sensory input [#71, p. 68].
- Hyposensitivity is an under-responsiveness to sensory input: individuals "often struggle to register low levels of sensory input or may receive less information from the senses". Hyposensitive individuals tend to seek out and need higher levels of sensory input and stimulation [#71, p. 68].

#### Examples of hypersensitivity (over-responsiveness), by sense
- Visual: sensitive to light, especially when sleeping; details are easier to focus on; lights appear too bright [#72, p. 69].
- Smell: sensitive to smells; perfumes and shampoos are overpowering; refusal to eat certain foods [#72, p. 69].
- Sound: sensitive to background noises; multiple sounds are overwhelming; startled by loud noises [#72, p. 69].
- Taste: certain textures cause discomfort and distress; a predictable diet and safe foods; flavours are overwhelming [#72, p. 69].
- Proprioception: prefers to sit down or remain grounded; often leaning; difficulties with fine motor skills [#72, p. 69].
- Touch: hair brushing can cause discomfort; textures on skin are irritating; difficulty with wet textures [#72, p. 69].
- Vestibular: often gets car and motion sickness; avoids swings, ladders and merry-go-rounds; loses balance easily [#72, p. 69].
- Interoception: easily overwhelmed by internal sensations; heightened sensitivity to physiological cues, which "can cause anxiety or discomfort" [#72, p. 69].

#### Managing hypersensitivity, by sense
The strategies reduce or soften the input that overwhelms each sense [#73, p. 70]:
- Visual: reduce fluorescent lighting; utilise lamps; use sunglasses; reduce clutter and visual distractions [#73, p. 70].
- Smell: use fragrance-free products; ask for events or parties to be perfume-free; keep rooms ventilated [#73, p. 70].
- Sound: use noise-cancelling headphones; try out earplugs; have a quiet area away from background noises [#73, p. 70].
- Taste: eat your preferred food; "don't force yourself to eat foods you don't want"; use chewellery (chew jewellery) [#73, p. 70].
- Proprioception: reduce items with buttons/laces; use different seating like bean bags; use grippy tools for items like pens or keys [#73, p. 70].
- Touch: remove tags from clothing; use seamless socks and items; ask for firmer pressure over lighter pressure [#73, p. 70].
- Vestibular: reduce car ride length; sit in the front to reduce motion sickness; take regular breaks from movement activities [#73, p. 70].
- Interoception: use visual prompts and communication scripts; take frequent self-care breaks; use regulation strategies [#73, p. 70].

#### Examples of hyposensitivity (under-responsiveness), by sense
- Visual: poor depth perception; trouble locating an item; difficulty identifying differences in pictures, words and similar [#74, p. 71].
- Smell: difficulty interpreting smells; enjoys foods with strong smells; uses smell to engage with objects or people [#74, p. 71].
- Sound: attracted to loud spaces; difficulty localising a sound; often turns music or the TV up louder to register it [#74, p. 71].
- Taste: likes foods with intense flavours; has a predictable diet; "big on tactile stimming"; putting items in mouth [#74, p. 71]. (!) UNCLEAR: "big on tactile stimming" is listed in the taste block in the extracted text; it may belong to the touch column.
- Proprioception: difficulty navigating a room; crashes into furniture; prefers tight clothing and heavy blankets; trouble balancing [#74, p. 71].
- Touch: high pain threshold; likes tight clothing and weighted blankets; requires tight hugs "to register the pressure" [#74, p. 71].
- Vestibular: seeks all forms of movement, like swinging; frequent rocking back and forth; always seeking stimuli [#74, p. 71].
- Interoception: difficulty sensing when hungry or thirsty; requires more input to sense; trouble interpreting what we're feeling [#74, p. 71].

#### Managing hyposensitivity, by sense
The strategies make input easier to register or supply more of it [#75, p. 72]:
- Visual: have items on open shelves so they are easier to find; have multiple forms of lighting; reduce clutter [#75, p. 72].
- Smell: have scented items on hand; "CLEARLY label foods and items" [#75, p. 72]. (!) UNCLEAR: the labelling item sits in the smell block of the extracted text; it may belong to the visual column.
- Sound: provide visual cues and instructions; ask for instructions to be broken down; listen to music for sensory input [#75, p. 72].
- Taste: wear chew jewellery; keep a list of safe or same foods; have crunchy snacks on hand [#75, p. 72].
- Proprioception: keep a room clear of furniture; use input like weighted blankets; use grip pens or weighted pens [#75, p. 72].
- Touch: provide lots of fidget items; use weighted blankets; ask friends/partners for tighter hugs and pressure [#75, p. 72].
- Vestibular: get a chair swing, exercise ball or mini trampoline at home; introduce a sensory diet; take frequent movement breaks [#75, p. 72].
- Interoception: regular check-ins/breaks; alternative descriptions; accessible hydration options; simple, quick snacks [#75, p. 72].

## Sensory overload and mapping your sensory profile

### What this covers

This topic explains sensory overload — what it is, why neurodivergent people are more prone to it, how it shows up and how to respond — and provides two worksheets for mapping your own sensory profile [#76, #77, #78, pp. 73–75]. According to the book, the best response to overload is reducing or removing the input causing it, which requires understanding your own sensory needs and differences — the purpose of the profile worksheets [#76, p. 73].

### Core ideas

- Sensory processing varies day to day. Neurodivergent individuals "process sensory information differently every day", and may be hypersensitive or hyposensitive, "or even both, at different times" [#76, p. 73].
- Sensory differences have wide effects. They can affect how we feel and cope and "can have a significant impact on our lives", including functioning and everyday life "from emotional regulation to getting things done to socialising" [#76, p. 73].
- Society is part of the problem. Because we live "in a society that is full of sensory input that doesn't necessarily accommodate our needs, we can end up overstimulated from our environments" [#76, p. 73].
- Everyone has limits; neurodivergent people reach them sooner. It is "normal and common for every human to have limits on the amount of sensory input we need and can handle", but because neurodivergent brains receive and process input differently, "we're more likely to reach our limits and experience sensory overload" [#76, p. 73].
- Respond by reducing input, which requires self-knowledge. The best response is reducing or removing the contributing input; to do that you need to understand your sensory needs and differences [#76, p. 73].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### What sensory overload is
"Sensory overload is what happens when we receive too much input from our senses." (sensory overload is what happens when we get too much sensory input.) The senses involved are the five known ones — visual, touch, smell, taste and sound — and the three internal senses — vestibular, proprioception and interoception [#76, p. 73]. When some or even all of these senses receive too much input for the brain to process and handle, "we become overwhelmed which can cause a lot of discomfort, pain and distress" [#76, p. 73].

#### Signs of Sensory Overload
The book lists eleven signs [#76, p. 73]:
1. Irritability [#76, p. 73]
2. Restlessness [#76, p. 73]
3. Fidgeting [#76, p. 73]
4. Increased stimming [#76, p. 73]
5. Need to escape or avoid [#76, p. 73]
6. Everything is moving too fast [#76, p. 73]
7. Difficulty focusing [#76, p. 73]
8. Feeling wound up [#76, p. 73]
9. Headaches [#76, p. 73]
10. Freeze response [#76, p. 73]
11. Feeling instantly exhausted [#76, p. 73]

#### Responding to sensory overload
1. Reduce or remove the input. "The best way to respond to sensory overload is by reducing or removing the sensory input that is contributing to the sensory overload." [#76, p. 73]
2. Know your sensory needs first. To do this "you need to have an understanding of your sensory needs and differences" [#76, p. 73].
3. Build that understanding by filling in the sensory profile worksheets (below) and exploring ways to manage your sensory differences [#76, p. 73].

## Building a sensory toolkit and a sensory-friendly safe space

### What this covers

This topic gathers two practical tools for meeting sensory needs: a sensory safe space — a physical spot to escape to in times of need, introduced in the book's Everyday Well-being section [#14, p. 11] — and a sensory toolkit — a collection of items to soothe and comfort during distress or sensory overload, from the Managing Sensory Needs section, followed by a fill-in worksheet [#79, #80, pp. 76–77]. According to the book, sensory needs are "an important part of managing our every day well-being" for neurodivergent individuals, and having a sensory safe space is one way to take care of well-being [#14, p. 11].

### Core ideas

- Sensory needs are part of everyday well-being. "An neurodivergent individuals [sic], our sensory needs are an important part of managing our every day well-being." (As neurodivergent individuals, our sensory needs are an important part of managing everyday well-being.) [#14, p. 11]
- You don't need a whole room. A corner, the space under the stairs or a little indoor tent can serve as a safe space [#14, p. 11].
- Choose by the input you need right now, and give yourself choice. Seating should match the sensory input needed in the moment; including a few options lets you choose [#14, p. 11].
- Personal and changeable. "Everyone is different", so judge each sensation by whether you find it pleasant or unpleasant; you may need different items depending on the day and your sensory needs [#79, p. 76].
- Accessible to everyone in the household. The toolkit should be easy to grab for you or anyone you live with, and can include instructions for others or for yourself [#79, p. 76].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Creating a Sensory Safe Space
Purpose: to have "a sensory safe space we can escape to in times of need" as one way of taking care of well-being [#14, p. 11]. Four steps:
1. Find your space. You might not have access to turning an entire room into a sensory safe space, but other options include finding a corner of your room, under the stairs, or setting up a little tent indoors [#14, p. 11].
2. Choose your lighting. Think about what kind of colours or lighting are most comforting for you. The author says: "I tend to go for purple, blue and white lights." The book notes that many people prefer a "warm white" rather than a "cool white". Options: hang up fairy lights, use a galaxy projector light, or find some cool lamps [#14, p. 11].
3. Where to sit. Think about what kind of sensory input you need in the moment. The book asks: "Would you feel more comfortable on a bean bag wrapped up in a blanket, swinging in a chair, or bouncing on an exercise ball?" Its advice: "Include a few, give yourself choice!" [#14, p. 11]
4. What to put in it. Described as "the fun part": fill the space with sensory-friendly items and all your favourite things. The book's starter list [#14, p. 11]:
   - fidget items [#14, p. 11]
   - weighted blanket [#14, p. 11]
   - sensory tray [#14, p. 11]
   - ear plugs/headphones [#14, p. 11]
   - chewellery [#14, p. 11]
   - favourite pictures and affirmations [#14, p. 11]
   - soothing music and sound machine [#14, p. 11]
   - puzzles, slime or putty [#14, p. 11]

#### Build a Sensory Toolkit — four tips
What it is: "A sensory toolkit is something designed to help soothe and comfort you during times of distress or sensory overload." [#79, p. 76] Tips to get started:
1. Test each sensation for yourself. Everyone is different, so ask yourself: "do I find this sensation pleasant or unpleasant?" — "Comforting or uncomfortable?" [#79, p. 76]
2. Expect to need different items on different days. You might need different types of sensory items depending on the day and your sensory needs [#79, p. 76].
3. Keep it accessible. Keep the toolkit somewhere accessible "because the idea is that you or anyone you live with can easily pull it out whenever you need it" [#79, p. 76].
4. Include instructions. Consider keeping a set of instructions in the toolkit for your partner, a loved one or even yourself [#79, p. 76].
