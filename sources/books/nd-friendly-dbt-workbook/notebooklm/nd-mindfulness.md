## Mindfulness adapted: attention, interoception and aphantasia differences, when to use it, and reflection

### What this covers

This topic is the book's introduction to its Mindfulness section and its closing summary. It defines mindfulness and its place in DBT, explains why conventional practices can be hard for neurodivergent people, lists what mindfulness can help with, gives practical tips for practising it in a neurodivergent-friendly way, matches three mindfulness skills to the situations in which to use them, and ends with reflection questions. According to the author, it matters because mindfulness is often seen as necessary for using the emotional regulation and distress tolerance skills, yet "everyone responds to mindfulness differently" [#19, #20, #37, pp. 16, 17, 34].

### Core ideas

- Mindfulness is foundational in DBT. It "is often seen as being necessary to utilise emotional regulation and distress tolerance skills" [#19, p. 16].
- Definition with a non-judgement core. Mindfulness is being present in the moment, aware of what is happening within and outside us — and "Most importantly, we do this without judgement" [#19, p. 16].
- It can be genuinely hard for neurodivergent people. Especially for those who already struggle with attention or interoception; visualising and breathing-based practices may be uncomfortable or difficult; and people with aphantasia cannot use visualisation-based exercises effectively [#19, p. 16].
- No right or wrong way. The workbook therefore offers exercises beyond breathing and visualisation, and the author insists on figuring out what works for you [#19, #20, pp. 16–17].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### What mindfulness can help with
The book's complete list of what mindfulness can help with [#19, p. 16]:
1. focusing and paying attention
2. allowing intrusive or judgemental thoughts to pass
3. making intentional choices and decisions
4. identifying your needs
5. recognising when you're reaching sensory overload
6. increasing self-awareness
7. regulating emotions
8. coping with rejection and criticism
9. practicing compassion

#### Tips for practising mindfulness
"When practicing mindfulness, here's some things to remember" — the complete set of the author's tips [#20, p. 17]:
1. One skill at a time. You don't have to try all these skills at once; practise one skill at a time [#20, p. 17].
2. Do not judge yourself. Let any thoughts, emotions or sensations pass "with compassion and an open mind" [#20, p. 17].
3. Drifting attention is okay. If your attention drifts, try to guide yourself back, or incorporate stimming into your mindfulness activity [#20, p. 17].
4. Eyes can stay open. You don't need to close your eyes; you can stare at a spot on the ground if that feels more natural or comfortable [#20, p. 17].
5. No right or wrong way. "There's actually no right or wrong way to be mindful" — figure out what works for you, for example focusing on an external object or selecting movement-based activities [#20, p. 17].

A two-column table matching each mindfulness skill taught in the section to the situations it suits [#20, p. 17]:

| Mindful appreciation | when you need a reminder; when you need to practice being in the present |
| Self-check in | when you need to figure out your needs; when you're reaching a point of distress or meltdown |

#### Mindfulness summary reflection questions
At the end of the Mindfulness section the book asks you to reflect, in writing, on four questions [#37, p. 34]:
1. What mindfulness practices are most helpful for you?
2. What are some signs that you need to use these skills?
3. Can you identify any barriers or accommodations needed in using these skills?
4. What reminders do you need to use these mindfulness tools?

The purpose is to consolidate personal learning: which practices work, how to recognise the moment to use them, what gets in the way or needs adapting, and how to remember to use them [#37, p. 34].

### Detailed insights

#### Why standard mindfulness can be hard for neurodivergent people
The author acknowledges that mindfulness can sound difficult, "that's because it really can be - especially if you're someone who might already struggle with attention or interoception." Some practices, "such as visualising and breathing-based mindfulness, may also be uncomfortable or difficult for some people." Rather than treating this as failure, the author asks readers "to remember (and celebrate!) that everyone responds to mindfulness differently" [#19, p. 16].

#### Aphantasia
"Some neurodivergent individuals, especially autistic folks, may experience aphantasia, an inability to visualise something in one's mind. For these folks, mindfulness exercises that rely on visualisation aren't really effective." This is the stated reason the workbook includes exercises that "explore mindfulness in other ways besides breathing and visualisation" [#19, p. 16].

### Notable quotes

> "To put simply, mindfulness is the ability to be present in the moment where we are aware of what's happening within us and outside of us. Most importantly, we do this without judgement." — Sonny Jane Wise [#19, p. 16]

> "There's actually no right or wrong way to be mindful - it's important to figure out what works for you like focusing on an external object or selecting movement based activities." — Sonny Jane Wise [#20, p. 17]

## Wise Mind: balancing logical mind and emotional mind

### What this covers

This topic covers the book's opening framework in the Mindfulness section: DBT's three states of mind — the logical mind, the emotional mind and the wise mind [#21, p. 18]. It describes each state, what each is good for, a diagram of what each mind is "ruled by" [#22, p. 19], a worksheet for describing your own three minds [#23, p. 20], and four strategies for practising engaging wise mind [#24, p. 21]. According to the book it matters because we can get stuck in logical or emotional mind, and while both have strengths, difficulties occur when we remain in either one [#21, p. 18].

### Core ideas

- Three states of mind. "In DBT, there are three states of mind. These are described as: the logical mind, the emotional mind and the wise mind." (In DBT there are three states of mind: logical, emotional and wise.) [#21, p. 18]
- Wise mind takes the best of both. The goal of wise mind is to take the best of both worlds from the logical and the emotional mind [#21, p. 18].
- Wise mind is individual. The author frames wise mind not as one fixed state but as something each person defines: "We all have our own unique wise mind." [#21, p. 18]
- Neither logic nor emotion is the enemy. Both logical and emotional mind have strengths and benefits; the problem is getting stuck in one and remaining there [#21, p. 18].
- Wise mind can be practised. Mindfulness, curiosity, compassion and considering your values are offered as ways to engage it [#24, p. 21].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### The three states of mind
Purpose: to recognise which state of mind you are in, appreciate what each is useful for, and aim for a wise mind that combines both [#21, p. 18]. The book notes that "Often, for whatever reason, we can get stuck in our logical mind or emotional mind" (often, for whatever reason, we get stuck in one of them) [#21, p. 18]. The diagram on the next page adds adjectives and what each mind is "ruled by" [#22, p. 19]; both are merged below.

1. Logical mind. "A state of mind that is based on facts, reason and logic." In the logical mind, emotions are not taken into consideration; when using it you might be looking at things analytically [#21, p. 18].
   - *Might be good for:* planning, researching, making decisions, analysing, control, grounding, learning skills [#21, p. 18].
   - *Diagram descriptors:* cautious, analytical, logical, factual [#22, p. 19].
   - *Ruled by:* facts, logic, control [#22, p. 19].
2. Emotional mind. "A state of mind during which your emotions influence your behaviour and thoughts." In the emotional mind it can be hard to access the logical mind and think clearly; you may act impulsively and intensely [#21, p. 18].
   - *Might be good for:* passion, motivation, creativity, empathy, adapting, relationships, communication [#21, p. 18].
   - *Diagram descriptors:* impulsive, creative, passionate, mood-dependent [#22, p. 19].
   - *Ruled by:* urges, feelings, emotions [#22, p. 19].
3. Wise mind. "A state of mind that's a combination of both the logical mind and emotional mind." In wise mind "we respect and listen to both our emotional and logical mind" (we respect and listen to both) [#21, p. 18].
   - *Might be good for:* communicating, making decisions, responding to events/situations, relationships, balance [#21, p. 18].
   - *Diagram descriptors:* "THE MIDDLE PATH" and "INNER WISDOM" [#22, p. 19].
   - *Ruled by:* choice, facts, reason, values, emotion [#22, p. 19].

Note that "making decisions" appears as a strength of both the logical mind and the wise mind, and "relationships" and "communication/communicating" as strengths of both the emotional mind and the wise mind [#21, p. 18].

#### Finding Your Wise Mind worksheet

#### Engaging the Wise Mind — four strategies
Purpose: "Below are some strategies you can use to practice engaging your wise mind." (strategies to practise engaging wise mind) [#24, p. 21].
1. Mindfulness. Notice your feelings, reactions, thoughts and any sensations happening in your body. Observe what is happening "without any judgement or criticism" [#24, p. 21].
2. Curiosity. Investigate what is happening with curiosity and compassion. Ask yourself: "What is this reaction trying to tell me?" and "What do I need right now?" [#24, p. 21]
3. Compassion. Practise being compassionate, caring and understanding with yourself, "just like you would a friend". The book adds: "You can even have a conversation with yourself out loud." [#24, p. 21]
4. Consider your values. "One way to engage the wise mind is to remind yourself of your values." Check: "Does the action, behaviour or choice align with a value?" [#24, p. 21]

## Identifying your values and a value action plan

### What this covers

This topic covers the book's three "Identify your values" pages in the Mindfulness section: a definition of values and why they help, reflection questions and a list of example values, a worksheet for sorting values by life area, and a Value Action Plan for putting values into practice. According to the author, discovering your values can be helpful in stressful situations such as work, studying or relationships because values guide your choices and actions [#25, #26, #27, pp. 22–24].

### Core ideas

- Values give meaning and priorities. Values are your morals, ethics, principles, or what you value and how you aspire to live; they give life meaning and importance and help you define your priorities. Everyone has different values [#25, p. 22].
- Values help under stress. Knowing and remembering your values helps with making choices, tolerating stressful situations, and responding to your needs accordingly [#25, p. 22].
- Values can differ by life area. "It's okay if you value different things in different areas of your life!" [#26, p. 23]
- Values become real through actions. The action plan asks what you can do to prioritise your values — small everyday things or things you want to remember to do more of [#27, p. 24].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Values reflection questions
Purpose: questions "to help you identify what's important to you." Answer each in writing or in thought [#25, p. 22]:
1. When were you happiest? What were you doing?
2. When were you most proud?
3. When have you been most satisfied and fulfilled?
4. What are your special interests?
5. What do you feel strongly about?
6. Have there been times you have stood up for something?

#### Explore the example values
The book offers examples of different values to explore: "See if you identify with some of them or any of them. You can also add your own." The complete list of 18 [#25, p. 22]:

2. pleasure
3. courage
4. wisdom
5. strength
6. freedom
7. authenticity
8. creativity
9. honesty
10. passion
11. empathy
12. fairness
13. adventure
14. compassion
15. security
16. learning
17. humour
18. kindness

#### Identify your values worksheet (by life area)
Purpose: "It might be helpful to identify what your values look like." The worksheet has four boxes to fill in with the values that apply in each area, accepting that they can differ between areas [#26, p. 23]:
1. My social values — the values that apply in your social life.
2. My personal values — the values that apply to you personally.
3. My political values — the values that apply in politics.
4. My work values — the values that apply at work.

(!) The book gives only the four headings; the glosses after each heading simply restate the area name [#26, p. 23].

#### Value Action Plan
Purpose: answer "What can you do to prioritise your values in your life?" The actions can be "small everyday things or things you might want to remember to do more of in your life" (the book prints "These actions come be small" [sic]) [#27, p. 24].

How to fill it in: the worksheet repeats the same pair of boxes three times, so you can plan for three values [#27, p. 24]:
1. Identify your value — write one value (for example one you chose from the list or your own).
2. Identify some actions that align with your value — write the concrete actions that put that value into practice.
3. Repeat for a second and a third value.

### Detailed insights

#### Why values matter in DBT-style coping
The author places values within mindfulness and connects them to everyday stress: in stressful situations "such as work, studying or relationships", values can "help guide your choices and actions". Knowing and remembering them supports three things: making choices, tolerating stressful situations, and responding to your needs accordingly. [#25, p. 22].

## Self check-in: noticing emotions, body, energy, breathing and focus, then acting on it

### What this covers

This topic covers the book's Self Check-in skill from the Mindfulness section: a set of prompts for noticing your emotions, thoughts, mood, energy, breathing, focus and body, followed by a "What next?" page that pairs each area with concrete actions to take. According to the author, "Checking in with ourselves is probably the most helpful thing we can do every day", because it lets you figure out your needs in the moment and use that information to take care of yourself [#29, #30, pp. 26–27].

### Core ideas

- Check in daily to find your needs. When we check in, we can figure out what our needs are in the moment and use that information to respond to them and take care of ourselves [#29, p. 26].
- Basic and sensory needs both count. The skill can be used for basic needs — making sure you've had some water or eaten food — and to determine whether you need a sensory break or support [#29, p. 26].
- Noticing leads to acting. The check-in is paired with a menu of actions for each area, with space to write your own [#30, p. 27].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Self check-in prompts
Purpose: "You can use the prompts below to guide your self check-in." Work through them in order, noticing (and optionally writing) your answer to each [#29, p. 26]:
1. What emotions am I feeling right now? [#29, p. 26]
2. What are these feelings trying to tell me? [#29, p. 26]
3. How is my body reacting? [#29, p. 26]
4. What am I thinking right now? [#29, p. 26]
5. How is my mood? — sub-questions: how am I feeling? are my emotions fleeting or long lasting? [#29, p. 26]
6. How is my energy? — sub-questions: am I tired, energetic or average? do I need to eat something or rest? [#29, p. 26]
7. How is my breathing? — sub-questions: am I breathing fast or slow? am I exhaling from my stomach or chest? [#29, p. 26]
8. How is my focus? — sub-questions: am I able to concentrate or am I distracted? am I able to focus for a long or short time? [#29, p. 26]
9. How is my body doing? — sub-questions: do I have any tension or pain? do I need to rest my body? [#29, p. 26]

## Accessible mindfulness exercises: senses, music, stimming, anchor objects, pets and mindful appreciation

### What this covers

This topic gathers every concrete mindfulness practice the book offers for neurodivergent people: prompts for mindful appreciation, ideas for observing with each sense, a series of "accessible mindfulness exercises" that move attention outward (environment, music, objects, stimming, eating, clay, the sound of the breath), the personal-anchor method with its prompts, and mindfulness with pets. According to the author, it matters because everyone has a different brain and responds to mindfulness differently — some people find visualising, breathing-based practice or focusing on internal sensations difficult, uncomfortable or even triggering, and need techniques that work for them [#32, p. 29].

### Core ideas

- Different brains, different practices. The author says it is important "to acknowledge and even celebrate" that everyone responds to mindfulness differently; if a practice does not work for you, that is not a failure on your part [#32, p. 29].
- Move attention outward when inward is hard. Many exercises deliberately direct attention to something outside yourself — the environment, music, objects, a pet — because tuning into yourself, sitting in silence, or focusing on breathing and body checks can be uncomfortable or triggering for some people [#32, #35, pp. 29, 32].
- Stimming and sensory joy are legitimate mindfulness tools. Stimming can help us focus, ground and regulate; sensory items, fidgets, special interests and pets are all used as anchors to the present [#28, #34, #36, pp. 25, 31, 33].
- Practise gently and check in. While exploring, check how your body responds and what feelings come up before, during or after a practice [#32, p. 29].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Mindful appreciation prompts — "neurodivergent style"
Purpose: the author notes that it can be really difficult to remember the good things in your life, "especially when as neurodivergent individuals, we might struggle with permanence or emotion flooding." The page therefore provides prompts to help you remember things that bring you joy or comfort. It is a fill-in page with four headings to write under [#28, p. 25]:
1. Current special interests or hyperfixations — list what you are into right now [#28, p. 25].
2. Your favourite sensory or fidget items — list the objects that feel good to use [#28, p. 25].
3. Your favourite ways to stim — list the stims you enjoy [#28, p. 25].
4. People who have made you happy lately — list recent people who brought you happiness [#28, p. 25].

#### Observing with your senses
Purpose: "some ideas to get you started observing using your senses, with room to add your own" — the worksheet gives examples for four senses and leaves space for your own ideas [#31, p. 28].

Observe with your eyes [#31, p. 28]:
- Go for a walk in a park and observe the birds and nature.
- Find an object in your room or backyard and observe the details.
- Lay on the ground watching the clouds float by.
- Set up a light projector and focus on the moving lights.

Observe using taste [#31, p. 28]:
- Suck on a lollipop or hard candy and observe the flavours — "Bonus if it has layers!"
- Drink a cup of tea and observe the flavours and temperature.

Observe using sound [#31, p. 28]:
- Listen to music and observe each note and instrument.
- Take a walk and listen to the sounds of nature.
- Put on white noise, waves, or whale music.

Observe using smell [#31, p. 28]:
- Light some candles and breathe in, noticing the scent.
- Take a walk, focusing on the scents of nature.
- As you cook a meal, focus on the smell of the ingredients.

(No touch section is given on this page; touch-based practices appear in the exercises below.) [#31, p. 28]

#### Accessible mindfulness exercises
Purpose: alternatives for people for whom visualising, breathing-based practices, or focusing on internal sensations like breathing or body checks do not work. The book presents them in this order [#32, #33, #34, pp. 29–31]:

1. Use your environment. When: you find yourself being pulled into your inner thoughts and sensations during mindfulness. How: ground yourself by focusing on your surroundings and naming objects — "like the books on your shelf, the patterns in the rug, the number of tiles in the bathroom." If it helps, name the objects out loud or focus on the details [#32, p. 29].
2. Mindfully listen to music. Why: "Tuning into ourselves and sitting in silence can be uncomfortable or even triggering for some people"; mindful listening directs attention from your internal experience to something outside yourself. How: pay attention to what you are hearing — the layers of the music, the complexities, the sounds of each instrument [#32, p. 29].
3. Sound of your breath. How: hold your hand in front of your mouth and exhale so you can hear the sound of your breath; keep exhaling and inhaling while maintaining the sound. Concentrate on the sound of your breathing rather than counting breaths, "letting the sounds be your anchor." This makes the breath an external, audible focus rather than an internal sensation [#33, p. 30].
4. Compare two. How: find two similar objects — a pair of shoes, a couple of pillows, two mugs on your coffee table, or a pair of candles next to each other — and focus on noticing their differences: the shape and height, the texture, the colour and the small details of each [#33, p. 30].
5. Visual stimming. How: trace patterns in the environment around you with your finger or your eyes, like the cracks in the ceiling or the patterns in the rug. Alternative: choose a word or even a sentence and trace it in front of you, focusing on the way your finger moves through the air [#33, p. 30].
6. Explore with play-doh, clay or plasticine. How: explore the modelling material with your hands and notice the texture, the smell and the colour; experience it "free from any expectation." As you knead it, focus on any changes and how those changes feel; roll it into a ball or flatten it with your hand [#34, p. 31].
7. Mindful eating. How: pick a fruit you can peel with your hands — a banana, an orange, or the author's favourite, a mandarin. As you peel it, notice the texture and what the layer reveals underneath; with each bite notice the taste, texture, smell, colours and temperature [#34, p. 31].
8. Mindful stimming. Why: the book asks, "Did you know that stimming can help us to focus and ground ourselves, as well as help us to regulate?" Sometimes freeing the stim is "the perfect way to create harmony and calm within us and bring us into the present moment." How: try stim dancing or using some fidget items [#34, p. 31].

#### Find a personal anchor
Purpose: breathing is a common anchor in mindfulness, "but when we can't trust or use our breathing, we can use a physical anchor like an object." Find an anchor that works for you — it may be a personal object or a sensory object [#35, p. 32].

How: sit with the anchor and explore it with whatever senses you prefer; use it to practise mindfulness and ground yourself to the present moment. The book's complete list of helpful prompts [#35, p. 32]:
1. What are your eyes or hands drawn to first?
2. How does the object feel in your hand? Focus on the weight of the object…
3. Is the object stationary or is it moving around?
4. What does the surface of the object or item look like? Focus on the texture… Shiny? Dull? Multi-textured?
5. Does the surface have one colour or multiple colours? Try looking at it in a different light or from a different angle…
6. Does the object have round or sharp corners?
7. Does the object have multiple surfaces? Notice how they all connect…
8. Can you feel the difference in the surfaces and textures?
9. Is the object rigid or is it bendable?
10. What do you notice that's unique about it?

#### Mindfulness with pets
Three ways to use a pet for mindfulness [#36, p. 33]:
1. Observe your pet. Observe your pet and the way they look at you or the way they make you feel — "What do you notice about your pet?" Describe how your pet looks by focusing on the texture of their fur, feathers or scales and the way their coat feels. Participate by staying in the moment with your pet — perhaps talk to them or try cuddling them to stay present. [#36, p. 33]
2. Your senses with your pet. "You probably do this already if you have a soft and cuddly friend": when you're upset, hold or cuddle them and use your senses to focus on the way they feel, the way they sound when they purr, and the way they smell, "especially if you bury your face in their fur." The author jokes: "...Maybe don't taste your pet ;)" [#36, p. 33]
3. Activities with your pet. The author finds it easier to be mindful while doing an activity ("I know I do!"). Do an activity with your pet — washing them, grooming them or taking them for a walk — and focus on remaining present in the activity with your pet [#36, p. 33].

### Detailed insights

#### How to approach these practices
"Remember, just because certain practices don't work for you doesn't mean you're doing it wrong - it just means it doesn't work for you and you need to find different techniques that do work for you." While exploring, the author asks you to be gentle and check in with yourself: ask how your body is responding, and what feelings come up before, during or after the practices. Some people may find visualising and breathing-based practices difficult or uncomfortable; some may struggle to focus on internal sensations like breathing or body checks — "and that's okay too" [#32, p. 29].

#### Why appreciation needs prompts for neurodivergent people
The author links difficulty remembering good things to struggles with "permanence" and "emotion flooding"; writing down special interests, sensory/fidget items, stims and people creates a record you can come back to [#28, p. 25].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- Tuning into yourself and sitting in silence "can be uncomfortable or even triggering for some people" — hence the outward-facing alternatives [#32, p. 29].
- Be gentle with yourself and check in on body responses and feelings before, during and after each practice [#32, p. 29].
- (!) UNCLEAR: "permanence" in the mindful appreciation page is not defined [#28, p. 25].
