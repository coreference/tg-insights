## Understanding emotions: their role, the emotional triangle and emotional responses

### What this covers

This topic opens the book's Emotional Regulation section: what emotions are for, how they send their messages through body, mind and behaviour, and how an emotional response unfolds from trigger to expression [#55–57, pp. 52–54]. According to the author, understanding how emotional responses work "can be empowering and helpful when it comes to identifying them and regulating them", and recognising emotions is a helpful skill when learning to regulate them [#56, #57, pp. 53–54].

### Core ideas

- No good or bad emotions. "Emotions aren't good or bad. They all serve a purpose, even if the emotion itself isn't entirely pleasant to feel." Their overarching role is communication — giving information and drawing attention to important things happening around and to us [#55, p. 52].
- Listen, but don't always act. Emotions are necessary for survival, so it is important to at least listen to them, but that "doesn't mean we have to always act on them" [#55, p. 52].
- Emotions are messengers that get louder when ignored. Like a phone notification that keeps beeping until you attend to it, ignored emotions produce sensations, urges and thoughts that get louder, harder to ignore and more difficult to manage [#56, p. 53].
- Responses can rest on unhelpful factors. Emotions are useful messengers, but "sometimes our responses can be based on other factors that might not be helpful in the moment" — such as interpretations shaped by beliefs and RSD [#57, p. 54].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### The role of emotions — four functions
1. Communication. Emotions are a form of communication that lets us communicate verbally and non-verbally with other people; they also communicate with ourselves by providing information and input [#55, p. 52].
2. Motivation. Emotions motivate and prepare us for action. Per the book, they "help activate and deactivate systems like perception, memory, attention, learning, and motivation" [#55, p. 52].
3. Information. Emotions give us information about a situation; they are necessary for survival, which is why we should at least listen to them, without always acting on them [#55, p. 52].
4. Influence our thoughts. Emotions are connected to thoughts and memories. The author's illustration is the movie *Inside Out*, where spheres of memories were colour coded — "It's kind of like that." This is why it is easier to think sad thoughts when sad, or remember happy memories when happy [#55, p. 52].

#### The emotional triangle
What it is: the way emotions "send us messengers" — the three things emotions can impact, arranged around the EMOTION at the centre of a triangle diagram [#56, p. 53]:
1. Behaviour — actions and urges [#56, p. 53].
2. Body — physiological reactions (physical responses) [#56, p. 53].
3. Mind — thoughts and memories [#56, p. 53].
Why it matters: when we become aware of the clues our body, behaviour and mind give us, it becomes easier to identify and figure out which emotion we might be feeling; the emotions are trying to communicate with us [#56, p. 53].

#### Emotional triangle self-observation exercise
Choose an emotion to focus on and ask yourself [#56, p. 53]:
1. "how does your body respond - what sensations do you notice?"
2. "how does your mind respond - are your thoughts racing?"
3. "how do you respond - what urge do you need to act upon?"
The answers are the clues from each corner of the triangle that help you recognise the emotion before it gets "louder" [#56, p. 53].

#### Emotional responses — how an emotion unfolds
Why: emotions are complex and involve a whole-body response; understanding the stages helps identify and regulate them [#57, p. 54]. The four stages in the book's order:
1. Prompting event. "First, there's the prompting event" — it triggers the first emotion. It is not always external (someone saying something to you, something going wrong); it can be internal, like your own thoughts, feeling tired or even sensory overload [#57, p. 54].
2. Interpretation and secondary emotion. The first emotion often triggers a second emotion in response — e.g. feeling guilty after having felt angry, or feeling jealous when we feel insecure. Being human, we also have an interpretation of the event that is influenced by many things, the book naming our beliefs and RSD among them [#57, p. 54].
3. Physical responses. Because emotions involve the whole body, there are always physical changes with any emotion: heart rate changes, temperature changes, breathing changes, and other bodily changes [#57, p. 54].
4. Expressions. Emotions and physical responses affect our thoughts, words and behaviours — the things we say or want to say and the actions we want to take — especially with intense emotions like anger, fear or anxiety [#57, p. 54].

## Alexithymia and describing your emotions

### What this covers

This topic covers the book's treatment of alexithymia — difficulty identifying, describing and expressing emotions — with practical workarounds, a model for breaking an emotional experience into four parts, a reference list of common sensations for four emotions, and a blank worksheet for recording your own [#58–61, pp. 55–58]. The author presents it as "a common experience in Autistic and ADHD individuals", which makes ordinary emotion-naming exercises hard and calls for alternative routes into one's feelings [#58, p. 55].

### Core ideas

- Alexithymia is a processing difference, not a deficit of feeling. It is "a difference in emotional processing" where we struggle identifying, describing and expressing emotions [#58, p. 55].
- The body can stand in for the missing label. People with alexithymia often rely on physical sensations to work out what they feel, so describing sensations, urges and activities is a valid way to communicate emotion [#58, #60, pp. 55, 57].
- Breaking an emotion into parts makes it identifiable. Separating trigger, interpretations, body changes and urges helps identify emotions and also lets you step back to see how to meet your needs and make accommodations [#59, p. 56].
- Emotions are personal. Everyone experiences emotions differently, with different sensations, thoughts and urges, so the book's lists are a starting point and a personal map is the goal [#61, p. 58].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Signs of alexithymia
Alexithymia "can look like" [#58, p. 55]:
1. having trouble finding the right words to describe what you're feeling;
2. difficulty expressing emotions using verbal words;
3. difficulty discriminating between emotions and bodily sensations;
4. difficulty in identifying faces and facial expressions.

#### Workarounds for alexithymia
1. Use alternative descriptions. Describe emotions indirectly in two ways. (a) *Physical sensations*: e.g. "feeling tense" to describe anger, or a sore stomach to describe anxiousness. (b) *Activities you feel like doing*: e.g. "I just want to lie in bed all day" to describe sadness, or "I want to throw something" to describe frustration [#58, p. 55].
2. Emotion wheel. There are a number of emotion wheel charts available that help identify which emotion you might be feeling. They are most helpful when you find it difficult to recognise and label an emotion, "especially if it's a complex emotion" [#58, p. 55].
3. Use music or lyrics. Music and lyrics can describe emotions. The author says they often use them both to communicate and describe their emotions and as a way to figure out their own emotions [#58, p. 55].

#### Describing your emotions — the four components
Why: when trying to identify or understand emotions, breaking down the emotional experience "can be really helpful"; it also allows us "to take a step back and see how we can meet our needs better and make any accommodations" [#59, p. 56]. Model: emotions consist of the trigger (prompting event), any interpretations, beliefs or assumptions you make, physiological changes or sensations in the body, and any urges or behaviours you feel because of the emotion [#59, p. 56]. The worksheet has four boxes, each with its prompt:
1. What was the prompting event? Describe the prompting event. Remember it can be a combination of things, and it can be an external or an internal event [#59, p. 56].
2. Interpretations. Can you identify any thoughts or emotions you might have had in response? Are there any beliefs or past experiences that might have influenced this? [#59, p. 56]
3. Physical responses. Notice physical responses and changes during the emotional response; per the book, this is helpful both for identifying emotions and for regulating them [#59, p. 56].
4. Expressions. Notice the expressions — the actions or urges you might want to act upon, or the words you want to say [#59, p. 56].

## The Window of Tolerance and emotional dysregulation in neurodivergent people

### What this covers

This topic opens the book's Emotional Regulation section. It introduces the Window of Tolerance (a concept the author credits to Dr. Dan Siegel) with three states — hyperarousal, the zone of tolerance and hypoarousal — and the complete list of signs the book gives for each, then offers a worksheet for mapping your own window. It defines emotional regulation and emotional dysregulation, lists the factors that make regulation harder (especially for neurodivergent people) and the ways dysregulation can look. According to the author, it matters because neurodivergent people often feel emotions easily and intensely, and DBT emotional regulation skills can shift you back into the zone where you function best [#62, #64, pp. 59, 61].

### Core ideas

- There is an optimal zone for each person. The Window of Tolerance is the "perfect window" between being overstimulated/overwhelmed and being understimulated/underwhelmed; inside it you can feel different emotions and still stay regulated, think clearly and get done what you need to do. Everyone's window is unique [#62, p. 59].
- Two ways out of the window. Emotions that "get too much" push you into hyperarousal; things getting you down or feeling numb push you into hypoarousal. Outside the window "things start happening" — anxiety, a fast heart, racing thoughts on one side; dissociating, numbness, tiredness on the other [#62, p. 59].
- Neurodivergent people feel more, both ways. The author says neurodivergent people often feel emotions "more easily and more intensely" — which can be "a beautiful thing" (intense joy and excitement over small things) but also means the unpleasant emotions are felt intensely too [#62, p. 59].
- Regulation keeps you in the window. Emotional regulation is important precisely because it keeps us within the optimal zone, the Window of Tolerance; dysregulation is its absence [#64, p. 61].
- Regulation is hard and is a skill. Eight listed factors (see *Factors that impact the ability to regulate emotions*) make it harder; DBT skills focus on regulation, and like every skill they take practice and finding what works for you [#64, p. 61].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Window of Tolerance (Dr. Dan Siegel, as adopted in the book)
Purpose: a way to understand our emotions by locating ourselves in one of three states — too much (hyperarousal), just right (zone of tolerance) or too little (hypoarousal). The author notes the concept "was coined by Dr. Dan Siegel and it's since been adopted to understand our emotions" [#62, p. 59].

1. Hyperarousal (above the window) — what happens when emotions get too much. The book's full list of signs [#62, p. 59]:
   - flight or fight response
   - anxiety and panic
   - fear
   - racing thoughts
   - inability to keep still
   - reactive emotions
   - shaking
   - aggression
   - defensiveness
2. Zone of tolerance (inside the window) — the regulated state where you are neither overwhelmed nor underwhelmed; "Here you can think clearly, process information and do what you need to do, so it's the ideal state to be in." Notably, being in the zone does not mean having no emotions: the list includes feeling all the emotions. The book's full list of signs [#62, p. 59]:
   - feeling safe
   - feeling calm
   - thinking clearly
   - self-soothing
   - regulating your emotions
   - insight and awareness
   - being present in the moment
   - feeling all the emotions
3. Hypoarousal (below the window) — what happens when things are getting you down or you feel numb. The book's full list of signs (the book spells it "disassociation" [sic]) [#62, p. 59]:
   - disassociation
   - unable to think
   - intrusive thoughts
   - no energy
   - depression
   - numb
   - withdrawn
   - disconnected from self
   - shutting down
   - fatigue

(!) The three lists are printed on the page as a diagram (hyperarousal above, zone of tolerance in the middle, hypoarousal below); the text extraction preserves only the headings and items, not the visual layout [#62, p. 59].

#### Your Window of Tolerance worksheet
Purpose: because everyone's zone is unique, the author suggests it "can be helpful to understand what it looks like when we're in our zone and what it looks like when we're outside our zone." Since emotions also influence our thoughts, physical sensations and our actions or urges, "there's often a lot going on and a lot of signs to notice" — so the worksheet asks you to collect your own signs across all of these channels [#63, p. 60].

How to do it — the worksheet has three boxes, each with the same prompt, "Write down the signs or things you notice..." [#63, p. 60]:
1. Hyperarousal — write down the signs or things you notice when you are above your window (your own version of the list above: thoughts, body sensations, actions/urges) [#63, p. 60].
2. Zone of tolerance — write down the signs or things you notice when you are inside your window and regulated [#63, p. 60].
3. Hypoarousal — write down the signs or things you notice when you are below your window [#63, p. 60].

The book's general lists in *Window of Tolerance* above serve as examples of what can go in each box [#62, #63, pp. 59–60].

#### Factors that impact the ability to regulate emotions
The author stresses that "Emotional regulation isn't easy at all" and lists factors that impact our ability to regulate. Two are explained in the text: for neurodivergent people, emotional sensitivity and heightened emotions make regulation harder; and if you did not have access to a safe environment or to caregivers who were emotionally regulated, you might have trouble regulating "because you didn't have an opportunity to learn" (this is what "unlearned skills" refers to). The full list [#64, p. 61]:
1. executive function
2. heightened emotions
3. biological factors
4. unlearned skills
5. sensory processing
6. masking
7. alexithymia
8. environment

#### Signs of emotional dysregulation
Definition: "If emotional regulation is the process of regulating and controlling our emotions, emotional dysregulation is the inability to regulate or control emotions, including our emotional experiences, actions and responses." The book says it can look like [#64, p. 61]:
1. impulsiveness
2. intense emotions
3. acting on urges
4. avoidance or attempts at numbing
5. poor problem-solving
6. fear of abandonment
7. extreme emotional responses
8. poor attempts at coping

### Detailed insights

#### What emotional regulation covers
The book defines emotional regulation as "basically the ability to control and manage your emotions as well as your reactions to your emotions." It spans three aspects: when you have emotions, how you experience them, and how you express or act on them. So regulation is not only about suppressing feelings — it includes the experience and the response [#64, p. 61].

#### Why neurodivergent people benefit especially
Emotional regulation skills are helpful for everyone, the author writes, but "especially for us neurodivergent individuals" because they often experience big emotions and have difficulties with executive function and with regulating emotions. This is where DBT skills "come in handy": they focus on emotional regulation and "can help shift you back to that zone of tolerance where you function best." The author adds that, "Just like every kind of skill, it takes practice and figuring out what works best for you" — i.e. no single skill is prescribed; you experiment [#64, p. 61].

> "Emotional regulation is important because it keeps us within our optimal zone- our Window of Tolerance." — Sonny Jane Wise [#64, p. 61]

> "Just like every kind of skill, it takes practice and figuring out what works best for you." — Sonny Jane Wise [#64, p. 61]

## Check the Facts and Act Intentionally (neurodivergent-friendly Opposite Action)

### What this covers

This topic covers two adapted DBT emotional regulation skills and the closing reflection of the Emotional Regulation section [#65–69, pp. 62–66]. Check the Facts is reframed so that emotions are never judged — only responses are examined for fit and helpfulness — and always preceded by checking needs [#65, p. 62]. A flowchart turns this into decisions [#66, p. 63]. Act Opposite is renamed Act Intentionally because the author found "opposite" confusing and invalidating as a neurodivergent person; the book supplies concrete intentional actions for common emotions [#67, #68, pp. 64–65].

### Core ideas

- Emotions are always valid; responses get checked. "While our emotions are always valid, our responses to our emotions sometimes need double-checking before we act on them or allow them to steer us" [#65, p. 62].
- Don't ask whether the emotion fits the facts — ask whether the response does. The focus is on whether the desired action or behaviour fits the facts and whether responding that way is helpful in the moment [#65, p. 62].
- Why responses can misfire. Responses can be set off by how we interpret or think about an event rather than the event itself, and are shaped by previous experiences, beliefs, and "sometimes, just poor emotional regulation and impulsivity" — "And that's okay" [#65, p. 62].
- Needs come first. Before checking the facts, always check your needs, "because often, our emotions are communicating a need and we need to learn to listen" [#65, p. 62].
- Acting on the emotion is sometimes right. "Ask yourself if acting on the emotion is effective - sometimes it is!" [#65, p. 62]; and "Sometimes it really does help to throw a soft toy or scream into a pillow though" [#67, p. 64].
- Intentional, not necessarily opposite. Urges often make sense (e.g. running when afraid as a survival response); when the response does not fit the facts, act intentionally — sometimes by picking the opposite of the urge [#67, p. 64].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Check the Facts — "Is it helpful?"
Purpose: to examine emotions, responses and the facts of an event to determine whether your responses are helpful; if they aren't, to become aware and figure out what would be helpful [#65, p. 62]. Step 0 — check your needs first: emotions often communicate a need, so listen for it before anything else [#65, p. 62]. Then work through the book's three prompts:
1. Identify your emotion and your response to your emotion. The response is something you want to act upon — e.g. if you're angry, you may want to scream or throw something [#65, p. 62].
2. Identify your interpretations, thoughts, assumptions and beliefs about the event or situation. This separates the event itself from how you are reading it, since responses are often triggered by the interpretation [#65, p. 62].
3. Is your response to your emotion helpful to you? Ask: will your response meet your needs? Is acting on the emotion effective? The book stresses that sometimes it is [#65, p. 62].

#### Act Intentionally (renamed "Act Opposite")
Origin: the standard DBT skill "Act Opposite" helps manage emotions by responding in an opposite way. The author says that as a neurodivergent individual they always struggled with it: they struggle to figure out what the opposite is, acting opposite isn't always helpful for them, and it often feels invalidating. They therefore renamed it "Act Intentionally", "because this skill is about exploring how to respond intentionally to your emotions" [#67, p. 64].
How it works:
1. Recognise the urge. Emotions are "important messengers" and often come with an urge or behaviour — sad: the need to isolate or cry; anger: the need to scream or throw something [#67, p. 64].
2. Check whether the urge fits. Urges often make sense for the situation, especially survival responses like running when we experience fear; but sometimes the emotional response does not fit the facts [#67, p. 64].
3. Choose an intentional action. When it doesn't fit, act intentionally, perhaps picking the opposite of the urge — e.g. walking away when angry instead of throwing something when it's not appropriate, or reaching out for company instead of isolating [#67, p. 64].
4. Allow safe expression. Sometimes it really does help to throw a soft toy or scream into a pillow [#67, p. 64].
