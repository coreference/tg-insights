## Distress tolerance basics: the STOP skill and a personal crisis plan

### What this covers

This topic gathers the book's foundation for distress tolerance: its definition and neurodivergent framing, the situations in which the skills are useful, the STOP skill, the personal crisis plan worksheet from the Everyday Well-being section, and the reflection questions that close the Distress Tolerance section [#13, #39, #53, pp. 10, 36, 50]. According to the author, these skills matter because neurodivergent people are "likely to experience high levels of stress living in a neurotypical society" [#39, p. 36].

### Core ideas

- Definition. Distress tolerance is about managing stress and distress to keep it at a manageable level — a level we can cope with [#39, p. 36].
- Why neurodivergent people need it. Living in a neurotypical society tends to bring high levels of stress; distress commonly arises during sensory overload, Rejection Sensitivity from RSD, difficulty regulating emotions, or being close to a meltdown [#39, p. 36].
- Listen first. The skills help manage and tolerate distress, but "distress tolerance should always come from a place of listening to what we need and responding accordingly" — they are not about pushing through regardless of needs [#39, p. 36].
- Prepare before the crisis. During a crisis or high stress we may have a harder time remembering important and basic things, so a plan written in advance acts as a reminder [#13, p. 10].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### When to use distress tolerance skills
The book lists five situations in which distress tolerance skills can be used — when you are [#39, p. 36]:
1. overwhelmed by stressors like multiple tasks;
2. feeling the urge to act on negative emotions;
3. feeling overwhelmed and you can't think clearly;
4. experiencing emotional or sensory overload;
5. close to a meltdown.

#### STOP skill
Purpose: when you are feeling overwhelmed or distressed, remember STOP "so you can proceed mindfully and intentionally" rather than reacting [#39, p. 36]. The four letters, in order:
1. S — Stop, pause, and don't react. The first move is to halt the automatic reaction — not acting on the urge in the moment [#39, p. 36].
2. T — Take a step back, walk away and take a deep breath. Create physical and mental distance from the situation and use a deep breath to settle [#39, p. 36].
3. O — Observe: notice your surroundings and how you feel. Take in both what is around you (relevant to sensory triggers) and your internal state [#39, p. 36].
4. P — Proceed mindfully: what can you do? Having paused and observed, choose a deliberate next action by asking what you can do [#39, p. 36].
(!) INCOMPLETE: the book gives each step as a one-line instruction only; it does not add examples or pitfalls for the individual steps [#39, p. 36].

#### Personal crisis plan (worksheet)
Purpose: a plan "ready to go" that you can use as a reminder whenever you have reached a point of distress, because in a crisis or times of high stress it can be harder to remember the important and basic things [#13, p. 10]. How to do it: fill in each section in advance, when calm; then consult it when distressed. The worksheet's sections, each to be completed by you [#13, p. 10]:
1. "I know I'm triggered or in need of support when:" — your personal signs that you are triggered or need support.
2. "Three ways I can distract myself are:" — three specific distractions you can turn to.
3. "Safe people I can call or message:" — with fields for each person's Name and Contact details, so you do not have to look them up in the moment.
4. "Coping tools or skills that help are:" — the tools or skills you know work for you.

#### Distress tolerance summary (reflection)
Purpose: the closing page of the Distress Tolerance section, for personalising what you have learned [#53, p. 50]. Answer each question in writing:
1. "Which distress tolerance skills are most helpful for you?"
2. "What are some signs that you need to use these skills?"
3. "Can you identify any barriers or accommodations needed in using these skills?"
4. "What reminders do you need to use your distress tolerance skills?"
These questions push you to pick your own skills, recognise your early warning signs, anticipate obstacles and needed accommodations, and plan prompts to actually use the skills [#53, p. 50].

## Calming the body fast: mammalian diving reflex and the TIPP skill

### What this covers

Two fast, body-based distress-tolerance tools: using the mammalian diving reflex (cold on the face plus breath-holding) to switch off the fight-or-flight response, and the DBT TIPP skill. Both aim to bring quick physiological relief when distress, panic or overwhelm is high. The author explains that the physical symptoms of fight-or-flight make the experience "even more distressing", so lowering them through the body is their "first go-to" [#40, p. 37], and that TIPP's relief lets you "think more clearly, process information and utilise other skills" [#42, p. 39].

### Core ideas

- Cold water on the face calms you because of a built-in reflex. The mammalian diving reflex is an innate survival response, present in everyone, that the author says also explains why breathing exercises help anxiety [#40, p. 37].
- Fight-or-flight symptoms feed distress; the reflex reverses them. Racing heart, uncontrolled breathing and odd limb sensations make things worse; the diving reflex activates the parasympathetic nervous system and gives immediate relief [#40, p. 37].
- You can trigger it anywhere you have something cold — no diving needed — but the cold must reach the cheeks and above the eyes, and you must hold your breath as if really diving [#40, #41, pp. 37–38].
- TIPP gives relief from the physiological side of distress first, so other skills become usable; start by trying one component and see what works [#42, p. 39].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### How the mammalian diving reflex works
1. The trigger: the reflex "activates when you're submerged in freezing water"; it's why mammals like whales and dolphins can survive in the deep ocean — "a survival mechanism, basically" [#40, p. 37].
2. The problem it solves: in anxiety, panic or distress the body enters "fight or flight" mode — heart racing, struggling to control breathing, maybe "an odd feeling in our limbs" — which makes things even more distressing [#40, p. 37].
3. The effect: the reflex activates the parasympathetic nervous system, which "creates or mimics relaxation". Result: immediate relief because the heart rate slows, breathing slows, and as blood flows away from the limbs you start sweating less and your muscles relax [#40, p. 37].
4. Practical upshot: "You can't go diving every time you're feeling overwhelmed, but (as long as you have access to something cold) you CAN activate this reflex whenever you need to." [#40, p. 37]

#### Mammalian diving reflex — Method one (bowl or sink of ice water)
1. Prepare ice-cold water. Fill a bowl or a sink with ice-cold water; "water from the tap with some ice cubes works a treat" [#41, p. 38].
2. Get comfortable. You'll need to bend over, so make sure you are comfortable — sit down if you need to [#41, p. 38].
3. Hold your breath and submerge your face in the cold water "for as long as is safe for you - try for 30 seconds" [#41, p. 38].
4. Sit back up and notice. As you sit back up and open your eyes, notice your heart rate and breathing start to slow down [#41, p. 38].

#### Mammalian diving reflex — Method two (soft ice pack)
The author prefers this method because "it's a lot more simple and quicker to do especially when I'm in a panic" [#41, p. 38].
1. Prepare in advance. Keep a soft ice pack in the freezer "for times like this"; a bag of frozen peas also works [#41, p. 38].
2. Sit so you can lean forward [#41, p. 38].
3. Cover your eyes with the ice pack, lean forward and hold your breath, counting to 20 or 30 seconds in your mind [#41, p. 38].
4. Release and breathe out. As you lean back and remove the ice pack, breathe out slowly and notice your heart rate slowing down [#41, p. 38].

Key rule for both methods: "you need to hold your breath as if you were truly diving for it to activate the reflex. The longer you can hold your breath (safely), the better the results." [#41, p. 38]

#### TIPP skill
Purpose: for "moments of distress, panic or when you're feeling overwhelmed"; it provides relief from the physiological effects of distress or emotions so you can think more clearly, process information and use other skills. How to start: try one of the four components and see what works [#42, p. 39].

1. T — Temperature (to calm down immediately): use an ice pack or something frozen; lean over and hold your breath; hold for 30 seconds. This is the diving-reflex technique in brief (see *Method two*) [#42, p. 39].
2. I — Intense exercise (to calm your body down): engage in intense exercise for a short period of time to expend your body's stored-up energy; the author adds "stim dancing is a great way!" [#42, p. 39].
3. P — Paced breathing (slow down your breathing): breathe in for four seconds, hold for four, breathe out for four; then figure out what pace works for you — "3, 5, 7 works too!" [#42, p. 39].
4. P — Paired muscle relaxation (to help relax your body): work on one section of your body at a time; while breathing in, tense your muscles; while breathing out, relax them [#42, p. 39].

### Detailed insights

#### The author's personal verdict
The author calls the mammalian diving reflex "my favourite tool in my coping toolbox" and their "first go-to" because it lowers fight-or-flight responses through an innate physiological response — "Super handy, hey?" Between the two methods they prefer the ice pack, as it's simpler and quicker in a panic [#40, #41, pp. 37–38].

### Notable quotes

> "Using the mammalian diving reflex is my favourite tool in my coping toolbox." — Sonny Jane Wise [#40, p. 37]

> "Remember, you need to hold your breath as if you were truly diving for it to activate the reflex." — Sonny Jane Wise [#41, p. 38]

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- Safety, verbatim: "If you have blood pressure or heart problems, please speak to your medical professional before trying this skill." [#40, p. 37]
- For the skill to work, "you need to ensure the area around your cheeks and above your eyes receive the input from the water" [#40, p. 37].
- Hold your breath only "for as long as is safe for you"; longer holding gives better results only when done "(safely)" [#41, p. 38].
- Source: the diving reflex page is "Adapted from Linehan, M. (June 6-7, 2011). Updates to emotion regulation and crisis survival skills in dialectical behavior therapy. Austin, TX: Behavior Tech, LLC." [#40, p. 37]
- The claim that the diving reflex is "actually why" breathing exercises help anxiety is the author's explanation; no further evidence is given [#40, p. 37].


## Managing meltdowns: signs, aftercare, prevention, Meltdown Mapping and a tip sheet for loved ones

### What this covers

The distress-tolerance section's part on meltdowns, which the author added to DBT to make it neurodivergent friendly: what meltdowns are and what causes them, how they can look, why compassion and aftercare matter, a set of prevention tips, and two worksheets — one to map your own meltdowns and one to tell loved ones how to support you. According to the author, mapping meltdowns, triggers and warning signs "can help us better respond to and manage our meltdowns as well as find ways to prevent or minimise them" [#43, p. 40].

### Core ideas

- A meltdown is involuntary. It is a response that can occur in a time of crisis or distress in which "we often lose control of our behaviour and responses" [#43, p. 40].
- It signals a surpassed limit. Meltdowns often happen when we become dysregulated or overstimulated by stress, sensory input, our emotions and other factors, because "our body and brain is communicating to us that we have reached (and surpassed) a limit" [#43, p. 40].
- It is never just one trigger. Others may see a meltdown follow a single event, but meltdowns "are always due to ongoing combination of factors that lead up to the big show" [#43, p. 40].
- Meltdowns are not tantrums. The author says this explicitly [#43, p. 40].
- Not intentional, so meet it with compassion. Meltdowns can lead to behaviours that may cause hurt, but those behaviours are not intentional "because we aren't in control" — which is why it's important to hold compassion for yourself when a meltdown happens [#43, p. 40].
- Aftercare is essential. Meltdowns are "super tiring and emotionally draining", so taking care of yourself afterwards is really important [#43, p. 40].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Signs of a meltdown
Meltdowns can look like (the complete list) [#43, p. 40]:

2. throwing things [#43, p. 40]
3. crying or loss of verbal words [#43, p. 40]
4. self-injury like hitting your head [#43, p. 40]
5. rocking back and forth [#43, p. 40]
6. shutting down [#43, p. 40]
7. inability to communicate [#43, p. 40]
8. pacing or escaping [#43, p. 40]
9. hyperventilating [#43, p. 40]
10. physically curling inwards [#43, p. 40]

## Sensory self-soothing, IMPROVE the environment, stims and special interests as regulation

### What this covers

This part of the book's Distress Tolerance section gives four sensory- and environment-based tools for getting through distress: sensory self-soothing through the five senses (with a suggestion list and a personal worksheet), the author's neurodivergent version of a classic DBT skill, "IMPROVE the environment", a list of stims to try, and special interests (SpIns) used as a coping strategy [#47–52, pp. 44–49]. The author treats these as especially important for neurodivergent people because of their sensory differences and because the environment itself often contributes to sensory overload, meltdowns and emotional overwhelm [#47, #49, pp. 44, 46].

### Core ideas

- The senses are a direct route to relief. Focusing on the senses can bring comfort and even joy, reduce the intensity of emotions and distract from overwhelming thoughts and feelings; the author calls sensory self-soothing "one of the most helpful distress tolerance skills in my opinion" for neurodivergent people with sensory differences [#47, p. 44].
- Change the environment, not just the moment. Instead of the standard DBT "IMPROVE the Moment", the book focuses on improving the environment, because surroundings often drive overload and meltdowns — especially relevant when you cannot simply leave [#49, p. 46].
- Distraction is not invalidation. None of the distraction tools are meant to deny current emotions; they help you tolerate or distract yourself from powerful emotions until you are in a better space to deal with them more effectively [#49, p. 46].
- Stimming is a regulation tool, and a list helps because distress clouds thinking. Many neurodivergent people are used to masking or have never explored stimming, and during distress they cannot think clearly, so a ready list of stims is a starting point [#51, p. 48].
- Special interests are a legitimate coping strategy. SpIns are a source of well-being, coping and community; engaging with them distracts, regulates and brings positive feelings, so they "should be utilised as a positive strategy in times of need" [#50, #52, pp. 47, 49].
- Everyone is different. Take time to find what works for you; the lists are suggestions to get started [#47, p. 44].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Sensory self-soothing — suggestions by sense
What it is: a distress tolerance skill whose aim "is to relieve any distress or overwhelm by soothing ourselves with our senses" [#47, p. 44]. Why it works (per the author): focusing on the senses provides comfort and even joy, reduces the intensity of what you feel, and distracts from overwhelming thoughts and feelings [#47, p. 44]. How to use: the author stresses everyone is different, so take time to figure out what works for you; the following suggestions are "to get you started" [#47, p. 44]. The complete suggestion list, by sense:

1. Look [#47, p. 44]
   - go for a walk in nature
   - "walk a feel-good movie" [sic] — i.e. watch a feel-good movie
   - look at funny memes
   - watch TikTok videos
2. Taste [#47, p. 44]
   - sip on a cup of tea
   - bake cookies
   - focus on a hard candy
3. Hear [#47, p. 44]
   - listen to a calming playlist
   - listen to an angry playlist (the book offers this alongside the calming one — soothing does not have to mean calm music)
   - call a friend
   - listen to music as a distraction
4. Smell [#47, p. 44]
   - light a candle
   - bake cookies (listed under both taste and smell)
   - keep a favourite scent on hand
5. Touch [#47, p. 44]
   - have a shower or a bubble bath
   - cuddle your pet
   - use a weighted blanket

#### Sensory self-soothing worksheet
Purpose: a personal list of "objects, smells or sounds that ground you and calm you" [#48, p. 45]. Layout: five fill-in boxes headed VISUAL, TASTE, SOUND, SMELL and TOUCH; in each you write the things that soothe you through that sense [#48, p. 45]. How to use it: when you need it, come back to the list as a reminder; you can also share it with your partner, family, parents or your doctor so they can support you too [#48, p. 45]. The point of writing it in advance is that it can be consulted (and others can act on it) when you are already distressed [#48, p. 45].

#### IMPROVE the environment
Origin and purpose: the author notes the familiar DBT skill "IMPROVE the Moment" — "a skill to help you tolerate emotions until they feel less intense by improving the moment in a variety of ways" — and replaces it with improving the *environment* [#49, p. 46]. The environment often contributes to sensory overload, meltdowns, distress and emotional overwhelm, so it helps to change what you can in your current environment, "especially if we can't simply remove ourselves" [#49, p. 46]. Goal: improve the environment in any way you can, to help you tolerate whatever emotions you are feeling until their intensity reduces, and to lower distress [#49, p. 46]. Key principle: the distraction tools do not invalidate or deny current emotions; they buy time until you can deal with the emotions more effectively [#49, p. 46].

Providing proprioceptive input such as a weighted blanket or a weighted pillow can bring your system to a more balanced state and help with distress [#50, p. 47].
5. Remove yourself — Sometimes the environment itself is contributing to the distress, and the best thing to do, if possible, is take a break by removing yourself from the situation or environment [#50, p. 47].
6. Remind yourself that any emotions are temporary and that you have got through times like this in the past; tell yourself "you've got this". What and why: special interests ("otherwise known as SpIns") are "a source of well-being, coping and community" and "can actually be used as a positive strategy as neurodivergent individuals"; engaging with them helps you distract yourself, regulate, and bring positive feelings [#52, p. 49]. ### Detailed insights

#### Why the environment matters for neurodivergent distress
The author's reasoning for shifting the focus from "the moment" to "the environment" is that environmental factors (light, noise, people, overstimulation) frequently cause or feed sensory overload, meltdowns, distress and emotional overwhelm. Changing them is often the most direct lever — and when leaving is impossible, adjusting what is around you is the fallback; when leaving is possible, "Remove yourself" is itself one of the components [#49, #50, pp. 46–47].

#### Sharing your soothing list with others
The self-soothing worksheet is meant not only for you but also as a communication tool: sharing it with a partner, family, parents or doctor lets them support you with the right objects, smells or sounds when you are struggling [#48, p. 45]. The "Encourage boundaries" component works the same way in the moment — asking someone else to change the environment (blinds, lights) on your behalf [#50, p. 47].
