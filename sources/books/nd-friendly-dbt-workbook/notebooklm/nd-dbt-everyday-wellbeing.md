## DBT adapted for neurodivergent people: how the workbook works and choosing a skill by emotional intensity

### What this covers

This topic covers the opening pages of the workbook: who the author is and why they wrote it, what Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT) is, how the author changed DBT to make it neurodivergent friendly, how the self-guided workbook is meant to be used, and a simple guide for matching a skill area to how intense an emotion feels. According to the author, neurodivergent people experience several kinds of distress from living in a society not designed for their differences, and learning to manage that distress is "one of the best things we can do to support ourselves" — DBT skills being one way to do it [#4, p. 1].

### Core ideas

- Neurodivergent distress is more than emotional. Besides emotional distress, neurodivergent people can experience meltdowns and sensory distress, which the author attributes to living in a society that isn't designed for their differences [#4, p. 1].
- Standard DBT doesn't always fit Autistic and ADHD people. The author holds that DBT skills can be valuable and helpful but "don't always meet the needs of neurodivergent individuals - especially Autistic and ADHD individuals"; this gap is the stated reason the workbook exists [#4, p. 1].
- The adaptation: interpersonal effectiveness is removed; a whole section on sensory needs is added; distress tolerance is extended with information and tools for managing meltdowns [#4, #5, pp. 1–2].
- DBT is not about compliance. The author stresses that DBT is not about changing who we are, but about skills to identify and regulate emotions, manage distress, and recognise, fulfil and advocate for one's needs [#4, p. 1].
- Choice is central. The workbook is self-guided, its chapters can be read in any order and any skill may be skipped — "a strong focus on choice, always choice" [#5, p. 2].
- Match the skill to the intensity. A 1–10 emotional-intensity guide suggests mindfulness for low intensity, emotional regulation for moderate, and distress tolerance for high intensity — offered only as a suggestion, because everyone is different [#5, p. 2].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### The four components of DBT and the neurodivergent friendly adaptation
DBT was founded by Dr Marsha Linehan (written "Lineham" in the book) for individuals living with Borderline Personality Disorder. It focuses on four components [#4, p. 1]:
1. Mindfulness — kept in the workbook.
2. Interpersonal effectiveness — removed. The author says this was done "in order to remain neurodivergent friendly", and repeats on the next page that it "has intentionally been left out". (!) INCOMPLETE: the book does not explain further why interpersonal effectiveness is not neurodivergent friendly [#4, #5, pp. 1–2].
3. Distress tolerance — kept and extended to include information on and tools for managing meltdowns [#4, p. 1].
4. Emotional regulation — kept [#4, p. 1].

Added in place of interpersonal effectiveness: a whole section on sensory needs [#4, #5, pp. 1–2].

#### Skill areas of the workbook
The workbook takes the reader through skills in these areas, each defined by the author [#5, p. 2]:
1. Mindfulness — "Skills to help you ground yourself, create awareness & focus on the present."
2. Distress tolerance — "Skills and tools to help you manage moments of distress, overwhelm and overload." Includes the parts on managing meltdowns.
3. Emotional regulation — "Skills to help you understand, manage and respond to your emotions."
4. Sensory needs — the added area addressing sensory needs (no one-line definition is given for it on this page).

(!) CONTRADICTION: the text says the workbook covers "three areas" but then names four (mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotional regulation and sensory needs); only three of them get a definition box on the page [#5, p. 2].

How to use the workbook: it is self-guided; the chapters can be read in any order; you are welcome to skip any skill; it is meant to let neurodivergent people learn DBT skills "in a way that works for you, at your own pace" [#4, #5, pp. 1–2].

#### Emotional intensity guide for choosing a skill
Purpose: to answer "So, how can you determine what skill you should use?" — the author created "a little guide to help you choose a skill that matches the intensity of your emotion" [#5, p. 2].

How to use it: rate how intense your emotion is on a scale of 1 to 10, then try the skill area matching that band [#5, p. 2]:

| Emotional intensity | Skills to try |
|---|---|
| 1–2 | Mindfulness |
| 3–6 | Emotional Regulation |
| 7–10 | Distress Tolerance |

1. Intensity 1–2 → Mindfulness: at low intensity, use grounding, awareness and present-focus skills [#5, p. 2].
2. Intensity 3–6 → Emotional Regulation: at moderate intensity, use skills to understand, manage and respond to the emotion [#5, p. 2].
3. Intensity 7–10 → Distress Tolerance: at high intensity, use the skills and tools for moments of distress, overwhelm and overload [#5, p. 2].

The author's caveat: "Remember, everyone is different so this is only a suggestion!" [#5, p. 2].

### Detailed insights

#### Who the author is and why it matters
The author, Sonny Jane (pronouns they/them), describes themselves as multiply neurodivergent — "an Autistic ADHDer living with Bipolar and in recovery with Borderline Personality Disorder" — and as a lived experience educator, consultant and advocate passionate about neurodiversity and supporting neurodivergent individuals. Their own BPD recovery connects to DBT's origin as a therapy for BPD, and their Autistic/ADHD experience frames the adaptation [#4, p. 1].

#### What DBT skills are for, in the author's framing
The author lists the purposes of DBT as giving people the skills to: (1) identify their emotions; (2) regulate their emotions; (3) find ways to manage their distress; and (4) recognise, fulfil and advocate for their needs. This is explicitly set against a reading of DBT as compliance training or as a way to change who neurodivergent people are [#4, p. 1].

## Self-care, a guide to my needs and the support and accommodations wheel

### What this covers

The opening of the workbook's "Everyday Well-being" section: what self-care is (meeting four kinds of needs) with example lists, a planning page for regular self-care, a fill-in sheet ("A guide to my needs") for communicating how others can help, and the Support & Accommodations wheel for mapping eight areas of life by skills, impact and support needed. The author's reason: if everyday well-being isn't looked after, "it becomes harder to regulate our emotions and we're more likely to reach a point of distress" — so self-care and support are the base on which the DBT skills rest [#7, #10, pp. 4, 7].

### Core ideas

- Self-care prevents distress. Self-care is an important part of looking after well-being and helps prevent reaching points of distress [#7, p. 4].
- Learning skills is itself tiring. Practising self-care is also helpful because "learning new skills can be exhausting and even frustrating" [#7, p. 4].
- Self-care = meeting needs, of four kinds: physical, emotional, social and sensory. Needs differ between people, and one person may need more self-care in one area than another — "and that's okay!" [#7, #8, pp. 4–5].
- Asking for support is self-care, and should be normalised. The author says "We need to normalise asking for support and accomodations as a way of looking after our well-being" [#10, p. 7].
- Make needs communicable. For people who struggle to ask for support or to express what they need, a shareable written guide and a visual wheel let them tell others what helps and where they need support [#7, #9, #10, pp. 4, 6, 7].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Four types of self-care needs
Self-care is "all about meeting our needs"; the book gives example needs for each type "to get you started", noting everyone's needs differ [#7, p. 4]:

1. Physical — taking your meds; getting enough sleep; having breaks; drinking enough water; stretching or movement [#7, p. 4].
2. Emotional — therapy; expressing boundaries; getting affection; things that make you feel good; special interests [#7, p. 4].
3. Social — visiting friends; date nights; spending time alone; curating social media; boundaries [#7, p. 4].
4. Sensory — having sensory breaks; wearing comfy clothing; avoiding busy places; using sunglasses or earplugs; stimming [#7, p. 4].

Note that "spending time alone" counts as a social need and "boundaries" appears under both emotional (expressing boundaries) and social [#7, p. 4].

#### Regular self-care worksheet
Purpose: a space to "list the things you can do on a regular basis to meet your self-care needs" — i.e. to work out what you can do to meet your needs every day [#7, #8, pp. 4–5].
How to do it: the page has four boxes — Physical, Emotional, Social, Sensory. In each, write the regular things you can do to meet that type of need (the example lists above can serve as a starting point). Expect the boxes to be uneven: "you might even find that you need to practice self-care more in one area than another" [#8, p. 5].

#### A guide to my needs worksheet
Purpose: for anyone who struggles with asking for support or has a difficult time expressing what they need. You fill it in and then use it to communicate, or share it, with "a friend, partner, caregiver or even a housemate whenever you need support" [#7, #9, pp. 4, 6].
The four prompts to complete [#9, p. 6]:
1. "I NEED YOUR HELP WHEN..." — the situations or states in which you need support. [#9, p. 6]
2. "I WILL ASK FOR HELP BY..." — how you will signal that you need help. [#9, p. 6]
3. "YOU CAN HELP ME BY..." — the concrete things the other person can do. [#9, p. 6]
4. "I WOULD LIKE TO HEAR..." — the words or phrases you would like the other person to say. [#9, p. 6]

#### Support and accommodations wheel
Purpose: a wheel model to "communicate to the people around us which areas of life we might need support and accomodations in" [#10, p. 7]. The book presents an example wheel of eight life areas, each with four example sub-areas, described as "an example of the different areas where someone may need support and accomodations" [#10, #11, pp. 7–8].

(!) UNCLEAR: the wheel is a diagram whose text was extracted as a flat sequence; the grouping below assigns sub-items to areas by their order and meaning and is a reconstruction [#10, p. 7].

1. Sleep — falling asleep; staying asleep; sleep schedules; nightmares [#10, p. 7].
2. Work/Study — due dates; instructions; flexibility; uniforms [#10, p. 7].
3. Communication — phone calls; appointments; advocacy; non-verbal [#10, p. 7].
4. Daily living — cleaning; organisation; hygiene; reminders [#10, p. 7].
5. Sensory — home; meltdowns; clothing; accommodations [#10, p. 7].
6. Finances — costs of aids; debt; impulsivity; tracking/planning [#10, p. 7].
7. Eating/Cooking — sensory; intolerances; executive function; going shopping [#10, p. 7].
8. Relationships — social rules; stigma; communication; boundaries [#10, p. 7].

The wheel is then filled in three times, each with its own 1–5 rating scale (see the three scales below): once for skills and strengths, once for how much each area impacts your life, and once for how much support you need [#11, #12, pp. 8–9].

#### Skills and strengths rating scale
Instruction: "FILL IN AREAS WHERE YOUR SKILLS AND STRENGTHS ARE" — rate each life area of the wheel [#11, p. 8]:
1. I need to work on some skills [#11, p. 8]
2. I'm developing skills/strengths [#11, p. 8]
3. I've got a few skills in this area [#11, p. 8]
4. I can name at least four [#11, p. 8]
5. I'm nailing this area [#11, p. 8]

#### Life impact rating scale
Instruction: "FILL IN THE AREAS OF YOUR LIFE THAT ARE IMPACTED THE MOST" — rate how much each area affects your quality of life [#12, p. 9]:
1. No impact on my quality of life [#12, p. 9]
2. Occasionally but minimal impact [#12, p. 9]
3. Sometimes but easy to manage [#12, p. 9]
4. Regularly interferes [#12, p. 9]
5. Uses up all my spoons [#12, p. 9]

#### Support needed rating scale
Instruction: "FILL IN AREAS OF YOUR LIFE WHERE YOU NEED THE MOST SUPPORT" — rate how much support you need in each area [#12, p. 9]:
1. I need minimal support [#12, p. 9]
2. I need support occasionally [#12, p. 9]
3. I need support day to day [#12, p. 9]
4. A carer would be nice [#12, p. 9]
5. Just do it for me, thanks [#12, p. 9]

How the three wheels work together: comparing them shows, per life area, where your strengths lie, where life is most impacted and where support is most needed — information you can then communicate to the people around you, in line with the wheel's stated purpose [#10, #11, #12, pp. 7–9].

## Affirmations for neurodivergent people and for rejection sensitive dysphoria (RSD)

### What this covers

Part of the workbook's "Everyday Well-being" section: two ready-made lists of affirmations — one for neurodivergent people in general, one for Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria (RSD) — plus a page for writing your own. The author's rationale: being neurodivergent in a neurotypical world means often hearing and internalising negative views, beliefs and stereotypes, and RSD actively reinforces messages that "we aren't good enough or no-one likes us"; the affirmations are meant to bring comfort and to combat those messages [#15, #16, pp. 12–13].

### Core ideas

- Internalised negativity comes from the mismatch with a neurotypical world. Living as a neurodivergent person in a neurotypical world exposes you to negative views, beliefs and stereotypes that you can come to internalise; neurodivergent-friendly affirmations are offered as a source of comfort against them [#15, p. 12].
- RSD is an intense, message-reinforcing reaction to rejection and criticism. It is common in neurodivergent people, especially ADHDers, and the intensity of the emotional response is what drives home beliefs of not being good enough or not being liked — hence affirmations aimed specifically at those messages [#16, p. 13].
- Your own words count. The reader is invited to write their own affirmations, return to them when needed, and even share them with someone who can say them aloud in hard moments [#17, p. 14].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Affirmations for neurodivergent folks
Purpose: to counter internalised negative views, beliefs and stereotypes absorbed from living in a neurotypical world; the author hopes they "will bring you some comfort" [#15, p. 12]. The full list (six affirmations, verbatim):
1. "It's okay if most strategies and tools that were designed for and by neurotypical people don't work for me." — permission for standard (neurotypical) tools to fail without that being a personal failing [#15, p. 12].
2. "My brain works exactly the way it was designed to, and I deserve to find ways to support my brain." — reframes the neurodivergent brain as working correctly and deserving support, not correction [#15, p. 12].
3. "I will not hide my identity and neurodivergent traits just because they make people uncomfortable." — counters masking for others' comfort [#15, p. 12].
4. "I can forget to close cabinets, brush my teeth, and reply to messages and still be worthy." — separates worth from everyday executive-function slips (cabinets, teeth, messages) [#15, p. 12].
5. "I do not have to hold myself to neurotypical standards, rules, or expectations." — rejects neurotypical norms as the yardstick [#15, p. 12].
6. "My sensory differences are real and valid, and they deserve to be accommodated." — validates sensory needs and the right to accommodation [#15, p. 12].

(The first affirmation is printed run into the introductory paragraph on the page; it reads as the first item of the list.) [#15, p. 12]

#### Affirmations for Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria
Purpose: RSD "causes an intense emotional response that reinforces messages to us that we aren't good enough or no-one likes us"; these affirmations are included "to combat these messages" [#16, p. 13]. The full list (six affirmations, verbatim):
1. "My interests are valid, and I am allowed to be excited and passionate about them even if other people aren't." — protects enthusiasm from others' lack of interest [#16, p. 13].
2. "People disagreeing with me does not mean my lived experience or my opinion are invalid." — separates disagreement from invalidation [#16, p. 13].
3. "I am allowed to ask for reassurance and I am worthy of people's time and attention." — legitimises seeking reassurance [#16, p. 13].
4. "I am allowed to ask questions and require certainty in order to feel safety and control." — legitimises needing certainty to feel safe and in control [#16, p. 13].
5. "Some people won't like me no matter what and that is not a reflection of how likeable I am." — decouples individual dislike from one's likeability [#16, p. 13].
6. "I decide what thoughts to reject and let pass." — reclaims agency over which thoughts to accept [#16, p. 13].

#### Write your own affirmations exercise
What it is: a page for writing and adding your own affirmations [#17, p. 14]. How to use it, per the author:
1. Write your own affirmations on the page (the page is open space after the prompt) [#17, p. 14].
2. Come back to the list "when you need to remind yourself or whenever you might need comfort" [#17, p. 14].
3. Optionally share it: "Maybe you can even share this list with a partner or a friend so they can say these things to you in times of need" — so that a trusted person can voice the affirmations to you when you're struggling [#17, p. 14].

### Detailed insights

#### What RSD is, according to the author
The author defines Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria as "a sensitivity to rejection and criticism that is a common experience for neurodivergent individuals, especially ADHDers". The mechanism they describe: the emotional response to rejection or criticism is intense, and that intensity reinforces negative messages about the self — that one isn't good enough, or that no-one likes them. The RSD affirmations target exactly those messages (validity of interests and opinions, worthiness of attention, likeability, and control over thoughts) [#16, p. 13].
