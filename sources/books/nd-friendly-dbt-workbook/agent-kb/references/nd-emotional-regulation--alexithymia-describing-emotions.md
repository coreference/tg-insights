# Alexithymia and describing your emotions

## What this covers

This topic covers the book's treatment of alexithymia — difficulty identifying, describing and expressing emotions — with practical workarounds, a model for breaking an emotional experience into four parts, a reference list of common sensations for four emotions, and a blank worksheet for recording your own [msg #58–61, pp. 55–58]. The author presents it as "a common experience in Autistic and ADHD individuals", which makes ordinary emotion-naming exercises hard and calls for alternative routes into one's feelings [msg #58, p. 55].

## Core ideas

- **Alexithymia is a processing difference, not a deficit of feeling.** It is "a difference in emotional processing" where we struggle identifying, describing and expressing emotions [msg #58, p. 55].
- **The body can stand in for the missing label.** People with alexithymia often rely on physical sensations to work out what they feel, so describing sensations, urges and activities is a valid way to communicate emotion [msg #58, #60, pp. 55, 57].
- **Breaking an emotion into parts makes it identifiable.** Separating trigger, interpretations, body changes and urges helps identify emotions and also lets you step back to see how to meet your needs and make accommodations [msg #59, p. 56].
- **Emotions are personal.** Everyone experiences emotions differently, with different sensations, thoughts and urges, so the book's lists are a starting point and a personal map is the goal [msg #61, p. 58].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Signs of alexithymia
Alexithymia "can look like" [msg #58, p. 55]:
1. having trouble finding the right words to describe what you're feeling;
2. difficulty expressing emotions using verbal words;
3. difficulty discriminating between emotions and bodily sensations;
4. difficulty in identifying faces and facial expressions.

### Workarounds for alexithymia
1. **Use alternative descriptions.** Describe emotions indirectly in two ways. (a) *Physical sensations*: e.g. "feeling tense" to describe anger, or a sore stomach to describe anxiousness. (b) *Activities you feel like doing*: e.g. "I just want to lie in bed all day" to describe sadness, or "I want to throw something" to describe frustration [msg #58, p. 55].
2. **Emotion wheel.** There are a number of emotion wheel charts available that help identify which emotion you might be feeling. They are most helpful when you find it difficult to recognise and label an emotion, "especially if it's a complex emotion" [msg #58, p. 55].
3. **Use music or lyrics.** Music and lyrics can describe emotions. The author says they often use them both to communicate and describe their emotions and as a way to figure out their own emotions [msg #58, p. 55].

### Describing your emotions — the four components
**Why:** when trying to identify or understand emotions, breaking down the emotional experience "can be really helpful"; it also allows us "to take a step back and see how we can meet our needs better and make any accommodations" [msg #59, p. 56]. **Model:** emotions consist of the trigger (prompting event), any interpretations, beliefs or assumptions you make, physiological changes or sensations in the body, and any urges or behaviours you feel because of the emotion [msg #59, p. 56]. The worksheet has four boxes, each with its prompt:
1. **What was the prompting event?** Describe the prompting event. Remember it can be a combination of things, and it can be an external or an internal event [msg #59, p. 56].
2. **Interpretations.** Can you identify any thoughts or emotions you might have had in response? Are there any beliefs or past experiences that might have influenced this? [msg #59, p. 56]
3. **Physical responses.** Notice physical responses and changes during the emotional response; per the book, this is helpful both for identifying emotions and for regulating them [msg #59, p. 56].
4. **Expressions.** Notice the expressions — the actions or urges you might want to act upon, or the words you want to say [msg #59, p. 56].

**If you're angry, you might experience** [msg #60, p. 57]:

- hurt
- heart racing

- feeling hot

**If you're sad, you might experience** [msg #60, p. 57]:
- lonely
- rejected
- empty or hollow
- a need to be alone
- like crying
- tired or fatigue
- like you aren't interested
- like you don't care

**If you're anxious, you might experience** [msg #60, p. 57]:
- breathlessness
- racing heart
- fidgeting
- frozen or tense
- lump in throat
- nausea
- muscles tightening
- overthinking
- feeling clammy/cold

**If you're jealous, you might experience** [msg #60, p. 57]:
- rejection
- insecure
- racing heart
- difficulty breathing
- tight chest
- feeling a need for control
- racing thoughts
- overthinking

Several sensations recur across emotions (racing heart in anger, anxiety and jealousy; muscles tightening in anger and anxiety; overthinking in anxiety and jealousy), so a single sensation does not identify an emotion on its own [msg #60, p. 57].

### Describing your emotions — personal worksheet
**Purpose:** "Everyone experiences emotions differently which means we might find we experience different sensations, thoughts or urges/behaviours"; this worksheet records how you personally experience certain emotions [msg #61, p. 58]. **How:** four boxes, each reading "When I'm ___ I may experience:" — write an emotion in the blank and list your own sensations, thoughts and urges/behaviours beneath it. You can use the previous list (common experiences of anger, sadness, anxiety and jealousy) as a starting point if you like [msg #61, p. 58].

## Detailed insights

### The author's own experience of alexithymia
The author says they experience alexithymia and describe it "as lacking an internal label maker for emotions": they often have to rely on physical sensations to figure out what they are feeling. A consequence they name is that they usually give vague approximations like "I'm fine" when someone asks how they are — an illustration of why "I'm fine" from an alexithymic person may reflect a missing label rather than an actual state [msg #58, p. 55].
