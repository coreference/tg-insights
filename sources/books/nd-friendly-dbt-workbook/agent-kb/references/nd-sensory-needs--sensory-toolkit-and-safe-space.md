# Building a sensory toolkit and a sensory-friendly safe space

## What this covers

This topic gathers two practical tools for meeting sensory needs: a sensory safe space — a physical spot to escape to in times of need, introduced in the book's Everyday Well-being section [msg #14, p. 11] — and a sensory toolkit — a collection of items to soothe and comfort during distress or sensory overload, from the Managing Sensory Needs section, followed by a fill-in worksheet [msg #79, #80, pp. 76–77]. According to the book, sensory needs are "an important part of managing our every day well-being" for neurodivergent individuals, and having a sensory safe space is one way to take care of well-being [msg #14, p. 11].

## Core ideas

- **Sensory needs are part of everyday well-being.** "An neurodivergent individuals [sic], our sensory needs are an important part of managing our every day well-being." (As neurodivergent individuals, our sensory needs are an important part of managing everyday well-being.) [msg #14, p. 11]
- **You don't need a whole room.** A corner, the space under the stairs or a little indoor tent can serve as a safe space [msg #14, p. 11].
- **Choose by the input you need right now, and give yourself choice.** Seating should match the sensory input needed in the moment; including a few options lets you choose [msg #14, p. 11].
- **Personal and changeable.** "Everyone is different", so judge each sensation by whether you find it pleasant or unpleasant; you may need different items depending on the day and your sensory needs [msg #79, p. 76].
- **Accessible to everyone in the household.** The toolkit should be easy to grab for you or anyone you live with, and can include instructions for others or for yourself [msg #79, p. 76].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Creating a Sensory Safe Space
**Purpose:** to have "a sensory safe space we can escape to in times of need" as one way of taking care of well-being [msg #14, p. 11]. Four steps:
1. **Find your space.** You might not have access to turning an entire room into a sensory safe space, but other options include finding a corner of your room, under the stairs, or setting up a little tent indoors [msg #14, p. 11].
2. **Choose your lighting.** Think about what kind of colours or lighting are most comforting for you. The author says: "I tend to go for purple, blue and white lights." The book notes that many people prefer a "warm white" rather than a "cool white". Options: hang up fairy lights, use a galaxy projector light, or find some cool lamps [msg #14, p. 11].
3. **Where to sit.** Think about what kind of sensory input you need in the moment. The book asks: "Would you feel more comfortable on a bean bag wrapped up in a blanket, swinging in a chair, or bouncing on an exercise ball?" Its advice: "Include a few, give yourself choice!" [msg #14, p. 11]
4. **What to put in it.** Described as "the fun part": fill the space with sensory-friendly items and all your favourite things. The book's starter list [msg #14, p. 11]:
   - fidget items [msg #14, p. 11]
   - weighted blanket [msg #14, p. 11]
   - sensory tray [msg #14, p. 11]
   - ear plugs/headphones [msg #14, p. 11]
   - chewellery [msg #14, p. 11]
   - favourite pictures and affirmations [msg #14, p. 11]
   - soothing music and sound machine [msg #14, p. 11]
   - puzzles, slime or putty [msg #14, p. 11]

### Build a Sensory Toolkit — four tips
**What it is:** "A sensory toolkit is something designed to help soothe and comfort you during times of distress or sensory overload." [msg #79, p. 76] Tips to get started:
1. **Test each sensation for yourself.** Everyone is different, so ask yourself: "do I find this sensation pleasant or unpleasant?" — "Comforting or uncomfortable?" [msg #79, p. 76]
2. **Expect to need different items on different days.** You might need different types of sensory items depending on the day and your sensory needs [msg #79, p. 76].
3. **Keep it accessible.** Keep the toolkit somewhere accessible "because the idea is that you or anyone you live with can easily pull it out whenever you need it" [msg #79, p. 76].
4. **Include instructions.** Consider keeping a set of instructions in the toolkit for your partner, a loved one or even yourself [msg #79, p. 76].

### Build Your Own Sensory Toolkit worksheet
**Purpose:** a fill-in page with one space per sense for planning your own toolkit, each with a guiding prompt [msg #80, p. 77]:
1. **Visual** — "experiment with objects that are visually mesmerising or calming." [msg #80, p. 77]
2. **Sounds** — "what you might need may change depending on the day." [msg #80, p. 77]
3. **Touch** — "experiment with different textures and things to keep your hands busy" [msg #80, p. 77]
4. **Smell** — "do you have any smells associated with positive memories?" [msg #80, p. 77]

## Detailed insights

### Overlap between the safe space and the toolkit
Several items appear in both the safe-space list and the toolkit lists — weighted blanket, fairy lights, affirmations (pictures and affirmations in the space, affirmation cards in the kit), slime and puzzles, and headphones — so the same items can serve a fixed space and a portable kit [msg #14, #79, pp. 11, 76].
