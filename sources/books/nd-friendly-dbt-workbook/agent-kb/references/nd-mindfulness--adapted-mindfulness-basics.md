# Mindfulness adapted: attention, interoception and aphantasia differences, when to use it, and reflection

## What this covers

This topic is the book's introduction to its Mindfulness section and its closing summary. It defines mindfulness and its place in DBT, explains why conventional practices can be hard for neurodivergent people, lists what mindfulness can help with, gives practical tips for practising it in a neurodivergent-friendly way, matches three mindfulness skills to the situations in which to use them, and ends with reflection questions. According to the author, it matters because mindfulness is often seen as necessary for using the emotional regulation and distress tolerance skills, yet "everyone responds to mindfulness differently" [msg #19, #20, #37, pp. 16, 17, 34].

## Core ideas

- **Mindfulness is foundational in DBT.** It "is often seen as being necessary to utilise emotional regulation and distress tolerance skills" [msg #19, p. 16].
- **Definition with a non-judgement core.** Mindfulness is being present in the moment, aware of what is happening within and outside us — and "Most importantly, we do this without judgement" [msg #19, p. 16].
- **It can be genuinely hard for neurodivergent people.** Especially for those who already struggle with attention or interoception; visualising and breathing-based practices may be uncomfortable or difficult; and people with aphantasia cannot use visualisation-based exercises effectively [msg #19, p. 16].
- **No right or wrong way.** The workbook therefore offers exercises beyond breathing and visualisation, and the author insists on figuring out what works for you [msg #19, #20, pp. 16–17].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### What mindfulness can help with
The book's complete list of what mindfulness can help with [msg #19, p. 16]:
1. focusing and paying attention
2. allowing intrusive or judgemental thoughts to pass
3. making intentional choices and decisions
4. identifying your needs
5. recognising when you're reaching sensory overload
6. increasing self-awareness
7. regulating emotions
8. coping with rejection and criticism
9. practicing compassion

### Tips for practising mindfulness
"When practicing mindfulness, here's some things to remember" — the complete set of the author's tips [msg #20, p. 17]:
1. **One skill at a time.** You don't have to try all these skills at once; practise one skill at a time [msg #20, p. 17].
2. **Do not judge yourself.** Let any thoughts, emotions or sensations pass "with compassion and an open mind" [msg #20, p. 17].
3. **Drifting attention is okay.** If your attention drifts, try to guide yourself back, or incorporate stimming into your mindfulness activity [msg #20, p. 17].
4. **Eyes can stay open.** You don't need to close your eyes; you can stare at a spot on the ground if that feels more natural or comfortable [msg #20, p. 17].
5. **No right or wrong way.** "There's actually no right or wrong way to be mindful" — figure out what works for you, for example focusing on an external object or selecting movement-based activities [msg #20, p. 17].

### Mindfulness skill — when you might use it
A two-column table matching each mindfulness skill taught in the section to the situations it suits [msg #20, p. 17]:

| Mindfulness skill | When you might use it |
|---|---|
| Observe using your senses | when you need to stop thoughts from spinning; when you need to practice being in the present |
| Mindful appreciation | when you need a reminder; when you need to practice being in the present |
| Self-check in | when you need to figure out your needs; when you're reaching a point of distress or meltdown |

The page closes: "Use the following skills and tools in the rest of this section to explore and practice mindfulness." (The exercises themselves — observing with the senses, mindful appreciation prompts, the self-check-in — are taught later in the Mindfulness section.) [msg #20, p. 17]

(!) UNCLEAR: "when you need a reminder" for mindful appreciation does not say a reminder of what; these pages do not specify [msg #20, p. 17].

### Mindfulness summary reflection questions
At the end of the Mindfulness section the book asks you to reflect, in writing, on four questions [msg #37, p. 34]:
1. What mindfulness practices are most helpful for you?
2. What are some signs that you need to use these skills?
3. Can you identify any barriers or accommodations needed in using these skills?
4. What reminders do you need to use these mindfulness tools?

The purpose is to consolidate personal learning: which practices work, how to recognise the moment to use them, what gets in the way or needs adapting, and how to remember to use them [msg #37, p. 34].

## Detailed insights

### Why standard mindfulness can be hard for neurodivergent people
The author acknowledges that mindfulness can sound difficult, "that's because it really can be - especially if you're someone who might already struggle with attention or interoception." Some practices, "such as visualising and breathing-based mindfulness, may also be uncomfortable or difficult for some people." Rather than treating this as failure, the author asks readers "to remember (and celebrate!) that everyone responds to mindfulness differently" [msg #19, p. 16].

### Aphantasia
"Some neurodivergent individuals, especially autistic folks, may experience aphantasia, an inability to visualise something in one's mind. For these folks, mindfulness exercises that rely on visualisation aren't really effective." This is the stated reason the workbook includes exercises that "explore mindfulness in other ways besides breathing and visualisation" [msg #19, p. 16].

## Notable quotes

> "To put simply, mindfulness is the ability to be present in the moment where we are aware of what's happening within us and outside of us. Most importantly, we do this without judgement." — Sonny Jane Wise [msg #19, p. 16]

> "There's actually no right or wrong way to be mindful - it's important to figure out what works for you like focusing on an external object or selecting movement based activities." — Sonny Jane Wise [msg #20, p. 17]

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- Visualising and breathing-based mindfulness "may also be uncomfortable or difficult for some people"; visualisation-based exercises are not effective for people with aphantasia [msg #19, p. 16].
