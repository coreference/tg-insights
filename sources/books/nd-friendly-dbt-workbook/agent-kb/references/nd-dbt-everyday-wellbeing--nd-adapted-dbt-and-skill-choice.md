# DBT adapted for neurodivergent people: how the workbook works and choosing a skill by emotional intensity

## What this covers

This topic covers the opening pages of the workbook: who the author is and why they wrote it, what Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT) is, how the author changed DBT to make it neurodivergent friendly, how the self-guided workbook is meant to be used, and a simple guide for matching a skill area to how intense an emotion feels. According to the author, neurodivergent people experience several kinds of distress from living in a society not designed for their differences, and learning to manage that distress is "one of the best things we can do to support ourselves" — DBT skills being one way to do it [msg #4, p. 1].

## Core ideas

- **Neurodivergent distress is more than emotional.** Besides emotional distress, neurodivergent people can experience meltdowns and sensory distress, which the author attributes to living in a society that isn't designed for their differences [msg #4, p. 1].
- **Standard DBT doesn't always fit Autistic and ADHD people.** The author holds that DBT skills can be valuable and helpful but "don't always meet the needs of neurodivergent individuals - especially Autistic and ADHD individuals"; this gap is the stated reason the workbook exists [msg #4, p. 1].
- **The adaptation:** interpersonal effectiveness is removed; a whole section on sensory needs is added; distress tolerance is extended with information and tools for managing meltdowns [msg #4, #5, pp. 1–2].
- **DBT is not about compliance.** The author stresses that DBT is not about changing who we are, but about skills to identify and regulate emotions, manage distress, and recognise, fulfil and advocate for one's needs [msg #4, p. 1].
- **Choice is central.** The workbook is self-guided, its chapters can be read in any order and any skill may be skipped — "a strong focus on choice, always choice" [msg #5, p. 2].
- **Match the skill to the intensity.** A 1–10 emotional-intensity guide suggests mindfulness for low intensity, emotional regulation for moderate, and distress tolerance for high intensity — offered only as a suggestion, because everyone is different [msg #5, p. 2].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### The four components of DBT and the neurodivergent friendly adaptation
DBT was founded by Dr Marsha Linehan (written "Lineham" in the book) for individuals living with Borderline Personality Disorder. It focuses on four components [msg #4, p. 1]:
1. **Mindfulness** — kept in the workbook.
2. **Interpersonal effectiveness** — **removed**. The author says this was done "in order to remain neurodivergent friendly", and repeats on the next page that it "has intentionally been left out". (!) INCOMPLETE: the book does not explain further why interpersonal effectiveness is not neurodivergent friendly [msg #4, #5, pp. 1–2].
3. **Distress tolerance** — kept and **extended** to include information on and tools for managing meltdowns [msg #4, p. 1].
4. **Emotional regulation** — kept [msg #4, p. 1].

Added in place of interpersonal effectiveness: **a whole section on sensory needs** [msg #4, #5, pp. 1–2].

### Skill areas of the workbook
The workbook takes the reader through skills in these areas, each defined by the author [msg #5, p. 2]:
1. **Mindfulness** — "Skills to help you ground yourself, create awareness & focus on the present."
2. **Distress tolerance** — "Skills and tools to help you manage moments of distress, overwhelm and overload." Includes the parts on managing meltdowns.
3. **Emotional regulation** — "Skills to help you understand, manage and respond to your emotions."
4. **Sensory needs** — the added area addressing sensory needs (no one-line definition is given for it on this page).

(!) CONTRADICTION: the text says the workbook covers "three areas" but then names four (mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotional regulation and sensory needs); only three of them get a definition box on the page [msg #5, p. 2].

How to use the workbook: it is self-guided; the chapters can be read in any order; you are welcome to skip any skill; it is meant to let neurodivergent people learn DBT skills "in a way that works for you, at your own pace" [msg #4, #5, pp. 1–2].

### Emotional intensity guide for choosing a skill
Purpose: to answer "So, how can you determine what skill you should use?" — the author created "a little guide to help you choose a skill that matches the intensity of your emotion" [msg #5, p. 2].

How to use it: rate how intense your emotion is on a scale of 1 to 10, then try the skill area matching that band [msg #5, p. 2]:

| Emotional intensity | Skills to try |
|---|---|
| 1–2 | Mindfulness |
| 3–6 | Emotional Regulation |
| 7–10 | Distress Tolerance |

1. **Intensity 1–2 → Mindfulness**: at low intensity, use grounding, awareness and present-focus skills [msg #5, p. 2].
2. **Intensity 3–6 → Emotional Regulation**: at moderate intensity, use skills to understand, manage and respond to the emotion [msg #5, p. 2].
3. **Intensity 7–10 → Distress Tolerance**: at high intensity, use the skills and tools for moments of distress, overwhelm and overload [msg #5, p. 2].

The author's caveat: "Remember, everyone is different so this is only a suggestion!" [msg #5, p. 2].

## Detailed insights

### Who the author is and why it matters
The author, Sonny Jane (pronouns they/them), describes themselves as multiply neurodivergent — "an Autistic ADHDer living with Bipolar and in recovery with Borderline Personality Disorder" — and as a lived experience educator, consultant and advocate passionate about neurodiversity and supporting neurodivergent individuals. Their own BPD recovery connects to DBT's origin as a therapy for BPD, and their Autistic/ADHD experience frames the adaptation [msg #4, p. 1].

### What DBT skills are for, in the author's framing
The author lists the purposes of DBT as giving people the skills to: (1) identify their emotions; (2) regulate their emotions; (3) find ways to manage their distress; and (4) recognise, fulfil and advocate for their needs. This is explicitly set against a reading of DBT as compliance training or as a way to change who neurodivergent people are [msg #4, p. 1].
