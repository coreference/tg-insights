# Calming the body fast: mammalian diving reflex and the TIPP skill

## What this covers

Two fast, body-based distress-tolerance tools: using the mammalian diving reflex (cold on the face plus breath-holding) to switch off the fight-or-flight response, and the DBT TIPP skill. Both aim to bring quick physiological relief when distress, panic or overwhelm is high. The author explains that the physical symptoms of fight-or-flight make the experience "even more distressing", so lowering them through the body is their "first go-to" [msg #40, p. 37], and that TIPP's relief lets you "think more clearly, process information and utilise other skills" [msg #42, p. 39].

## Core ideas

- **Cold water on the face calms you because of a built-in reflex.** The mammalian diving reflex is an innate survival response, present in everyone, that the author says also explains why breathing exercises help anxiety [msg #40, p. 37].
- **Fight-or-flight symptoms feed distress; the reflex reverses them.** Racing heart, uncontrolled breathing and odd limb sensations make things worse; the diving reflex activates the parasympathetic nervous system and gives immediate relief [msg #40, p. 37].
- **You can trigger it anywhere you have something cold** — no diving needed — but the cold must reach the cheeks and above the eyes, and you must hold your breath as if really diving [msg #40, #41, pp. 37–38].
- **TIPP gives relief from the physiological side of distress first**, so other skills become usable; start by trying one component and see what works [msg #42, p. 39].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### How the mammalian diving reflex works
1. **The trigger:** the reflex "activates when you're submerged in freezing water"; it's why mammals like whales and dolphins can survive in the deep ocean — "a survival mechanism, basically" [msg #40, p. 37].
2. **The problem it solves:** in anxiety, panic or distress the body enters "fight or flight" mode — heart racing, struggling to control breathing, maybe "an odd feeling in our limbs" — which makes things even more distressing [msg #40, p. 37].
3. **The effect:** the reflex activates the parasympathetic nervous system, which "creates or mimics relaxation". Result: immediate relief because the heart rate slows, breathing slows, and as blood flows away from the limbs you start sweating less and your muscles relax [msg #40, p. 37].
4. **Practical upshot:** "You can't go diving every time you're feeling overwhelmed, but (as long as you have access to something cold) you CAN activate this reflex whenever you need to." [msg #40, p. 37]

### Mammalian diving reflex — Method one (bowl or sink of ice water)
1. **Prepare ice-cold water.** Fill a bowl or a sink with ice-cold water; "water from the tap with some ice cubes works a treat" [msg #41, p. 38].
2. **Get comfortable.** You'll need to bend over, so make sure you are comfortable — sit down if you need to [msg #41, p. 38].
3. **Hold your breath and submerge your face** in the cold water "for as long as is safe for you - try for 30 seconds" [msg #41, p. 38].
4. **Sit back up and notice.** As you sit back up and open your eyes, notice your heart rate and breathing start to slow down [msg #41, p. 38].

### Mammalian diving reflex — Method two (soft ice pack)
The author prefers this method because "it's a lot more simple and quicker to do especially when I'm in a panic" [msg #41, p. 38].
1. **Prepare in advance.** Keep a soft ice pack in the freezer "for times like this"; a bag of frozen peas also works [msg #41, p. 38].
2. **Sit so you can lean forward** [msg #41, p. 38].
3. **Cover your eyes with the ice pack, lean forward and hold your breath**, counting to 20 or 30 seconds in your mind [msg #41, p. 38].
4. **Release and breathe out.** As you lean back and remove the ice pack, breathe out slowly and notice your heart rate slowing down [msg #41, p. 38].

Key rule for both methods: "you need to hold your breath as if you were truly diving for it to activate the reflex. The longer you can hold your breath (safely), the better the results." [msg #41, p. 38]

### TIPP skill
Purpose: for "moments of distress, panic or when you're feeling overwhelmed"; it provides relief from the physiological effects of distress or emotions so you can think more clearly, process information and use other skills. How to start: try one of the four components and see what works [msg #42, p. 39].

1. **T — Temperature** (to calm down immediately): use an ice pack or something frozen; lean over and hold your breath; hold for 30 seconds. This is the diving-reflex technique in brief (see *Method two*) [msg #42, p. 39].
2. **I — Intense exercise** (to calm your body down): engage in intense exercise for a short period of time to expend your body's stored-up energy; the author adds "stim dancing is a great way!" [msg #42, p. 39].
3. **P — Paced breathing** (slow down your breathing): breathe in for four seconds, hold for four, breathe out for four; then figure out what pace works for you — "3, 5, 7 works too!" [msg #42, p. 39].
4. **P — Paired muscle relaxation** (to help relax your body): work on one section of your body at a time; while breathing in, tense your muscles; while breathing out, relax them [msg #42, p. 39].

## Detailed insights

### The author's personal verdict
The author calls the mammalian diving reflex "my favourite tool in my coping toolbox" and their "first go-to" because it lowers fight-or-flight responses through an innate physiological response — "Super handy, hey?" Between the two methods they prefer the ice pack, as it's simpler and quicker in a panic [msg #40, #41, pp. 37–38].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- Safety, verbatim: "If you have blood pressure or heart problems, please speak to your medical professional before trying this skill." [msg #40, p. 37]
- For the skill to work, "you need to ensure the area around your cheeks and above your eyes receive the input from the water" [msg #40, p. 37].
- Hold your breath only "for as long as is safe for you"; longer holding gives better results only when done "(safely)" [msg #41, p. 38].
- Source: the diving reflex page is "Adapted from Linehan, M. (June 6-7, 2011). Updates to emotion regulation and crisis survival skills in dialectical behavior therapy. Austin, TX: Behavior Tech, LLC." [msg #40, p. 37]
- The claim that the diving reflex is "actually why" breathing exercises help anxiety is the author's explanation; no further evidence is given [msg #40, p. 37].
