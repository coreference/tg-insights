# Sensory self-soothing, IMPROVE the environment, stims and special interests as regulation

## What this covers

This part of the book's Distress Tolerance section gives four sensory- and environment-based tools for getting through distress: sensory self-soothing through the five senses (with a suggestion list and a personal worksheet), the author's neurodivergent version of a classic DBT skill, "IMPROVE the environment", a list of stims to try, and special interests (SpIns) used as a coping strategy [msg #47–52, pp. 44–49]. The author treats these as especially important for neurodivergent people because of their sensory differences and because the environment itself often contributes to sensory overload, meltdowns and emotional overwhelm [msg #47, #49, pp. 44, 46].

## Core ideas

- **The senses are a direct route to relief.** Focusing on the senses can bring comfort and even joy, reduce the intensity of emotions and distract from overwhelming thoughts and feelings; the author calls sensory self-soothing "one of the most helpful distress tolerance skills in my opinion" for neurodivergent people with sensory differences [msg #47, p. 44].
- **Change the environment, not just the moment.** Instead of the standard DBT "IMPROVE the Moment", the book focuses on improving the environment, because surroundings often drive overload and meltdowns — especially relevant when you cannot simply leave [msg #49, p. 46].
- **Distraction is not invalidation.** None of the distraction tools are meant to deny current emotions; they help you tolerate or distract yourself from powerful emotions until you are in a better space to deal with them more effectively [msg #49, p. 46].
- **Stimming is a regulation tool, and a list helps because distress clouds thinking.** Many neurodivergent people are used to masking or have never explored stimming, and during distress they cannot think clearly, so a ready list of stims is a starting point [msg #51, p. 48].
- **Special interests are a legitimate coping strategy.** SpIns are a source of well-being, coping and community; engaging with them distracts, regulates and brings positive feelings, so they "should be utilised as a positive strategy in times of need" [msg #50, #52, pp. 47, 49].
- **Everyone is different.** Take time to find what works for you; the lists are suggestions to get started [msg #47, p. 44].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Sensory self-soothing — suggestions by sense
**What it is:** a distress tolerance skill whose aim "is to relieve any distress or overwhelm by soothing ourselves with our senses" [msg #47, p. 44]. **Why it works (per the author):** focusing on the senses provides comfort and even joy, reduces the intensity of what you feel, and distracts from overwhelming thoughts and feelings [msg #47, p. 44]. **How to use:** the author stresses everyone is different, so take time to figure out what works for you; the following suggestions are "to get you started" [msg #47, p. 44]. The complete suggestion list, by sense:

1. **Look** [msg #47, p. 44]
   - go for a walk in nature
   - "walk a feel-good movie" [sic] — i.e. watch a feel-good movie
   - look at funny memes
   - watch TikTok videos
2. **Taste** [msg #47, p. 44]
   - sip on a cup of tea
   - bake cookies
   - focus on a hard candy
3. **Hear** [msg #47, p. 44]
   - listen to a calming playlist
   - listen to an angry playlist (the book offers this alongside the calming one — soothing does not have to mean calm music)
   - call a friend
   - listen to music as a distraction
4. **Smell** [msg #47, p. 44]
   - light a candle
   - bake cookies (listed under both taste and smell)
   - keep a favourite scent on hand
5. **Touch** [msg #47, p. 44]
   - have a shower or a bubble bath
   - cuddle your pet
   - use a weighted blanket

### Sensory self-soothing worksheet
**Purpose:** a personal list of "objects, smells or sounds that ground you and calm you" [msg #48, p. 45]. **Layout:** five fill-in boxes headed VISUAL, TASTE, SOUND, SMELL and TOUCH; in each you write the things that soothe you through that sense [msg #48, p. 45]. **How to use it:** when you need it, come back to the list as a reminder; you can also share it with your partner, family, parents or your doctor so they can support you too [msg #48, p. 45]. The point of writing it in advance is that it can be consulted (and others can act on it) when you are already distressed [msg #48, p. 45].

### IMPROVE the environment
**Origin and purpose:** the author notes the familiar DBT skill "IMPROVE the Moment" — "a skill to help you tolerate emotions until they feel less intense by improving the moment in a variety of ways" — and replaces it with improving the *environment* [msg #49, p. 46]. The environment often contributes to sensory overload, meltdowns, distress and emotional overwhelm, so it helps to change what you can in your current environment, "especially if we can't simply remove ourselves" [msg #49, p. 46]. **Goal:** improve the environment in any way you can, to help you tolerate whatever emotions you are feeling until their intensity reduces, and to lower distress [msg #49, p. 46]. **Key principle:** the distraction tools do not invalidate or deny current emotions; they buy time until you can deal with the emotions more effectively [msg #49, p. 46].

Visual distraction also belongs here: "Sometimes we need a distraction from our emotions", so putting on your comfort show or favourite movie can give you time to calm down [msg #49, p. 46].
2. **Movement and stimming** — Stimming can help regulate and manage emotions, sensations or thoughts "when they're feeling too much", and can help you avoid distress or a meltdown. The book points to its list of stims for ideas (reproduced below as *A list of stims*) [msg #49, p. 46].
3. **Encourage boundaries** — Improve the environment by encouraging or enforcing boundaries in the moment. The book's example: ask someone to close the blinds and turn off lights [msg #50, p. 47].
4. **Proprioceptive input** — Per the author, deep pressure "has been found to impact the part of the nervous system that regulates breathing, heart rate and more." Providing proprioceptive input such as a weighted blanket or a weighted pillow can bring your system to a more balanced state and help with distress [msg #50, p. 47].
5. **Remove yourself** — Sometimes the environment itself is contributing to the distress, and the best thing to do, if possible, is take a break by removing yourself from the situation or environment [msg #50, p. 47].
6. **Occupy attention with special interests** — Special interests bring joy, confidence and passion and are a source of well-being, coping and community, which is why they should be used as a positive strategy in times of need. The prompt: "Do you have a special interest or something you've been hyperfocusing on at the moment? Immerse yourself in your special interest." (See *Special interests (SpIns)* below.) [msg #50, p. 47]
7. **Validate** — Be kind to yourself and give yourself hopeful encouragement. Ask yourself: "What do you need to hear to get through difficult times?" Remind yourself that any emotions are temporary and that you have got through times like this in the past; tell yourself "you've got this". "Activate your inner cheerleader" and talk yourself through the crisis the way you would talk to a good friend [msg #50, p. 47].

### Special interests (SpIns)
**What and why:** special interests ("otherwise known as SpIns") are "a source of well-being, coping and community" and "can actually be used as a positive strategy as neurodivergent individuals"; engaging with them helps you distract yourself, regulate, and bring positive feelings [msg #52, p. 49]. 1. **SpIns that are an activity you can do** — make up; cooking and baking; taking care of your plants; looking at the stars; arts and crafts; photography [msg #52, p. 49].
2. **SpIns you can listen to and watch** — TV shows; podcasts; music; YouTube videos [msg #52, p. 49].
3. **SpIns you can deep dive and distract yourself with** — space and stars; plants; dinosaurs; neurodiversity [msg #52, p. 49].

The same interest can fall into several categories (e.g. plants as an activity and as a deep-dive topic; cooking/baking as a solo activity and as something to share) [msg #52, p. 49].

## Detailed insights

### Why the environment matters for neurodivergent distress
The author's reasoning for shifting the focus from "the moment" to "the environment" is that environmental factors (light, noise, people, overstimulation) frequently cause or feed sensory overload, meltdowns, distress and emotional overwhelm. Changing them is often the most direct lever — and when leaving is impossible, adjusting what is around you is the fallback; when leaving is possible, "Remove yourself" is itself one of the components [msg #49, #50, pp. 46–47].

### Sharing your soothing list with others
The self-soothing worksheet is meant not only for you but also as a communication tool: sharing it with a partner, family, parents or doctor lets them support you with the right objects, smells or sounds when you are struggling [msg #48, p. 45]. The "Encourage boundaries" component works the same way in the moment — asking someone else to change the environment (blinds, lights) on your behalf [msg #50, p. 47].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The author frames the suggestions as starting points: "Remember, everyone is different so take some time to figure out what works for you" [msg #47, p. 44].
- "Curl up in a dark corner" is offered only "if you're comfortable" [msg #49, p. 46]; removing yourself is advised "if it's possible" [msg #50, p. 47].
- The claim that deep pressure affects the part of the nervous system regulating breathing and heart rate is stated as "has been found" without a named study [msg #50, p. 47].
