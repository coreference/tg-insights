# Understanding emotions: their role, the emotional triangle and emotional responses

## What this covers

This topic opens the book's Emotional Regulation section: what emotions are for, how they send their messages through body, mind and behaviour, and how an emotional response unfolds from trigger to expression [msg #55–57, pp. 52–54]. According to the author, understanding how emotional responses work "can be empowering and helpful when it comes to identifying them and regulating them", and recognising emotions is a helpful skill when learning to regulate them [msg #56, #57, pp. 53–54].

## Core ideas

- **No good or bad emotions.** "Emotions aren't good or bad. They all serve a purpose, even if the emotion itself isn't entirely pleasant to feel." Their overarching role is communication — giving information and drawing attention to important things happening around and to us [msg #55, p. 52].
- **Listen, but don't always act.** Emotions are necessary for survival, so it is important to at least listen to them, but that "doesn't mean we have to always act on them" [msg #55, p. 52].
- **Emotions are messengers that get louder when ignored.** Like a phone notification that keeps beeping until you attend to it, ignored emotions produce sensations, urges and thoughts that get louder, harder to ignore and more difficult to manage [msg #56, p. 53].
- **Responses can rest on unhelpful factors.** Emotions are useful messengers, but "sometimes our responses can be based on other factors that might not be helpful in the moment" — such as interpretations shaped by beliefs and RSD [msg #57, p. 54].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### The role of emotions — four functions
1. **Communication.** Emotions are a form of communication that lets us communicate verbally and non-verbally with other people; they also communicate with ourselves by providing information and input [msg #55, p. 52].
2. **Motivation.** Emotions motivate and prepare us for action. Per the book, they "help activate and deactivate systems like perception, memory, attention, learning, and motivation" [msg #55, p. 52].
3. **Information.** Emotions give us information about a situation; they are necessary for survival, which is why we should at least listen to them, without always acting on them [msg #55, p. 52].
4. **Influence our thoughts.** Emotions are connected to thoughts and memories. The author's illustration is the movie *Inside Out*, where spheres of memories were colour coded — "It's kind of like that." This is why it is easier to think sad thoughts when sad, or remember happy memories when happy [msg #55, p. 52].

### The emotional triangle
**What it is:** the way emotions "send us messengers" — the three things emotions can impact, arranged around the EMOTION at the centre of a triangle diagram [msg #56, p. 53]:
1. **Behaviour** — actions and urges [msg #56, p. 53].
2. **Body** — physiological reactions (physical responses) [msg #56, p. 53].
3. **Mind** — thoughts and memories [msg #56, p. 53].
**Why it matters:** when we become aware of the clues our body, behaviour and mind give us, it becomes easier to identify and figure out which emotion we might be feeling; the emotions are trying to communicate with us [msg #56, p. 53].

### Emotional triangle self-observation exercise
Choose an emotion to focus on and ask yourself [msg #56, p. 53]:
1. "how does your body respond - what sensations do you notice?"
2. "how does your mind respond - are your thoughts racing?"
3. "how do you respond - what urge do you need to act upon?"
The answers are the clues from each corner of the triangle that help you recognise the emotion before it gets "louder" [msg #56, p. 53].

### Emotional responses — how an emotion unfolds
**Why:** emotions are complex and involve a whole-body response; understanding the stages helps identify and regulate them [msg #57, p. 54]. The four stages in the book's order:
1. **Prompting event.** "First, there's the prompting event" — it triggers the first emotion. It is not always external (someone saying something to you, something going wrong); it can be internal, like your own thoughts, feeling tired or even sensory overload [msg #57, p. 54].
2. **Interpretation and secondary emotion.** The first emotion often triggers a second emotion in response — e.g. feeling guilty after having felt angry, or feeling jealous when we feel insecure. Being human, we also have an interpretation of the event that is influenced by many things, the book naming our beliefs and RSD among them [msg #57, p. 54].
3. **Physical responses.** Because emotions involve the whole body, there are always physical changes with any emotion: heart rate changes, temperature changes, breathing changes, and other bodily changes [msg #57, p. 54].
4. **Expressions.** Emotions and physical responses affect our thoughts, words and behaviours — the things we say or want to say and the actions we want to take — especially with intense emotions like anger, fear or anxiety [msg #57, p. 54].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The claim that emotions activate and deactivate perception, memory, attention, learning and motivation is stated without a cited source [msg #55, p. 52].
- The *Inside Out* reference is an analogy, not a scientific model ("It's kind of like that") [msg #55, p. 52].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the list of influences on interpretation ends with "and more"; only beliefs and RSD are named [msg #57, p. 54].
