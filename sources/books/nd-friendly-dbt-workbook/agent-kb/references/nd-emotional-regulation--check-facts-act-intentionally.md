# Check the Facts and Act Intentionally (neurodivergent-friendly Opposite Action)

## What this covers

This topic covers two adapted DBT emotional regulation skills and the closing reflection of the Emotional Regulation section [msg #65–69, pp. 62–66]. Check the Facts is reframed so that emotions are never judged — only responses are examined for fit and helpfulness — and always preceded by checking needs [msg #65, p. 62]. A flowchart turns this into decisions [msg #66, p. 63]. Act Opposite is renamed Act Intentionally because the author found "opposite" confusing and invalidating as a neurodivergent person; the book supplies concrete intentional actions for common emotions [msg #67, #68, pp. 64–65].

## Core ideas

- **Emotions are always valid; responses get checked.** "While our emotions are always valid, our responses to our emotions sometimes need double-checking before we act on them or allow them to steer us" [msg #65, p. 62].
- **Don't ask whether the emotion fits the facts — ask whether the response does.** The focus is on whether the desired action or behaviour fits the facts and whether responding that way is helpful in the moment [msg #65, p. 62].
- **Why responses can misfire.** Responses can be set off by how we interpret or think about an event rather than the event itself, and are shaped by previous experiences, beliefs, and "sometimes, just poor emotional regulation and impulsivity" — "And that's okay" [msg #65, p. 62].
- **Needs come first.** Before checking the facts, always check your needs, "because often, our emotions are communicating a need and we need to learn to listen" [msg #65, p. 62].
- **Acting on the emotion is sometimes right.** "Ask yourself if acting on the emotion is effective - sometimes it is!" [msg #65, p. 62]; and "Sometimes it really does help to throw a soft toy or scream into a pillow though" [msg #67, p. 64].
- **Intentional, not necessarily opposite.** Urges often make sense (e.g. running when afraid as a survival response); when the response does not fit the facts, act intentionally — sometimes by picking the opposite of the urge [msg #67, p. 64].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Check the Facts — "Is it helpful?"
**Purpose:** to examine emotions, responses and the facts of an event to determine whether your responses are helpful; if they aren't, to become aware and figure out what would be helpful [msg #65, p. 62]. **Step 0 — check your needs first:** emotions often communicate a need, so listen for it before anything else [msg #65, p. 62]. Then work through the book's three prompts:
1. **Identify your emotion and your response to your emotion.** The response is something you want to act upon — e.g. if you're angry, you may want to scream or throw something [msg #65, p. 62].
2. **Identify your interpretations, thoughts, assumptions and beliefs about the event or situation.** This separates the event itself from how you are reading it, since responses are often triggered by the interpretation [msg #65, p. 62].
3. **Is your response to your emotion helpful to you?** Ask: will your response meet your needs? Is acting on the emotion effective? The book stresses that sometimes it is [msg #65, p. 62].

### Act Intentionally (renamed "Act Opposite")
**Origin:** the standard DBT skill "Act Opposite" helps manage emotions by responding in an opposite way. The author says that as a neurodivergent individual they always struggled with it: they struggle to figure out what the opposite is, acting opposite isn't always helpful for them, and it often feels invalidating. They therefore renamed it "Act Intentionally", "because this skill is about exploring how to respond intentionally to your emotions" [msg #67, p. 64].
**How it works:**
1. **Recognise the urge.** Emotions are "important messengers" and often come with an urge or behaviour — sad: the need to isolate or cry; anger: the need to scream or throw something [msg #67, p. 64].
2. **Check whether the urge fits.** Urges often make sense for the situation, especially survival responses like running when we experience fear; but sometimes the emotional response does not fit the facts [msg #67, p. 64].
3. **Choose an intentional action.** When it doesn't fit, act intentionally, perhaps picking the opposite of the urge — e.g. walking away when angry instead of throwing something when it's not appropriate, or reaching out for company instead of isolating [msg #67, p. 64].
4. **Allow safe expression.** Sometimes it really does help to throw a soft toy or scream into a pillow [msg #67, p. 64].

### Emotional regulation summary (reflection)
The closing page of the Emotional Regulation section asks you to answer in writing [msg #69, p. 66]:
1. "What emotional regulation skills/tools are most helpful for you?"
2. "What are some that signs you need to use these skills?" [sic]
3. "Can you identify any barriers or accommodations needed in using these skills?"
4. "What reminders do you need to use these skills and tools?"
These make the skills personal: which ones work, your warning signs, what gets in the way or needs accommodating, and what will prompt you to use them [msg #69, p. 66].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The author disagrees with standard DBT on two points: emotions should not be checked against the facts (only responses), and acting "opposite" can be unhelpful and invalidating for neurodivergent people [msg #65, #67, pp. 62, 64].
