# Accessible mindfulness exercises: senses, music, stimming, anchor objects, pets and mindful appreciation

## What this covers

This topic gathers every concrete mindfulness practice the book offers for neurodivergent people: prompts for mindful appreciation, ideas for observing with each sense, a series of "accessible mindfulness exercises" that move attention outward (environment, music, objects, stimming, eating, clay, the sound of the breath), the personal-anchor method with its prompts, and mindfulness with pets. According to the author, it matters because everyone has a different brain and responds to mindfulness differently — some people find visualising, breathing-based practice or focusing on internal sensations difficult, uncomfortable or even triggering, and need techniques that work for them [msg #32, p. 29].

## Core ideas

- **Different brains, different practices.** The author says it is important "to acknowledge and even celebrate" that everyone responds to mindfulness differently; if a practice does not work for you, that is not a failure on your part [msg #32, p. 29].
- **Move attention outward when inward is hard.** Many exercises deliberately direct attention to something outside yourself — the environment, music, objects, a pet — because tuning into yourself, sitting in silence, or focusing on breathing and body checks can be uncomfortable or triggering for some people [msg #32, #35, pp. 29, 32].
- **Stimming and sensory joy are legitimate mindfulness tools.** Stimming can help us focus, ground and regulate; sensory items, fidgets, special interests and pets are all used as anchors to the present [msg #28, #34, #36, pp. 25, 31, 33].
- **Practise gently and check in.** While exploring, check how your body responds and what feelings come up before, during or after a practice [msg #32, p. 29].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Mindful appreciation prompts — "neurodivergent style"
Purpose: the author notes that it can be really difficult to remember the good things in your life, "especially when as neurodivergent individuals, we might struggle with permanence or emotion flooding." The page therefore provides prompts to help you remember things that bring you joy or comfort. It is a fill-in page with four headings to write under [msg #28, p. 25]:
1. **Current special interests or hyperfixations** — list what you are into right now [msg #28, p. 25].
2. **Your favourite sensory or fidget items** — list the objects that feel good to use [msg #28, p. 25].
3. **Your favourite ways to stim** — list the stims you enjoy [msg #28, p. 25].
4. **People who have made you happy lately** — list recent people who brought you happiness [msg #28, p. 25].

### Observing with your senses
Purpose: "some ideas to get you started observing using your senses, with room to add your own" — the worksheet gives examples for four senses and leaves space for your own ideas [msg #31, p. 28].

**Observe with your eyes** [msg #31, p. 28]:
- Go for a walk in a park and observe the birds and nature.
- Find an object in your room or backyard and observe the details.
- Lay on the ground watching the clouds float by.
- Set up a light projector and focus on the moving lights.

**Observe using taste** [msg #31, p. 28]:
- Suck on a lollipop or hard candy and observe the flavours — "Bonus if it has layers!"
- Drink a cup of tea and observe the flavours and temperature.

**Observe using sound** [msg #31, p. 28]:
- Listen to music and observe each note and instrument.
- Take a walk and listen to the sounds of nature.
- Put on white noise, waves, or whale music.

**Observe using smell** [msg #31, p. 28]:
- Light some candles and breathe in, noticing the scent.
- Take a walk, focusing on the scents of nature.
- As you cook a meal, focus on the smell of the ingredients.

(No touch section is given on this page; touch-based practices appear in the exercises below.) [msg #31, p. 28]

### Accessible mindfulness exercises
Purpose: alternatives for people for whom visualising, breathing-based practices, or focusing on internal sensations like breathing or body checks do not work. The book presents them in this order [msg #32, #33, #34, pp. 29–31]:

1. **Use your environment.** When: you find yourself being pulled into your inner thoughts and sensations during mindfulness. How: ground yourself by focusing on your surroundings and naming objects — "like the books on your shelf, the patterns in the rug, the number of tiles in the bathroom." If it helps, name the objects out loud or focus on the details [msg #32, p. 29].
2. **Mindfully listen to music.** Why: "Tuning into ourselves and sitting in silence can be uncomfortable or even triggering for some people"; mindful listening directs attention from your internal experience to something outside yourself. How: pay attention to what you are hearing — the layers of the music, the complexities, the sounds of each instrument [msg #32, p. 29].
3. **Sound of your breath.** How: hold your hand in front of your mouth and exhale so you can hear the sound of your breath; keep exhaling and inhaling while maintaining the sound. Concentrate on the sound of your breathing rather than counting breaths, "letting the sounds be your anchor." This makes the breath an external, audible focus rather than an internal sensation [msg #33, p. 30].
4. **Compare two.** How: find two similar objects — a pair of shoes, a couple of pillows, two mugs on your coffee table, or a pair of candles next to each other — and focus on noticing their differences: the shape and height, the texture, the colour and the small details of each [msg #33, p. 30].
5. **Visual stimming.** How: trace patterns in the environment around you with your finger or your eyes, like the cracks in the ceiling or the patterns in the rug. Alternative: choose a word or even a sentence and trace it in front of you, focusing on the way your finger moves through the air [msg #33, p. 30].
6. **Explore with play-doh, clay or plasticine.** How: explore the modelling material with your hands and notice the texture, the smell and the colour; experience it "free from any expectation." As you knead it, focus on any changes and how those changes feel; roll it into a ball or flatten it with your hand [msg #34, p. 31].
7. **Mindful eating.** How: pick a fruit you can peel with your hands — a banana, an orange, or the author's favourite, a mandarin. As you peel it, notice the texture and what the layer reveals underneath; with each bite notice the taste, texture, smell, colours and temperature [msg #34, p. 31].
8. **Mindful stimming.** Why: the book asks, "Did you know that stimming can help us to focus and ground ourselves, as well as help us to regulate?" Sometimes freeing the stim is "the perfect way to create harmony and calm within us and bring us into the present moment." How: try stim dancing or using some fidget items [msg #34, p. 31].

### Find a personal anchor
Purpose: breathing is a common anchor in mindfulness, "but when we can't trust or use our breathing, we can use a physical anchor like an object." Find an anchor that works for you — it may be a personal object or a sensory object [msg #35, p. 32].

How: sit with the anchor and explore it with whatever senses you prefer; use it to practise mindfulness and ground yourself to the present moment. The book's complete list of helpful prompts [msg #35, p. 32]:
1. What are your eyes or hands drawn to first?
2. How does the object feel in your hand? Focus on the weight of the object…
3. Is the object stationary or is it moving around?
4. What does the surface of the object or item look like? Focus on the texture… Shiny? Dull? Multi-textured?
5. Does the surface have one colour or multiple colours? Try looking at it in a different light or from a different angle…
6. Does the object have round or sharp corners?
7. Does the object have multiple surfaces? Notice how they all connect…
8. Can you feel the difference in the surfaces and textures?
9. Is the object rigid or is it bendable?
10. What do you notice that's unique about it?

### Mindfulness with pets
Three ways to use a pet for mindfulness [msg #36, p. 33]:
1. **Observe your pet.** Observe your pet and the way they look at you or the way they make you feel — "What do you notice about your pet?" Describe how your pet looks by focusing on the texture of their fur, feathers or scales and the way their coat feels. Participate by staying in the moment with your pet — perhaps talk to them or try cuddling them to stay present. [msg #36, p. 33]
2. **Your senses with your pet.** "You probably do this already if you have a soft and cuddly friend": when you're upset, hold or cuddle them and use your senses to focus on the way they feel, the way they sound when they purr, and the way they smell, "especially if you bury your face in their fur." The author jokes: "...Maybe don't taste your pet ;)" [msg #36, p. 33]
3. **Activities with your pet.** The author finds it easier to be mindful while doing an activity ("I know I do!"). Do an activity with your pet — washing them, grooming them or taking them for a walk — and focus on remaining present in the activity with your pet [msg #36, p. 33].

## Detailed insights

### How to approach these practices
"Remember, just because certain practices don't work for you doesn't mean you're doing it wrong - it just means it doesn't work for you and you need to find different techniques that do work for you." While exploring, the author asks you to be gentle and check in with yourself: ask how your body is responding, and what feelings come up before, during or after the practices. Some people may find visualising and breathing-based practices difficult or uncomfortable; some may struggle to focus on internal sensations like breathing or body checks — "and that's okay too" [msg #32, p. 29].

### Why appreciation needs prompts for neurodivergent people
The author links difficulty remembering good things to struggles with "permanence" and "emotion flooding"; writing down special interests, sensory/fidget items, stims and people creates a record you can come back to [msg #28, p. 25].

- Be gentle with yourself and check in on body responses and feelings before, during and after each practice [msg #32, p. 29].
