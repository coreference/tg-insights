# Self-care, a guide to my needs and the support and accommodations wheel

## What this covers

The opening of the workbook's "Everyday Well-being" section: what self-care is (meeting four kinds of needs) with example lists, a planning page for regular self-care, a fill-in sheet ("A guide to my needs") for communicating how others can help, and the Support & Accommodations wheel for mapping eight areas of life by skills, impact and support needed. The author's reason: if everyday well-being isn't looked after, "it becomes harder to regulate our emotions and we're more likely to reach a point of distress" — so self-care and support are the base on which the DBT skills rest [msg #7, #10, pp. 4, 7].

## Core ideas

- **Self-care prevents distress.** Self-care is an important part of looking after well-being and helps prevent reaching points of distress [msg #7, p. 4].
- **Learning skills is itself tiring.** Practising self-care is also helpful because "learning new skills can be exhausting and even frustrating" [msg #7, p. 4].
- **Self-care = meeting needs, of four kinds:** physical, emotional, social and sensory. Needs differ between people, and one person may need more self-care in one area than another — "and that's okay!" [msg #7, #8, pp. 4–5].
- **Asking for support is self-care, and should be normalised.** The author says "We need to normalise asking for support and accomodations as a way of looking after our well-being" [msg #10, p. 7].
- **Make needs communicable.** For people who struggle to ask for support or to express what they need, a shareable written guide and a visual wheel let them tell others what helps and where they need support [msg #7, #9, #10, pp. 4, 6, 7].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Four types of self-care needs
Self-care is "all about meeting our needs"; the book gives example needs for each type "to get you started", noting everyone's needs differ [msg #7, p. 4]:

1. **Physical** — taking your meds; getting enough sleep; having breaks; drinking enough water; stretching or movement [msg #7, p. 4].
2. **Emotional** — therapy; expressing boundaries; getting affection; things that make you feel good; special interests [msg #7, p. 4].
3. **Social** — visiting friends; date nights; spending time alone; curating social media; boundaries [msg #7, p. 4].
4. **Sensory** — having sensory breaks; wearing comfy clothing; avoiding busy places; using sunglasses or earplugs; stimming [msg #7, p. 4].

Note that "spending time alone" counts as a social need and "boundaries" appears under both emotional (expressing boundaries) and social [msg #7, p. 4].

### Regular self-care worksheet
Purpose: a space to "list the things you can do on a regular basis to meet your self-care needs" — i.e. to work out what you can do to meet your needs every day [msg #7, #8, pp. 4–5].
How to do it: the page has four boxes — **Physical**, **Emotional**, **Social**, **Sensory**. In each, write the regular things you can do to meet that type of need (the example lists above can serve as a starting point). Expect the boxes to be uneven: "you might even find that you need to practice self-care more in one area than another" [msg #8, p. 5].

### A guide to my needs worksheet
Purpose: for anyone who struggles with asking for support or has a difficult time expressing what they need. You fill it in and then use it to communicate, or share it, with "a friend, partner, caregiver or even a housemate whenever you need support" [msg #7, #9, pp. 4, 6].
The four prompts to complete [msg #9, p. 6]:
1. **"I NEED YOUR HELP WHEN..."** — the situations or states in which you need support. [msg #9, p. 6]
2. **"I WILL ASK FOR HELP BY..."** — how you will signal that you need help. [msg #9, p. 6]
3. **"YOU CAN HELP ME BY..."** — the concrete things the other person can do. [msg #9, p. 6]
4. **"I WOULD LIKE TO HEAR..."** — the words or phrases you would like the other person to say. [msg #9, p. 6]

### Support and accommodations wheel
Purpose: a wheel model to "communicate to the people around us which areas of life we might need support and accomodations in" [msg #10, p. 7]. The book presents an example wheel of eight life areas, each with four example sub-areas, described as "an example of the different areas where someone may need support and accomodations" [msg #10, #11, pp. 7–8].

(!) UNCLEAR: the wheel is a diagram whose text was extracted as a flat sequence; the grouping below assigns sub-items to areas by their order and meaning and is a reconstruction [msg #10, p. 7].

1. **Sleep** — falling asleep; staying asleep; sleep schedules; nightmares [msg #10, p. 7].
2. **Work/Study** — due dates; instructions; flexibility; uniforms [msg #10, p. 7].
3. **Communication** — phone calls; appointments; advocacy; non-verbal [msg #10, p. 7].
4. **Daily living** — cleaning; organisation; hygiene; reminders [msg #10, p. 7].
5. **Sensory** — home; meltdowns; clothing; accommodations [msg #10, p. 7].
6. **Finances** — costs of aids; debt; impulsivity; tracking/planning [msg #10, p. 7].
7. **Eating/Cooking** — sensory; intolerances; executive function; going shopping [msg #10, p. 7].
8. **Relationships** — social rules; stigma; communication; boundaries [msg #10, p. 7].

The wheel is then filled in three times, each with its own 1–5 rating scale (see the three scales below): once for skills and strengths, once for how much each area impacts your life, and once for how much support you need [msg #11, #12, pp. 8–9].

### Skills and strengths rating scale
Instruction: "FILL IN AREAS WHERE YOUR SKILLS AND STRENGTHS ARE" — rate each life area of the wheel [msg #11, p. 8]:
1. I need to work on some skills [msg #11, p. 8]
2. I'm developing skills/strengths [msg #11, p. 8]
3. I've got a few skills in this area [msg #11, p. 8]
4. I can name at least four [msg #11, p. 8]
5. I'm nailing this area [msg #11, p. 8]

### Life impact rating scale
Instruction: "FILL IN THE AREAS OF YOUR LIFE THAT ARE IMPACTED THE MOST" — rate how much each area affects your quality of life [msg #12, p. 9]:
1. No impact on my quality of life [msg #12, p. 9]
2. Occasionally but minimal impact [msg #12, p. 9]
3. Sometimes but easy to manage [msg #12, p. 9]
4. Regularly interferes [msg #12, p. 9]
5. Uses up all my spoons [msg #12, p. 9]

### Support needed rating scale
Instruction: "FILL IN AREAS OF YOUR LIFE WHERE YOU NEED THE MOST SUPPORT" — rate how much support you need in each area [msg #12, p. 9]:
1. I need minimal support [msg #12, p. 9]
2. I need support occasionally [msg #12, p. 9]
3. I need support day to day [msg #12, p. 9]
4. A carer would be nice [msg #12, p. 9]
5. Just do it for me, thanks [msg #12, p. 9]

How the three wheels work together: comparing them shows, per life area, where your strengths lie, where life is most impacted and where support is most needed — information you can then communicate to the people around you, in line with the wheel's stated purpose [msg #10, #11, #12, pp. 7–9].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The example need lists and the example wheel are starting points: "Everyone is different with different needs" [msg #7, #11, pp. 4, 8].
