## DBT for autistic adults: rationale, adaptations and evidence

### What this covers

This topic gathers everything the channel's authors — autistic DBT therapist(s) who run a DBT skills group for autistic adults — wrote about using dialectical behaviour therapy with autistic clients: the conceptual and clinical arguments for DBT over CBT, how common emotion dysregulation is in autism and what causes it, the role of alexithymia, the authors' own adaptations of the skills training, and the evidence from a 2024 UK conference, a 2026 Russian conference and the Strasbourg randomized controlled trial. It matters, according to the source, because suicidality, self-harm and emotion dysregulation are far more frequent in autistic people, while protocols with proven effectiveness specifically for dysregulation in autistic adults have been lacking [#2, #266].

### Core ideas

- No therapy treats autism; therapy treats the problems autistic people have. The author stresses that any therapy "in autism" is therapy of the difficulties autistic people face, and DBT's target is emotion dysregulation and related problems, not autistic traits [#2, #266].
- DBT's premise fits autistic experience better than CBT's. DBT allows emotions that are not mediated by thoughts, which matches sensory overload and communication burnout (see *DBT vs CBT premise*) [#2].
- Emotion dysregulation is very common in autism — four times more frequent than in the general population (England-Mason, 2020), 40–60% of autistic people per the 2024 conference — and it drives self-harm and suicidality [#2, #170, #266].
- Alexithymia is the bottleneck. Regulation starts with being aware of the emotion; alexithymia breaks the first step, and the Strasbourg RCT found DBT reduced dysregulation through reducing alexithymia [#170, #266].
- Even unadapted DBT works, but adapted DBT is better, and adaptations concern the form of delivery more than the content [#170, #193, #228, #258].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Six arguments for DBT for autistic clients (author's rationale, 2023)
Purpose: explains why the author — who was a DBT client, is autistic, is a DBT therapist and is interested in autism therapy ("Мы с темой “ДБТ для РАС” были созданы друг для друга" — *"The topic 'DBT for ASD' and I were made for each other"*) — believes DBT can be an excellent therapy option for people with ASD. Her one-line answer: the problems autistic people face can be solved well by DBT [#2].

1. Conceptual argument: emotions without thoughts. DBT differs from CBT in one key premise about where emotions come from (fully described in *DBT vs CBT premise* below). DBT's view fits sensory overloads and communication burnout much better. The author puts this argument first deliberately, while noting the next one is no less important [#2].
2. DBT was built for chronic suicidality. DBT was created as a therapy for chronic suicidality — for people who for a long time think about killing themselves or harming themselves. The author read about four large studies on suicidality in the autistic population and, generalising, says it is high, as is the frequency of non-suicidal self-injury. For this reason a large study of DBT for ASD was underway in the Netherlands at the time of writing (2023), which looked very promising to her [#2].
3. Emotion regulation is the core of DBT. Emotion regulation problems are four times more frequent in autistic people than in the general population (England-Mason, 2020). Episodes of aggression and self-harm are behavioural manifestations of these problems. Work on emotion regulation is essentially the main part of DBT: it teaches people to notice when emotions arise, to recognise and name them (the author reminds that alexithymia often comes "в комплекте" — *bundled* — with ASD), to reduce their intensity, and to regulate their behaviour under their influence so as not to harm themselves [#2].
4. Crisis management fits meltdown prevention. DBT gives a lot of attention to crises: how to recognise that a crisis is coming, how to prepare in advance and "где подстелить соломки" (*where to lay down straw*, i.e. cushion the fall). This matches very well with building a sensory profile in ASD and finding ways to avoid meltdowns before they start. DBT helps a person compile a "мануал" (manual) to themselves and to handle high vulnerability to external factors skilfully [#2].
5. Clear, unambiguous structure. DBT is built on clear instructions and unambiguous algorithms with minimal room for interpretation; it is straightforward and offers rules for dealing with oneself and with other people — in the author's words, the dream of a person who prefers everything to be orderly ("четенько") [#2].
6. A social-skills block — with caveats. Skills training includes a block on social (interpersonal) skills. The author thinks it is rather simplistic for ASD, but research says socialising skills of autistic people who completed DBT skills training increased. A further caveat: teaching the "form" (how to meet a new person, how to start a dialogue) without feeling the content is not liked by everyone as a concept [#2].

#### DBT vs CBT premise on where emotions come from
- CBT premise (as the author describes it): all emotional reactions are a consequence of our thoughts — not the event but thoughts about the event cause emotions. Her examples with a falling cup: the cup falls → "какой идиот поставил чашку на край стола" (what idiot put the cup on the edge of the table) → anger; the cup falls → "it was my favourite cup and now it's gone" → sadness; the cup falls → "my cup broke and Masha's didn't" → envy [#2].
- DBT premise: this is not necessarily so. Some emotions are caused by thoughts and some are not. Example: "Я резко выбежала к краю обрыва - я испугалась" (I suddenly ran out to the edge of a cliff — I got scared) — no intermediate links [#2].
- Why it matters for autism: the second view fits sensory overloads and communication burnout. Example: the neighbour turns on a drill and "меня колбасит" (I'm shaking/falling apart); the author does not think "the neighbour is a jerk" or "poor me, forced to listen" — she just feels bad. This fits DBT and fits CBT worse. It may seem a small thing, but she asks the reader to imagine the total number of CBT therapists "вытрясающих из аутистов автоматические мысли там, где их нет" (shaking automatic thoughts out of autistic people where there are none) [#2].

#### Three-stage model of emotion regulation
Presented at the 2024 conference "Adapting DBT for Neuro-Divergent Individuals"; the author had not heard of this model before. People regulate in three stages [#170]:
1. Become aware of the emotion — recognise what one is feeling.
2. Form an intention to change it somehow.
3. Take purposeful actions in that direction.

Why it explains autistic dysregulation: alexithymia, or difficulty understanding one's own emotions, is present in more than half of autistic people, so the problem appears already at the first step of the model and blocks managing one's state. The Strasbourg RCT later confirmed this, in the authors' reading: naming a feeling is "той самой ступенькой" (that very step) without which doing anything further with the feeling does not work [#170, #266].

#### Biological and social causes of emotion dysregulation in autism
As reported from the 2024 conference, dysregulation is so common because of biological and social factors [#170]:
- Biological factors:
  1. Difficulties with social interaction — everything is constantly unclear, there is constant risk of error, uncertainty is very high.
  2. Rigid behaviour patterns — sensory overloads contribute, plus low tolerance of uncertainty and of disruptions to routines.
  3. Difficulties recognising emotions.
- Social factors:
  1. Lack of proper learning of regulation from adults.
  2. "наказывание за аутизм" (punishment for autism).
  3. Elevated risk of bullying and of further violent situations.
  4. Limited access to suitable therapy.
  5. Stigmatisation of autism by society.

#### Four standard modules of DBT skills training
Kept in full in the authors' adapted program and used unchanged in the Strasbourg RCT [#193, #228, #266]:
1. Mindfulness (осознанность) — to be in contact with oneself and notice one's emotions and states better. [#193, #228, article #228]
2. Distress tolerance (стрессоустойчивость) — skills for acute and crisis states. [#193, #228, article #228]
3. Emotion regulation (эмоциональная регуляция) — to regulate difficult emotions and states (emotions, overloads). [#193, #228, article #228]
4. Interpersonal effectiveness (межличностная эффективность) — to feel more comfortable among people; "коммуникация, которая работает" (communication that works). [#193, #228, article #228]

#### Autism-specific additions to DBT skills training (authors' adapted protocol)
Katya Klimochkina (clinical and social psychologist) and Lena Foer (psychologist), both DBT and CBT therapists and channel admins, built a skills group for autistic adults after doing extensive research on adaptations ("Мы провели большой ресерч по адаптациям прежде чем запускать тренинг" — *we did a big research on adaptations before launching the training*). Their rationale, taken from the 2024 conference and existing research, has three points:
1. Emotion regulation difficulties are very widespread in ASD. [#193, #228, article #228]
2. Even the basic classic DBT skills training is effective, gives a noticeable effect and helps people learn to handle themselves better. [#193, #228, article #228]
3. There are observations about which changes make the standard training more effective, and these concern the form of delivery of the material more than corrections to its content. [#193, #228, article #228]

The program exists in two formats. The first version (2025) had 21 weeks: 14 weeks of the standard skills-training protocol ("Нексью", the protocol they name) plus 7 weeks of additions. The shortened version described in the program longread runs 12 sessions over 12 weeks of 2 hours each: part of the sessions come from the standard DBT skills-training protocol (Нексью), and several weeks are filled with the additions; groups are capped at no more than 10 people and joining requires a preliminary interview with the leaders. The authors say they have good experience with autistic clients in other skills groups and outside groups too. The additions [#193, #228, article #228]:
1. More time for getting acquainted, explaining rules, accommodations and the session plan — a slower, more explicit start. [#193, #228, article #228]
2. More sessions — and in the 12-week version "Больше времени и подробностей" (more time and detail) — than in standard training on studying one's own emotions, because the alexithymia characteristic of ASD makes this harder. [#193, #228, article #228]
3. Distinguishing skills use from masking. People with ASD often try to look socially desirable, so the authors found it important to draw a line between actions done "так надо для других" (because others need it so) and "так стоит сделать, чтобы помочь себе" (worth doing to help oneself) — "делать для себя vs делать, чтобы понравиться" (doing for yourself vs doing to be liked). [#193, #228, article #228]
4. Building a sensory profile and teaching about meltdowns, shutdowns and sensory overloads and how to help oneself with them. [#193, #228, article #228]
5. A session dedicated to unmasking — kept both in the 21-week version and in the 12-week program described in the longread (the short Telegram announcement #228 does not list it). [#193, article #228]
All of this is built on top of the four standard modules, and delivered as visually as possible and as little abstractly as possible ("максимально наглядно, минимально абстрактно" — *maximally visual, minimally abstract*) — with pictures, memes and presentations [#193, #228, article #228].

#### Comprehensive DBT with four treatment components (Strasbourg RCT)
The DBT tested in Strasbourg was comprehensive, with all four components [#266]:
1. Group skills training, 2 hours 15 minutes per week (standard modules: mindfulness, emotion regulation, distress tolerance, interpersonal effectiveness).
2. Individual session once a week.
3. Phone coaching between sessions.
4. Weekly consultation meeting for the therapists themselves.

#### Six DERS subscales (as the authors explain them)
Purpose: the DERS (Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale) is used by the authors to measure change during DBT; for every subscale, the lower the score, the fewer the regulation difficulties. The authors describe the six subscales like this [#258]:
1. Non-acceptance of emotions (Непринятие эмоций) — difficulty accepting one's own emotions: the person is angry at themselves for their own emotions, ashamed of them, or believes they «не должен так чувствовать» (shouldn't feel this way). These are secondary reactions to an emotion. [#258]
2. Difficulties with goal-directed behaviour (Трудности целенаправленного поведения) — how much emotions interfere with concentrating, making decisions, doing things and solving tasks: «если мне плохо, то я ничего не могу делать» (if I feel bad, I can't do anything). [#258]
3. Impulsivity (Импульсивности) — problems controlling behaviour under strong emotions: breakdowns, self-harm, abrupt actions or words; a deficit of impulse control in a "state of affect". [#258]
4. Emotional awareness (Осознанности эмоций) — difficulties recognising emotions: "не понимаю, что чувствую" (I don't understand what I feel), confusion between bodily sensations and emotions, emotions recognised only "после" (afterwards). In the authors' words, it is about a deficit of recognition — whether I notice emotions at all. [#258]
5. Emotional clarity (Ясности эмоций) — how "understandable" emotions are versus felt as blurred and mixed, how hard they are to name or separate from each other; it is about understanding which emotion exactly it is. [#258]
6. Regulation strategies (Стратегий регуляции эмоций) — beliefs that emotions cannot be regulated, that «ничего не помогает» (nothing helps), plus the absence or shortage of effective coping strategies. [#258]

The distinction between subscale 4 (do I notice an emotion at all) and subscale 5 (do I know which emotion it is) mirrors the alexithymia problem discussed in *Three-stage model of emotion regulation* [#258].

#### Authors' own DERS before/after observations, autistic vs neurotypical participants
Klimochkina presents a bar chart titled "Регуляция эмоций: до и после" (Emotion regulation: before and after), DERS subscale scores on a 0–30 axis for four groups: before, neurodivergent ("ДО нейроотлич"); before, neurotypical; after, neurodivergent; after, neurotypical. Approximate values read from the chart (before → after) [#258, article #258]:

| Subscale | Neurodivergent before → after | Neurotypical before → after |
|---|---|---|
| Non-acceptance (непринятие) | ~19.5 → ~10.7 | ~20.6 → ~9.9 |
| Goal-directed behaviour (цель) | ~18.6 → ~11.4 | ~21.4 → ~18.3 |
| Impulsivity (импульс) | ~13.9 → ~9.2 | ~16.4 → ~8.4 |
| Awareness (осведом) | ~18.3 → ~12.7 | ~16.9 → ~11.4 |
| Strategies (стратегии) | ~25.6 → ~16.4 | ~27.4 → ~17.1 |
| Clarity (ясность) | ~13.2 → ~9.2 | ~12.4 → ~10.5 |

The authors' interpretation, subscale by subscale [#258]:
1. Non-acceptance: the program effectively reduces secondary reactions (shame, anger at oneself) in both groups. Importantly, ASD does not hinder learning the skill of accepting emotions; the effect is, visually, even slightly more pronounced in autistic participants. [#258]
2. Goal-directed behaviour under emotions: skills related to structuring, pausing and planning "ложатся" (fit) especially well for autistic participants. For neurotypical participants emotions continue to interfere more with activity (in the chart their score barely drops). [#258]
3. Impulsivity: "Вопреки стереотипам, импульсивность не является ведущей трудностью при РАС." (Contrary to stereotypes, impulsivity is not the leading difficulty in ASD.) The autistic group's baseline here was lower than the neurotypical group's; the program effectively reduces impulsive reactions in both groups. [#258]
4. Emotional awareness: autistic participants learn emotion-tracking skills fairly effectively; the authors call this an important argument against the idea that autistic people do not feel or are not aware of emotions. [#258]
5. Emotional clarity: a skill that improves, but more slowly than acceptance or regulation strategies. The authors call this expected: clarity requires time, practice and linguistic mediation. [#258]
6. Regulation strategies: the key effect of the program — participants with and without ASD gain a sense that tools for regulating their state are available to them. [#258]

Overall: the strongest effects are in regulation strategies, acceptance of emotions and goal-directed behaviour — exactly the areas where structured, DBT-oriented programs are most effective, according to the authors. Klimochkina's personal conclusion: emotion regulation problems in ASD are about the lack of accessible and adapted strategies, and DBT provides them [#258].

### Detailed insights

#### What DBT is and who it was made for
DBT is an approach for clients with chronic suicidality, self-harm and, more broadly, disrupted emotion regulation, including strong emotional swings; it is most often used for people with borderline personality disorder. Recent research, the authors say, shows it can also be very useful for autistic adults [#193, #228, article #228].

#### What emotion dysregulation is
Two definitions appear. At the 2024 conference: emotion dysregulation is when there are many emotions, the perception and/or expression of emotions is painful, they last long and they disrupt functioning [#170]. In the 2026 post: emotions hit a person quickly, strongly, for a long time and often — what would not be a trigger for others can become one for people with dysregulation. In autistic adults it is common and pulls self-harm and suicidal thoughts along with it [#266].

#### The 2024 UK conference "Adapting DBT for Neuro-Divergent Individuals"
The author spent two days at this conference of the British DBT society. Her impressions [#170, #193, #228]:
1. "I told you so!" — a year earlier she had written that DBT looks like an excellent option for emotion dysregulation in ASD, and many experts there said the same.
2. 40–60% of people with ASD were said to have emotion dysregulation; she knew it was typical of autistic people but not to this degree.
3. Causes are biological and social (see *Biological and social causes*).
4. Alexithymia's large contribution was explained via the three-stage regulation model.
5. Surprisingly, even unadapted standard comprehensive DBT (and skills training alone) works well for ASD clients: emotion dysregulation, depression and suicidality decrease, including long-term. But adapting is still better than not adapting. Observations show the changes that make standard training more effective are less about correcting content than about the way the material is delivered.
6. She liked that autism was discussed not as a disorder that must itself be cured but as a neurotype that must be taken into account when working with autistic clients.

#### Findings shared from the 2026 Russian conference (Katya Klimochkina)
Klimochkina presented "ДБТ для взрослых с РАС" (DBT for adults with ASD) at the conference "Аутизм. Врачи, пациенты, родители: вместе в интересах людей с РАС" within the Congress of Paediatricians of Russia; her section also included a talk on self-injurious behaviour in ASD, and colleagues spoke on masking, sensory features and comorbid AuDHD. Her summary of the research: in recent years a considerable number of good studies have shown high effectiveness of DBT for autism, and even more benefit when DBT is adapted in format. Benefits range from a low rate of abrupt dropout (pleasing for specialists) to large-scale reductions in suicidality and increased ability to manage emotions, both through new constructive strategies and through better recognising and being aware of one's state. This in turn led to reduced depression, higher quality of life, social functioning and overall wellbeing [#258].

She adds that she and Lena asked participants of their own programs — and, per the longread, also clients in comprehensive DBT — to complete a set of questionnaires to measure the dynamics of change over therapy, for example the DERS, one of the "basic" DBT questionnaires (the scale of difficulties in emotion regulation). She calls it "еще круче" (even cooler) to "add" their own results to the results of others' studies. In the longread she notes that the body of good studies on DBT for autism deserves a separate post of its own [#258, article #258]. Their own results are written out under *Six DERS subscales* and *Authors' own DERS before/after observations* below.

#### The Strasbourg randomized controlled trial (2026 post)
Context: suicide mortality in autistic adults is, by various estimates, 3.7–9 times higher than in the general population, yet protocols with proven effectiveness specifically for dysregulation in autistic adults have been lacking [#266].

Design: conducted by the university clinic of Strasbourg with 63 autistic adults without intellectual disability. Diagnosis was confirmed with ADI-R, ADOS-2 and WAIS-IV. Inclusion required a high DERS score (36-item questionnaire on emotion regulation difficulties) and self-harm, suicidal thoughts or a suicide attempt in the last six months. Participants were randomised: one group started DBT immediately, the other waited five months on a waiting list while continuing usual treatment [#266].

Sample: mean age 29; 94% current suicidal thoughts; 59% self-harm; 81% taking psychotropic medication; 43% comorbid ADHD; 60% had received the autism diagnosis less than a year earlier; 48% women, 44% men, 8% non-binary [#266].

Results:
1. Emotion regulation difficulties dropped by mid-therapy; by the end the difference from the waiting list was almost 32 DERS points; the improvement held six months later.
2. Depressive symptoms decreased and quality of life increased, most strongly on the psychological wellbeing scale (life satisfaction and sense of meaning).
3. Anxiety and suicidal thoughts changed about equally in both groups.
4. Most interesting to the authors: the effect on dysregulation was mediated by reduced alexithymia — people first began to distinguish and name their feelings better, and through this regulation became more accessible. The authors see this as confirming the conference point about alexithymia.
5. Higher autistic traits on the AQ at baseline predicted stronger improvement at six months; masking, gender, age and borderline traits did not affect the outcome [#266].

Limitations (as the post lists them):
1. The control was a waiting list, so one can say "DBT is better than waiting" but not "DBT is better than another therapy"; the study authors say this themselves and propose an active control next.
2. All main measures were self-reports.
3. 10% of participants had comorbid BPD, so part of the dysregulation could come from there.
4. Waiting-list participants knew therapy awaited them, and this expectation alone may have raised their scores, blurring the difference on anxiety.
5. One RCT with 63 participants in one centre is good news, but more data are needed [#266].

Conclusion: the target of DBT is emotion dysregulation and what is linked to it; autistic traits were not targets, and the therapy was not "therapy of autism" [#266].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The arguments in #2 are the author's opinion ("почему мне кажется" — *why it seems to me*), written before the conference evidence [#2].
- The author herself thinks the DBT social-skills block is rather simplistic for ASD, and teaching social "form" without felt content is not acceptable to everyone [#2].
- Prevalence figures differ by framing: "four times more often than the general population" (England-Mason, 2020) vs "40–60% of people with ASD" (conference) — not contradictory but different measures [#2, #170].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the conference report (#170) says standard DBT reduces suicidality, and #258 reports large reductions in suicidality, while in the Strasbourg RCT suicidal thoughts changed about equally in both groups (possibly due to waiting-list expectation effects, which the post mentions only regarding anxiety) [#170, #258, #266].
- Strasbourg limitations: waiting-list control, self-report measures, 10% comorbid BPD, expectation effects, single centre, n = 63 [#266].
- The authors' own DERS data (#258) are observational: no group sizes, no control group, no statistics and no exact numbers are given — only a bar chart (values above are approximate readings) — and the data pool program participants with clients in comprehensive DBT. The authors' comparisons ("visually even slightly more pronounced") are by eye [#258].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the longread does not say how many participants were in each group or how "neurodivergent" vs "neurotypical" was determined [#258].
- The authors' finding that awareness and clarity improve fits the Strasbourg mediation finding (reduced alexithymia drives reduced dysregulation), though the authors themselves judge clarity the slower-improving skill and strategies the key effect [#258, msg #266].

## Distress tolerance and self-care skills for neurodivergent people

### What this covers

This topic gathers two kinds of material. The first is crisis-survival skills from dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT), written by the channel author Lena Foer after a terrorist attack (the Crocus City Hall attack in March 2024): when avoiding emotions is legitimate, the ПЕРЕЖИТЬ acronym of distraction methods, the STOP skill for stopping yourself before a harmful "comforting" behaviour, and a list of ways to take care of yourself when you feel bad [#66, #67, #68]. The second is a card set by the CBT therapist Nastasya Krysko, written for the channel, on how neurodivergent people can take care of themselves: self-acceptance, a support team, professional help, a structured environment, time and task management, technology, and kindness to the body [#110, #120].

Why it matters, according to the source: some emotional pain is so intense that it blocks effective action, and some ways we calm ourselves (doomscrolling, alcohol) only look helpful while actually making things worse; and for neurodivergent people self-support strategies improve adaptation, quality of life and functioning in different areas of life [#66, #68, #110].

### Core ideas

- Avoiding emotions is not always bad. Therapists repeat that emotion avoidance is bad so often it became a cliché, and the author says that this is not true: avoidance is usually ineffective because it leads to more suffering, but sometimes it is the best thing we can do. The distraction methods are for surviving peak emotions, not for everyday use. Details in *Two criteria for when avoiding emotions is useful* and *ПЕРЕЖИТЬ* [#66].
- Some coping only looks helpful. Long walks and favourite music help; doomscrolling and alcohol relieve you in the moment but maintain or worsen your state overall. The STOP skill is a way to catch yourself and choose something healthier [#68].
- Self-care when you are in pain starts with the basics (sleep, food, water, walks, rest, hygiene) and with keeping your usual routines, because they ground you and create stability; do not trust the inner voice saying it is "not the time" for kindness to yourself [#67].
- Self-care for neurodivergent people is universal in concept but individual in practice. Krysko's position: the concept of self-care is common to everyone, but how we build it into life differs, so every tip should be adapted; self-care is a process of exploring yourself and finding what fits you [#110, #120].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Two criteria for when avoiding emotions is useful
Lena Foer gives two situations in which avoiding (distracting from) your emotions is useful [#66]:
1. When the emotional pain is unbearable and could stop you from acting effectively where action is necessary. Here distraction protects your ability to function [#66].
2. When the emotional pain is unbearable and the situation causing it is outside your control and cannot be resolved immediately. The author states that the tragedy that had just happened (the terrorist attack) falls under criterion 2 [#66].

Caveat from the author: "Если вы чувствуете, что способны справляться со своими эмоциями, лучше позволить себе их чувствовать и переживать" (If you feel able to cope with your emotions, it is better to let yourself feel and experience them). The techniques that follow are for situations when you feel you cannot do that [#66].

#### ПЕРЕЖИТЬ — DBT distraction skills for emotional distress
Purpose: a mnemonic from DBT ("аббревиатура ПЕРЕЖИТь", the acronym "ПЕРЕЖИТЬ", literally "to live through") to remember ways of distracting yourself from emotional suffering when emotions feel crushing. Use it only for peak emotions in the situations defined above [#66].

1. П — "помогите кому-нибудь" (help someone). Help someone else who is also suffering from what is happening. The author calls it a good decision from every angle and gives three reasons: (a) the focus of attention switches very effectively, because most of us are strongly tuned to react to other people's pain; (b) when we help, we feel like our better selves and generally better; (c) helping can give more meaning to your own emotions, because through them you empathise with another person [#66].
2. Е — "если сравнить" (if you compare). Comparison with something worse. The author notes the saying that in times like these people watch either comedies to distract themselves or horror films to realise things are still not that bad as long as zombies have not crawled out of the ground; this method is the second one. Examples: rewatch *Alien* and be glad you are not on a spaceship with an alien monster; recall the frightening things you have already gone through and survived. "Звучит сомнительно, но опыт показывает, что это работает" (It sounds doubtful, but experience shows it works) [#66].
3. Р — "используйте Различные эмоции" (use different emotions). To switch away from emotions that seem able to crush you, you need other strong emotional stimuli — something able to absorb you completely. Ask yourself what evokes strong emotions in you: favourite novels, computer games, night raves, anything else. Remember what grips you most and is hardest to tear yourself away from, and use exactly that [#66].
4. Е — "есть другие действия" (there are other activities). Some people are very good at escaping into activity when they cannot cope with feelings; the author says that in such cases she can, for example, clean her flat until it shines in about three hours. Look for an activity that is easy for you to sink into. The recommendation is to search in two categories: (1) something that gives pleasure — for example self-care/grooming procedures, if they happen to be pleasurable for you; (2) something in which you feel your mastery. It does not have to be special: some people feel they are awesome at washing dishes or walking the dog [#66].
5. Ж — "постройте Железную стену" (build an iron wall). Blocking feelings you cannot bear right now. Scarlett O'Hara's line "Я подумаю об этом завтра" (I'll think about it tomorrow) is described as such a blocking method. You can do as she did, imagine a wall, go in your imagination to "внутреннюю Монголию" (Inner Mongolia), or simply tell yourself "стоп, сюда мы не думаем" (stop, we don't think in this direction) [#66].
6. И — "используйте ощущения" (use sensations). Intense physical sensations pull attention away even from strong emotions. The author cites Oscar Wilde's phrase "лечите душу ощущениями, а ощущения пусть лечит душа" (cure the soul by means of the senses, and the senses by means of the soul). A good head-on way to reduce the intensity of an emotion: take an ice cube from the fridge, hold it in your hand until it melts. Other options: tastes — sour, spicy, bitter; very intense music [#66].
7. Т — "течение мыслей перенаправьте" (redirect the flow of thoughts). Deliberately switch from the thoughts that cause crisis emotions to anything else: count passers-by on the street, solve equations in your head, choose the ideal dog breed — the source ends this list with "и так далее" (and so on) [#66].

Warning repeated by the author: these are methods for surviving peak emotions. "Если использовать их постоянно, они скорее всего навредят, чем принесут пользу" (If you use them constantly, they will most likely do more harm than good). But in certain hard cases only they can help [#66].

#### СТОП — the DBT STOP skill
Purpose: a skill that helps you stop when you want to do something that may make you feel worse later, change your mind and choose something healthier instead. Context from the author: after terrible events we soothe ourselves in different ways; some help (long walks, favourite music), but some only look helpful — doomscrolling lowers anxiety in the moment but maintains it overall; alcohol distracts in the moment but in general only destabilises your state [#68].

1. С — Стоп (Stop). Literally stop and freeze — whether you are reading a Facebook news feed, about to do something impulsive, or have already started. The author warns this is a hard choice in the moment, because you are wound up, and running on (reading, shouting, anything) is easier than not running [#68].
2. Т — Только шаг назад (Just a step back). Step back physically, by leaving the room, or internally, for example by putting the laptop aside. Focus your attention on your breathing. Tell yourself you are taking a break — literally for a couple of minutes [#68].
3. О — Осмотритесь (Look around). During strong emotions the horizon of attention shrinks to a single point in space, so it can help just to look around, look out of the window, examine the objects around you. Think about what you are feeling. Try to describe only the facts, without judgement: what is happening, what others are doing and saying [#68].
4. П — Попытайтесь действовать осознанно (Try to act mindfully). Think about what would be useful for you at this moment. What would be effective? Which actions could improve or worsen the situation? Only after that return to action [#68].

#### Self-care when you feel bad — 5 practices
Written by Lena Foer for people struggling after the attack [#67]:
1. Start with basic needs. Get enough sleep, eat at least three times a day, drink enough, take yourself for walks, rest, keep up hygiene. Simply attending to these things already grounds you and gives you back the ground under your feet. If it is hard, keep a checklist and enjoy the ticks, or literally give yourself gold stars for each thing done [#67].
2. Don't neglect your usual rituals and routines — they create a space of stability in a world that is shaking. If you always did morning exercises, keep doing them; if you always walked the dog in the park in the evening, go now too, even if it is harder. Think about which rituals could support you and try to find at least two [#67].
3. Ask yourself, every time you remember, "что будет хорошо для меня сейчас?" (what would be good for me right now?). Each time honestly try to find an answer — something you can change in your behaviour in the moment, even something very small. In behavioural science thoughts are considered part of behaviour, so "switching the focus of attention from a to b" also counts [#67].
4. Return to the body. Set a timer for a random interval; when it rings, quickly scan your body with attention and think how to make yourself more comfortable in the moment: does anything hurt? are you hungry? thirsty? do you want to change posture? relax your shoulders? [#67]
5. Look at what makes you happy. Listen to music, smell, touch and eat what makes you happy (ignore the eating point if you have an eating disorder). Try to come up with at least one idea for each of the five senses, and add these ideas to your calendar straight away [#67].

Closing rule: do not trust the voice that says now is not the time for this. "Нет времени, когда быть добрыми к себе было бы важнее" (There is no time when being kind to yourself would matter more) [#67].

#### Self-care and neurodivergence — card set by Nastasya Krysko
CBT therapist Nastasya Krysko wrote the cards for the channel in two parts (part one on 2024-06-20, part two on 2024-06-26) [#110, #120].

Introductory principles.
- For all people, including neurodivergent people, it is important to find self-support strategies that can improve adaptation, raise quality of life and improve functioning in various areas [#110].
- Each point can and should be adapted to yourself: the concept of self-care is common to all ("нотная" in the OCR, probably "общая"; see Caveats), but how we build it into our lives will differ [#110].
- It is important to notice your needs and give time to restoring your strength. "Забота о себе это процесс, это исследование себя и поиск того, что подходит именно вам" (Self-care is a process — exploring yourself and searching for what suits you specifically). Tips and advice are reference points; our task is to lean on them and adapt them for ourselves [#120].

Block 1 — Self-acceptance and self-understanding.
- Card 01: awareness of and emphasis on your personal strengths, together with a wise understanding of your weaknesses, helps reduce stress and supports a kind attitude toward yourself [#111].
- Card 02 (as labelled in the set): taking part in support groups or communities lets you exchange experience, get emotional support or learn tips that might suit you [#112]. (!) UNCLEAR: this card carries the same text as the support-team card 02 [#122]; it may be a layout duplicate in the original set.

Block 2 — Support team.
- Card 01: social support from people who understand and accept you — possibly also people who understand neurodivergence — improves your psycho-emotional state and helps develop communication skills [#121].
- Card 02: participating in support groups or communities lets you share experience, receive emotional support or pick up advice that may suit you [#122].

Block 3 — Professional help.
- Card 01: consultations with psychologists or coaches who specialise in neurodivergence help develop effective strategies of self-management and adaptation [#123].
- Card 02: medical and therapeutic help may be necessary to treat co-occurring conditions and improve overall health [#124].

Block 4 — Creating a structured environment.
- Card 01: organising your workspace and creating a clear daily schedule minimise distracting factors and improve cognitive functioning [#115].
- Card 02: stable, predictable routine actions and habits reduce anxiety and improve general wellbeing — for example morning self-care practices, evening rituals before sleep, having breaks [#116].

Block 5 — Time and task management.
- Card 01: behavioural strategies such as making task lists or using the Pomodoro technique help structure activity and improve time management. Krysko acknowledges this skill can be difficult and will need to be learned, "но именно он будет помогать в будущем чувствовать себя лучше" (but it is exactly this skill that will help you feel better in the future) [#113].
- Card 02: splitting large tasks into smaller stages ("baby steps") improves concentration and reduces the feeling of being overloaded. Mechanism: anxiety and overload in thoughts about what we have to do maintain procrastination and low mood, which lowers motivation to do anything — and it is precisely starting to do something that helps get out of this trap [#114].

Block 6 — Technology and self-care.
- Card 01: apps for organising, planning and self-care can help us not forget — and not "give up on" ("не забывать и не забивать") — small but important actions. There is no single app that suits everyone; choose one you like for its design, simplicity of use and accessibility [#125].
- Card 02: special devices — noise-cancelling headphones, room lighting/backlighting, smart-home-type programs — help reduce exposure to distracting factors; again, this is all individual [#126].

Block 7 — Kindness to your body.
Sleep, food and some physical activity matter for all of us; they are a kind of base of self-care. Physical activity (any kind that suits you), sufficient and varied nutrition and regular rest — meaning breaks in every day, days off, and holidays — are the foundation of feeling well. Krysko notes it sounds simple but of course it can be hard to keep up [#127].

### Tools and resources mentioned

- Ice cube — held in the hand until it melts, a direct way to lower emotional intensity through sensation (ПЕРЕЖИТЬ, "И") [#66].
- Pomodoro technique — recommended by Krysko as a behavioural time-management strategy alongside task lists [#113].
- Organisation, planning and self-care apps — no universal app; pick by design, ease of use and accessibility [#125].
- Noise-cancelling headphones, room lighting, smart-home programs — to reduce distractions, chosen individually [#126].
- Nastasya Krysko's Telegram channel (t.me/nastasya_kr) — a channel on self-kindness by the CBT therapist; Lena Foer calls it "свой (отличный!) канал" (her (excellent!) channel) [#110].
- Checklist / gold stars — for tracking basic needs when they are hard to keep up [#67].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The ПЕРЕЖИТЬ methods are explicitly for peak emotions and for the two avoidance criteria; used constantly they are likely to harm more than help, and if you can bear your emotions it is better to feel them [#66].
- The "eat what makes you happy" suggestion should be ignored by people with an eating disorder [#67].
- (!) UNCLEAR: card #112 is labelled as card 02 of the self-acceptance and self-understanding block, but its text is identical to the support-team card 02 [#122]; the source does not explain whether this is intentional [#112].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the OCR of the intro card reads "концепция заботы о себе в целом нотная для всех" — "нотная" is likely an OCR error (probably "общая", common); the meaning "the concept is common for everyone, the way it is built into life differs" follows from the sentence [#110].
- Krysko repeatedly hedges that everything is individual (devices, apps) and that tips are reference points to adapt, not prescriptions [#120, #125, #126].
- The "if you compare" method is self-described as sounding doubtful, supported by "experience" rather than cited evidence [#66].
