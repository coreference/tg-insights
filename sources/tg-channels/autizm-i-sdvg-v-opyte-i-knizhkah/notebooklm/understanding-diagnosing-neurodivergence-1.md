## Autism traits beyond the stereotypes: lesser-known features and aphantasia

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's posts on what autism looks like beyond the familiar stereotypes ("autistic people avoid eye contact and don't communicate much"). It covers the official ICD-11 definition and the co-occurring conditions ICD mentions, the unexpected consequences of the two core criteria (sensory hypo- and hyper-sensitivity across eight senses, unusual facial expression and intonation, echolalia and echopraxia, selective mutism), traits that autistic people themselves report on Reddit, the broader autism phenotype (sub-clinical autistic traits), and aphantasia, which research reportedly links to autism. [#14, #15, #20, #30, #31, #33, #38]

According to Lena Foer, only "the most meticulous and curious (and autistic people themselves)" know that autism includes much more than the official symptoms; she aims to balance research and personal experience, so the material is layered from research-backed criteria down to unverified self-observations. Her stated hope is that observations noticed by individuals will one day be taken seriously, studied, and help people better understand and respect each other's needs. [#30, #33]

### Core ideas

- Autistic differences go beyond communication. Katya Klimochkina: the features of people with ASD are not limited to communication; differences show up in behavioural strategies in general — routines, emotional regulation, special interests, task switching, processing speed, sensory reactions, preference for solitude (full list under *Behavioural traits of autism beyond communication*). [#14]
- ICD-11 defines autism with only two huge, blurry criteria — social interaction difficulties and restricted/repetitive patterns — which have many consequences; many other conditions occur in autistic people but are not required for diagnosis. [#30]
- The two core "symptoms" are so broad that they produce many non-obvious manifestations, including under-sensitivity, problems with the less-known senses (vestibular, proprioception, interoception), opposite extremes of facial expression, echolalia/echopraxia and selective mutism. [#31]
- "Spectrum" is about variety, not degree — yet sub-clinical autism exists. Autistic people object that the spectrum is about the great difference in how traits manifest, not "more" or "less" autistic; but scientists do describe a broader autism phenotype of traits that don't reach clinical significance. [#38]
- Aphantasia — the inability to picture things mentally — is more common among autistic people than average, according to research the author refers to; absence of visual imagery is not absence of imagination, as such people manipulate ideas and concepts instead of pictures. [#20]

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Behavioural traits of autism beyond communication (11-item list)
Katya Klimochkina's checklist of behavioural features of ASD, written in the second person ("you…"). Every item: [#14]
1. Routines and constancy. You like routines and constancy; if your schedule or order of things is disrupted, you may react very emotionally or, on the contrary, freeze ("впасть в ступор" — fall into a stupor). [#14]
2. Distress at the unexpected. You may feel sad and scared when something unexpected happens that breaks your plans. [#14]
3. Emotion regulation. It is hard for you to regulate your emotional reactions. [#14]
4. Irritation over your things and space. It can be hard to hold back irritation when someone moves or uses your things, makes noise, takes your food and the like. [#14]
5. Daily repetitive rituals. You have repetitive behavioural rituals that you perform every day. [#14]
6. Making odd sounds where silence is expected. You may make strange sounds in places where you are supposed to keep quiet ("and the granny librarian tells you so"). [#14]
7. Special interests. You have special interests and hobbies that take up a lot of your time. [#14]
8. Multitasking and switching. It is hard to do several tasks at once or to switch between them. [#14]
9. Processing time. Sometimes you need more time and effort to understand information or to answer questions. [#14]
10. Sensory reactions. There may be a strong reaction — or no reaction at all — to sensory stimuli such as sounds, smells, the taste or texture of food. [#14]
11. Preference for solitude. You prefer doing things alone. [#14]

#### Three layers of lesser-known autism features
Lena Foer's series "Малоизвестные симптомы аутизма" (Lesser-known symptoms of autism) is organised in layers, from best-established to least established. Beyond the well-known stereotypes and the "official" diagnostic symptoms, there are: [#30]
1. Non-obvious consequences of the "official" symptoms, sometimes very unexpected (covered in part 2, see *Unexpected consequences of the core symptoms*). [#30, #31]
2. Symptoms, traits and syndromes that are not among the core ones but are still directly linked to ASD. [#30]
3. Observations by autistic people themselves — probable manifestations of ASD that scientists have not yet studied, but that are discussed at the level of personal stories and generalised experience (covered in part 4, see *Autistic self-reported traits*). [#30, #33]

#### ICD-11 core criteria for autism and associated conditions
The author bases the "official" definition on ICD-11 (МКБ-11). It consists of only two symptoms — "огромных, довольно размытых и имеющих кучу следствий" (huge, rather blurry, and having a heap of consequences). These two criteria determine the diagnosis: [#30]
1. Problems with social interaction — difficulty reading nonverbal signals and responding to them, problems understanding social context and acting in accordance with it (the source ends this list with "и т.д."). [#30]
2. Repetitive, rigid patterns of behaviour, interests and activities — repetitive movements, rigid thinking, difficulty adapting to the new, rigid routines, love of rules and rituals; sensory sensitivities also belong to this criterion. [#30]

ICD also mentions symptoms and other disorders that occur in autistic people but are not required for a diagnosis: [#30]
1. Intellectual impairment. [#30]
2. Disorders of speech development. [#30]
3. Developmental delays. [#30]
4. Anxiety and depressive disorders (and in general a high number of co-occurring mental disorders). [#30]
5. Self-injurious behaviour (for example, banging one's head against something). [#30]
6. Epilepsy and non-epileptic seizures. [#30]
7. Catatonia — a state in which a person does not, or almost does not, respond to the outside world. [#30]
8. Tuberous sclerosis — a rare genetic disease in which benign tumours develop. [#30]
9. Chromosomal and genetic abnormalities. [#30]
10. Cerebral palsy — a group of disorders affecting the ability to move and keep balance. [#30]
11. Neurofibromatosis — another genetic disorder leading to the development of tumours. [#30]

#### Unexpected consequences of the core symptoms (5 items)
Lena Foer, part 2 of the series: the two main "symptoms" of ASD are so all-encompassing that they can have a huge number of manifestations; these are the ones that may look non-obvious: [#31]
1. Sensory sensitivity is not necessarily hypersensitivity. A person may just as well be weakly sensitive to some sensory stimuli and seek additional stimulation. Autistic people who love fiery-hot food, heavy-music concerts and raves with light shows exist — just as do those who cannot stand any of that. Lesson: a love of intense stimulation does not rule out autism. [#31]
2. Eight senses, not five (see *The eight senses model* below) — the vestibular sense, proprioception and interoception can also be hyper- or hypo-sensitive and show up unexpectedly as dehydration, lifelong motion sickness, or clumsiness. [#31]
3. Difficulty reading facial expressions and intonation can lead to two opposite manifestations: either absent facial expressions and weak intonation in autistic people themselves, or, on the contrary, excessively pronounced facial expressions and vivid (but possibly unusual for listeners) intonation. Mechanism offered by the author: the same reason hard-of-hearing people speak loudly — if you cannot hear well yourself, it seems you must try harder so that others hear. If you feel that facial expressions are hard to tell apart, you want to make the task easier for others and convey the expression unmistakably. [#31]
4. Echolalia and echopraxia belong to repetitive behaviours. Echolalia is repeating sounds and words after other people, film characters and singers; echopraxia is mirroring body positions, which can also feel almost involuntary (see *Echopraxia* below). [#31]
5. Selective mutism. Among anxiety disorders, autistic people more often than neurotypicals have selective mutism — a state in which at certain moments a person is unable to speak. [#31]

#### The eight senses model
Per Lena Foer, a human has not 5 senses but 8: hearing, sight, smell, touch and taste, plus: [#31]
- Vestibular sense — the feeling of moving through space. If too sensitive, a person may get sick in transport all their life. [#31]
- Proprioception — the feeling of the body in space. When it works differently than in neurotypicals, the result is carelessness and constant bumping into objects, furniture and doors. [#31]
- Interoception — sensations inside the body. Dehydration can be frequent in autistic people simply because thirst first has to be felt ("жажду еще надо почувствовать" — *thirst still has to be felt*). [#31]
All three can be hyper- or hypo-sensitive and may manifest unexpectedly. [#31]

#### Autistic self-reported traits (12 items, from Reddit)
Part 4 of the series: personal observations that autistic people shared publicly on Reddit. The author stresses these are not research results and not even symptoms, but private stories of what, in their view, may also be connected with autism; some may be studied and described in papers later (and then move into one of the lists above). The items: [#33]
1. A strange, atypical or changeable accent. [#33]
2. Toe walking — not because one can't walk otherwise, but because it is much more comfortable / pleasant / better. [#33]
3. Belonging to fandoms. [#33]
4. Hating drinking water. [#33]
5. Breathing through an open mouth. [#33]
6. Sitting in strange, twisted postures. [#33]
7. Looking down at one's feet while walking. [#33]
8. Feeling uncomfortable walking ahead of other people or leading them. [#33]
9. A voice that is too loud or too quiet. [#33]
10. Not wanting to wear socks (or, conversely, not wanting to live without them even a little). [#33]
11. Fear of losing balance. [#33]
12. Hands held at the chest all the time — "руки динозавра" (dinosaur hands), also called raptor hands. [#33]

#### Two criteria for when echopraxia is a symptom
Mirroring someone's posture and gestures is not always a symptom of something unpleasant: firstly, children learn through imitation; secondly, when people of any age synchronise emotionally (in psychology this is called rapport), they may automatically adjust to each other, including nonverbally. To tell when echopraxia is "normal" and when not, two criteria are usually cited: [#15]
1. Frequency — how often it happens. [#15]
2. Sense of uncontrollability — whether it happens against your wish. [#15]
So if it happens often and clearly against your will, it may be a symptom of something. [#15]

#### Autism vs broader autism phenotype (5 paired dimensions)
An infographic the author translated from verywellhealth.com compares autism (left) with the broader autism phenotype (right): [#38]
1. Speech: autism — repetitive speech patterns, atypical use of words; BAP — later speech development. [#38]
2. Text and literacy: autism — difficulty understanding a text one has read; BAP — reading and writing come harder than to peers. [#38]
3. Social interaction: autism — persistent difficulties in social/emotional interaction and communication; BAP — gathering one's thoughts to respond in conversation takes time. [#38]
4. Rigidity: autism — rigid verbal and nonverbal behaviour, own rituals; BAP — it can be hard to change one's point of view, likes routines. [#38]
5. Interests: autism — pronounced, rigid, fixed interests; BAP — fixations on certain objects or hobbies may occur. [#38]

#### Aphantasia as a spectrum (4 levels)
Aphantasia is presumably not a "switch" with two positions (on/off) but a spectrum: [#20]
1. People able to imagine detailed images. [#20]
2. People who can imagine something dimly and blurrily. [#20]
3. People who can imagine simple shapes. [#20]
4. People for whom it does not work at all. [#20]

### Detailed insights

#### Echopraxia in detail
Many people know echolalia — automatic repetition of words heard from someone — and that it occurs in autism. Echopraxia is less known: exactly the same involuntary repetition, but of other people's movements, gestures, posture or facial expressions. Per Lena Foer it occurs in Tourette syndrome, epilepsy, autoimmune disorders, dementia, and also in autism. [#15]

The channel's "standard disclaimer": even if you have echopraxia, it does not automatically mean you have autism or anything else from the list. But it's reasonable to see a psychiatrist or a neurologist — a neurologist because echopraxia is classed among tics. Echopraxia in itself is not dangerous; if it noticeably interferes with life, you may be prescribed medication or referred to some behavioural therapy to ease the condition. [#15]

#### Aphantasia: what it is and what it's like
Definition and example. Aphantasia is a condition characterised by the inability to create visual images in one's mind. Most people asked to imagine a glass of water, the bed in their bedroom or an acquaintance's face do so easily (the author notes readers have probably already done it while reading). People with aphantasia cannot: they can recall separate characteristics of, say, the glass (glass, tall, faceted) but cannot see an image in their head. In some people aphantasia concerns only images; in others the ability to imagine sounds, smells and information from other senses is also absent. [#20]

Link with autism. Although anyone can have aphantasia, studies (per the author) have shown a connection between aphantasia and autism: autistic people may be more prone to aphantasia than people on average. [#20]

Imagining the absence of imagination (cat meme). Lena Foer points out how hard it is for people to imagine having no imagination. She uses a meme claiming that cats have no imagination, so when the owner leaves for work the cat thinks he is just standing behind the door for hours ("Олег, ты стоишь за дверью уже 7 часов. Ты дурак, Олег?" — *Oleg, you've been standing behind the door for 7 hours. Are you a fool, Oleg?*). She takes the meme apart: to imagine the owner behind the door, the cat would need to be able to imagine. Without imagination, you cannot picture where someone is — he left, and that's it, he's gone, end of story. [#16, img #16]

What it feels like — Blake Ross's account. The "very detailed answer" is a first-person essay by Blake Ross (see *Tools and resources*). The excerpt the author translated: when asked to imagine a beach, he thinks about the "concept" of a beach — he knows there is sand, water, sun, maybe lifeguards; he knows facts about beaches, recognises a beach when he sees one and can do verbal gymnastics with the word. But he cannot recall beaches he has been to; he has no visual, sound, emotional or other sensory experience of them, and cannot create any mental image of a beach whether his eyes are closed or open, whether he reads the word or concentrates on the idea for hours — or stands on the beach itself. "И я вырос в Майами." (And I grew up in Miami.) [#20]

The general answer. Absence of visual imagination is not absence of imagination: apparently, people without images in their heads manipulate ideas and concepts instead of pictures. [#20]

Interesting facts listed by the author: [#20]
1. Some people with aphantasia dream, some don't. Dreams, like waking imagination, can be about various notions — but without illustrations to them. [#20]
2. Aphantasia is presumably a spectrum (see *Aphantasia as a spectrum*). [#20]
3. According to research, people with aphantasia tend to have above-average IQ, to be less susceptible to PTSD, to think analytically, and to be more reliable witnesses for the police ("guess why" — the author leaves the reason implicit). [#20]
4. People without visual imagination also do excellently in creative professions. [#20]

The author's stance. For Lena Foer, aphantasia is something like a Zen koan: she cannot imagine how one could imagine the idea of an object other than through a visual image — which is exactly what makes it such an interesting phenomenon to her. [#20]

#### "Spectrum" and the broader autism phenotype
Lena Foer frames the debate as a three-way joke: everyone thinks "autism spectrum disorders" sounds as if there are more-autistic and less-autistic people; autistic people say no, the spectrum is not about how pronounced ASD traits are but about the great difference in how they manifest; and scientists say that "autists on minimal settings" ("аутисты на минималках") do exist after all. [#38]

The broader autism phenotype (BAP) is the set of states in which autistic traits are present but do not reach the level of clinical significance — that is, they don't particularly interfere with life. Examples given: routines are important and help a lot, but life without them is okay too; sensory sensitivities exist but at the level of some discomfort, not meltdown; special interests absorb you, but small talk is also a fine activity. [#38]

BAP is not a diagnosis but rather a cluster of traits that scientists observe in relatives of diagnosed autistic people (but not only in them). Studying it helps scientists understand autism better (per the article the author recommends). [#38]

#### Why self-observations matter
Lena Foer's reflection closing the series: autism has existed throughout human history, but humanity only recently began paying attention to how people are built psychologically, and understanding people who differ from the majority is a huge task for the future; regarding autism "we are only at the very beginning of this path." Hence the hope that individual observations noticed by specific people will one day be taken seriously, studied and gain more meaning, helping different people better understand and respect each other's needs ("надо же верить во что-то, да?" — *one has to believe in something, right?*). She invites readers to share their own observations about such connections. [#33]

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The self-reported traits list (#33) is explicitly not research and not even symptoms — only autistic people's personal stories from Reddit about what may be autism-related. [#33]
- Having echopraxia does not automatically mean autism or any other listed condition (the channel's standard disclaimer). [#15]
- The aphantasia–autism link and the facts about IQ, PTSD, analytical thinking, witness reliability and creative work are attributed to unnamed "studies"; the author gives no specific references. Aphantasia being a spectrum is stated with a hedge ("предположительно" — *presumably*). [#20]
- (!) UNCLEAR: "guess why" about aphantasic people being more reliable police witnesses — the author does not state the reason. [#20]
