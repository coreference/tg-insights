## Masking and camouflaging: forms, costs and measurement

### What this covers

This topic gathers everything the channel taught about masking (камуфляж, маскировка, маскинг): what it is, the many shapes it takes (from imitating facial expressions to sitting silently in a corner), the forces that produce it (ableism, the constant experience of "doing it wrong", rejection sensitivity), what it costs (exhaustion, loss of authenticity, burnout, overcontrol), and how to measure it with the CAT-Q questionnaire, which the channel shared in two Russian translations. According to the authors, masking matters because much of the suffering of autistic people comes not from autism itself but from the added burden of constant camouflage, and noticing one's own masking strategies is the first step toward reducing them [#50, #140].

This part adds two longreads by Lena Foer that put masking into wider contexts. The first (#48) asks how autistic people perceive their own "I", lists the features of autism that make identity formation hard — masking among them — and gives five ideas for growing a sense of self. The second (#53), a retelling of a talk by psychiatrist Anastasia Afanasyeva at the Psydocfest conference, explains why girls and women with ADHD and autism are underdiagnosed, how both conditions present in women, and closes with why masking in women produces burnout. Relation to the existing topic file: it extends the list of masking's costs (the existing file names exhaustion, loss of authenticity, burnout, overcontrol and rejection sensitivity) with a new one — fragmentation or loss of identity — and adds a gendered explanation of why women mask more; nothing in these posts contradicts the earlier material [#48, article #53].

### Core ideas

- Masking is a behavioural strategy for seeming neurotypical. It is used by autistic people and people with ADHD, deliberately or not, to blend into social norms and be more accepted; it consists of hypervigilance to others' expectations plus tight control of one's self-expression (see *Two-part definition of masking*) [#131].
- Masking is not only active imitation. Being quiet, rephrasing others, saying nothing extra and merging into the background — "shyness" — can also be masking (see *Shyness as masking*) [#251].
- Masking is a product of the social context, not of autism itself. Following Devon Price, the channel argues that ableism teaches autistic people that to be valuable they must constantly override themselves, so they mask; the masking, not the autism, eats up resources and causes burnout (see *Devon Price's ableism → masking → burnout argument*) [#50].
- Rejection sensitivity and masking feed each other. Autistic people are set up to experience rejection more often; masking can intensify rejection sensitivity and contribute to emotional burnout [#36].
- Long-term control turns into overcontrol. Years of suppressing stimming, masking, following social rules and hiding emotions "works — up to a point", then leads to burnout, loss of self and loneliness [#242].
- Masking can be measured. The CAT-Q gives a total score from 25 to 175 and three factor scores; higher scores mean more masking and are linked to psychological distress, but the Russian versions are not standardised, so results must be read cautiously [#40, #42, #140].

- Masking undermines the sense of self. People who mask a lot tend to feel they have no personality, only a set of roles; the self becomes context-bound, changeable and fragmented, assembled from pieces of others. This is a cost of masking not described in the earlier posts of this topic (see *Masking and identity*) [#48].
- Feeling you must mask already concedes your own unacceptability. The author's "sad thought": deciding you need to mask is signing off on being unacceptable as you are [#48].
- Identity is harder for autistic people for several converging reasons — difficult transitions, social difficulties, trouble with abstract (especially people-related) concepts, rigid thinking, masking, alexithymia, and negative views of autism — but the analytic autistic mind is also a major asset for self-understanding (see *Seven features of autistic self-perception*) [#48].
- Avoiding masking is one of the five ways to build an identity, to be done gradually, gently and in a safe setting (see *Five ways to help yourself form an identity*) [#48].
- Women are underdiagnosed because diagnostic criteria were built on boys, whose symptoms are more externalised; women's internalised symptoms, gender socialisation toward suppression, and presentations that fit expectations of women in general hide the condition [#53].
- Women mask more, and adjusting is not adapting. A woman who imitates interest in small talk does not come to dislike it less; the constant self-control and anxiety about the result cause burnout, fatigue, depression and exacerbations, so work on masking is as important a therapy target as work on sensory sensitivity [#53].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Two-part definition of masking
Katya Klimochkina defines masking as a strategy of behaviour used by autistic people or people with ADHD, consciously or not, "чтобы *казаться* нейротипичными, чтобы не выделяться из общества и его норм, быть более принятыми" (to *seem* neurotypical, not to stand out from society and its norms, to be more accepted). She adds that masking is also two things [#131]:
1. Hypervigilance and constant adjustment — "повышенная бдительность и постоянная подстройка к ожиданиям и предпочтениям других" (heightened vigilance and constant adjustment to the expectations and preferences of others). The person continuously scans others and tunes themselves to what they seem to want [#131].
2. Rigid control of self-expression — "жесткий контроль и адаптация стратегий самовыражения" (tight control and adaptation of self-expression strategies), covering needs, preferences, opinions, interests, personality traits, manners or appearance, adjusted on the basis of others' real or *assumed* attitude. The point is that the adjustment may be driven purely by what the person imagines others think [#131].

A later post gives a compatible definition: autistic masking is the conscious or not-so-conscious suppression of autistic traits and imitation of neurotypical behaviour; a person with ASD may hide stimming, memorise social scripts and copy other people's facial expressions and gestures to meet others' expectations [#251].

#### Masking "bingo" — 12 behavioural examples
Purpose: the cards were offered as a self-recognition exercise — "Собирайте свое «бинго» из примеров на картинках" (collect your own "bingo" from the examples in the pictures), i.e. tick the behaviours that apply to you. Each card is titled "КАК МОЖЕТ ВЫГЛЯДЕТЬ МАСКИНГ?" (What can masking look like?) [#131].

Card 1 [#131]:
1. Imitating other people's facial expressions — e.g. smiling because everyone else is smiling.
2. Looking people in the eyes even when you don't want to and it is uncomfortable.
3. Hiding your own interests.
4. Pretending to be interested in topics that others like.

Card 2 [#132]:
5. Asking more questions than you are actually interested in.
6. Changing the tone or volume of your voice.
7. Holding back your reactions to sensory sensitivity — for example, not squinting your eyes.
8. Suppressing stimming or replacing it with a less noticeable form.

Card 3 [#133]:
9. Planning in advance the scenarios of a meeting: what you will say and how, before events.
10. Studying acceptable "social rules".
11. Monitoring others' reactions to you and changing your behaviour to fit expectations.
12. Repeating phrases or little words you hear from others that are not natural to you.

#### Six consequences of rejection sensitivity in autistic people
Context: Katya Klimochkina explains rejection sensitive dysphoria (дисфория чувствительная к отвержению) — "состояние сильнейшей эмоциональной боли, которая может ощущаться даже физически" (a state of extreme emotional pain that can even be felt physically) when a person faces disapproval and social non-acceptance. Disapproval or failure hurts everyone, but for some it is felt far more painfully, and for autistic people the situation is different again. Atypical sensory perception, differing behaviour and social styles are "fertile ground" for not being accepted by "muggles"; autistic people may constantly feel they are doing something wrong, which produces heightened vigilance for disapproval. In essence, autistic people are set up to experience rejection more often than non-autistic people because they must function in a world not designed for them, and autistic people with strong emotional sensitivity experience social and interpersonal rejection more intensely than non-autistic people [#36].

The case of Dasha illustrates it: "Быть аутисткой в мире, созданном для нейротипиков, приводит к почти постоянному ощущению, что я все делаю неправильно" (Being an autistic woman in a world made for neurotypicals leads to an almost constant feeling that I am doing everything wrong), so she overthinks many of her interactions; this sense of "wrongness" plus past experiences of not being accepted made her hypersensitive to the possibility that people will not understand, accept, and will reject her [#36].

The six consequences, in the author's order [#36]:
1. Living on guard (жизнь на чеку). Hypervigilance forces Dasha to watch for atypical behaviour in others; when someone's behaviour differs from what she expects, she notices and often feels guilty, "как будто я, должно быть, сделала что-то, из-за чего человек расстроился" (as if I must have done something that upset the person), and that is why their behaviour has changed [#36].
2. Inflated responsibility (завышенная ответственность). This heightened sense of responsibility for others' states can, over time, itself become traumatising for an autistic person [#36].
3. "Mind reading" — "telekinesis". The pain of rejection pushes the person to "read the minds" of others and "fill in their reactions". When trying to understand another's behaviour and not finding an explanation (which is hard), Dasha looks for the cause in herself: if someone behaves "somehow wrong", then "the problem is me", and self-criticism follows because their behaviour is read as a signal of disapproval directed at her [#36].
4. Focus on others (сосредоточенность на других). Dasha assumes people are angry or upset with her, or that she did something wrong, if they answer her questions briefly, are slow to reply, or are quieter than usual. She feels strong sadness and insecurity, and a sense of urgency, "как будто я должна исправить все, что не так с тем, что я сделала, или с тем, кто я есть" (as if I must fix everything that is wrong with what I did or with who I am) to get rid of the feeling of rejection [#36].
5. "Seeming rather than being" (казаться, а не быть). Autistic people often resort to masking or hide their natural traits to fit into the neurotypical world. Dasha might want to ask a friend why he is speaking quietly and slowly, but she cannot always allow herself to. As a result, masking can intensify rejection sensitivity [#36].
6. Exhaustion (истощенность). Masking on top of all this contributes to emotional burnout and depletion [#36].

The author's closing point: criticism and failure are unpleasant for everyone, but there are nuances that make them felt more acutely; the good news is that "меняя свое отношение к значимости неодобрения - меняется и интенсивность переживаний на этот счет" (by changing your attitude to the significance of disapproval, the intensity of the feelings about it changes too) [#36].

#### Devon Price's ableism → masking → burnout argument
Lena Foer's retelling of *Unmasking Autism*, which she says writes very little about autism itself and much more about the context autistic people live in [#50]:
1. Ableism defined. Ableism (эйблизм) is discrimination by degree of capacity to work, where people who "toil" more are treated as if they are better than those who cannot. It appears to be a logical consequence of the values of capitalism (the author strikes through "and the Protestant ethic"), where whoever works is the one who is praised [#50].
2. Capacity to toil as the measure of health. In our world, the ability to keep working while hiding pain and suffering to the last is the measure of a person's health and worth. Disease classifications reflect this: the boundaries of disorders are drawn by incapacity to work. The psychiatric care system works on this basis, and consequently society's attitude to autistic people is formed on it — including autistic people's attitude to themselves [#50].
3. The lesson autistic people absorb. To be valuable you must constantly override yourself, imitate others, meet expectations — therefore you must mask. This is the origin of the masking problem in ASD [#50].
4. The cost. Constant masking eats up resources, robs a person of authenticity and leads to burnout [#50].
5. The conclusion. Millions of people with ASD suffer not because suffering is an inherent part of the condition but because the burden of masking is added to it: "Маскинг - следствие необходимости принимать как данность условия жизни, которые не подходят" (Masking is a consequence of having to accept as given living conditions that do not fit) [#50].
6. Personal-level solutions. Part of the book searches for individual solutions: what to do about masking, how to help yourself, where to find an accepting community, and how to add authenticity and freedom to your life (the review does not detail these) [#50].

#### CAT-Q — Camouflaging Autistic Traits Questionnaire
What it is and when to use it. The CAT-Q (Опросник камуфляжа аутичных черт) is a self-report questionnaire on camouflaging (masking) in the social behaviour of people over 16. It helps assess how strongly you mask and/or compensate for your autistic traits during social interactions, and the sharer notes it is especially relevant for women and non-binary people. It does not diagnose autism; it helps explore individual autistic features. Authors: Laura Hull et al., 2018; reference: Hull, Mandy, Lai, Baron-Cohen, Allison, Smith & Petrides (2019), "Development and validation of the camouflaging autistic traits questionnaire (CAT-Q)", Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders, 49, 819–833 [#40, #42]. A second post adds that the scale helps you understand your own masking strategies better and, more basically, start noticing them in yourself [#140].

Instructions. Read each statement and choose the answer that best describes your experience in social interactions; tick or cross the corresponding cell [#42].

Response scale (7 points). 1 = completely disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = partly disagree, 4 = can neither agree nor disagree, 5 = partly agree, 6 = agree, 7 = completely agree [#42]. (The #140 translation labels 3 and 5 as "rather disagree / rather agree" [#140].)

The 25 items (English rendering of the Russian version by Yulia An; * = reverse-scored; letter = factor: C Compensation, M Masking, A Assimilation) [#42, msg #41]:
1. When I interact with someone, I deliberately copy their body language or facial expressions. (C)
2. I monitor my body language and facial expressions so as to appear relaxed. (M)
3. I rarely feel the need to put on an act (play a role) to get through a social situation.* (A)
4. I have developed a script to follow in social situations. (C) — the #140 version adds the example "a list of questions or topics of conversation" [#140].
5. I repeat phrases I have heard others say in exactly the same way I first heard them. (C)
6. I adjust my body language and facial expressions so I look interested in the person I am interacting with. (M)
7. In social situations I feel as though I am "playing a role" rather than being myself. (A)
8. In my social interactions I use behaviours I learned by watching other people interacting. (C)
9. I am always thinking about the impression I make on other people. (M)
10. I need the support of other people in order to socialise. (A)
11. I practise my facial expressions and body language to make sure they look natural. (C)
12. I don't feel the need to make eye contact with other people if I don't want to.* (M)
13. I have to force myself to interact with people when I am in social situations. (A)
14. I have tried to improve my understanding of social skills by watching other people. (C)
15. I monitor my body language and/or facial expressions so that I appear interested in the person I am interacting with. (M)
16. When in social situations, I try to find ways to avoid interacting with others. (A)
17. I have researched the rules of social interactions to improve my own social skills. (C) — the #140 version adds "for example, by studying psychology or reading books about human behaviour" [#140].
18. I am always aware of the impression I make on other people. (M)
19. I feel I can be myself when I am with other people.* (A)
20. I learn how people use their faces and bodies to interact by watching television or films, or reading fiction. (C)
21. I adjust my body language or facial expressions so I appear relaxed. (M)
22. When talking to other people, I feel like the conversation flows naturally.* (A)
23. I have spent time learning social skills from television shows and films, and try to use these in my interactions. (C)
24. In social situations, I do not pay attention to my gestures/movements and facial expressions.* (M)
25. In social situations, I feel like I am pretending to be "normal". (A)

Scoring, step by step [#42, #140]:
1. Give each answer 1 to 7 points, where 1 = "completely disagree" and 7 = "completely agree".
2. For the starred items — 3, 12, 19, 22 and 24 — reverse the scoring: 7 = "completely disagree", 1 = "completely agree". These are the items where agreeing means *less* masking (e.g. "I can be myself with others").
3. Add up all items. The total ranges from 25 to 175; a higher total reflects more effective camouflaging.
4. Optionally add up the items of each factor (below) to see which kind of strategy dominates.

Interpretation. The Yulia An version states that a total of 100 or higher indicates that you mask autistic traits; higher scores are associated with psychological stress and functioning problems, "поэтому в идеале вам нужно, чтобы ваш балл был как можно ниже" (so ideally you want your score to be as low as possible) [#42]. The DBT-team version is more cautious: there is no established cut-off defining "high" or "low" masking, though scores above 100 usually indicate stronger masking; Hull and colleagues (2020) found significant differences not only between autistic and non-autistic people but also between men and women, so you should add up your points and compare them with the table of means to see where your result falls [#140]. Both versions warn that the questionnaire was not tested or standardised on a Russian-speaking sample, so interpretation must be cautious [#40, file #42].

#### CAT-Q factor structure — Compensation, Masking, Assimilation
The 25 items split into three factors [#42]:
1. Compensation (Компенсация) — items 1, 4, 5, 8, 11, 14, 17, 20, 23. Strategies used to actively compensate for difficulties in social situations. Examples: copying body language and facial expressions, learning social cues from films and books.
2. Masking (Маскировка) — items 2, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21, 24. Strategies used to hide autistic characteristics or portray a non-autistic persona. Examples: altering face and body to look confident and/or relaxed, forced eye contact.
3. Assimilation (Ассимиляция) — items 3, 7, 10, 13, 16, 19, 22, 25. Strategies used to try to fit in with others in social situations. Examples: pretending, avoiding, or forcing oneself to interact with others.

#### Why identity matters (the author's premise)
A stable idea of oneself helps to determine faster and more accurately what matters in the moment. It becomes a support for self-confidence in hard situations and helps one not to fall apart under criticism or life stress. It is the base of healthy self-esteem and the foundation of inner stability; understanding one's "I" helps in communication with others and improves quality of life [#48].

#### Seven features of autistic self-perception (plus one strength)
Lena Foer wrote the text after an earlier piece on difficulties with the sense of self in people generally, asking what specific difficulties autistic people ("они (мы)" — they (we)) meet in searching for identity [#48]. The features, in her order [#48]:

1. Problems with transitional periods. Change is much harder for autistic people than for neurotypicals, and adolescence is perhaps the largest change in a person's life: society, demands and the body all change, and at the same time this is where the foundation of identity is laid. In this period autistic people very often meet bullying, a feeling of their own social inadequacy, social isolation and negative judgements from others. "Когда все вокруг говорят тебе, какой ты тупой, странный и неправильный - это не может не влиять на восприятие себя" (When everyone around tells you how stupid, weird and wrong you are, it cannot but affect your self-perception). That is how many autistic people's idea of their "I" fills with negativity [#48].
2. Problems in the social sphere. Difficulty understanding social interaction is a key symptom of ASD and affects self-perception in several ways — see *Five social-interaction mechanisms* below [#48].
3. Difficulties with abstract thinking. Self-perception consists largely of abstract concepts: you must not only understand what "introvert", "feminine" or "organised" mean but also be able to try such categories on yourself ("безумие, да" — madness, yes). So many autistic people may (a) lack words to describe their identity and (b) lack the possibility and skill to attach such words to their own experience. Abstract thinking tied to *people* is especially hard: mathematical concepts can be much easier than describing the personality traits of the person opposite, because maths has logic and concrete meaning while traits have "только метафоры и смутные ощущения" (only metaphors and vague sensations). The author says this is associated, for example, with the prevalence of gender dysphoria in people with ASD: gender is a social construct no simpler than "phlegmatic temperament" — first it is unclear how to apply it to oneself, second unclear why one should. Difficulty with blurry abstract ideas strongly complicates forming a "Self" [#48].
4. Rigid, black-and-white thinking. Rigid thinking and trouble adapting beliefs to changing conditions mean that ideas about oneself can also be black-and-white, rigid and categorical, with little built-in potential to change [#48].
5. Masking. Defined here as what autistic people do to mimic society and meet expectations. Among its many problems ("начать с того, что от него выгорают" — starting with the fact that people burn out from it), it prevents forming and keeping a sense of "I" — detailed under *Masking and identity* [#48].
6. Alexithymia. We form identity by observing our thoughts, behaviour and emotional reactions; without the ability to name one's states, the possibilities for such analysis are limited [#48].
7. Opinion of autism. For diagnosed autistic people, ideas about autism contribute substantially to self-image. The more positive the view of autism (more focus on benefits, less sense of helplessness), the higher self-esteem and the better self-perception. Those who think better of themselves usually associate autism with giftedness, emotional resilience and strength: "чем больше хорошего аутисты находят в аутизме, тем больше хорошего они находят в себе" (the more good autistic people find in autism, the more good they find in themselves) [#48].
8. Strength — the analytic mind. Left "for last as the nicest": the autistic mind and nervous system think analytically, assembling the big picture piece by piece through deep, unhurried analysis and logic rather than intuition and fragmentary ideas. Add the love of spending time alone, which gives much time for self-analysis, and people with ASD have huge potential to understand their own inner workings with great attention, subtlety and thoroughness [#48].

#### Five social-interaction mechanisms that shape autistic self-image
Component of feature 2 above, each in the author's words and order [#48]:
1. Self-image is built from interaction experience. Our idea of ourselves forms largely from our interactions with people around us; if that experience is very often perceived as failure, it is harder to assemble any self-image at all, and especially hard to assemble a positive one. [#48]
2. Higher intelligence, higher perceived social incompetence. Research (as the author reports it) shows that the higher autistic people's intelligence, the higher their perceived social incompetence — which is not always objectively confirmed (others see the situation differently). The higher this perceived incompetence, the less people trust their own judgement ("Я же социально некомпетентная!" — "But I'm socially incompetent!") and the more they trust others. So autistic people may rely more on others' feedback and judgements to define who they are (because others "understand better, see better") and less on themselves. [#48]
3. Not understanding social life hides one's own social problems. At a certain level of not understanding communication, the ability to notice that there is a problem at all decreases; autistic people may see themselves very differently from how others see them, and without understanding the problem there is no possibility of changing anything. [#48]
4. Social roles matter less in the autistic self. Social life and interaction play a smaller role in the self-image of people with ASD than of neurotypicals. Describing their identity, autistic people talk less about social roles and relationships and more about what they usually do in life — the author's examples: "вяжу, хожу по холмам, веду сессии" (I knit, I walk the hills, I run sessions). [#48]
5. Hypothesis on agency. There is also a hypothesis that the feeling of social inadequacy negatively affects one's sense of agency — the ability to influence one's life. [#48]

#### Five ways to help yourself form an identity
The author's ideas, in order [#48]:
1. Develop theory of mind. Developing the ability to understand other people's thoughts and emotions helps you understand yourself better. How: social-competence skills trainings, or simply thorough, regular self-analysis of what happens in communication, plus asking the other participants for feedback to cross-check. [#48]
2. Expand your vocabulary of personality-trait terms. Building a more varied vocabulary for describing your identity helps form it more clearly (this answers the "no words" problem of abstract thinking). [#48]
3. Develop emotional competence. The better you understand and can describe emotions, the more material you have for analysing and assembling your self-image. One way to learn it: keeping an emotion diary. [#48]
4. Avoid masking. Attempts to imitate others and create false personalities harm self-understanding, so it is important to work "постепенно, бережно и в безопасном сеттинге" (gradually, gently and in a safe setting) on finding your own authentic reactions to the outside world. [#48]
5. Develop mindfulness in general. The better you notice your thoughts and emotions and their link with behaviour, the more you can conclude who you really are. Conclusion: "медитация рулит, как и неформальные практики осознанности" (meditation rules, as do informal mindfulness practices). [#48]

#### Why women are underdiagnosed
Retold from psychiatrist Anastasia Afanasyeva's talk [#53, article #53]:
1. Criteria built on boys. For both ADHD and ASD, the criteria were formed from observing boys — first because in those times only boys attended school, second because boys have more externalised symptoms, visible from outside: hyperactivity, banging one's head against the wall, not answering the doctor's questions. [#53]
2. Internalised symptoms ignored. Internal criteria — racing thoughts, inner restlessness, inability to relax — were simply not counted or known. Girls have more internalised symptoms, which are both harder to notice and less considered in diagnosis. [#53]
3. Female presentations fit expectations of women. For autism specifically, a quiet woman who relies on others and is overwhelmed by emotions "отлично попадает в ожидания от женщин вообще" (fits perfectly into the expectations of women in general) — the author calls this a very important reason for underdiagnosis [#53].

#### Five consequences of a missed diagnosis in women
The author lists [#53]:
1. Sharply reduced self-esteem, even compared with men with ADHD and ASD. [#53]
2. Difficulties with work and relationships. [#53]
3. Higher risk of codependent relationships. [#53]
4. Risk of comorbid disorders. [#53]
5. Burnout and exhaustion from masking. [#53]

#### Twelve signs of ADHD in women
Context: women have more symptoms invisible to others. On one side this is gender socialisation — more demands on women to suppress everything (inside you twitch and "run in circles on the ceiling", but from the habit of suppressing and sitting quietly it is almost invisible; or you are angry and boiling inside but show nothing). On the other side, women more typically have the inattentive subtype rather than the hyperactive one. Women are more prone to comorbid anxiety and depression, so psychiatrists are more likely to put all their problems down to anxiety and depression; perfectionism and comorbid OCD can also complicate diagnosis. Women with ADHD meet fewer DSM criteria overall, but their symptoms cause as much discomfort. Women also mask and suppress more, suffer more from emotional dysregulation, and the menstrual cycle affects symptoms [#53]. How ADHD may look in women [#53]:
1. Time-management difficulties — while women's load and demands in this respect are often greater than men's. [#53]
2. Difficulty organising oneself and the space around. [#53]
3. Frequent feeling of being overwhelmed (not reaching depression; in the spirit of "I've most likely missed something"). [#53]
4. A history of anxiety and depression. [#53]
5. Difficulty managing money. [#53]
6. A lot of sensory overload (more in women than in men). [#53]
7. Somatic complaints — headaches, migraines, stomach aches and nausea, including because of the load of masking. [#53]
8. Sleep difficulties. [#53]
9. Gender-atypical behaviour: controlling, demanding, irritable. [#53]
10. High-risk behaviour — speeding, extreme sports. [#53]
11. Tendency to addictions. [#53]
12. Hyperactivity often showing as self-harm or skin picking, nail biting, lip biting, hair pulling — done not as punishment "а потому что это только и доступно" (but because that is all that is available). [#53]

#### Ten signs of autism (ASD) in women
Context: girls usually have less stereotyped behaviour and less specific, less unusual interests than boys. They may be interested in the same Barbies and ponies as neurotypical girls, but the immersion is more intense and specialised: e.g. an autistic girl may be into a teen series just like a non-autistic one, but is more likely to know everything about the characters and nothing about the plot, and to show little interest in talk beyond it. What women may be diagnosed with instead: if ADHD is present, diagnosis favours ADHD because it is easier to observe; there is a high chance of an OCD or eating-disorder diagnosis — people with ASD are selective about food and follow strict rules in it, from which restrictive behaviour may be inferred and, e.g., anorexia diagnosed (which, the author notes, really can be comorbid with autism) [#53]. How ASD may look in women [#53]:
1. A tendency to rely on other people who will act and speak for you. [#53]
2. Dependence and codependence in relationships — because it is unclear what to do with the outside world, and adjusting through someone else is easier. Where an autistic boy will just give up and not do it, a girl will wriggle out, try through imitation, ask others to do it for her. [#53]
3. High sensory sensitivity. [#53]
4. Passionate but limited interests. [#53]
5. Difficulty making and keeping friends. [#53]
6. Conversations limited to a few topics of interest. [#53]
7. Choosing behaviour that looks shy, quiet and passive from outside. [#53]
8. Problems with emotional control. [#53]
9. High rejection sensitivity. [#53]
10. Anxiety symptoms and co-occurring disorders in general. [#53]

#### Adjusting is not adapting — how masking burns women out
Masking is characteristic of women with both ASD and ADHD. Women adjust fairly well to high demands, "но подстраиваться - не значит адаптироваться" (but adjusting does not mean adapting) — adaptation is exactly what does not happen [#53]:
1. The situation: a woman who has to take part in small talk at work. [#53]
2. What does not change: she does not start hating it less. [#53]
3. What she does: she sees that others approve of taking part, and suppresses the natural wish to leave immediately; she may imitate interest in colleagues' holidays so well that nobody suspects anything. [#53]
4. The cost: constant self-control plus anxiety about the result ("удалось угадать или нет?" — did I guess right or not?) take huge energy; this is how burnout, fatigue, depression and "обострения всего, что может обостряться" (exacerbations of everything that can flare up) arise. [#53]
5. The therapeutic conclusion: working with masking is as important a target as, for example, working with sensory sensitivity. [#53]

### Detailed insights

#### Shyness as masking
A channel post (author not named) reports coming across "простую, но классную мысль" (a simple but great thought): masking is not necessarily active imitation of neurotypical behaviour. We are used to thinking that people who mask try to talk appropriately, guess fitting facial expressions, make on-topic jokes and copy the social patterns around them. But masking is also about sitting quietly in a corner, rephrasing what the other person said in different words, trying not to say anything superfluous and blending into the background as much as possible. "Молчание может быть самым безопасным способом существовать среди людей" (Silence can be the safest way to exist among people) when every word feels like a risk of saying something "wrong". So "shyness" also becomes a kind of camouflage, when it is safer to seem quiet and timid than to risk showing oneself "incorrectly". The post is illustrated with a drawing of a person sitting curled up in the corner of an empty room, face hidden in their arms [#251].

#### Overcontrol as the other side of emotion regulation
The channel usually discusses difficulties with emotional regulation, but notes the reverse side of the coin: overcontrol (гиперконтроль). It is "не диагноз, а черта" (not a diagnosis but a trait) that occurs in various people and is frequent in ASD. Many autistic people spend years learning to control themselves — suppressing stimming, masking their features, following social rules, hiding emotions. This works up to a certain point and then leads to burnout, a sense of losing oneself, and loneliness. Overcontrol is defined as a state in which a person suppresses their emotions and needs so much that they lose contact with themselves, and relaxing, asking for help or being vulnerable feels impossible. The channel points to RO DBT as an approach developed specifically for therapy of overcontrol disorders, offered by the colleagues' Albatros centre, and suggests that people for whom overcontrol is a problem may benefit from such group work (group dates and price dropped as logistics) [#242].

#### Two Russian translations of the CAT-Q
The channel shared two different Russian versions. The first (#40–#42), translated and adapted by Yulia An and edited by Frankie Drug, was forwarded from the channel (нейро)разнообразная психология ("(neuro)diverse psychology") in both Word (.docx) and PDF form; the two files have identical content — the 25 items, scoring, interpretation, factors and the table of means by gender [#40, #41, #42]. The second (#140), translated by the DBT team "Цветочки" and written in feminine grammatical forms, was shared after a stream about masking; it adds examples inside items 4 and 17, gives the "no established cut-off" interpretation and a simpler table of rounded means [#140].

#### Masking and identity
In the identity article, masking is given its own mechanism: people who mask a lot tend to think they have no personality at all, only a set of roles. Switching roles prevents a stable sense of "I": it becomes tied to context and therefore highly changeable and fragmented. These fragments are usually taken from the personalities of people around — those with whom one wants to form a connection and from whom one needs acceptance. The author adds "еще одна грустная мысль" (one more sad thought): "если вы считаете, что вам необходимо маскироваться, вы уже расписываетесь в собственной неприемлемости какой вы есть" (if you believe you need to mask, you are already signing off on your own unacceptability as you are). This parallels the existing file's Devon Price argument (masking as accepting unfitting conditions as given) and Dasha's "seeming rather than being", now framed at the level of identity [#48].

#### Masking signs in the women's lists that connect to the rest of this topic
Several items in the women's presentation lists overlap with masking forms described earlier: "choosing behaviour that looks shy, quiet and passive from outside" matches the existing *Shyness as masking* insight; "high rejection sensitivity" matches the rejection-sensitivity framework; somatic complaints are explicitly attributed partly to the load of masking; and burnout from masking appears as a consequence of missed diagnosis. These are the new posts' links to the earlier material, not new claims beyond what is written above [#53].

| Compensation | 41.85 | 27.18 | 36.81 | 30.06 | 43.50 | 35.48 |

| Assimilation | 44.63 | 29.00 | 39.93 | 30.48 | 39.88 | 35.26 |

Read each cell pair as autistic first, non-autistic second: e.g. autistic women average 124.35 total vs 90.87 for non-autistic women; autistic men 109.64 vs 96.89; autistic non-binary people 122.00 vs 109.44. Notably, on the Masking factor alone, non-autistic men (36.34) and non-autistic non-binary people (38.70) score higher than their autistic counterparts (32.90 and 36.06); the gaps are largest on Compensation and Assimilation [#42].

Typical CAT-Q totals (Hull et al. 2019), DBT-team version [#140]: autistic women 124, autistic men 110, non-autistic women 91, non-autistic men 97.

- Teacher vignette study (ADHD): the same case descriptions of children with ADHD were read to school teachers with either girls' or boys' names; with boys' names teachers wanted to refer the child to a psychologist, while the "girls'" cases were ignored (as reported by the author; researchers not named) [#53].
- Twin study (ASD), Francesca Happe and colleagues: researchers observed 15,000 twins; with the same problems, boys were diagnosed with ASD far more often than girls [#53].
- Intelligence and perceived social incompetence: "research shows" that higher intelligence in autistic people goes with higher perceived social incompetence, not always objectively confirmed (studies not named in the text) [#48].
- Women with ADHD meet fewer DSM criteria overall but experience as much discomfort; sensory overload is more frequent in women than men with ADHD (claims from the retold talk) [#53].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- Not a diagnostic tool, not normed in Russian. The CAT-Q does not diagnose autism, and neither Russian version has been tested or standardised on a Russian-speaking sample [#40, file #42].
- (!) CONTRADICTION: the Yulia An version states flatly that a total of 100 or higher "указывает на то, что вы маскируете" (indicates that you mask), while the DBT-team version says there is no established cut-off for high or low masking and that scores above 100 only "usually" indicate stronger masking [#42, #140].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the DBT-team file (#140) contains only items 1–22 and has no item 24 to reverse, yet states a score range of 25 to 175, which requires 25 items; the missing items 23–25 are available only in the Yulia An version [#140].
- (!) UNCLEAR: dates of the source study are cited inconsistently — "Laura Hull et al., 2018" as authors, the reference paper as 2019, and "Hull and colleagues (2020)" for the gender-difference finding [#42, #140].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the Yulia An version says a higher total reflects "лучшую маскировку" (better masking) yet that you ideally want it as low as possible — "better" here means more effective/extensive camouflaging, not a desirable result [#42].
- The rejection-sensitivity post's account is a single case (Dasha) generalised with hedges ("может", "may"); the claim that changing one's attitude to disapproval lowers its intensity is offered without a method [#36].
- The account of ableism and capitalism is Lena Foer's retelling of Devon Price's opinion, not established fact [#50].
- Overcontrol is explicitly a trait, not a diagnosis; #242 also contained an advertisement for a paid therapy group, from which only the conceptual content was kept [#242].



- The research claims in both articles are reported without full citations (the teacher-vignette study is unattributed; "research shows" for intelligence and perceived incompetence); only the twin study is attributed (Francesca Happe et al.) [#48, article #53].
- The link between abstract-thinking difficulty and gender dysphoria, and the link between social inadequacy and agency, are presented as associations/hypotheses ("связывают", "есть также гипотеза"), not established fact [#48].
- The women's article is a retelling of another specialist's talk (Anastasia Afanasyeva), so its claims are hers as relayed by Lena Foer; the lists are phrased as what ADHD/ASD "may look like", not diagnostic criteria [#53, article #53].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the companion text on identity in people in general, which #48 says is linked in the comments, is not part of the source [#48].
- The header images of both articles contained no text [#48, article #53].
