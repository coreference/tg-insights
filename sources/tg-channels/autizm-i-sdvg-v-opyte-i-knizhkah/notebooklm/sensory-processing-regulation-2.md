## Sensory regulation, stimming and sensory safety plans

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's practical material on regulating one's own sensory system in autism: what stimming is and why it is a healthy, adaptive behaviour; what sensory regulation and dysregulation are; general calming techniques (deep pressure, breathing, calm environment, sound, movement, smell, visuals); the "sensory safety" (#сенсорнаябезопасность) series of soother checklists for lighting, visuals, smells and sounds; a five-part sensory safety plan; a holiday checklist built on interoception; the strategy of tolerating discomfort with sensory sensitivity, with its full step-by-step longread; community-recommended tools; and the rejection-sensitive dysphoria (RSD) longread shared in the community compilation (definition, self-check, medication and behavioural coping strategies). According to the source it matters because sensory regulation is the foundation of all self-regulation — emotions, executive functioning and behaviour — and because attempts to manage emotions or behaviour while ignoring sensory dysregulation bring only limited results [#37].

### Core ideas

- Stimming is adaptive self-regulation, not a defect. Repetitive "stereotyped" behaviour was originally linked to sensory sensitivity; today it is seen as serving many functions, and competent psychologists no longer believe it should be changed by default — only harmful stims, or stims the person themself wants to swap, are candidates for replacement (see *Functions of stimming*) [#4].
- Sensory and nervous systems are intertwined, so the same actions calm both; calming the sensory system means both relaxation techniques and building an environment that meets your sensory needs, which requires knowing your sensory profile (hyper- and hyposensitivities by sense) [#24].
- Sensory regulation underlies emotional regulation. Dysregulation distorts emotions, focus and self-regulation; the author claims even psychotherapy's success largely depends on the ability to regulate the sensory system [#37].
- Soothers are different from preferences. Preferences set your everyday baseline of comfort; soothers are the "lifebuoys" you reach for in overload, and it helps to collect them deliberately for each sense [#43].
- Have a plan before overload hits. With at least eight senses that can each overload, a written sensory safety plan (profile, regulation tools, exit plan, communication scripts, nonverbal signals) turns a crisis into something navigable [#46].
- Basic bodily needs lower overload tolerance. Hunger, thirst, tiredness and an ignored need for the toilet reduce the ability to cope with sensory-rich settings; mindful attention to them increases resilience [#51].
- Tolerating discomfort is harder in autism because sensory overload is layered on top of fear or anxiety, and the nervous system habituates differently; progress is gradual and combines sensory regulation, emotional regulation and self-compassion; the longread turns this into 18 concrete strategies, from graded habituation to rest after exposure (see *Step-by-step discomfort tolerance*) [#178, article #178].
- Rejection-sensitive dysphoria is managed, not cured. The RSD longread says there is no quick single fix and neurodivergent people may stay more emotionally vulnerable, but medication and behavioural strategies can reduce RSD's power over decisions and life; the goal is regaining control over reactions, not eliminating RSD [#159].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Functions of stimming and when to replace a stim
Definition and background. One of the symptoms of autism is repetitive, uniform behaviour with no obviously observable purpose, called stereotyped behaviour. The author's illustration: clapping in a theatre after a performance has a visible purpose (it is customary); clapping when others do not expect it has no purpose visible to them, and if you do it regularly it may be considered stereotyped. Stereotyped behaviour can be a symptom of autism but does not automatically mean you are autistic — as with any isolated criterion. The most recognisable autistic examples are hand-clapping, rocking, spinning around one's own axis and finger-snapping [#4].

Original sensory explanation. Several studies from the 1970s, the author reports, directly tied stereotyped behaviour to sensory sensitivity: it was described as a way either to add stimulation or to shut out overly intense stimuli. Examples: with insufficient vestibular stimulation it can feel great to spin; hand-clapping can be visual stimulation; focusing on your own whistling can reduce discomfort from a noisy street [#4].

What changed since the 1970s [#4]:
1. Autistic people reclaimed the behaviour and renamed it stimming (стимминг).
2. It turned out to have more than one function. Stimming is now considered a way to:
   1. manage sensory sensitivity;
   2. process information better;
   3. reduce stress or anxiety;
   4. express emotions;
   5. communicate;
   6. cope with uncertainty;
   7. express oneself;
   8. get pleasure.
3. Psychologists ("ok, adequate psychologists", in the author's words) no longer believe something must be done about this behaviour by default.

Rule for intervening. Stimming is important for self-regulation and is generally harmless to the person and those around them, so "while everything works, nothing needs to be touched". Exceptions: (a) specific stims that cause harm — e.g. banging one's head against something or biting oneself — can be replaced with something less harmful; (b) if the person themself sets a goal such as "look socially acceptable at a meeting", this too can be solved by substituting one behaviour for another. But such substitution is optional, and it is "not ok" if someone else wants the person to stop [#4].

More examples of stims listed by the author (her list closes with «И многое другое!» — "lots of others too"; the 13 items below are all she names): whistling/humming; twirling objects in the hands or just moving the fingers; walking; spinning and rocking; jumping; clapping; blinking; fiddling with strands of hair; repeating phrases and sounds; sniffing, licking or biting objects; lining objects up or ordering them in some other way; snapping/clicking; flapping the arms. The concluding thesis: autism means a nervous system organised differently from the "neurotypical", and with that organisation stimming is an adaptive and healthy part of life [#4].

#### Seven sensory calming techniques (retold from Dr. Neff's guide)
Purpose: common techniques that calm both the nervous system and the senses, part of "adapting the world to yourself and yourself to the world" once you know your sensory profile. Katya presents this as a retelling of a guide by Dr. Neff, written in place of a planned (procrastinated) post on sensory diets [#24].
1. Deep pressure — weighted blankets, compression clothing (the author specifies: "not Skims from the Kardashians" but something firm or corset-like), massage or tight hugs [#24].
2. Deep, slow breathing — calms body and mind. Try filling the belly with air on the inhale and then exhaling slowly, e.g. inhale for 4 counts and exhale for 6 [#24].
3. A calm environment — dimmer lighting, minimising loud noises, ordering the space around you. Having at least one place you can escape to that is pleasant to perceive can make a huge difference. Even if your whole home is chaotic, your computer desktop or your Telegram chat folders can be structured [#24].
4. Music or sounds — experiment with what calms you: classical music, nature sounds, brown noise (low-frequency noise like the rumble of thunder or a waterfall) or binaural beats (two sound waves of different frequencies, one in each ear simultaneously); others prefer Kadysheva or Slipknot — "U do u!" [#24].
5. Light exercise or other movement — relieves tension and gives sensory unloading. Yoga, stretching, walking, roller-skating or swimming work; weightlifting or cardio is a good option for people who need intense sensory input [#24].
6. Scents — some, such as lavender or chamomile, calm many people; consider essential oils or diffusers and "don't underestimate the power of smell" [#24].
7. Visual stimuli — they can strongly affect sensory state: surround yourself with calming colours or try adult colouring books; or choose visual emptiness — a sleep mask, "and there you are, practising self-care" [#24].

#### Signs of sensory regulation (what a regulated state looks like)
Context: sensory dysregulation arises when internal arousal is disconnected from the environment, causing problems processing sensory information and a mismatch between inner state and surroundings. In a state of heightened alert ("hell", the author first writes and crosses out) every sensory signal is amplified, and even the slightest touch or sound can push you into a meltdown; to an outsider this may look like a simple emotional outburst or tantrum, but underneath the sensory system is overloaded. The storm affects emotions, focus and self-regulation and can feel disorienting and overwhelming. Sensory regulation, by contrast, is the process by which the nervous system integrates and regulates sensory information so we keep balance and an optimal level of arousal (energy); essentially, our capacity to respond to various stimuli so we can navigate the world smoothly [#37, #43]. The six markers [#37]:
1. Focused attention — we can concentrate on tasks and conversations without being distracted by sensory stimuli. [#37]
2. Optimal sensory responses — we react proportionately, staying calm at a sudden noise or calmly tolerating different textures. [#37]
3. Smooth transitions between activities — we switch activities and sensory environments without becoming overloaded or disoriented. [#37]
4. Comfortable engagement with sensory experiences — we seek out and enjoy experiences matching our preferences, such as hugs, a concert or rock climbing; we seek pleasant experience. [#37]
5. Emotional balance — sensory regulation supports emotional stability and lets us manage emotional responses to sensory input; there is no need to run to another room or fuss. [#37]
6. Enjoyment of sensory experiences — pleasure from varied sensations, from the taste of food to the sights and sounds around us ("the buzz from chocolate ice cream!"). [#37]

"Fun fact" claim by the author: trying to cope with emotions or behaviour without accounting for sensory dysregulation may give limited results, so, for example, the success of psychotherapy largely depends on the skill of regulating the sensory system (in order to regulate emotions and thoughts) [#37].

#### Sensory soothers vs sensory preferences
Sensory regulation includes the ability to process and adapt to sensory information, keep equilibrium and optimal arousal, and manage attention and emotional responses. Understanding your preferences and recognising your triggers lets you make life a little more pleasant. Sensory preferences guide your choice of sensory environment and provide a baseline of comfort and equilibrium. Sensory soothers serve a different purpose: they are *resources* that come to the rescue in hard times, offering respite and relief when the sensory systems are overloaded — lifebuoys when you find yourself in sensory overload. A soothers checklist works as a guide to identify which activities, objects and experiences bring the greatest sense of calm and recovery, from visual and auditory calming to tactile and olfactory pleasure. Because it is hard to find your soothers alone, and for each sensory domain, the series goes through them sense by sense [#43].

#### Calming lighting checklist
Instruction: consider lighting options that create a calming atmosphere for you; experiment with different light sources, find those that match your preferences and support relaxation, and personalise your surroundings. Ten ideas [#43]:
1. Darkness — comfort in a completely dark room free of any light source: blackout curtains or a windowless room ("a closet or the bathroom?"). [#43]
2. Soft lighting — dimmed light for a cosy, relaxed atmosphere: smart bulbs with a dimmer or LED strips with adjustable brightness. [#43]
3. Calming (diffused) lighting — soft, diffused light creating overall calm; paper lamps (like the round ball lamps IKEA used to sell) give a nice soft effect and are very common in Japan as a way not to overload the home with artificial light. [#43]
4. Natural light / daylight — calm from natural light or the warmth of sunshine; even on a not-very-sunny day, try being without electric light and observe the "grey" light of the space. [#43]
5. Himalayan salt lamp — very popular worldwide, especially among US bloggers, and obtainable locally; gives pleasant soft orange-pink light. [#43]
6. Candles — relaxation in their gentle flicker and warm glow. [#43]
7. Twinkling lights (like on a Christmas tree) — comfort and peace from a gentle flickering effect of bulbs or similar light decor. [#43]
8. Light-filtering glasses — glasses with special lenses that filter and reduce harsh artificial light for a calmer visual impression. [#43]
9. Light projector with calming images — comfort in projected light that displays soothing images. [#43]
10. Mini projectors with sky, space or stars patterns — also work as a night light in any colour you want. [#43]

#### Visual soothers checklist
Explore visual elements, colours and stimuli that match your preferences; ask yourself "what is pleasant and calming for me to look at?" Consider assembling a set of pleasant visual images so you can easily use them when needed. Six ideas [#47]:
1. Nature landscapes — Pinterest, travel videos on YouTube, or sitting on a park bench; finding peace in forests, mountains, meadows or any serene outdoor scenes. "Clever people invented hiking too." [#47]
2. Views of water — the same vibe with water: lake, river, ocean; YouTube or a trip to nature, and the minimum plan is watching the water in the bath or shower. Watching moving water is called "maximally mesmerising and meditative". [#47]
3. Soft, muted colour palettes — gentle, muted colour schemes create a soothing visual atmosphere; just as PowerPoint lets you choose a presentation's "colour theme", why not create a "colour theme" for your space? [#47]
4. Lava lamp or bubble lamp — mesmerising movement plus soft light; marketplaces offer them for every taste and budget (the author says she checked). [#47]
5. Relaxation or guided-imagery videos — videos offering relaxation techniques or guided imagery; meditation apps often include video that helps reach calm. [#47]
6. Calming art or photographs — works evoking calm and serenity, e.g. nature-inspired or abstract pieces; for some, paradise is the MoMA online collection, for others Pinterest or Behance with tons of illustrations are a magical way to slow down. [#47]

#### Smell soothers checklist
Caveat first: smell seems even more individual than other senses, so advising can be risky — everyone has their "own" pleasant and disgusting smells. Still, the scents below more often than usual turned out to be pleasant to people. Six ideas [#49]:
1. Lavender essential oil or sachet — creates a calming atmosphere; tea tree or any other "herbal" (or not only herbal) scent can easily replace lavender; diffusers too. [#49]
2. Freshly brewed coffee or tea — can give warmth and relaxation; bonus: distinguish the aroma notes in the package, when poured into the cup, when brewed and when drinking — "nuances". [#49]
3. Scented candles — cosy, calming atmosphere; prefer unobtrusive, light scents. [#49]
4. Fresh flowers or plants — bring fresh, natural scents for a calming, mood-lifting atmosphere. [#49]
5. Chamomile or eucalyptus — a "+100 to karma" tip: hang a sprig of eucalyptus in the shower right on the shower head. [#49]
6. Baking or comfort-food smells — scents that evoke warmth and relaxation for you personally, perhaps pleasant nostalgia; something associated with safety and warmth. Katya's own example: the smell of chewing gum. [#49]

#### Sound soothers checklist
Context: most people with ASD, "at least among those I know", are triggered precisely by sounds — loud, unpredictable, harsh. Few enjoy a fingernail on plastic or a neighbour's drill, but sometimes subtler waves can also seriously unbalance you. Equally, for many an "understandable" strategy for comfort is music, nature sounds or ASMR, and having a calming playlist doesn't hurt. Six ideas [#75]:
1. Music — calm acoustic or instrumental melodies aid relaxation; some like jazz or classical, others ambient playlists without words; for some, Slipknot or Bring Me the Horizon bring a pleasant sense of detachment. Katya's own go-tos in overload: Coldplay, Bon Iver and pop on the car radio. [#75]
2. Nature sounds — birdsong, crashing waves or falling rain; YouTube and nature-sound apps help. Her recommendation: whale song. [#75]
3. White (or brown) noise app — masks other sounds and gives, in a non-ringing, comfortable quiet, a feeling of serenity. [#75]
4. ASMR videos — Autonomous Sensory Meridian Response: sounds or whispers that can cause tingling and promote relaxation; she mentions Gosha Kartsev's many shopping-themed ASMR videos. [#75]
5. Guided meditation — recordings with a guide who leads with words, or apps with calming voice instructions to relax mind and body; Insight Timer is her favourite. [#75]
6. Soft, soothing voices — audiobooks, podcasts or recordings that help you find zen; "podcasts are the past, present and future", and Harry Potter read by Stephen Fry. [#75]

#### Five-part sensory safety plan
Premises [#46]: you have at least eight senses — sight, hearing, touch, smell, taste, movement, balance and interoception; any sense can become overexcited; overload causes distress; overload can occur in one or several senses at once. Where there is overload, it is important to have a sensory safety plan. Its five parts:
1. Define your sensory profile. Understand your sensory preferences, triggers and soothers; learn to recognise the specific sounds, smells, tastes and textures that affect you (overload or calm you). Why: recognising your triggers makes you more aware of when you are stepping onto vulnerable sensory territory and how to soften their impact. [#46]
2. Clarify sensory regulation — for yourself and for others. Identify tools and techniques that help you regain balance during overload; build a kit for stressful situations from self-soothing means (see *Sensory soothers*); write down three of your working strategies for regulating and coping with overload — e.g. deep-breathing exercises, grounding techniques, listening to music or walking. [#46]
3. Create an exit plan. Identify "retreat strategies" and work out how to leave the places you visit often and where you may get overloaded; note position or seating preferences that support comfort — sit near the exit, choose an aisle seat, or face a particular direction. Why: it makes sensory-rich environments easier to navigate with a greater sense of control; proactive planning lets you handle difficult situations knowing you have a clear path to sensory safety. [#46]
4. Develop communication scripts. Prepare and write down phrases in advance that help you express your sensory needs effectively and advocate for yourself in difficult situations, e.g. "Я чувствую себя подавленным, и мне нужно время, чтобы прийти в себя" (I feel overwhelmed and need time to recover); communicate needs clearly and confidently. Keep at hand contacts of trusted loved ones or specialists if overload tends to lead to depressive states, self-harm or suicidal thoughts — people you can call and enjoy talking to when overloaded, or who can come and pick you up in an emergency. [#46]
5. Establish nonverbal signals. Create code words or hand-gesture signals to signal overload and step aside when verbal communication is difficult. The same works in text chats when you get overloaded — having an emoji with an agreed extra meaning "won't hurt". [#46]

Making the plan may feel overwhelming at first, but step by step you end up with your personal "roadmap" for crisis situations [#46].

#### Four holiday overload checks and four tips
Context: holiday (or near-holiday) bustle causes stress, "full stop", which can affect both physical and mental state. The author cites the APA: holiday stress is a health risk — almost half of women and 40% of men experience heightened anxiety in this period. For people with heightened sensory sensitivity, sensory-heavy holidays can be especially hard [#51].

Four key questions to ask yourself or loved ones [#51]:
1. Hunger — check yourself and family members regularly; hunger can lower your tolerance to sensory overload. [#51]
2. Thirst — dehydration can add to stress and reduce coping capacity; make sure everyone gets enough fluid during festivities. [#51]
3. Tiredness — lack of sleep can intensify stress; keep a bedtime routine even on holidays and schedule rest breaks for every family member. [#51]
4. Toilet break — "unbelievable but true": ignoring your body's toilet signals can lead to sensory overload; make regular toilet breaks part of your routine. [#51]

Why these matter: they are linked to interoception, the internal sense of your body that informs you of pressing needs. When basic needs are ignored, your ability to manage sensory experiences drops; mindful attention to them increases tolerance, resilience and the ability to cope with intense, stimulating environments [#51].

Four matching tips [#51]:
1. Eat — avoid hunger: take snacks, apples, cheese or nuts to fight hunger during a busy holiday schedule. [#51]
2. Drink — avoid thirst: take water bottles, even to a party — amid the bustle of preparations it can be hard to draw attention to yourself and ask for water. [#51]
3. Sleep and rest: keep your bedtime routine; plan rest breaks and quiet time before and after big events. [#51]
4. Take toilet breaks: regularly notice your urges; make it a routine before leaving for any event or gathering. [#51]

Lesson: sometimes the most banal, obvious things make us vulnerable, yet we forget them; planning days around these needs greatly supports a pleasant time. For holidays other than New Year, the author points to the sensoryfriendly.net category on religious and seasonal holidays for similar instructions [#51].

#### Tolerating discomfort in autism
One important strategy of psychotherapy (and of life) is overcoming and tolerating discomfort from activities that cause negative emotions (fear, anxiety, shame or guilt) or strong reluctance. Why: this way we learn, in a safe and moderate form, to be there and do what matters to us but is hard (or at times unbearable). It will still be hard, but making it a bit easier is within our power [#178].

Why it is harder in autism: you must tolerate not only the emotional discomfort (e.g. being very afraid to go somewhere) but also the effects of sensory sensitivity during the process (noisy, bright), and account for the specifics of the nervous system, which affect how habituation happens and how reactions to stimuli change [#178].

Worked example: you want your first job, in a bookshop, but must pass an interview (scary). At the interview you talk to a stranger (scary), possibly have to mask and make eye contact (exhausting) or smile (exhausting), and certainly answer strange questions (hard). To get there you ride a noisy metro (sensory hypersensitivity), the office has bright light (hypersensitivity), and the interviewer talks too slowly (sensory hyposensitivity). The author notes that even writing this scenario was painful; managing sensory sensitivity in the moment — so as not to have a meltdown or get so tired that it takes a week to recover — while also having to think on the spot does not make it easier [#178].

Approach: coping with discomfort and sensory features is a fragile balance that can be achieved. Progress is gradual, using a combination of (1) sensory regulation methods, (2) emotional regulation strategies, and (3) self-compassion. Preparation, creating a supportive setting and breaks are very important, as is understanding when to step back and try again later if the situation becomes unbearable [#178].
The longread «Преодолеваем дискомфорт при аутизме» (by the channel @autismis and Katya Klimochkina) repeats this introduction verbatim and then gives the "what to do" part as the method below [#178].

#### Step-by-step discomfort tolerance with sensory features (18 strategies)
Purpose: how to overcome discomfort "step by step" while accounting for your sensory features — for situations where you need or want to do something important that is frightening, shameful or tense and the world around is also overloading (the running example is getting a first job in a bookshop). The strategies are grouped in four blocks [#178].

Block A — Preparation ("Кто хорошо готовится, тому и проще жить" — whoever prepares well has an easier life) [#178]
1. Recognise triggers. Understand your specific *sensory* triggers (loud sounds, bright light, strong smells, crowds) and *emotional* triggers (e.g. social situations, a new environment). Why: knowing what to expect lets you prepare better [#178].
2. Gradual habituation. If the stimulus is loud sound, start with quiet sounds and raise the volume very gradually. Worked example: go to the bookshop in noise-cancelling headphones the first time; the second time take them off and stay there without headphones for 20 seconds; the third time stay without them for a minute. "Постепенность - ключ к успеху" (gradualness is the key to success). Autism-specific reason: in autism habituation to sensory stimuli is reduced, which demands patience and even more gradualness [#178].
3. Start with familiar places or people. Being in an uncomfortable situation is uncomfortable enough already, so start small and with what is FAMILIAR and within your control. Example: if you are practising visiting busy places in order to get the bookshop job, go there with a friend who understands your needs and can encourage you [#178].
4. Set small, achievable goals. Break big goals into smaller stages; instead of plunging straight into a hard situation or into fear, aim for smaller but more achievable steps that gradually build confidence. Rule of thumb: better to make 5 steps than to fail at the first, too-hard one. Example: if the goal is an interview at the bookshop, maybe start by meeting friends there [#178].
5. Discomfort hierarchy. Write a list of situations that cause discomfort, ordered from least unpleasant to most unpleasant. Work through the list gradually starting with the least unpleasant; over time take on scarier situations, keeping sensory and emotional regulation strategies at hand to support yourself [#178].
6. Calm down in advance. Before entering a hard situation, do a sensory activity that gives a sense of regulation or grounding — e.g. a few minutes of deep breathing, stretching, or a calming scent; "stand in the hallway and sniff your favourite candle". This "calming ritual" before entering the "uncomfortable world" can help with sensory and emotional discomfort; the author calls it a preventive measure "that gives a couple of karma points" [#178].
7. Create scripts. Social scripts — short descriptive stories about what to expect in a specific situation — help psychological preparation. Rehearsing how the situation might go makes it more predictable and less overwhelming [#178].
8. Visualisation. Mentally rehearse or imagine yourself handling the situation successfully — both "how it will go" and "how I will cope if it gets hard". Imagine how you would handle potential sensory triggers and what steps you could take to stay calm. Why: it can reduce worry about the unknown and increase self-efficacy [#178].

Block B — Self-help during exposure [#178]
9. Regulate sensory sensitivity. All your life hacks for making the world feel less bright, noisy and overloading belong here. The principle: reduce the intensity of stimuli *without avoiding them completely*. Tools named: noise-cancelling headphones, sunglasses, anti-stress toys (fidgets) or barefoot shoes can make sensations less overwhelming [#178].
10. Add something pleasant. If you like a certain texture, smell or sound, include it in the process, balancing "the unpleasant with the pleasant". Example: if you like a music genre, listen to it in headphones in a crowd; combining a hard experience "outside" with calming sensory input "in the ears" can reduce overall discomfort [#178].
11. Practise mindfulness. Deep breathing, progressive muscle relaxation or grounding exercises (e.g. count all the red objects around you) can bring attention back to the body and help cope with stress when the environment "overdoes it" [#178].
12. Focus on one sense at a time. If a situation is hard because of many stimuli at once, focus on only one sense. Example: if the bookshop is noisy, bright and crowded, minimise distractions and focus only on hearing — "Только одно" (only one): sit on an armchair in a corner, close your eyes, take off your jacket, help yourself with a favourite fidget and listen to what is around you for a minute at first, or even less [#178].

Block C — Emotion regulation [#178]
13. Cognitive reframing. A strategy of rethinking negative thoughts, challenging and doubting them. A thought like «Я не справлюсь» (I won't cope) is so frightening and "heavy" that it is hard to ignore, and focusing on it makes it bigger; reformulating it into a more realistic one — «Будет непросто, но я попробую и посмотрю что получится» (it won't be easy, but I'll try and see what happens) — makes things a little easier [#178].
14. Recognise emotions and feelings. If you are able to recognise and name emotions, don't push them away; the awareness "I feel anxious and worried" or "I feel overwhelmed" can sometimes lower their intensity [#178].
15. Take breaks and recover. Situations where you "leave the comfort zone" to do something "important but scary" are very hard — harder and heavier than you may realise. Even one step towards what causes discomfort is powerful, takes a huge amount of energy and is simply tiring. Giving yourself time to rest, relax and forget about it is as valuable as the overcoming itself, and matters long-term so as not to burn out and give up. Example dose: if you went to the bookshop with friends and stayed 10 minutes, you definitely need time in quiet and comfort — maybe an hour or two, maybe a whole day under a blanket [#178].

Block D — "And also" [#178]
16. Track progress. Tracking your sensations and reactions while overcoming discomfort helps you notice progress, even if it is smaller than you expect of yourself [#178].
17. Be flexible. Overcoming discomfort is, "I'll say it for the eighth time", hard, so planning matters but so does breaking your plans to avoid pressure. If you wake up with a headache, no energy or simply no wish to follow the plan, forcing yourself is a bad idea — skip it ("Забейте"). Adjust goals or step back when needed; progress can be non-linear and that is normal [#178].
18. Celebrate successes, however small (in your view). Mark every small win — a few extra minutes in a busy shop, an extra second without headphones in loud sound, or taking part in a hard social interaction. Positive reinforcement helps keep motivation [#178].

Closing assessment by the authors: honestly, coping with discomfort and fear while balancing sensory sensitivity is genuinely hard; many struggle with sensory and emotional regulation, which makes "out of the comfort zone" situations involving fear, shame or tension especially intense. Still, being in such situations can sometimes help build resilience and the ability to run your life "as I want" — on the condition that everything is done at a manageable ("посильном") level, thoughtfully, and with the help of these strategies [#178].

#### Rejection-sensitive dysphoria (RSD): what it is, self-check and coping
Context: the community compilation post #159 answers "what to read about rejection-sensitive dysphoria?" with a Telegraph longread «Чувствительная к отказу дисфория и как с ней справиться» — a Russian translation (translator А. М. Шефер, editors Мун and Юрий Каан, adapted for the project «Нейросеть», 2024) of Dr. Neff's article "How to Deal with Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria" (neurodivergentinsights.com). The author writes as a clinical psychologist working with neurodivergent people who has repeatedly seen RSD's real damage; the article is meant both for neurodivergent people and for those supporting them [#159].

Thesis. RSD (ЧОД) is a serious emotional problem that often accompanies ADHD and deeply affects relationships and the emotional and professional spheres; an "overwhelming" number of neurodivergent people consider it the hardest aspect they live with. The author says she would like to offer a "top 5 ways to get rid of RSD", but there is no fast, single solution; we will probably keep living with greater emotional vulnerability than neurotypical people, yet the methods help manage emotions so they have less power over our decisions and life [#159].

Definition. An intense emotional reaction characterised by extreme sensitivity to criticism or rejection by others. It often shows up as overwhelming feelings, anxiety and self-doubt in situations involving evaluation or judgement, and can trigger a cascade of negative thoughts and emotions leading to self-criticism, low self-esteem and strained relationships [#159].

Five features of RSD [#159]:
1. Heightened emotional reactions — an intense emotional response to rejection or criticism.
2. Fear of rejection — constant worry about how others perceive you and fixation on not provoking a negative reaction towards yourself.
3. Sharp mood swings — even a minor interaction can cause fast, sudden mood jumps.
4. Negative self-perception — feelings of worthlessness, guilt and shame that distort the real picture of oneself.
5. Difficulty with criticism — even constructive feedback can wound deeply and knock you off balance for a long time.

Self-check: "Do I have RSD?" Not a full diagnostic tool, but it can help see a link to RSD. Eight questions [#159]:
1. Do you often feel strong emotions in response to presumed rejection or criticism?
2. Does others' opinion bother you a lot, even when it is neutral or positive?
3. Do you feel overwhelming anxiety and fear imagining situations where you might be criticised or refused?
4. Does presumed criticism change your attitude towards yourself for the worse?
5. Does your mood worsen after presumed criticism? Do you scold yourself for decisions you made?
6. Does fear of a negative reaction interfere at work or in dealing with people?
7. Have you noticed that your emotional reactions to refusal or criticism are sometimes excessive?
8. Do you often adjust to and please others to avoid possible negative reactions?
Interpretation: answering "yes" to several suggests you are probably on the spectrum of heightened rejection sensitivity. If it fits you fully and/or you have serious difficulties with emotional reactions, consult a specialist for a more accurate assessment and recommendations [#159].

Two routes: medication and behavioural strategies. Both pharmaceutical support and behavioural strategies can be effective; the article focuses on behavioural ones, based on psychological practices that let you respond to thoughts and emotions in ways that support self-acceptance and stability. For medication, always consult a doctor before any decision about specific drugs and regimens; the article states explicitly that it does not call for taking any medication [#159].

Pharmacological support (as reported in the article) [#159]:
- Psychiatrist and ADHD researcher Dr. William Dodson noticed a recurring pattern: patients in long-term therapy, often more than ten years, saw only small reductions in RSD symptoms, while those who tried medication reported gaining a kind of "emotional protection" that in turn let them engage more effectively in therapy.
- In the 1960s, Dr. Peter Wender's findings showed MAO inhibitors (ИМАО) could improve concentration and attention and help manage the emotional reactions now called RSD symptoms. The catch: MAOIs require a strict diet to avoid side effects, which is hard for many, making them a less convenient option.
- Dodson highlights a newer class — alpha-agonists such as clonidine and guanfacine. They are more convenient than MAOIs yet reasonably effective for RSD; although they differ from usual stimulants, they have had FDA approval for decades for hyperactivity in ADHD. A significant share of people with RSD report substantial improvement: emotional blows that once hurt now seem harmless. They are not universal but help many reach remission.
- Effectiveness numbers: in clinical trials clonidine and guanfacine showed a response rate of 30% — lower than one would like but higher than for other drugs. "Response rate" is defined as the percentage of people who experience significant improvement in their condition or symptoms with a given treatment. If a person does not respond to one of the two, there is still a 30% chance the other works; taking them in turn raises the probability to 55%.
- Finding the right drug is often trial and error, requiring patience and persistence, so it is important to work with a specialist who knows ADHD symptoms well. (The article also mentions a full English interview with Dodson for more on this approach.)

Behavioural strategies (the author's favourite practices) — for some, medication's "emotional protection" makes other therapy more effective, but it does not suit everyone and many prefer behavioural strategies only; ideally choose together with a specialist [#159]:
1. Monologue tracking (отслеживание монологов). After rejection the brain may tell negative stories, replaying them and leading to self-flagellation. To break the cycle, practise recognising your types of "narrative": take a minute to breathe and observe arising thoughts and feelings without judgement, and give each "story" a name to create distance — e.g. "right now I'm observing the story 'Rejection'" or "'Bad friend'". Over time you can relate critically to these thoughts, recognise patterns faster and break negative ones, stopping monologues in time and becoming more emotionally stable, understanding it is "just a story", not objective reality [#159].
2. Writing practices. Writing down heavy thoughts can be surprisingly calming: you "talk it out" and learn to notice and understand your feelings — e.g. impostor syndrome, dislike of something, or lack of attention. Creative people can illustrate their experience or keep a literary-style diary. (The article mentions a set of English workbooks by the author for self-work on RSD.) [#159]
3. Radical acceptance. Accepting emotions without judgement or resistance helps handle unpleasant feelings more effectively — especially important for neurodivergent people prone to avoidance. It can be hard because of alexithymia (reduced ability to understand one's own state), social stigma or past trauma. To practise: acknowledge emotions and let them be; observe them without trying to change or hide them. It takes time but develops mindfulness and lowers self-criticism [#159].
4. Emotional distancing. If radical acceptance feels too hard, look at your experience from outside — e.g. as a passer-by or a friend; this perspective can significantly speed up the passing of negative emotions. Monologue tracking is one example of distancing. Additionally, if an emotion becomes especially overwhelming, use distraction — watching a favourite series, film or funny social-media clips until the emotions feel less intense and better controllable [#159].
5. Working with the amygdala. RSD triggers a stress response including "fight or flight", activating the fear centre (amygdala), which can intensify the emotional reaction. Techniques to regain control and register that nothing threatens you (explore them to find yours) [#159]:
   - Deep breathing: (1) inhale deeply through the nose counting to 4; (2) hold 1–2 seconds; (3) exhale slowly through the mouth counting to 6. The lengthened exhale is described as crucial for triggering the relaxation response.
   - Progressive muscle relaxation (ПМР): tense then relax different muscle groups starting from the feet and moving up; this activates the body's "relaxation response", reduces the emotional impact of stress and rejection and relieves muscle tension. If that is hard, try a body "scan" releasing tension through visualisation.
   - Meditation: teaches observing anxious thoughts and emotions objectively, improving self-regulation; caveat: meditation does not work for all neurodivergent people — experiment to find what suits you.
   - Grounding practices: engaging all 5 senses distracts attention from anxious thoughts and emotions and returns you to the present. Identify what calms you — e.g. a warm bath, pleasant scents, putting hands under cool water, or drinking tea; concentrate on the sensation and redirect attention as needed to regulate the nervous system and reduce rejection sensitivity. Prepare a list of calming activities or objects in advance so they are easier to use during strong emotions.
   - Visualisation: immersion in calming mental images or scenarios can also trigger the relaxation response and helps stop rumination; some enhance it with music such as binaural beats or drumming to relax fully and focus.
   Building these practices into daily life can help you distance from anxious thoughts and emotions [#159].
6. Self-compassion and acceptance. Rejection often activates negative inner talk; self-compassion "flips the script" and changes the narrative. Use mental affirmations or affirmation cards to remind yourself of your inner worth, choosing statements that resonate now. Example list: «Это трудный момент.» (This is a difficult moment.); «Это болезненный опыт. Мне больно, потому что мне не всё равно.» (This is a painful experience. It hurts because I care.); «Этот опыт болезненный, и это нормально.» (This experience is painful, and that is okay.); «Я учусь и расту благодаря этому.» (I learn and grow from this.); «Моя ценность не определяется одним этим опытом.» (My worth is not defined by this one experience.); «Ошибки — это часть человеческого бытия, и они не определяют меня.» (Mistakes are part of being human and do not define me.); «Я устойчив(а) и могу преодолевать трудности.» (I am resilient and can overcome difficulties.). Affirmation cards shown in the article: «У меня достаточно сил, чтобы преодолеть конфликты и недопонимания в дружеских отношениях» (I have enough strength to overcome conflicts and misunderstandings in friendships); «Моя чувствительность делает меня чутким к чувствам окружающих и моим собственным. Это обогащает мои отношения» (My sensitivity makes me attuned to others' feelings and my own; it enriches my relationships); «Я работаю над тем, чтобы избавиться от потребности постоянно быть уверенным(ой) в стабильности моих дружеских отношений» (I'm working on letting go of the need to be constantly sure my friendships are stable); «Я привлекаю людей, которые ценят мои качества и ту глубину, которую я привношу в наши отношения» (I attract people who value my qualities and the depth I bring to our relationships). Self-compassion and affirmations take time, but with patience and consistency they become powerful tools [#159].
   - Translator's note: you do not have to speak about yourself positively if that feels fake or unnatural; instead you can treat yourself with care, at least by meeting basic needs for food and sleep — this alone can noticeably improve how you feel when vulnerable [#159].

Conclusion of the article. With ADHD, RSD is often a serious problem; some vulnerability may always remain, but we can learn to handle it. The goal is not to eliminate RSD but to regain control over emotional reactions and build a trusting relationship with oneself. Finding the right approach takes time and experimentation, so patience and collaboration with an ADHD-savvy specialist are needed; cultivating self-compassion and self-acceptance is central; you are not alone, and turning to peers, therapists or support groups can be incredibly helpful. Building emotional resilience is gradual and needs practice: celebrate every bit of progress, however small, and be kind to yourself during setbacks. RSD may be part of your experience but does not define your personality or limit your potential [#159].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The 1970s studies linking stereotyped behaviour to sensory sensitivity are mentioned without names or details [#4].
- Stereotyped behaviour alone is not diagnostic of autism [#4].
- #24 and the soother checklists are explicitly a retelling/adaptation of Dr. Neff's material, mixed with Katya's personal preferences (music choices, chewing-gum smell, Insight Timer); all suggestions are framed as options to experiment with, with "U do u" individuality [#24, #43, #75].
- The claim that psychotherapy success "largely depends" on sensory regulation is the author's "fun fact", given without a cited study [#37].
- Smell advice is hedged: smells are highly individual and recommending them "may be dangerous" [#49].
- "Most people with ASD are triggered by sounds" is based on people Katya knows, not on data [#75].
- The APA holiday-stress figures are cited without year or survey details [#51].
- Product/tool recommendations in #159 come from conference participants, not from tested reviews by the authors [#159].
- The discomfort-tolerance longread is practical advice from the channel authors, not a cited protocol; its claim that habituation to sensory stimuli is reduced in autism is stated without a reference [#178].
- The RSD longread is a translation of Dr. Neff's article; its medication content reports Dr. Dodson's observations and response-rate figures (30% per drug, 55% if both tried in turn) without naming the trials. It states explicitly that it does not call for taking any medication and that a doctor must be consulted [#159].
- The RSD self-check is explicitly not a diagnostic tool [#159].
- Meditation is flagged as not working for all neurodivergent people; radical acceptance may be hindered by alexithymia, stigma or trauma [#159].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the RSD longread was shared in a community compilation and concerns emotional (rejection) sensitivity, mainly in ADHD, rather than sensory regulation; it is included here because it is the source of post #159 [#159, article #159].
