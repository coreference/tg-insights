## Meltdowns and shutdowns: triggers, differences and recovery

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's series "Мелтдауны и шатдауны 101" (Meltdowns and shutdowns 101) by Katya Klimochkina (with comments by Lena Foer), a video recommendation showing what a meltdown feels like, and a later channel post on what to do in the hours after a meltdown. It covers causes (overload sources and vulnerability factors), the definitions of meltdown and shutdown and how they differ, everyday coping strategies, and a recovery protocol for oneself and for close people. [#17, #18, #19, #58, #279]

According to the source, meltdowns and shutdowns are "то, что может случаться, когда людям с РАС становится невыносимо жить" (what can happen when life becomes unbearable for people with ASD) and are "очень интенсивные и изнуряющие переживания" (very intense and exhausting experiences). The channel's later post explains why recovery deserves attention: "Про сами мелтдауны написано порядочно, а про то, что происходит следующие несколько часов, почти ничего" (Quite a lot has been written about meltdowns themselves, but almost nothing about what happens in the following few hours). [#17, #279]

### Core ideas

- Overload is the root cause. Both meltdowns and shutdowns are reactions to stress stronger than the person can "digest"; the stress comes from sensory, social, change-related, demand-related and anxiety sources, and is made more likely by hunger, sleep loss and overwork. See *Five overload sources plus vulnerability factors*. [#17]
- Meltdown is outward, shutdown is inward. A meltdown is loss of control expressed outwardly (screams, crying, aggression); a shutdown is loss of control toward withdrawal and switching off. A shutdown looks less intense from outside but can be just as exhausting. [#18]
- A meltdown is not a tantrum. It may look like anger, hysterics, panic or anxiety, but its cause is overload and the inability to "withstand" what has piled up; it arises from a combination of sensory overload, stressors and difficulties with emotional regulation. [#18, #19]
- Coping is about knowing your triggers and arranging your life around them: identify triggers, have a safe space, agree with close people to be left alone there, use self-regulation techniques, keep a routine and announce changes, and build support. [#19]
- Recovery after a meltdown is its own phase. Exhaustion, physical symptoms, memory gaps and almost always shame follow; shame slows recovery. Physical comfort first, no decisions, food and water, rest, the familiar; analysis later. [#279]
- The most useful help is prepared in advance — a plan made on a calm day, agreed with close people, because during and right after a meltdown access to one's own thinking drops. [#279]
- Frequent meltdowns are a signal about the environment, which demands more than the person can give; this is worth taking to a specialist who understands sensory and social load. [#279]

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Five overload sources plus vulnerability factors
Purpose: to understand what causes meltdowns and shutdowns. Overall, the cause is overload ("перегрузка"): both are a reaction to stress greater than the person is able to process ("более сильный, чем человек способен переварить"). For a person with ASD such stress can be caused by: [#17]
1. Sensory overload — too much sensory information that is too intense *for you* (the individual threshold matters). [#17]
2. Social overload — too much communication, or communication that is too complex. [#17]
3. Sudden changes in anything, for example in plans. [#17]
4. Too many demands about what needs to be done — especially when they have poured in from different directions and are unrelated to each other. [#17]
5. Strong anxiety. [#17]

Vulnerability factors sit on top of these immediate causes: if you have not eaten, have not slept and have overworked, you are more likely to be "knocked out" ("вышибет") than when you are fed and rested. The author ironically signs this as obvious — "(всегда ваш, Кэп)" ("always yours, Captain [Obvious]"). [#17]

The post is illustrated with a meme [#17]: the text "CANNOT STAND LIGHT TOUCH OR LOUD NOISE" followed by "FULL BLOWN SENSORY MELTDOWN" — illustrating that light touch or loud noise alone can tip into a full sensory meltdown. [#17]

The later post (#19) expands two of these sources with examples: [#19]
- Sensory overload, example: imagine being in a noisy, crowded place with bright lighting and strong smells — for a person with ASD this can be overwhelming, causing anxiety and irritability. Overload can also happen at home when something goes in an unaccustomed way, e.g. someone at home abruptly turns on music or switches on all the lights. [#19]
- Emotional overload: meltdowns are linked to strong emotions that flood and carry the person away; the emotion can be any — "good" or "bad", disappointment, anger or fear — as long as it is intense and all-consuming. Many people find it hard to recognise and express their emotions, and for a person with ASD it may be even harder, "но от этого эмоций меньше не становится" (but that does not make the emotions any fewer), which sometimes leads to meltdowns as a way of discharging accumulated stress. [#19]

#### Meltdown vs shutdown distinction
Meltdown (мелтдаун, срыв) — a state of loss of control during which the person, as a rule, actively expresses themselves in some way. It can include, in any combination: [#18]
1. Screaming, shrieking. [#18]
2. Crying, sobbing. [#18]
3. Physical aggression — undirected (e.g. breaking something), directed at oneself, or directed at another person. [#18]

To others meltdowns can look like outbursts of anger, tantrums ("истериками"), panic attacks or anxiety, but their cause is overload — the impossibility of "withstanding" what has piled up — while the behavioural form of expression can vary. [#18]

Shutdown (шатдаун, отключение) — also a reaction to overload and a state of loss of control over behaviour, but toward withdrawal and switching off. It can look like: [#18]
1. Silence and the inability to start speaking, even if you want to. [#18]
2. Inability to communicate in any way at all. [#18]
3. Retreating to a quiet dark corner, away from whatever caused the shutdown. [#18]
4. Inability to move from the spot, to move at all. [#18]
5. Lying motionless somewhere in the foetal position. [#18]

Mechanism offered: a shutdown may be connected with the brain not understanding how to react, or with you being unable to react the way you want because the mind has lost the ability to process information and "слился" (has dropped out / given up). [#18]

Summary of the difference: a meltdown manifests outwardly, while a shutdown consists of internal reactions and behaviour. A shutdown seems less intense from the outside, but can be just as exhausting an experience as a highly visible meltdown. [#18]

Meltdown vs ordinary emotional outburst (#19): a meltdown differs from explosive reactions — emotional outbursts can happen to anyone, whereas meltdowns in autism are caused by a combination of sensory overload, stressors and difficulties in emotional regulation. [#19]

#### Six coping rules for meltdowns
Purpose: ideas for "hacking the system" so one can cope even with such difficult states. The author frames them as shared ideas ("Делимся идеями"). [#19]
1. "Знать врага в лицо" — know the enemy by sight (identify your triggers). Called "the first rule of Fight Club". It is very hard to do anything without knowing what you are dealing with, so identify what exactly leads to your meltdowns — specific things such as loud sounds, bright light or crowded places. How: for some time, write down what exactly might have led to each meltdown, keep a record, and build your own sensory profile (the channel promised a more detailed post on sensory profiles). [#19]
2. «Я в домике» — "I'm in my little house" (a safe space). Everyone needs a safe place where they can go and know that everything there is calm and predictable. Create a safe, quiet place where you can withdraw in difficult moments, and equip it with things that help you relax — for example noise-cancelling headphones, stress-relief toys, knitting needles, or a Switch with Mario ready to go. [#19]
3. Agreement with close people to be left alone. If circumstances allow, try to agree with close people that you will not be touched when you are in your safe place with headphones on. If a person needs a time-out to calm down, it is important to give them that opportunity. Try to get across to close people — perhaps by letting them read the post — that a time-out and a safe environment are important and vital for keeping self-control; so if you are sitting in headphones in a dark room with your eyes closed, "пусть весь мир подождет, это право стоит уважать" (let the whole world wait — this right deserves respect). [#19]
4. Self-regulation techniques. Practise techniques that work for you and help regulate your emotions — deep breathing, mindfulness, or some activities/hobbies to relieve stress and switch over. "Не недооценивайте силу хорошей кружки чая и/или прогулки за ней" (Don't underestimate the power of a good mug of tea and/or a walk to get it). [#19]
5. Routine. Stick to a daily schedule: establishing a regular routine and being notified of changes reduces anxiety. Predictability creates a sense of safety and reduces sensory overload. Both you and the people close to you need to know this. Example: an announcement like «через 5 минут выезжаем на дачу» ("we're leaving for the dacha in 5 minutes") is far from suitable for everyone — Katya adds "(я бы не поехала - Катя)" ("I wouldn't go — Katya"). So take this trait into account: plan, discuss options, and give time to "принять информацию и начать жить согласно ей" (take in the information and start living according to it). [#19]
6. Seek support. Self-described as a "vanilla comment": create a support network of understanding friends, family or professionals. Meltdowns (and not only they) are hard, so if close people matter to you, sometimes they can just be nearby — "просто побыть" (simply be there). [#19]

#### Self-care protocol for after a meltdown (community experience)
Context from the channel (#279): there are no clinical trials testing ways of recovering after a meltdown. What exists is qualitative research in which autistic people describe their experience, a recent review of eighteen studies on communication in distress, and accumulated community experience; the post is based on these sources. [#279]

What happens afterwards: after the adrenaline surge people feel exhaustion and heaviness, sometimes a headache and muscles aching from tension. Some have a memory gap, and shame almost always comes. Shame was what autistic teenagers talked about most in interviews: "мне было стыдно, потому что из-за меня класс не мог учиться" (I was ashamed because because of me the class couldn't study). Mechanism: a meltdown happens when the load has exceeded the capacity of the nervous system, and shame lies on top as an additional burden and in turn slows recovery. [#279]

What helps oneself, per community experience: [#279]
1. Physical comfort first, including lowering sensory stimulation: dark, quiet, warm, a heavy blanket. [#279]
2. No decisions right afterwards. Shame and guilt may push you to act, but it is better not to make any decisions straight away: don't have it out with anyone, don't write apologies, don't quit your job, don't delete accounts. "Все это подождет до завтра" (All of this will wait until tomorrow). [#279]
3. Water and food, even if you don't want them — at least a little. Hunger and thirst after a meltdown are easy not to notice. [#279]
4. Lie down — or better, sleep. Many are helped by a horizontal position, and sleep is better still. [#279]
5. The familiar and predictable adds comfort: special interests, a series watched a hundred times, familiar music in headphones. [#279]
6. Postpone the debrief. Analysing what happened is better put off; and even when you get to it, many find it easier to analyse in writing than out loud. [#279]

#### What a close person can do (research-based)
From the studies the channel cites: [#279]
1. A calm environment. This is the most consistent factor found in the review of eighteen studies. [#279]
2. Fewer words. Asked what good things one can say to a person in a meltdown, one participant answered: "хорошо бы не говорить ничего и дать мне говорить" (it would be good to say nothing and let me talk). [#279]
3. Agree in advance rather than figuring it out in the moment — whether to touch or not, talk or stay silent, make eye contact or not. One of the studies lists this among working strategies: a plan made for the specific person and agreed upon in advance. [#279]
4. Don't leave the person alone unless they asked for it. This is another thing worth agreeing on beforehand. [#279]

#### What makes it worse (from teenagers' interviews)
The teenagers interviewed were completely unanimous on this. Things that make it worse: [#279]
1. A commanding (bossy) tone. [#279]
2. A mocking tone. [#279]
3. Shaming, e.g. «ведешь себя как маленький» ("you're acting like a little child"). [#279]
4. Demanding an immediate explanation. [#279]
5. And separately — an insistent «успокойся» ("calm down"). [#279]

#### Six shutdown patterns described by autistic adults (2026 study)
A shutdown often follows a meltdown: the body seems to switch off, speech disappears or comes with enormous difficulty, everything slows down. In a 2026 study the authors collected how autistic adults describe this state, and six recurring patterns emerged: [#279]
1. Freezing. [#279]
2. The feeling of a "frozen computer" ("зависшего компьютера"). [#279]
3. Withdrawing into oneself. [#279]
4. "Я перестаю успевать" ("I stop keeping up"). [#279]
5. Survival mode. [#279]
6. Playing a role (acting out a part). [#279]
(!) INCOMPLETE: the source names the six patterns but does not explain them further, and does not give the 2026 study's citation. [#279]

Key warning: from the outside a shutdown is easily read as "успокоился" ("has calmed down") or "дуется" ("is sulking"), and this is "самое вредное недоразумение из возможных" (the most harmful misunderstanding possible): the person has not gone anywhere and hears everything. [#279]

#### Post-meltdown plan made in advance
"Самое полезное - то, что сделано заранее" (The most useful thing is what is done in advance). A plan for after a meltdown is best drawn up on a calm day, not in the midst of a crisis. Its contents, per the source: [#279]
1. What helps. [#279]
2. What gets in the way. [#279]
3. Whom you can call. [#279]
4. What to say to the people around you. [#279]
5. Where to go. [#279]
Why: during a meltdown and immediately afterwards access to one's own thinking drops, and no decisions get made there. [#279]

If meltdowns have become frequent, it is important to examine what is happening in the external environment — where it demands more from you than you can give. This is worth taking to a specialist who understands what sensory and social load are. [#279]

## Autistic and ADHD burnout, boom-or-bust and pacing

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's material on autistic and ADHD burnout: what it is, what causes it and raises its risk, how it shows up, how long it lasts and how to recover; the boom-or-bust cycle of overworking on good days and crashing afterwards, and the traits that make autistic and ADHD people prone to it; pacing systems for spending energy evenly; a reminder that the 40-hour week is not a health benchmark; and a forwarded card series with the main results of a survey of 1027 Russian-speaking autistic and ADHD people about work resilience. Lena Foer frames autistic burnout as "одна из больших и важных тем" (one of the big and important topics) that a single post can only open [#23]; Katya Klimochkina presents pacing as the way to break a cycle that otherwise makes each recovery longer [#167, #169]. The survey shows burnout as the most widespread work problem in its sample [#271].

### Core ideas

- Autistic burnout is a distinct, long-lasting exhaustion state produced by stressors such as heavy expectations, masking and an unsuitable environment; it includes sensory vulnerability and loss of skills, and can last weeks to years (see *Autistic burnout 101*) [#23].
- Autistic and ADHD burnout arises when inner resources are depleted after long periods of strong stress, overwork and overexcitation; everyday tasks feel more numerous, cognition worsens and emotional well-being suffers [#167].
- Boom-or-bust is a self-worsening cycle: overworking on good days leads to crashes, and each round takes longer to recover from, ending in exhaustion and burnout [#167].
- Specific neurodivergent traits make people vulnerable to the cycle: hyperfocus, interoception problems, people-pleasing and internalized ableism [#168].
- Ups and downs cannot be avoided, but pacing can interrupt the cycle: the interest-driven, all-or-nothing nervous system makes swings inevitable, yet pacing systems spread energy more evenly and reduce "power outages" [#169].
- The 8-hour day / 5-day week is a historical compromise, not a health norm; weekly output is not a universal measure of health, and this matters for autism [#35].
- Employment does not end burnout: in the survey, burnout is the most common problem, many quit to protect themselves, and returning to work requires both manageable conditions and sufficient income [#271, #272, #274].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Autistic burnout 101
Lena Foer's overview, offered as "началом разговора на тему" (the start of a conversation on the topic) rather than a complete treatment [#23].

Definition. Autistic burnout is "состояние эмоционального, физического и психологического истощения" (a state of emotional, physical and psychological exhaustion) that results from exposure to stress factors, may last from weeks to years, and involves heightened vulnerability to sensory stimuli, loss of skills and loss of the ability to function at the previous level [#23].

What causes it (5 causes):
1. Trying to meet high, very draining expectations — for example keeping a full-time job or studying at university [#23].
2. Having to mask a lot — suppressing autistic traits and mimicking the people around you: giving up stimming, wearing uncomfortable office clothes, being required to take part in small talk [#23].
3. Living in an environment that strongly mismatches your needs — for example working a shift at a summer camp, or living with sound sensitivity in a neighbourhood full of clubs and bars [#23].
4. Having to interact with people more than is comfortable for you, over a prolonged period [#23].
5. Changed life circumstances — a transition to autonomy, a new need to handle many more tasks, and similar shifts [#23].

What raises the likelihood of burning out (4 risk factors):
1. Gaslighting and invalidation — when, in response to talk of tiredness, you hear something like "все так работают, и никто не жалуется" (everyone works like this and nobody complains) [#23].
2. An environment where you cannot set boundaries, decline part of the load or ask for help [#23].
3. Lack of support [#23].
4. Being unable to pause and step out of the stressful conditions [#23].

Symptoms (6 signs):
1. Constant tiredness [#23].
2. Sensory stimuli affect your state more strongly than before [#23].
3. Executive dysfunction — problems carrying out everyday and any other tasks, from brushing teeth to school homework [#23].
4. What used to come easily is now hard [#23].
5. Health problems appear [#23].
6. Many strong emotions that are hard to regulate — or, the opposite, emotional numbness [#23].

Often there is a sense that the state affects every area of life, alongside anxiety that it will never end [#23].

Duration. From weeks — some say days, others say at least a couple of months — up to years [#23].

How to recover (5 strategies):
1. Use support: ask for help where you need it, spend time with people who understand and accept you and with whom you do not need to mask [#23].
2. Meet your stimming needs and give yourself time for special interests [#23].
3. Reduce load: less work and fewer other tasks, and less sensory load through a suitable sensory diet ("подходящей сенсорной диеты") [#23].
4. Mask less and allow yourself to do things, communicate and live in ways that feel comfortable — for example replacing in-person meetings with messenger chat, dressing for comfort first, and investing less "отчаянного желания соответствовать" (desperate desire to fit in) into small talk [#23].
5. Protect yourself: build boundaries, do not agree to everything you are asked, give yourself breaks and take holidays [#23].

Re-entry and self-knowledge. When you are ready, return to everyday activities gradually; do not rush yourself and approach recovery with self-compassion. Studying yourself is another important part of the process: if you know what burnout looks like for you, what to expect from it and what helps you recover, it is easier to help yourself [#23].

#### The boom-or-bust cycle
Katya Klimochkina describes the cycle of «подъема» (rise) and «спада» (fall) as arising when, on "good days", we overwork and consequently become overtired, which leads to a lack of energy and motivation. It might seem you just need to recover, but after overworking it is not so easy to get back up [#167].

1. Boom phase («фаза бума»). On good days, when we are "full of energy" and enjoy doing things, we try to catch up on what we missed. We can overdo it, ignoring body signals and failing to notice that we have gone beyond our limits. During this phase we may experience heightened focus, creativity and productivity, be in flow and concentrated on one task or project, which raises output and achievement; it is often accompanied by absorption, enthusiasm and motivation. It happens especially often when we find a new special interest or an inspiring new project [#167].
2. Bust phase (фаза спада). After long periods of intense concentration and effort, we eventually enter the bust: fatigue, sensory overload, mental exhaustion and executive-functioning difficulties, leading to lower productivity, lower motivation and a general sense of being crushed [#167].
3. Triggers and variability. Many things can trigger the cycle — external demands, stressors, changes in routine, sensory overload; "буквально все" (literally anything). The cycle differs between people in length and intensity [#167].

What is wrong with boom-or-bust (3 harms):
1. When we constantly exceed our body's limits, recovery takes longer than usual and emotional burnout intensifies [#167].
2. Mood swings like a roller coaster: on good days we feel optimistic, positive and accomplished; on bust days depression, disappointment and melancholy roll in [#167].
3. Each pass through the cycle takes longer to return to baseline, so we eventually end up exhausted and emotionally burnt out [#167].

#### Four vulnerability factors for boom-or-bust
Klimochkina lists traits that make autistic and ADHD people prone to the cycle [#168]:
1. Hyperfocus + hyperfixation. In hyperfocus time passes us by; being in flow is so pleasant that we forget to drink, eat or go to bed. One night of hyperfocus can disrupt the sleep schedule for a week [#168].
2. Interoception problems. Hidden difficulties perceiving body signals mean we may miss early signs of stress or fatigue and stop obeying our body's signals. This makes us more vulnerable to overstrain, because we may not realize we are tired or stressed until we are on the edge of catastrophic exhaustion [#168].
3. People-pleasing («Пипл плизерство»). A person may prefer overexertion to the risk of disappointing someone. It relates to masking but goes beyond it: "Если пообещал - доделаю" (if I promised, I'll finish), even at the cost of one's own comfort or sleep — hence overwork. The author's example: asking for second helpings ("очень вкусно, можно добавки?" — "very tasty, may I have more?") of food so salty it makes your jaw cramp, then suffering heartburn [#168].
4. Ableism («Эйблизм»). We often hold ourselves to neurotypical standards "recognized by society", then shame ourselves for failing to meet unrealistic expectations; this makes us strain, often followed by emotional burnout. Ableism (from English "able") is defined as a type of discrimination in which people without health problems are treated as the "norm" and as superior to people with health problems or particularities. This creates stigma that forces neurodivergent people to mask and adapt to society — a very energy-costly activity that leads to burnout. The author greets everyone not ready to fit the "society standard" of working 5/2 with an 8-hour day while also keeping the home clean, running and learning languages [#168].

#### Pacing systems (systems of energy distribution)
Starting point. Given the autistic and ADHD nervous system, which is "управляется" интересом (driven by interest), prone to all-or-nothing reactions and to hyperfocus, ups and downs cannot be avoided; Klimochkina says to accept this fact without despairing. There is no panacea, but there are ways to get off the roller coaster: resisting ableism, setting effective boundaries with others, and prioritizing both tasks and recovery. By introducing pacing strategies we intervene in the cycle and break it, ensuring a more even distribution of energy during activity, which reduces the chance of "перебоев энергоснабжения" (power outages) [#169].

What they are. Pacing systems are often used by people with chronic pain, chronic fatigue or other chronic somatic illnesses — when not every day is sunny and lets you be productive. When your plans for the day are governed by your condition, the risk of overdoing it on a "good day" is high, so pacing strategies are a "must have". A pacing system is essentially any system that helps you stably monitor and manage energy and spend your strength in a balanced way [#169].

Why they matter. They help "выровнять наши энергетические затраты, чтобы мы не застревали в цикле выгорания" (even out our energy expenditure so we don't get stuck in the burnout cycle). The goal is a more sustainable life in which you feel more confident in your capacities and have more freedom of action in daily activities, rather than feeling out of control; you get predictability and a plan, which the author calls important [#169].

The three systems follow.

#### Energy accounting («Бухгалтерия энергии»)
1. Metaphor. Treat energy like the income-and-expenses tracking in a banking app — the circle that shows your salary arriving and your farewell to it [#169].
2. Assign costs and gains. Each activity gets an energy value (withdrawal or deposit), and you balance income and spending against your balance on the "счету сил" (energy account) [#169].
3. Worked example. Start with 100 energy dollars. You woke up not well rested but have to get up (−15$). Vacuuming the floor (estimated −20$). Repotting plants (−10$ for the dirt, +5$ for the aesthetics). Sitting in traffic (−30$). Meeting friends in a noisy bar (−30$ and +15$). Total: 15$ left — "ну такое" (so-so); the plans for the day probably need reworking [#169].
4. Battery variant. If the financial metaphor does not suit you, a phone battery works the same way: you wake up at 31% charge — consider whether you will listen to music (−5%) or watch YouTube (−20%), and factor in when you will find a socket or power bank (+%) [#169].

Lesson of the example: an activity can both cost and give energy (repotting, a bar with friends), and summing a planned day in advance reveals when it will leave too little in reserve [#169].

#### Spoon theory («Теория ложек»)
Similar to energy accounting but different: you start the day with a limited number of "spoons" representing your amount of energy — "Сколько есть - столько есть" (you have what you have). Activities deplete these spoons, so you must take into account how many spoons you have per day and distribute them sensibly among tasks [#169]. The difference from energy accounting, as presented, is that spoon theory counts only a fixed daily supply being spent, while energy accounting also books restorative "income" [#169].

#### Traffic-light system («Светофор»)
Classify activities into three colours [#169]:
- Red light — tasks requiring large energy expenditure.
- Yellow light — activities of medium or neutral intensity.
- Green light — restorative, recharging activities.

How to use it [#169]:
1. a) Identify your red-light activities and place them in the right slots in your routine.
2. b) Alternate yellow- and green-light activities.
3. c) Carefully watch how many red-light activities you do during a day or week.

### Detailed insights

#### The 40-hour week is not a norm
Lena Foer's "friendly reminder": an eight-hour working day and five-day week is NOT [#35]:
1. the optimal regime for a human;
2. average human productivity;
3. a norm to strive for;
4. a healthy way of living;
5. something that must suit you if nothing is wrong with you.

Forty hours a week is just what workers and capitalists managed to agree on at some distant point in history. There are people who work 12 hours a day and are fine; there are people who work 2 hours a day and are also fine and healthy — "не надо тыкать в них палочкой" (no need to poke them with a stick). People are built differently, and weekly output is not a universal measure of health, even if someone thinks so. Foer adds that although it might seem unrelated to autism, autism is very much relevant here [#35]. This links to the ableism factor above, where the 5/2, 8-hour standard is named as an unrealistic societal expectation [#168].

#### Survey: work resilience of autistic and ADHD people (Lena Movshina × Neurodivergent Colleagues)
A forwarded card series presents the main results of a study titled "Work resilience of autistic and ADHD people" (Трудовая устойчивость аутичных и СДВГ-людей, per the cover card), by Lena Movshina with the project Нейроотличные коллеги (Neurodivergent Colleagues) [#268].

About the study. Online survey of 1027 Russian-speaking autistic and ADHD people, all with at least one experience of paid work; answers collected May–July 2025. The sample has notably more women — 80.9%; ages are more evenly spread, from 18 to 59. Most participants came from neurodivergent communities, and the autistic respondents are people with low support needs. The authors caution that the results cannot be transferred to the whole neurodivergent community, but are still significant for representing part of the experience [#269].

Unemployment. 25.9% of respondents were without work — a high share, the authors stress, given that the sample includes only people with paid-work experience and low support needs. Among respondents without completed higher education the unemployment share is twice as high: 35.9% versus 17.5% among university graduates. The authors' interpretation: people with different education levels had access to different fields of work and probably different opportunities to adapt the work environment to their needs [#270].

Burnout is the most widespread problem. Symptoms of neurodivergent burnout were reported by: 74.7% of unemployed respondents (at their last job); 69.1% of working respondents (at their previous job); 48.9% of working respondents (at their current job). The share of burnt-out respondents falls toward the current job, but the problem does not disappear with employment: "человек может продолжать работать, но платить за это хроническим истощением" (a person can keep working but pay for it with chronic exhaustion) [#271].

Returning requires not just any job. Among non-working respondents: 75% want to influence the volume of tasks; 72.5% consider stress reduction important; 70.4% need flexible working hours; 69% say pay sufficient to support themselves matters; 48.9% want to be able to talk about their autism and ADHD without a negative reaction. Conclusion: "Гибкость не заменяет финансовую устойчивость" (flexibility does not replace financial stability) — to return to work and not burn out again, people simultaneously need manageable conditions and sufficient income [#272].

Over 80% of working respondents changed occupation. On their current job, 13.5% do the same as at their previous one; 44.4% changed occupation completely; 38.4% changed it partially. For neurodivergent people a non-linear career may be not "inconsistency" but a way to find work that better matches their needs and capacities. But a non-linear career carries specific financial risks, the need to change career positioning, and hiring prejudice [#273].

Quitting on one's own is not just a job change. Quit voluntarily: 60.2% of unemployed respondents (from their last job) and 68.6% of working respondents (from their previous job). The authors call this an important signal for teams and managers: behind such a decision may lie months of overload, sensory and social exhaustion, conflicts, unclear tasks and inability to change conditions. "Часто увольнение становится последним доступным способом защитить себя" (often quitting becomes the last available way to protect oneself) [#274].

Industries. The sample represents 160+ professions: 23.7% education, healthcare and helping people; 22% IT (development, tech support, product management); 14.1% digital (design, marketing, media); 13.4% trade, service, services; 8.3% physical labour, logistics, agriculture. The authors stress this is not a list of industries "suitable" for neurodivergent people: within one field, schedule, workload, communication, autonomy and availability of adaptations can differ greatly [#275].

Full report. The cards show only a small part of the results. Per the final card, the full report has eight sections on employment, industries, burnout, quitting, money and returning to work; the navigation page pictured lists them as: why the study is needed and how it was run; who took part; working and unemployed — basic comparison; which industries autistic/ADHD people work in; difficulties at work; reasons and features of quitting; money, financial stability and support; returning to work and adaptation at the current job. Each section opens with a short summary and a reading-time estimate (whole report estimated at 55–85 minutes), and readers may build an individual route — read in parts, combine summaries with full texts, or use a browser text-to-speech. A continuation is announced for winter 2027 covering values and their influence on work, the influence of the "good employee" norm and self-perception as an employee, monetizing special interests as a career strategy, and general conclusions and recommendations for neurodivergent people and employers [#277, #268]. ## Pathological demand avoidance (PDA) and the PANDA approach

### What this covers

A short series by the channel author, Katya Klimochkina, on pathological demand avoidance (ПИТ, "патологическое избегание требований", PDA): how it differs from ordinary reluctance and from autistic demand resistance in general, how it presents (active vs passive), how it unfolds as a staged process from quiet coping to panic and meltdown, why the main diagnostic manuals do not recognise it as a separate disorder, and a practical acronym-based skill, PANDA, for living with one's own demand avoidance or supporting someone who has it. The author frames this as relevant because PDA-level avoidance "actively affects quality of life" and its visible outbursts are, for the person, comparable to panic attacks rather than wilful choices [#118, #119].

### Core ideas

- Not wanting to do things is universal; PDA is the pathological extreme. Everyone avoids things to varying degrees; PDA is avoidance so extensive that it disrupts ordinary life, is not a choice, and persists across the lifespan [#118].
- The driver is overwhelming anxiety and a need for control, not routine preference or social anxiety as in autistic demand resistance more broadly [#118].
- PDA is a profile on a spectrum — what is avoided and how differs between people and situations, ranging from active/externalised to passive/internalised presentations [#118].
- PDA is a process with stages. Observers see only the tip (aggression, self-harm, flight, freezing), but many quieter, often hidden coping attempts precede it; when they fail, rapid escalation into panic follows [#119, img #119].
- Escalation is a reflex, not a decision — the fight-flight-freeze response to threat — so PDA meltdowns are comparable in experience to panic attacks [#119].
- Diagnostic status is contested. PDA is not a separate diagnosis in DSM-5 or ICD-11, mainly because its behaviour can be explained within existing conditions such as autism and anxiety; still, the author holds it has its own characteristic patterns [#118, #130].
- The case against a separate PDA category rests on six alternative explanations (anxiety + autism, autism, anxiety, many conditions, ODD, trauma) and six arguments: a separate label can steer people away from proven anxiety treatment such as exposure therapy, the evidence is mostly clinical observation and parent reports, criteria overlap, PDA behaviour appears outside autism, and labels risk misdiagnosis [#130].
- Living with PDA starts with lowering anxiety and preserving a sense of control, via a partnership built on trust, flexibility, cooperation and carefully balanced demands and language — summarised in the PANDA skill [#142, #143].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### PDA escalation pyramid ("быстрая эскалация под давлением" — rapid escalation under pressure)
Purpose: to show that PDA is a process of avoidance with stages, so that the visible outburst is understood as the end point of many earlier attempts to cope. The diagram is a pyramid; the base and middle (green) are quieter coping, the tip (pink) is panic. An arrow rises along the side, labelled with the drivers of escalation: "увеличение требований, возрастание тревоги, потеря контроля, снижение толерантности к неопределенности" (increasing demands, rising anxiety, loss of control, decreasing tolerance of uncertainty). The right side is labelled "БЫСТРАЯ ЭСКАЛАЦИЯ ПОД ДАВЛЕНИЕМ" (rapid escalation under pressure) [#119].

Levels from bottom to top:
1. "спокойный, способный" (calm, capable) — the baseline state, in which the person functions [#119].
2. "отвлечение, оправдания, прокрастинация" (distraction, excuses, procrastination) — the first, subtle avoidance strategies. The post adds that people with PDA may appear sociable and charming, may be crafty or use distraction techniques to avoid doing what they are asked; escapism and distraction count here too ("Эскапизму или отвлечению тоже ДА!" — "Escapism or distraction — also YES!") [#119, msg #119].
3. "физическая нетрудоспособность, уход в фантазии, сокращение содержательных бесед" (physical incapacity, withdrawal into fantasy, reduction of meaningful conversation) — deeper withdrawal while still not overtly refusing [#119].
4. control, full compliance with demands (followed by a breakdown) (контроль, полное соблюдение требований с последующим срывом) — the last "green" level: the person either seizes control or complies completely, but compliance is paid for later by a breakdown [#119].
5. Tip — "ПАНИКА" (panic), fight / flight / freeze ("БЕЙ БЕГИ ЗАМРИ"): distress (расстройство), agitation (ажитация), self-harm (селфхарм), shutdown (шатдаун), aggression (агрессия), leaving the situation (уход из ситуации). The post lists the visible tip behaviours as aggression, self-harm, fights and biting, or freezing and physically running away [#119, msg #119].

How the transition happens: if all the "беспалевные" (inconspicuous, "under the radar") strategies to escape the demand have failed — or others have noticed "что-то не то" (something is off) — rapid escalation can occur and panic begins. The earlier attempts are "попыток справиться экологичней" (attempts to cope in a gentler, more sustainable way) that are sometimes hidden from the observer and sometimes from the person themselves [#119].

Why it matters: the peak is "не осознанный выбор, а рефлекс" (not a conscious choice but a reflex) of fight-flight-freeze as in a threat situation; therefore vivid PDA manifestations, meltdowns, are comparable in experience to panic attacks [#119].

#### Active vs passive PDA presentation
The author argues PDA is more correctly viewed as a profile, using the idea of a spectrum in which "what is avoided and how" varies between people [#118]:
1. Externalised / active presentation — avoidance is overt, physical, aggressive or controlling. "Вы узнаете, если человек чего-то не хочет. Вам сообщат, возможно громко и утвердительно." (You will know if the person doesn't want something. You will be told, possibly loudly and assertively.) [#118]
2. Internalised / passive presentation — demands are rejected more quietly: the person simply "не пришел" (didn't show up) or "залипал в телефон" (got stuck in their phone), while the anxiety stays inside and the difficulties are masked [#118].
3. Situational variability — the way refusal shows can change with the situation and many factors: where I am, who I am with, what needs to be done, how well I am coping, age — the author ends the list with "и так далее" (and so forth), so it is open-ended [#118].

#### PANDA skill (ПАНДА)
Purpose: a "прикольный акроним" (fun acronym) for learning to live with one's own avoidance, and for supporting another person with it; presented as a skill that can help overcome the anxiety arising in response to "demands" of the environment and of one's own body. The author's starting point: organising life so as to minimise anxiety and provide a sense of control is a good basis for "уживания" (getting along with) attempts to avoid demands; a partnership based on trust, flexibility, cooperation, and balance in demands and language (choice of words, intonation, facial expression) works best for gradually facing demands and functioning productively. The panda image: big, needing attention and care, waiting for smiles and bamboo, sometimes cheeky and funny, with its own quirks, but great [#142, #143]. The Russian letters are П-А-Н-Д-А:

П — Подбирайте сражения с умом (Pick your battles wisely)
- Focus on what really matters and is necessary; avoid overloading the person (or yourself) with demands that can provoke resistance [#142].
- Choose what to insist on and what can and should be left unattended, based on the person's current capacity and stress level. Example: the dishes can be washed right away or a couple of hours later — not worth a fight [#142].
- Prioritise tasks and demands to reduce overload and keep a sense of control; insist on a task only when strictly necessary [#142].
- The cue card adds: fewer rules; give the option of choice; prioritise tasks; explain the reasons; accept that not everything will get done [#143].

А — Адаптируйте стиль общения (Adapt your communication style)
- Use indirect wording to reduce perceived demands: hints or suggestions instead of direct instructions. Example: instead of "пропылесось быстро" (vacuum, quickly), hint: "дома как-то пыльновато, не помнишь, мы что-то рассыпали что ли? или это тополь цветет? как-то не прикольно" (it's kind of dusty at home, do you remember, did we spill something? or is it the poplar fluff? not cool) [#142].
- Avoid a strict, commanding or authoritative tone; use a calm, neutral tone so as not to provoke the person's withdrawal. "Скажем хиханькам - да хахонькам ДА!" (Let's say YES to giggles and jokes!) [#142].
- Frame tasks positively and as part of a joint activity, which can be more appealing. Example: "давайте на спор, сможем генеральную уборку вместе навести?" (let's bet — can we do a big clean-up together?) rather than "нужно навести порядок немедленно" (we need to tidy up immediately) [#142].
- The cue card adds: use hints; a soft, friendly tone; doing things together is easier; use roleplay and learn a playful approach for motivation [#143].

Н — Не конфронтируйте, договаривайтесь и сотрудничайте (Don't confront; negotiate and cooperate)
- Compromise: be ready to negotiate and find a middle ground — for example by breaking tasks into smaller, doable stages or agreeing on a sequence that suits each particular person [#142].
- Encourage participation in decision-making: joint agreements can give a sense of ownership and help the person manage to do the task [#142].
- The cue card adds: look for compromises; cooperate proactively and negotiate to solve problems; joint agreements help solve tasks; balance "брать и давать" (give and take) [#143].

Д — Доверие и взаимоотношения (Trust and relationship)
- Build trust through consistency, reliability and understanding; trust can reduce anxiety and resistance [#142].
- Show empathy for the difficulties and confirm that this is all "РЕАЛЬНО ТРУДНО" (REALLY HARD). Understanding the perspective in which the world is one big "ДОЛЖЕН" (MUST) helps to understand and support better [#142].
- Rushing or pressure can increase resistance, whereas patience gradually reduces the tendency to avoid. "Улыбчивые черепахи и улыбки в помощь!" (Smiling turtles and smiles to the rescue!) [#142].
- The cue card adds: trust and fairness matter; show empathy and support; be patient, give time, be kind; use humour, distraction and roleplay [#143].

А — Адаптируйтесь к окружающей среде (Adapt to the environment)
- Reduce vulnerability factors: change the environment to minimise stress and anxiety — create a calm, predictable setting and prevent sensory overload [#142].
- Keep a flexible daily routine that allows adjustments depending on mood and capacity on any given day and moment [#142].
- The cue card adds: reduce vulnerability factors; be flexible; have a plan B; give yourself more time [#143].

### Detailed insights

#### Ordinary avoidance vs PDA
Not wanting to do something — getting up in the morning, going to work, cooking lunch, listening to a friend's whining, smiling "because that's what's expected", or your own variant — is a natural human trait that everyone shows to varying degrees and for various reasons: we don't want something and try every way not to do it [#118].

Autistic people may avoid demands or situations that cause anxiety or sensory overload, disrupt their daily routine, require switching activity, or tasks in which they see no point or which are simply "не интересны" (not interesting). In such a moment of avoidance the person refuses to participate, withdraws, "отключается" (switches off) or runs away. Illustration from the author: in one episode of The Big Bang Theory, Sheldon joined his friends much later and, when asked why he was late, simply said "я не хотел приходить" (I didn't want to come) — "это оно" (that's it) [#118].

PDA proper is defined as evading, running from and dodging everyday demands and expectations to the extent that it interferes with ordinary life: the person avoids so much that it actively affects quality of life. Per the author, such avoidance is not a choice and persists throughout life — seen in children and remaining into old age [#118].

#### Characteristic features of PDA
- People with PDA go to any lengths to avoid demands placed on them, even if the demands are trivial or self-imposed — including demands of their own body and its needs, such as wanting to drink or eat. Demands can be direct instructions, routine tasks or any perceived expectations [#118].
- Resistance can be obsessive in character, with avoidance becoming the central element of behaviour — visible as fixation on particular ways of dodging tasks or repetitive avoidance-related behaviour [#118].
- Key distinction: autistic people may resist demands because of routine preferences or social anxiety, whereas people with PDA avoid demands primarily because they feel overwhelming anxiety and a need to control the situation. This control may show as imposing conditions or avoiding any situation in which, in their view, they could lose the freedom of their own decisions [#118].

#### Diagnostic status ("ПИТ Шредингера")
PDA is not officially recognised as a separate diagnosis in the diagnostic manuals DSM-5 or ICD-11; there is no generally accepted set of diagnostic criteria, and there is debate over whether it should be a separate diagnosis or simply part of the autism spectrum. Nevertheless, the author holds that PDA has its own "особенные" (distinctive) behaviour patterns [#118].

The follow-up post and its longread, titled "ПИТ Шредингера (он как бы есть, но его как бы нет)" (Schrödinger's PDA — it sort of exists, but sort of doesn't), written by Katya Klimochkina together with @autismis, state the main reason PDA is not treated as a separate disorder in the world's main diagnostic manuals, DSM-5 and ICD-11: the behaviour observed in PDA "может быть объяснено в рамках других уже существующих расстройств" (can be explained within other already existing disorders), for example autism and anxiety ("но не только" — but not only). The longread then examines this viewpoint in two parts: what PDA is linked to (six alternative explanations) and the arguments against treating PDA as a separate category (six arguments) [#130, article #130].

#### What PDA is linked to: six alternative explanations (article)
The longread presents six "equations", each an alternative account of the behaviour labelled PDA. They are offered side by side as explanations discussed in the field, not as a single conclusion [#130].

1. ПИТ = тревожность + аутизм (PDA = anxiety + autism). PDA is usually understood as excessive avoidance of everyday duties, controlling behaviour and intense emotional reactions. All of these are often seen in autistic people who also experience high anxiety. Anxiety can show up as avoidance and controlling behaviour, especially in conditions perceived as unpredictable or overwhelming. Lesson: the PDA picture may simply be what autism looks like when combined with high anxiety [#130].
2. ПИТ = аутизм (PDA = autism). Some researchers argue that PDA should be considered part of the general autism phenotype. Autistic people can show a wide range of behaviour, including demand avoidance, influenced by their sensory sensitivity, communication difficulties and anxiety level. Reasoning: autism is treated as a spectrum because it includes a broad set of characteristics that differ greatly between people, appearing in countless combinations and intensities; the autism spectrum disorders were merged precisely because of overlapping, similar symptoms and unreliable diagnostic distinctions. By the same logic PDA may fit a broader diagnostic approach, because its boundaries are not clearly separated from other conditions [#130].
3. ПИТ = тревожность (PDA = anxiety). Many PDA behaviours can be caused by anxiety. For example, autistic children and adults who avoid demands may do so to cope with their anxiety, to escape it: when demands are made, a feeling of being overwhelmed (подавленности) appears, and avoidance works well to reduce the discomfort. Supporting point: PDA symptoms can be treated with medication — drugs that help suppress anxiety [#130].
4. ПИТ = много чего (PDA = many things). Besides anxiety and autism, PDA is often linked with ADHD, emotional instability, antisocial personality disorder, conduct disorders and epilepsy. There is a hypothesis that ADHD and emotional instability describe the behaviour attributed to PDA even better than autism does [#130].
5. ПИТ = оппозиционно-вызывающее расстройство (PDA = oppositional defiant disorder, ODD / ОВР). PDA and ODD overlap considerably in symptoms and share features: emotional reactivity and avoidance of complying with demands. Hence the logical suggestion that PDA and ODD may be connected by similar behaviour, while their causes may differ [#130].
6. ПИТ = реакция на травму (PDA = a trauma response). PDA behaviour may be a response to the need to control an unpredictable environment. A sense of threat is characteristic of people who have lived through traumatic experience, so some researchers suggest the behaviour observed in PDA may be a reaction to traumatic events or adverse childhood experiences. Trauma can lead to heightened anxiety and avoidance, which can be interpreted as PDA [#130].

#### Arguments against PDA as a separate category (article)
The longread lists six arguments ("Аргументы против ПИТ как отдельной категории") [#130]:

1. A separate diagnosis can block access to effective treatment. If anxiety is seen as part of an anxiety disorder, there is a clear set of strategies for getting rid of it — for example, in CBT (КБТ) exposure therapy works very well for anxiety: it lowers distress by facing what causes it slowly and carefully. If the same behaviour is seen as part of PDA, the strategies are different — indeed opposite to those that work for anxiety. A frequent PDA recommendation is to create a "low maintenance" environment with minimal demands. The article's point: this gives relief in the moment, but also long-term confirmation of the belief "я не вывожу тревогу, она мне не подконтрольна, я не могу ее ослабить своими силами" (I can't handle the anxiety, it is not under my control, I can't weaken it by my own efforts) [#130].
2. Weak evidence base. Evidence that PDA is a unique disorder rests mainly on clinical observations rather than empirically meaningful studies (RCTs, РКИ). Most PDA studies rely on parents' reports (complaints), without taking into account the views of people with established PDA. In addition, the subjective nature of some criteria — especially those that presuppose knowing someone's intentions, all the more so a child's — casts doubt on the validity and reliability of PDA as a separate category [#130].
3. Overlapping criteria. PDA diagnostic criteria overlap substantially with other disorders; so much in common is confusing and leads to potential overdiagnosis or a high probability of misdiagnosis [#130].
4. PDA behaviour occurs outside autism. Numerous studies and clinical reports identify PDA-related behaviour in people who do not meet autism criteria. This suggests PDA is not unique to autistic people and can arise in very different contexts — pointing to some other disorder that was not recognised at diagnosis rather than something "новое" (new) [#130].
5. Labelling risks wrong interventions. Labelling people with PDA without carefully considering other possible explanations of their problems can lead to misdiagnosis and inappropriate interventions, and such misreading can lead to ignoring better-known, more effective treatments [#130].
6. Framing PDA as new narrows treatment options. Focusing on PDA as a new and unique phenomenon/syndrome may limit access to effective treatments for anxiety and autism; understanding that PDA is caused by anxiety "может открыть более эффективные и менее стигматизирующие возможности лечения" (may open more effective and less stigmatising treatment options) [#130].

The longread ends by pointing to "хорошее ревью и еще одно" (a good review and another one) as further sources; their titles and content are not given in the saved text [#130].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- PDA lacks official diagnostic status and agreed criteria; whether it is a separate entity or part of the autism spectrum (or explainable by autism, anxiety and other conditions) is openly debated. The author nonetheless describes it as having distinctive patterns [#118, #130].
- (!) CONTRADICTION (tension within the channel): the longread criticises the frequent PDA recommendation of a "low maintenance" environment with minimal demands, arguing it brings momentary relief but long-term confirmation that anxiety cannot be controlled, and favours anxiety approaches such as exposure; the later PANDA post recommends minimising anxiety, reducing demands, choosing battles and insisting only when strictly necessary — though it also frames this as a basis for "постепенно сталкиваться с «требованиями»" (gradually facing "demands"). The channel does not reconcile the two explicitly [#130, msg #142].
