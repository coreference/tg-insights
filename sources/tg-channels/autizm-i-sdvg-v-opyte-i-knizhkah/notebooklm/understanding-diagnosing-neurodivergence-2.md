## AuDHD, differential diagnosis and self-report questionnaires

### What this covers

This topic collects the channel's posts on telling neurodevelopmental conditions apart and seeing how they combine: autism plus ADHD (AuDHD), autism versus bipolar disorder, ADHD versus antisocial personality disorder, and the dimensional HiTOP view in which ADHD and autism are bundles of traits spread over several spectra. It also covers childhood ADHD (presentation, comorbidities, first-line parent training), why high intelligence and good grades do not rule out adult ADHD, the positive traits people with ADHD report, and what self-report questionnaires can and cannot say. The source's recurring point is that overlapping symptoms make diagnosis genuinely difficult, so one needs to know both the shared features and the distinguishing ones, and to treat scores, grades and labels as partial information rather than verdicts [#57, #82, #243, #288, #289].

This part adds one post: the author's retelling, in a Telegraph longread «Нейроотличность у женщин: особенности проявления СДВГ и РАС» ("Neurodivergence in women: how ADHD and ASD present"), of a talk by psychiatrist Anastasia Afanasyeva at the Psydocfest conference. The author says she broke her content plan to write it because the talk "обязательно надо пересказать" (simply had to be retold). It covers why girls and women are underdiagnosed with both ADHD and autism (ASD, РАС), how each condition can present in women, what women get diagnosed with instead, what the missed diagnosis costs, and why masking matters clinically [#53, article #53].

Relation to the existing topic file: the existing file only notes briefly that hyperactivity is more pronounced in boys so ADHD in girls is harder to diagnose (msg #223) and that the autism+ADHD combination is more frequent in men. This post extends that substantially: it explains the mechanism (criteria built on boys, externalized vs internalized symptoms), adds gender-bias studies, gives full sign lists for women, and adds the differential-diagnosis angle of what women are misdiagnosed with (anxiety, depression, OCD, eating disorders, and ADHD instead of autism). ### Core ideas

- Autism and ADHD co-occur very often (50-80%), and it is hard to separate true co-occurrence from symptom overlap affecting diagnosis; the combination is harder to live with than either alone because the traits pull in opposite directions (see *Five internal contradictions*) [#82].
- Autism and bipolar disorder overlap in many surface features (mood swings, high energy, talkativeness, distractibility, repetitive movement) but differ in course (constant vs cyclic impulsivity), age of onset, depth of depression and in core autistic features absent from bipolar disorder [#57].
- ADHD and antisocial personality disorder share impulsivity and conflict with rules, but lack of empathy, lack of guilt/remorse, intentional harm and malicious intent belong only to ASPD; a sense of coherence protects people with ADHD from developing ASPD [#249].
- HiTOP sees diagnoses as trait combinations on continua: ADHD spreads over the externalizing, neurocognitive and internalizing spectra; autism over at least five spectra; researchers propose a separate neurodevelopmental spectrum [#243].
- High IQ and good grades do not exclude ADHD: school is a "greenhouse" for executive functions; studies show executive impairments and ADHD-typical burdens in high-IQ adults [#289].
- Questionnaires are a language, not a diagnosis: they help name experiences, track change over time and bring concrete items to an appointment, but give no diagnosis, often no universal cut-off, and break down with alexithymia [#288].
- ADHD has self-reported strengths too, and knowing them gives a more objective picture of the condition and supports self-esteem [#90].
- In children, parent training and classroom behavioural interventions are first-line, built on positive reinforcement, consistent rules and emotional work [#223].

- Diagnostic criteria for ADHD and ASD were built on boys, so they capture visible (externalized) symptoms and miss the internal (internalized) symptoms that dominate in girls [#53].
- Teachers and clinicians show measurable gender bias: identical cases are referred more often when the child is a boy [#53].
- A missed diagnosis has concrete costs for women: lower self-esteem, work and relationship problems, codependency, comorbidities and masking burnout [#53].
- Women's ADHD and autism hide behind other diagnoses (anxiety, depression, OCD, eating disorders) and behind social expectations of women as quiet, reliant on others and emotional [#53].
- Masking is adjusting, not adapting: the underlying discomfort does not decrease, and the constant self-control leads to burnout, fatigue and depression, so masking is a therapy target as important as sensory sensitivity [#53].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Autism vs bipolar disorder (BAR): prevalence, shared risk, differences and ten overlapping features
Purpose: to help separate autism (РАС) from bipolar affective disorder (БАР) and to recognise when both are present. The post was written by климочкина катя the day after her longread on emotion regulation in bipolar disorder [#57].

Prevalence. By estimates (a PubMed-indexed paper is cited), about 5% to 8% of autistic people have bipolar affective disorder. A study of a group of 700,000 children found bipolar disorder 6 times more often among those meeting autism criteria than in the control group. Studies also show that bipolar symptoms tend to appear earlier in autistic people, which the author says underlines the need for early detection and intervention [#57].

Shared risk factors.
1. Research points to shared genetic patterns between autism and bipolar disorder that influence their co-occurrence [#57].
2. Certain features are more common specifically in autistic people who also have bipolar disorder: mild distractibility, co-occurring conditions (ADHD, OCD), depressed mood, early appearance of low-mood symptoms, racing thoughts and social withdrawal [#57].

Why life is harder with both.
- Bipolar disorder worsens social problems for autistic people, leading to even greater disruption in relationships [#57].
- Bipolar symptoms beginning at a younger age can disrupt many areas of life: friendships and relationships, study/work and self-care [#57].

How they differ.
1. *Impulsivity pattern*: both can include impulsive behaviour, but in bipolar disorder impulsivity tends to be cyclical, while in autism it is constant [#57].
2. *Onset*: autism usually shows in early childhood; bipolar disorder becomes more visible in adolescence [#57].
3. *Severity of depression*: severe depression and suicidal thoughts appear more often in bipolar disorder than in autism [#57].
4. *Unique autistic symptoms*: communication difficulties, speech differences, sensory and cognitive particularities, which are not diagnostically characteristic of bipolar disorder [#57].

Where they overlap (the ten points, in the source's order).
1. *Rapid emotional "swings"*: people with both bipolar disorder and autism can show fast mood changes resembling the ebb and flow of mania/depression [#57].
2. *Elevated and low mood with different causes*: elevated mood in autism can be driven by the energy of a special interest, while low mood may result from emotional burnout or co-occurring depression [#57].
3. *Special-interest "energy"*: intense focus (including on people - fascination/obsessive ideas) can resemble hypomanic or manic energy [#57].
4. *Irritability/aggression*: strong irritability or aggression is common in hypomania/mania and may be linked with sensory sensitivity in autism. The author stresses that a meltdown is not aggression, but visually it can look like an outburst of anger [#57].
5. *Excessive talkativeness*: present in mania and can coincide with the autistic special interest in "info-dumping", which resembles repetitive behaviour. If an autistic person is enthusiastic about something, there will be talk; in hypomania the urge to share also shows as talkativeness, though sometimes jumpy and inconsistent [#57].
6. *Distractibility*: tied to hypomania/mania/depression (a general cognitive drop in attention) and to executive control functions in autism [#57].
7. *Tendency to "get into trouble"*: a sign of mania/hypomania linked with impulsivity and executive dysfunction [#57].
8. *More frequent "accidents"*: a result of manic impulsivity or of autistic visual-motor difficulties [#57].
9. *Repetitive actions or behaviour* such as pacing the room or rocking: may reflect excess energy in hypomania/mania or be stimming in autism [#57].
10. *Racing thoughts or trouble organising thoughts*: present in hypomania/mania, or connected with a flow of thoughts in context - for example special-interest energy or neurodivergent communication patterns [#57].

The author closes by saying she wants to learn about these similarities and differences at the brain level too, and asks readers whether there is much solid research on it and to share any they have seen - i.e. the neurobiological comparison is posed as an open question [#57].

#### Emotion regulation in bipolar disorder (the linked longread «Регуляция эмоций и БАР»)
The autism-vs-bipolar post was prompted by климочкина катя's longread, published the day before, on how emotion regulation works in bipolar affective disorder (БАР); she singles out its neurobiological aspects as the most interesting part. Its full content follows [#57, article #57].

1. Emotion dysregulation as the "glue" of mental difficulties. The article opens with the image that "Нарушение регуляции эмоций подобно клею, который связывает воедино различные ментальные трудности" (impaired emotion regulation is like glue that binds various mental difficulties together). Whether the problem is depressive, anxiety or personality disorders, the common thread is emotions getting out of control and causing chaos in the head, the soul and life [#57].

2. What "emotion-regulation difficulties" usually means. When people talk about emotion regulation they usually have in mind a cluster of features: high intensity of emotions; reactivity and impulsivity; mood swings; slow fading of the emotional reaction; and strategies for normalising one's state that do not work very well. Together these make it hard to function in a world full of people and obligations [#57].

3. Emotion-driven vs goal-directed behaviour (the car metaphor). Difficulty regulating emotions means that when it is hard for someone to control their experiences, those emotions start to shape behaviour - and "поведение под влиянием эмоции" (behaviour under the influence of emotion) and "целенаправленное поведение" (goal-directed behaviour) are very different things. The author's metaphor: you are driving a car, and a friend in the passenger seat who cannot drive grabs the wheel and starts turning it wildly, stopping you from going where you planned; you wanted to reach a bright future and ended up in a ditch ("Спасибо, друг" - thanks, friend). Two everyday examples: (a) you want to discuss how your partner is wrong in "reading my mind", but within seconds you start shouting and the conversation never happens; (b) you need to phone work to ask for a day off on a public holiday, but anxiety paralyses you and the call never gets made. The summary line: "Ваши эмоции управляют вами, а не ваши цели." (Your emotions run you, not your goals.) [#57]

4. Dysregulation is transdiagnostic. Emotion-regulation difficulty is a transdiagnostic aspect, seen in many disorders with varying prevalence. The article lists disorders that have impaired emotion regulation as a symptom or significant criterion:
1. depressive disorders;
2. anxiety disorders;
3. eating disorders;
4. personality disorders;
5. ADHD;
6. complex post-traumatic stress disorder;
7. bipolar affective disorder.
However, the nature and intensity of these impairments differ between disorders [#57].

5. What is said to be specific to bipolar disorder - three features. Bipolar disorder, the author writes, has its own particular "flavour" in emotion regulation ("перчинка или изюминка - выбирайте сами" - a dash of pepper or a raisin, take your pick):
1. *Emotional intelligence*, as a component of social cognition, is often impaired in people with bipolar disorder, leading to difficulties in perceiving, using, understanding and managing emotions.
2. *Social emotional intelligence* matters too, because how you read and predict other people's emotions plays a role in regulating your own; impairment makes regulation harder, since without references to other faces it is hard for a person with bipolar disorder to understand "how am I right now".
3. *Facial emotion recognition*: people with bipolar disorder may find it harder to recognise emotions on others' faces - "они похожи на эмоциональных детективов со слегка затуманенным увеличительным стеклом" (they are like emotional detectives with a slightly fogged magnifying glass). On top of that, emotions hit with greater force, increasing intensity, which also does not help keep a clear view when "investigating" objective reality.
[#57]

6. Why facial-emotion recognition and social intelligence are limited - four reasons.
1. *Mood swings*: mood changes can disrupt consistent processing of emotions, affecting recognition more than in disorders with more stable affective states. "Если жизнь эмоциональные американский горки, то навык понимать эмоции развить сложней" (if life is an emotional roller coaster, the skill of understanding emotions is harder to develop).
2. *Cyclicity*: mania or hypomania, and the resulting hyperarousal, affect emotional perception and disturb the accuracy of recognising and interpreting faces. During depressive episodes there may be a tendency to interpret facial expressions more negatively, which also limits recognition - "Мир в сером цвете" (the world in grey), where nuances of facial expression are indistinguishable.
3. *Less social practice*: social interactions become difficult because of mood fluctuations and unpredictable behaviour, so there may be less contact than the person would like, and less practice leads to more difficulty recognising how the other person feels.
4. *Neurobiological factors* (covered in point 8).
[#57]

7. The author's critique of these "differences". The author says that, to her taste, these differences between bipolar emotion regulation and other disorders sound unconvincing: presenting difficulty recognising others' emotions as almost the only specific feature of dysregulation in bipolar disorder seems poorly argued. "Этой части исследования ставлю дислайк" (I give this part of the research a dislike) [#57].

8. Neurobiology of emotion regulation in bipolar disorder. Emotion regulation requires interactions between cortical and subcortical brain areas. Changes in these neural networks have been observed in bipolar disorder, which helps explain the regulation difficulties. Neurobiological studies point to heterogeneity within bipolar disorder, with different patterns of neural activation: for example, some people show increased activation of the amygdala (an area associated with emotion processing), while others show decreased activation both in the amygdala and in the prefrontal cortex during emotional experiences. These individual brain differences influence which emotion-regulation strategies a person prefers [#57]. The article illustrates the point with a comic (Ah Hillam for BuzzFeed) whose text reads "THINGS ARE GOING WELL... TOO WELL... SELF DESTRUCT" [#57].

9. "Emergency help" and adaptive vs maladaptive strategies. Even a person without emotion-regulation difficulties can be overwhelmed: anyone can struggle with emotions in the moment, or long stress can make recovery impossible. What happens then is that the person, as if on autopilot, resorts to «неотложной помощи» (emergency help) - the ability to monitor, evaluate or correct one's emotional reactions. This involves using either:
- *adaptive strategies* - for example, cognitive reframing (rethinking the situation) or accepting the situation as it is; or
- *maladaptive strategies* - for example, rumination, worry, focusing on the negative, risky behaviour, avoidance.
Anyone can go down either path, but at the level of the brain and its activity there is a predisposition towards which strategies a person will use, adaptive or not [#57].

10. Findings: how people with bipolar disorder differ. Research shows that people with bipolar disorder more often use maladaptive coping strategies because of differences in the activation of brain areas. Comparisons with other disorders gave these highlights [#57]:

*Bipolar disorder vs depression:*
- In almost all comparisons there were no substantial differences in emotion regulation or dysregulation as such, but bipolar disorder showed altered connectivity in specific brain areas tied to the "emergency help" tasks, compared with depression. So different brain parts carry out the "how do I regulate myself" function in different disorders. Example: one person in a depressive episode ruminates (replays the same negative thoughts again and again), while another avoids all thought content - possible because of variability of neural activation across brain areas in bipolar disorder: different areas, different strategies.
- Risky behaviour as a way to regulate one's state was shown more often in bipolar disorder than in depression; "risky" means impulsive and without regard to negative consequences for life and health.
- At the same time, people with bipolar disorder showed a significant difference in positive thinking (позитивное размышление) compared with depressed patients, with higher expression of positive emotions - "Радоваться при БАР - получается, ура" (being glad works in bipolar disorder, hooray).

*Bipolar disorder vs borderline personality disorder (ПРЛ):*
- People with bipolar disorder had lower scores than BPD patients on maladaptive negatively-directed emergency strategies - self-blame/self-flagellation and catastrophising.
- People with bipolar disorder more often relied on more adaptive emergency strategies than BPD patients.

11. The conclusion: biology is not a sentence. In bipolar disorder, the biological and heritable predisposition to emotion-regulation difficulty is much greater than in BPD, yet people with bipolar disorder are more likely to apply adaptive strategies. The author reads this as evidence that "биология вообще не приговор" (biology is not a verdict at all) and that one can learn to respond adaptively by learning emotion regulation itself [#57].

The author says the article that inspired the text is attached ("Красивые картиночки МРТ мозга" - pretty pictures of brain MRIs), plus one more article that describes in more detail the activation of different brain areas in bipolar disorder and unipolar depression. #### ADHD inattentive-type diagnostic signs (as listed in the AuDHD post)
There are two types of ADHD, inattentive and hyperactive; the post focuses only on inattention because autistic people with co-occurring ADHD predominantly show the inattentive type. Signs of attention "deficit" identified in ADHD diagnosis [#82]:
1. Makes careless mistakes / does not pay due attention to details.
2. Has difficulty sustaining attention.
3. Seems not to listen when spoken to directly.
4. Does not follow through on tasks and instructions.
5. Shows poor organisation.
6. Avoids / dislikes tasks requiring sustained mental effort.
7. Loses things needed for tasks / activities.
8. Is easily distracted.
9. Is forgetful in daily activities.
[#82]

#### Autism social-communication criteria (as summarised in the AuDHD post)
In autism a person shows differences in social communication and interaction across contexts [#82]:
1. Differences in social-emotional reciprocity - for example, less "ping-pong in interaction" than non-autistic people.
2. Differences in nonverbal communication - for example, reduced eye contact or body language.
3. Differences in developing, maintaining and understanding relationships - for example, difficulty adapting to different social contexts.
4. The criteria also include certain patterns of behaviour, interests and activities, which accounts for special interests, stimming and sensory sensitivity.
[#82]

The author's observation: none of the ADHD criteria concern social differences or communication, and none of the autism criteria concern attention. Yet people do not live in a vacuum: ADHDers do have communication difficulties and autistic people have attention difficulties. She asks, half-jokingly, whether this is a conspiracy, wrong criteria, or something else - leaving the explanation open [#82].

#### Five internal contradictions of autism + ADHD
Purpose: to explain why, according to research the author cites, people with combined autism and ADHD have a harder time than those with a single diagnosis. Each item pairs an autistic trait with an opposing ADHD trait [#82]:
1. You thrive with an established daily routine (autistic trait), but find it hard to plan and stick to a schedule (ADHD trait).
2. You get very upset when a friend is late to your meeting (autistic trait), yet you yourself struggle hard to arrive at events on time (ADHD trait).
3. Engaging in a special interest calms you (autistic trait), but you also need to switch between several interests to stay engaged (ADHD trait).
4. You draw up your plans carefully (autistic trait), but sometimes undermine yourself with impulsive decisions (ADHD trait).
5. When processing information you tend to talk "too fast" (ADHD sign), but when someone asks an unrelated question your answers may be delayed or you may be unable to speak (autism sign).
[#82]

Heightened experience in AuDHD. Autistic people with ADHD show higher attention to detail than people with only ADHD or only autism, and more attention to detail can lead to heightened sensory sensitivity. Many autistic people work "bottom-up" ("снизу вверх"), already noticing every small thing and sensory aspect of their environment; add ADHD attention differences and you may be completely unable to ignore what is going on, which makes unpleasant sensory stimuli even louder and more disruptive [#82].

How to sort out ASD, ADHD or both. The author's suggested strategy: first, fill in questionnaires, for example the RAADS and an adult ADHD scale; second, look at the comparison table below for an overview of the features and difficulties of each condition [#82]. (For the limits of such questionnaires see *What not to expect and what to expect from self-report questionnaires*.)

#### Principles of parent training and classroom behavioural intervention for childhood ADHD
Alina Safi's summary of therapy for children with ADHD [#223]:
1. First line: parent-skills training and behavioural interventions in the classroom form the basis of treatment and are included in all international clinical guidelines as first-line therapy.
2. Positive reinforcement: the key principle is emphasis on positive reinforcement through praise, attention and an organised reward system; this raises the probability that desired behaviour will repeat. Participants also learn to work with *extinction* (угасание) of the child's unwanted behavioural strategies.
3. Clear, consistent rules: developing understandable and consistent rules with realistic expectations, and consistency of demands among all adults in the child's life.
4. Emotion work: validating the child's experiences, solving problems together, and building stress-coping skills. Teaching emotion recognition helps children understand their feelings, and seeing the link between emotions and actions supports more effective behaviour.
5. Training specialists and parents in concrete techniques of managing the child's behaviour and supporting emotional regulation improves executive functions and prevents secondary problems, including (the source says) raising self-esteem and stress resilience. (!) The reason positive reinforcement matters is explained in the same post: children with ADHD get considerably more negative feedback and criticism than neurotypical peers, and, paradoxically, because of a shortage of positive attention during the day, even negative attention can be experienced as preferable. #### HiTOP spectra and the mapping of ADHD and autism onto them
What HiTOP is. HiTOP (Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology) is not an ordinary classification like DSM or ICD. Instead of dividing everything into rigid categories, it views the psyche as a set of continuous traits and symptoms that naturally cluster in different people; disorders are not "present or absent" but patterns and combinations of traits distributed along a continuum [#243].

The main HiTOP spectra (as listed by климочкина катя):
1. Internalizing - anxiety, sadness, guilt, inward-directed experiences.
2. Externalizing - impulsivity, irritability, aggression, risky behaviour.
3. Thought disorder - distortions of perception and reality, delusional ideas.
4. Detachment - low social motivation, emotional coldness, loss of interest.
5. Somatoform - focus on bodily sensations and health anxiety.
6. Neurocognitive - problems with attention, memory, planning and flexibility of thinking.
7. Control vs impulsivity - a continuum between excessive control and rigidity on one side and impulsivity and weak self-regulation on the other.
[#243]

ADHD in HiTOP. ADHD is not treated as a separate "disorder" but as a set of different traits present to different degrees: impulsivity, inattention, hyperactivity, difficulty with self-control. These traits overlap with several spectra [#243]:
1. Externalizing - oppositional or defiant behaviour, aggression, when impulses are expressed outward.
2. Neurocognitive - difficulties with planning, working memory and attention switching, partly overlapping with autistic cognitive features.
3. Internalizing - anxiety, stress and depression arising from chronic failures, being misunderstood or social isolation.
Why it matters: instead of "you have ADHD", ADHD is a combination of traits distributed over different spectra, which helps explain why some people with ADHD are mainly impulsive while others mainly struggle with strong anxiety or emotional difficulties [#243].

Autism in HiTOP. Autism is even harder to fit: its traits stretch over five spectra, "and that is not the limit" ("Спектр на спектре, спектр, спектр!" - spectrum on spectrum, spectrum, spectrum!) [#243]:
1. Detachment - social isolation, low emotional expressiveness, weak social motivation; reflects low social drive specifically, not anxiety.
2. Compulsivity / rigidity - repetitive behaviour, adherence to routine, rigid habits; resembles compulsive traits from the OCD spectrum, but the motivation and causes differ.
3. Neurocognitive difficulties - problems with planning, working memory, cognitive inflexibility; partly overlaps with ADHD.
4. Thought disorder / cognitive-perceptual dysregulation - unusual sensory experiences, specific interests, unusual concentration of attention; resembles perceptual anomalies, but not of a psychotic nature.
5. Internalizing (secondary) - anxiety (especially social), depression, high stress sensitivity; often develop because of social isolation or chronic difficulties.
[#243]

Proposed neurodevelopmental spectrum. Researchers propose adding a neurodevelopmental spectrum to HiTOP to include the shared traits of autism, ADHD, learning disorders and other conditions that begin in childhood. This fits HiTOP's idea of continuous traits and acknowledges that some features can be adaptive (for example, sensory sensitivity or attention to detail) [#243].

#### ADHD vs antisocial personality disorder: shared features, differences, risk and protective factors
The post (first person; the author had recently written a longread on antisocial personality disorder and was asked in Facebook comments whether ADHD and ASPD overlap) summarises what she found, saying it impressed her [#249]:
1. Elevated risk: studies on large samples (several thousand people) indicate that children with ADHD have a higher probability of developing antisocial personality disorder (АРЛ). The primary predictor of ASPD was impulsivity.
2. A doubtful 25% figure: secondary sources (retellings of scientific articles) cite the Journal of Attention Disorders as saying 25% of people with ADHD meet ASPD criteria. The author finds the figure so large that she cannot take it on trust and did not find the confirming article in the journal's database; she mentions it anyway in case readers find it.
3. Sex difference: the combination is more frequent in men than in women.
4. Genetics: the two disorders share genetic factors, but - as usual - nobody knows to what extent or how exactly they act.
5. Environmental risk: inconsistent and chaotic upbringing, traumatic childhood and presence of violence raise the probability of ASPD, as one would expect.
6. Protective factor - sense of coherence: the main protective factor is "чувство когерентности" (sense of coherence): perceiving the world as controllable and predictable, seeing one's life as meaningful and oneself as able to influence reality. People with ADHD who had this showed antisocial traits substantially less often.
7. Confusability and the distinguishing features: ADHD and ASPD can be confused because they share impulsivity, problems with commitments, and conflicts with rules and authorities. Attributing all this to ADHD may be psychologically easier than mentally placing oneself in the same cohort as Patrick Bateman. The differences that help: problems with empathy, weak or absent guilt and remorse, intentionality of problematic actions and the presence of malicious intent belong only to ASPD, not to ADHD.
8. The catch: if you do not know what empathy really is, how can you tell that you lack it?
[#249]

#### What not to expect and what to expect from self-report questionnaires
Purpose: how to read autism-related self-report questionnaires and what not to expect from them (post by климочкина катя) [#288].

What not to expect:
1. *A diagnosis.* Even an excellent scale at best says "к этому стоит приглядеться" (this is worth a closer look).
2. *A universal threshold "the problem starts at this score".* Many scales simply have no official cut-off. For DERS, for example, different researchers pick different thresholds at their own discretion.
3. *Accuracy outside the validated sample.* A recent example: a study of interoception used a new scale of negative appraisal of bodily signals, and its authors themselves write that its psychometrics have not yet been checked in autistic samples.
4. *Robustness to alexithymia.* All self-reports break on this: if a person finds it hard to name their state, the score will be very approximate. In the same interoception study, adjusting for alexithymia alone removed the group difference on two of three dimensions.
5. *An answer to "am I autistic".* Trait severity and having a diagnosis behave differently, because a diagnosis is always more than one pronounced trait.
[#288]

What to expect:
1. *A language*: a questionnaire's items often put into words what you have felt for years without words.
2. *Tracking*: it can be useful for following changes in your state over time.
3. *Something to bring to an appointment*: and there it is more important to name the specific items that resonated than the total number.
The closing line: "А число внизу - просто число." (And the number at the bottom is just a number.) [#288]

#### Preparing for an ADHD assessment when grades were good
From the post on high IQ and ADHD: before going to a specialist it is useful to gather in advance what good school grades hide [#289]:
1. How things look in an unstructured setting.
2. How much time goes into things others do quickly.
3. What happens to tasks without an external deadline.
The phrase "вы же хорошо учились" (but you did well at school) only describes that in a very structured environment with other people's deadlines your abilities coped; it says something about abilities but nothing about the "conductor of this orchestra" [#289].

#### Externalized vs internalized symptoms as the source of diagnostic bias
Purpose: explains why the diagnostic criteria for both ADHD and ASD fit boys better than girls [#53].
1. The criteria were formed from observations of boys. Two reasons are given: first, at the time only boys attended school (so only boys were observed in that setting); second, boys show more externalized symptoms than girls [#53].
2. Externalized symptoms are what can be seen from the outside: hyperactivity, banging one's head against a wall, not answering the doctor's questions. These were noticed and entered the criteria [#53].
3. Internalized symptoms - racing thoughts (скачка мыслей), inner restlessness (внутреннее беспокойство), inability to relax - were "просто не учитывали и не знали о них" (simply not taken into account, nobody knew about them) [#53].
4. Girls have more internalized symptoms, which are both harder to notice and less taken into account during diagnosis - hence underdiagnosis [#53].

#### Consequences of a missed diagnosis in women (5 items)
The article lists the consequences of not having a correct diagnosis [#53]:
1. Sharply lowered self-esteem - even compared with men who have ADHD or ASD [#53].
2. Difficulties with work and relationships [#53].
3. Increased risk of codependent relationships [#53].
4. Risk of comorbid disorders [#53].
5. Burnout and exhaustion from masking [#53].

#### Why female ADHD is missed (six mechanisms)
The article gives these reasons why ADHD in women is less visible or gets attributed to something else [#53]:
1. Gender socialization: more is demanded of women in terms of suppressing everything. The author's illustration: inside you are twitching and "бегаешь кругами по потолку" (running in circles on the ceiling), but out of the habit of suppressing everything and sitting quietly it is almost invisible from outside; or you are angry and boiling inside but express nothing [#53].
2. Subtype: women in general more typically have the inattentive subtype of ADHD rather than the hyperactive one [#53].
3. Comorbid anxiety and depression: women are more prone to them than men, so psychiatrists are more likely to put all of a woman's problems down to anxiety and depression [#53].
4. Perfectionism and comorbid OCD can make diagnosis harder [#53].
5. Fewer DSM symptoms, same distress: women with ADHD overall have fewer symptoms matching DSM criteria, yet experience just as much discomfort [#53].
6. Masking, emotion dysregulation and the cycle: women mask and suppress more, suffer more from emotion dysregulation, and the menstrual cycle also affects how symptoms show [#53].

#### How ADHD may look in women (12 signs)
1. Difficulties with time management - while women's load and demands in this area are often greater than men's [#53].
2. Difficulties organizing oneself and the space around [#53].
3. A frequent feeling of being overwhelmed/low (подавленность), not reaching depression - in the spirit of "я скорее всего что-то упустила" (I've most likely missed something) [#53].
4. A history of anxiety and depression [#53].
5. Difficulties managing money [#53].
6. A lot of sensory overload (more in women than in men) [#53].
7. Somatic complaints - headaches, migraines, stomach pains and nausea - including because of the load of masking [#53].
8. Sleep difficulties [#53].
9. Gender-atypical behaviour: controlling, demanding, irritable [#53].
10. High-risk behaviour: speeding, extreme sports and similar [#53].
11. A tendency toward addictions [#53].
12. Hyperactivity often shows up as self-harm or skin picking, nail biting, lip biting, hair pulling. The article stresses this is done "не с целью наказания, а потому что это только и доступно" (not for the purpose of punishment, but because that is the only thing available) - i.e. it is the only outlet for hyperactivity a woman allows herself [#53].

#### How autism may look in women (10 signs)
1. A tendency to rely on other people who will act and speak for you [#53].
2. Dependence and codependence in relationships - because it is unclear what to do with the outside world at all, and adjusting is easier with someone else's help. Contrast given: where an autistic boy will just give up and not do something, a girl will wriggle out, try through imitation, or ask others to do it for her [#53].
3. High sensory sensitivity [#53].
4. Passionate but restricted interests [#53].
5. Difficulty making and keeping friends [#53].
6. Conversations restricted to a few topics of interest [#53].
7. Choosing behaviour that looks shy, quiet and passive from outside [#53].
8. Problems controlling emotions [#53].
9. High sensitivity to rejection [#53].
10. Anxiety symptoms and co-occurring disorders in general [#53].

The article draws the key conclusion from this list: it shows a very important cause of underdiagnosis, because "тихая, полагающаяся на других и перекрытая эмоциями женщина отлично попадает в ожидания от женщин вообще" (a quiet woman who relies on others and is overwhelmed by emotions fits perfectly into the expectations of women in general) [#53].

### Detailed insights

#### AuDHD: frequency and why the figure is so wide
AuDHD (СДВГ+РАС) is an unofficial term for people with autism and ADHD. Research shows that from 50 to 80% of people have co-existing autism and ADHD (the post's wording). Whether the diagnoses genuinely accompany each other or the similarity of symptoms influences diagnosis is hard to untangle - which, the author says, is why the range is as wide as 50-80% [#82].

#### Why high IQ and good grades do not rule out ADHD
The post "Вы же хорошо учились" (But you did well at school) argues against a popular diagnostic argument - "какой СДВГ, вы школу с медалью закончили" (what ADHD, you finished school with a medal) - and says research disproving it already exists [#289].

- TRAILS (Netherlands): researchers looked at 2221 children aged 10-12 and compared what parents and teachers said about their attention. The disagreement was strongest in children with high intelligence, and only in one direction: parents reported problems noticeably more often than teachers [#289].
- The researchers' explanation (the "greenhouse" logic): there is more than one possible explanation, but the researchers' logic is that a lesson lasts forty minutes, the task is formulated, the deadline is today, an adult is next to you, consequences are immediate and you can always quickly generate some answer. These are "тепличные условия для исполнительных функций" (greenhouse conditions for executive functions), and a smart child gets by on ability even when they have nothing to organise themselves with. Things break later - at university, where no one checks whether you made it to class, and at work, where you also have to set the task for yourself [#289].
- Yale study: 157 adults with ADHD and IQ of 120 and above were assessed with eight normed executive-function measures. 73% had significant impairments on at least five of the eight indicators, and on all eight the frequency of impairment was higher than in the general population. The authors explained they took up the work because very capable adults came to them who had been told for years by teachers and clinicians that high intelligence excludes ADHD [#289].
- The orchestra metaphor (from the Yale authors): imagine a symphony orchestra of wonderful, talented musicians with no conductor. The instruments are fine, but music still does not come out - intelligence is the musicians, executive function is the conductor [#289].
- Massachusetts General Hospital study (validity check): diagnostic validity at high intelligence was checked by comparing equally intelligent people with and without the diagnosis: 64 adults with IQ 120+ and ADHD vs 53 people with the same intelligence without the diagnosis. The ADHD group had lower quality of life, worse functioning at work and in the family, more functional difficulties and more often depression, OCD and anxiety disorders - exactly the same picture as ADHD at average intelligence [#289].
- Population-level nuance (TRAILS): across the population, higher intelligence is associated with fewer attention problems; the post describes this as a linear relationship with a high correlation ("Это линейная связь линейная и высокий уровень корелляции между параметрами" [sic]). The same authors write that attention problems predict functional difficulties across the whole range of intelligence, including the top [#289].

#### Childhood ADHD: presentation, sex differences and comorbidities
Facts from Alina Safi's post [#223]:
- ADHD is often diagnosed in preschool age, but not always. Marked hyperactivity usually draws others' attention, but if it does not cause significant difficulties, inattention symptoms can go unnoticed until the load increases [#223].
- Because hyperactivity is more pronounced in boys, diagnosing ADHD in girls is often harder [#223].
- ADHD in children often co-occurs with anxiety and with various learning disorders: dyslexia (difficulties with reading), dysgraphia (difficulties with writing), dyscalculia (impairment of mathematical abilities) and dyspraxia (difficulties with motor planning and coordination) [#223].
- Neuropsychological exercises can benefit children with ADHD, but they are not the gold standard of therapy; scientific evidence on the effectiveness of such "neurodevelopmental" methods remains contradictory [#223].

#### The individual questionnaires: what each is and how strong its evidence is
From климочкина катя's review of the four tools most often encountered around autism [#288]:
1. MQ, Monotropism Questionnaire: 47 items devised by a group of autistic adults from their own experience, tested on 1110 participants. Its obvious strength is that it was built from inside the community. But its validation still sits as a preprint; the author found no record of publication in a peer-reviewed journal - even though the questionnaire has already spread across social media [#288].
2. ABM, Autistic Burnout Measure: 27 items; validation published in the journal *Autism* "this year" (the post is from 2026). Internal consistency of the items is very high and the scale distinguishes well the people who are in burnout. A curious detail: after a year the correlation with one's own past result is moderate, which makes sense because burnout can pass and return [#288].
3. CAT-Q, masking questionnaire: 25 items gathered from autistic adults' accounts of exactly how they mask. Tested on 354 autistic and 478 neurotypical people; works the same across genders and for people with and without a diagnosis. The author has her own criticisms of it, promised for a later post on masking - (!) INCOMPLETE: the criticisms are not given here [#288].
4. NIAS, ARFID screening: a short nine-item questionnaire on food selectivity, reduced appetite and fear of food. In 2025 researchers checked that in autistic adults the scale is structured the same way as in everyone else - so comparisons are valid and elevated scores in autistic people mean the same as in neurotypical people [#288].
Also mentioned: RAADS and an adult ADHD scale as first-step questionnaires for sorting out ASD vs ADHD vs both [#82], and DERS as an example of a scale with no agreed cut-off [#288].

#### Studies on gender bias in diagnosis
- ADHD vignette study: the same case descriptions of children with ADHD were read to school teachers, sometimes with girls' names and sometimes with boys' names. Where the names were boys', teachers said they wanted to refer the child to a psychologist; the "girls'" cases were ignored. (!) INCOMPLETE: the study authors and year are not given [#53].
- Autism twin study (Francesca Happe and colleagues): researchers observed 15,000 twins; with the same problems, boys were diagnosed with ASD much more often than girls [#53].
- The author calls these "классные исследования недодиагностики девочек" (great studies of the underdiagnosis of girls) from recent decades [#53].

#### How autistic girls' interests differ
Girls usually show less stereotyped behaviour and less specific, less unusual ("вычурные", *quirky/elaborate*) interests than boys. They may be interested in the same Barbies and ponies as neurotypical girls, but the immersion is more intense and specialized. Example: an autistic girl may be into the same teen TV series as a non-autistic girl, but she is more likely to know everything about the characters while knowing nothing about the plot - and to show little interest in talking about anything beyond it. The lesson: the topic of an interest looks typical; the depth and narrowness are what differ [#53].

#### What women get diagnosed with instead of autism
- ADHD instead of ASD: when ADHD is present, diagnosis tends to favour ADHD, because it is easier to observe [#53].
- OCD or an eating disorder: the chances of getting one of these diagnoses are high. Autistic people are selective in food and follow strict rules about it; from that a clinician may infer restrictive behaviour and diagnose, for example, anorexia. The article adds that anorexia is in fact sometimes comorbid with autism [#53].
- For ADHD, the corresponding misattribution is to anxiety and depression (see *Why female ADHD is missed*) [#53].

#### Masking: adjusting is not adapting
Masking is characteristic of women with both ASD and ADHD. Women adjust fairly well to high demands, but "подстраиваться - не значит адаптироваться" (adjusting does not mean adapting): adaptation is precisely what does not happen. Example: a woman forced to take part in small talk at work does not start hating it less; she sees that others approve of participating and suppresses her natural urge to leave immediately. She may imitate interest in colleagues' holidays so well that nobody suspects anything [#53].

Mechanism of harm: constantly controlling oneself and worrying about the outcome ("удалось угадать или нет?", *did I guess right or not?*) costs a great deal of energy. This produces burnout, fatigue, depression and exacerbation of everything that can be exacerbated [#53].

Practical conclusion of the article: work with masking is no less important a therapeutic target than, for example, work with sensory sensitivity [#53].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- (!) UNCLEAR: the AuDHD post says "от 50 до 80% людей имеют сосуществующие РАС и СДВГ" (from 50 to 80% of people have co-existing ASD and ADHD) without saying of which population (autistic people, people with ADHD); the author herself says the range reflects difficulty separating true co-occurrence from symptom overlap [#82].
- The 25% ADHD-meets-ASPD-criteria figure is flagged by its own author as unverified: she found it only in secondary sources and could not locate the original article [#249].
- Genetic overlap between ADHD and ASPD and between autism and bipolar disorder is stated, but for ADHD/ASPD the author stresses that nobody knows to what extent or how it operates [#57, #249].
- The distinguishing features of ASPD (empathy problems, lack of remorse) are hard to self-assess - the author's own caveat about knowing what empathy is [#249].
- Neurobiological comparison of autism and bipolar disorder is left as an open question to readers [#57].
- In the emotion-regulation longread the author herself disputes the research claim that impaired recognition of others' emotions is the specific feature of dysregulation in bipolar disorder, calling it poorly argued [#57].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the longread says people with bipolar disorder more often use maladaptive strategies, yet also that they use more adaptive and fewer negative maladaptive strategies than people with BPD; the comparison groups differ (general vs BPD), so this is not a strict contradiction, but the source does not reconcile the two statements explicitly [#57].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the two research articles the longread is based on are not named in the saved text [#57].
- The paradox of overlapping criteria (ADHD criteria not about communication, autism criteria not about attention, yet both groups have both difficulties) is posed without a resolution [#82].
- (!) UNCLEAR: in the childhood-ADHD post, raising self-esteem and stress resilience is grammatically listed among "secondary problems" prevented; likely meant as benefits [#223].
- Neuropsychological "neurodevelopmental" exercises for children with ADHD: possibly beneficial, but evidence is contradictory and they are not the gold standard [#223].
- TRAILS: the researchers' greenhouse explanation is one of more than one possible explanation of the parent/teacher discrepancy [#289].
- MQ validation is still a preprint; CAT-Q has criticisms that the author defers to a later post (!) INCOMPLETE; self-report scores are unreliable with alexithymia and outside validated samples [#288].
- Questionnaire and table use for distinguishing ASD/ADHD [#82] is qualified by the later post saying questionnaires give no diagnosis and do not answer "am I autistic" [#288].

- The content is a secondhand retelling of a conference talk; the author does not separate which points are Afanasyeva's and which are her own [#53].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the teacher-vignette ADHD study is described without authors, sample or year; the twin study gives only the lead researcher and the sample size of 15,000 [#53].
- The sign lists are presented as "how it may look" (как может выглядеть), not as diagnostic criteria; no thresholds or numbers of required signs are given [#53].
