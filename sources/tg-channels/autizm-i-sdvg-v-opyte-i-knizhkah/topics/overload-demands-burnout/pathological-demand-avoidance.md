# Pathological demand avoidance (PDA) and the PANDA approach

## What this covers

A short series by the channel author, Katya Klimochkina, on pathological demand avoidance (ПИТ, "патологическое избегание требований", PDA): how it differs from ordinary reluctance and from autistic demand resistance in general, how it presents (active vs passive), how it unfolds as a staged process from quiet coping to panic and meltdown, why the main diagnostic manuals do not recognise it as a separate disorder, and a practical acronym-based skill, PANDA, for living with one's own demand avoidance or supporting someone who has it. The author frames this as relevant because PDA-level avoidance "actively affects quality of life" and its visible outbursts are, for the person, comparable to panic attacks rather than wilful choices [msg #118, #119].

## Core ideas

- **Not wanting to do things is universal; PDA is the pathological extreme.** Everyone avoids things to varying degrees; PDA is avoidance so extensive that it disrupts ordinary life, is not a choice, and persists across the lifespan [msg #118].
- **The driver is overwhelming anxiety and a need for control**, not routine preference or social anxiety as in autistic demand resistance more broadly [msg #118].
- **PDA is a profile on a spectrum** — what is avoided and how differs between people and situations, ranging from active/externalised to passive/internalised presentations [msg #118].
- **PDA is a process with stages.** Observers see only the tip (aggression, self-harm, flight, freezing), but many quieter, often hidden coping attempts precede it; when they fail, rapid escalation into panic follows [msg #119, img #119].
- **Escalation is a reflex, not a decision** — the fight-flight-freeze response to threat — so PDA meltdowns are comparable in experience to panic attacks [msg #119].
- **Diagnostic status is contested.** PDA is not a separate diagnosis in DSM-5 or ICD-11, mainly because its behaviour can be explained within existing conditions such as autism and anxiety; still, the author holds it has its own characteristic patterns [msg #118, #130].
- **The case against a separate PDA category** rests on six alternative explanations (anxiety + autism, autism, anxiety, many conditions, ODD, trauma) and six arguments: a separate label can steer people away from proven anxiety treatment such as exposure therapy, the evidence is mostly clinical observation and parent reports, criteria overlap, PDA behaviour appears outside autism, and labels risk misdiagnosis [article #130].
- **Living with PDA starts with lowering anxiety and preserving a sense of control**, via a partnership built on trust, flexibility, cooperation and carefully balanced demands and language — summarised in the PANDA skill [msg #142, #143].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### PDA escalation pyramid ("быстрая эскалация под давлением" — rapid escalation under pressure)
Purpose: to show that PDA is a **process** of avoidance with stages, so that the visible outburst is understood as the end point of many earlier attempts to cope. The diagram is a pyramid; the base and middle (green) are quieter coping, the tip (pink) is panic. An arrow rises along the side, labelled with the drivers of escalation: "увеличение требований, возрастание тревоги, потеря контроля, снижение толерантности к неопределенности" (increasing demands, rising anxiety, loss of control, decreasing tolerance of uncertainty). The right side is labelled "БЫСТРАЯ ЭСКАЛАЦИЯ ПОД ДАВЛЕНИЕМ" (rapid escalation under pressure) [img #119].

Levels from bottom to top:
1. **"спокойный, способный" (calm, capable)** — the baseline state, in which the person functions [img #119].
2. **"отвлечение, оправдания, прокрастинация" (distraction, excuses, procrastination)** — the first, subtle avoidance strategies. The post adds that people with PDA may appear sociable and charming, may be crafty or use distraction techniques to avoid doing what they are asked; escapism and distraction count here too ("Эскапизму или отвлечению тоже ДА!" — "Escapism or distraction — also YES!") [img #119, msg #119].
3. **"физическая нетрудоспособность, уход в фантазии, сокращение содержательных бесед" (physical incapacity, withdrawal into fantasy, reduction of meaningful conversation)** — deeper withdrawal while still not overtly refusing [img #119].
4. **control, full compliance with demands (followed by a breakdown)** (контроль, полное соблюдение требований с последующим срывом) — the last "green" level: the person either seizes control or complies completely, but compliance is paid for later by a breakdown [img #119].
5. **Tip — "ПАНИКА" (panic), fight / flight / freeze ("БЕЙ БЕГИ ЗАМРИ")**: distress (расстройство), agitation (ажитация), self-harm (селфхарм), shutdown (шатдаун), aggression (агрессия), leaving the situation (уход из ситуации). The post lists the visible tip behaviours as aggression, self-harm, fights and biting, or freezing and physically running away [img #119, msg #119].

How the transition happens: if all the "беспалевные" (inconspicuous, "under the radar") strategies to escape the demand have failed — or others have noticed "что-то не то" (something is off) — rapid escalation can occur and panic begins. The earlier attempts are "попыток справиться экологичней" (attempts to cope in a gentler, more sustainable way) that are sometimes hidden from the observer and sometimes from the person themselves [msg #119].

Why it matters: the peak is "не осознанный выбор, а рефлекс" (not a conscious choice but a reflex) of fight-flight-freeze as in a threat situation; therefore vivid PDA manifestations, meltdowns, are comparable in experience to panic attacks [msg #119].

### Active vs passive PDA presentation
The author argues PDA is more correctly viewed as a **profile**, using the idea of a spectrum in which "what is avoided and how" varies between people [msg #118]:
1. **Externalised / active presentation** — avoidance is overt, physical, aggressive or controlling. "Вы узнаете, если человек чего-то не хочет. Вам сообщат, возможно громко и утвердительно." (You will know if the person doesn't want something. You will be told, possibly loudly and assertively.) [msg #118]
2. **Internalised / passive presentation** — demands are rejected more quietly: the person simply "не пришел" (didn't show up) or "залипал в телефон" (got stuck in their phone), while the anxiety stays inside and the difficulties are masked [msg #118].
3. **Situational variability** — the way refusal shows can change with the situation and many factors: where I am, who I am with, what needs to be done, how well I am coping, age — the author ends the list with "и так далее" (and so forth), so it is open-ended [msg #118].

### PANDA skill (ПАНДА)
Purpose: a "прикольный акроним" (fun acronym) for learning to live with one's own avoidance, and for supporting another person with it; presented as a skill that can help overcome the anxiety arising in response to "demands" of the environment and of one's own body. The author's starting point: organising life so as to minimise anxiety and provide a sense of control is a good basis for "уживания" (getting along with) attempts to avoid demands; a partnership based on trust, flexibility, cooperation, and balance in demands and language (choice of words, intonation, facial expression) works best for gradually facing demands and functioning productively. The panda image: big, needing attention and care, waiting for smiles and bamboo, sometimes cheeky and funny, with its own quirks, but great [msg #142, #143]. The Russian letters are П-А-Н-Д-А:

**П — Подбирайте сражения с умом (Pick your battles wisely)**
- Focus on what really matters and is necessary; avoid overloading the person (or yourself) with demands that can provoke resistance [msg #142].
- Choose what to insist on and what can and should be left unattended, based on the person's current capacity and stress level. Example: the dishes can be washed right away or a couple of hours later — not worth a fight [msg #142].
- Prioritise tasks and demands to reduce overload and keep a sense of control; insist on a task only when strictly necessary [msg #142].
- The cue card adds: fewer rules; give the option of choice; prioritise tasks; explain the reasons; accept that not everything will get done [img #143].

**А — Адаптируйте стиль общения (Adapt your communication style)**
- Use indirect wording to reduce perceived demands: hints or suggestions instead of direct instructions. Example: instead of "пропылесось быстро" (vacuum, quickly), hint: "дома как-то пыльновато, не помнишь, мы что-то рассыпали что ли? или это тополь цветет? как-то не прикольно" (it's kind of dusty at home, do you remember, did we spill something? or is it the poplar fluff? not cool) [msg #142].
- Avoid a strict, commanding or authoritative tone; use a calm, neutral tone so as not to provoke the person's withdrawal. "Скажем хиханькам - да хахонькам ДА!" (Let's say YES to giggles and jokes!) [msg #142].
- Frame tasks positively and as part of a joint activity, which can be more appealing. Example: "давайте на спор, сможем генеральную уборку вместе навести?" (let's bet — can we do a big clean-up together?) rather than "нужно навести порядок немедленно" (we need to tidy up immediately) [msg #142].
- The cue card adds: use hints; a soft, friendly tone; doing things together is easier; use roleplay and learn a playful approach for motivation [img #143].

**Н — Не конфронтируйте, договаривайтесь и сотрудничайте (Don't confront; negotiate and cooperate)**
- Compromise: be ready to negotiate and find a middle ground — for example by breaking tasks into smaller, doable stages or agreeing on a sequence that suits each particular person [msg #142].
- Encourage participation in decision-making: joint agreements can give a sense of ownership and help the person manage to do the task [msg #142].
- The cue card adds: look for compromises; cooperate proactively and negotiate to solve problems; joint agreements help solve tasks; balance "брать и давать" (give and take) [img #143].

**Д — Доверие и взаимоотношения (Trust and relationship)**
- Build trust through consistency, reliability and understanding; trust can reduce anxiety and resistance [msg #142].
- Show empathy for the difficulties and confirm that this is all "РЕАЛЬНО ТРУДНО" (REALLY HARD). Understanding the perspective in which the world is one big "ДОЛЖЕН" (MUST) helps to understand and support better [msg #142].
- Rushing or pressure can increase resistance, whereas patience gradually reduces the tendency to avoid. "Улыбчивые черепахи и улыбки в помощь!" (Smiling turtles and smiles to the rescue!) [msg #142].
- The cue card adds: trust and fairness matter; show empathy and support; be patient, give time, be kind; use humour, distraction and roleplay [img #143].

**А — Адаптируйтесь к окружающей среде (Adapt to the environment)**
- Reduce vulnerability factors: change the environment to minimise stress and anxiety — create a calm, predictable setting and prevent sensory overload [msg #142].
- Keep a flexible daily routine that allows adjustments depending on mood and capacity on any given day and moment [msg #142].
- The cue card adds: reduce vulnerability factors; be flexible; have a plan B; give yourself more time [img #143].

## Detailed insights

### Ordinary avoidance vs PDA
Not wanting to do something — getting up in the morning, going to work, cooking lunch, listening to a friend's whining, smiling "because that's what's expected", or your own variant — is a natural human trait that everyone shows to varying degrees and for various reasons: we don't want something and try every way not to do it [msg #118].

Autistic people may avoid demands or situations that cause anxiety or sensory overload, disrupt their daily routine, require switching activity, or tasks in which they see no point or which are simply "не интересны" (not interesting). In such a moment of avoidance the person refuses to participate, withdraws, "отключается" (switches off) or runs away. Illustration from the author: in one episode of The Big Bang Theory, Sheldon joined his friends much later and, when asked why he was late, simply said "я не хотел приходить" (I didn't want to come) — "это оно" (that's it) [msg #118].

PDA proper is defined as evading, running from and dodging everyday demands and expectations to the extent that it interferes with ordinary life: the person avoids so much that it actively affects quality of life. Per the author, such avoidance is not a choice and persists throughout life — seen in children and remaining into old age [msg #118].

### Characteristic features of PDA
- People with PDA go to any lengths to avoid demands placed on them, even if the demands are trivial or self-imposed — including demands of their own body and its needs, such as wanting to drink or eat. Demands can be direct instructions, routine tasks or any perceived expectations [msg #118].
- Resistance can be obsessive in character, with avoidance becoming the central element of behaviour — visible as fixation on particular ways of dodging tasks or repetitive avoidance-related behaviour [msg #118].
- Key distinction: autistic people may resist demands because of routine preferences or social anxiety, whereas people with PDA avoid demands primarily because they feel overwhelming anxiety and a need to control the situation. This control may show as imposing conditions or avoiding any situation in which, in their view, they could lose the freedom of their own decisions [msg #118].

### Diagnostic status ("ПИТ Шредингера")
PDA is not officially recognised as a separate diagnosis in the diagnostic manuals DSM-5 or ICD-11; there is no generally accepted set of diagnostic criteria, and there is debate over whether it should be a separate diagnosis or simply part of the autism spectrum. Nevertheless, the author holds that PDA has its own "особенные" (distinctive) behaviour patterns [msg #118].

The follow-up post and its longread, titled "ПИТ Шредингера (он как бы есть, но его как бы нет)" (Schrödinger's PDA — it sort of exists, but sort of doesn't), written by Katya Klimochkina together with @autismis, state the main reason PDA is not treated as a separate disorder in the world's main diagnostic manuals, DSM-5 and ICD-11: the behaviour observed in PDA "может быть объяснено в рамках других уже существующих расстройств" (can be explained within other already existing disorders), for example autism and anxiety ("но не только" — but not only). The longread then examines this viewpoint in two parts: what PDA is linked to (six alternative explanations) and the arguments against treating PDA as a separate category (six arguments) [msg #130, article #130].

### What PDA is linked to: six alternative explanations (article)
The longread presents six "equations", each an alternative account of the behaviour labelled PDA. They are offered side by side as explanations discussed in the field, not as a single conclusion [article #130].

1. **ПИТ = тревожность + аутизм (PDA = anxiety + autism).** PDA is usually understood as excessive avoidance of everyday duties, controlling behaviour and intense emotional reactions. All of these are often seen in autistic people who also experience high anxiety. Anxiety can show up as avoidance and controlling behaviour, especially in conditions perceived as unpredictable or overwhelming. Lesson: the PDA picture may simply be what autism looks like when combined with high anxiety [article #130].
2. **ПИТ = аутизм (PDA = autism).** Some researchers argue that PDA should be considered part of the general autism phenotype. Autistic people can show a wide range of behaviour, including demand avoidance, influenced by their sensory sensitivity, communication difficulties and anxiety level. Reasoning: autism is treated as a spectrum because it includes a broad set of characteristics that differ greatly between people, appearing in countless combinations and intensities; the autism spectrum disorders were merged precisely because of overlapping, similar symptoms and unreliable diagnostic distinctions. By the same logic PDA may fit a broader diagnostic approach, because its boundaries are not clearly separated from other conditions [article #130].
3. **ПИТ = тревожность (PDA = anxiety).** Many PDA behaviours can be caused by anxiety. For example, autistic children and adults who avoid demands may do so to cope with their anxiety, to escape it: when demands are made, a feeling of being overwhelmed (подавленности) appears, and avoidance works well to reduce the discomfort. Supporting point: PDA symptoms can be treated with medication — drugs that help suppress anxiety [article #130].
4. **ПИТ = много чего (PDA = many things).** Besides anxiety and autism, PDA is often linked with ADHD, emotional instability, antisocial personality disorder, conduct disorders and epilepsy. There is a hypothesis that ADHD and emotional instability describe the behaviour attributed to PDA even better than autism does [article #130].
5. **ПИТ = оппозиционно-вызывающее расстройство (PDA = oppositional defiant disorder, ODD / ОВР).** PDA and ODD overlap considerably in symptoms and share features: emotional reactivity and avoidance of complying with demands. Hence the logical suggestion that PDA and ODD may be connected by similar behaviour, while their causes may differ [article #130].
6. **ПИТ = реакция на травму (PDA = a trauma response).** PDA behaviour may be a response to the need to control an unpredictable environment. A sense of threat is characteristic of people who have lived through traumatic experience, so some researchers suggest the behaviour observed in PDA may be a reaction to traumatic events or adverse childhood experiences. Trauma can lead to heightened anxiety and avoidance, which can be interpreted as PDA [article #130].

### Arguments against PDA as a separate category (article)
The longread lists six arguments ("Аргументы против ПИТ как отдельной категории") [article #130]:

1. **A separate diagnosis can block access to effective treatment.** If anxiety is seen as part of an anxiety disorder, there is a clear set of strategies for getting rid of it — for example, in CBT (КБТ) exposure therapy works very well for anxiety: it lowers distress by facing what causes it slowly and carefully. If the same behaviour is seen as part of PDA, the strategies are different — indeed opposite to those that work for anxiety. A frequent PDA recommendation is to create a "low maintenance" environment with minimal demands. The article's point: this gives relief in the moment, but also long-term confirmation of the belief "я не вывожу тревогу, она мне не подконтрольна, я не могу ее ослабить своими силами" (I can't handle the anxiety, it is not under my control, I can't weaken it by my own efforts) [article #130].
2. **Weak evidence base.** Evidence that PDA is a unique disorder rests mainly on clinical observations rather than empirically meaningful studies (RCTs, РКИ). Most PDA studies rely on parents' reports (complaints), without taking into account the views of people with established PDA. In addition, the subjective nature of some criteria — especially those that presuppose knowing someone's intentions, all the more so a child's — casts doubt on the validity and reliability of PDA as a separate category [article #130].
3. **Overlapping criteria.** PDA diagnostic criteria overlap substantially with other disorders; so much in common is confusing and leads to potential overdiagnosis or a high probability of misdiagnosis [article #130].
4. **PDA behaviour occurs outside autism.** Numerous studies and clinical reports identify PDA-related behaviour in people who do not meet autism criteria. This suggests PDA is not unique to autistic people and can arise in very different contexts — pointing to some other disorder that was not recognised at diagnosis rather than something "новое" (new) [article #130].
5. **Labelling risks wrong interventions.** Labelling people with PDA without carefully considering other possible explanations of their problems can lead to misdiagnosis and inappropriate interventions, and such misreading can lead to ignoring better-known, more effective treatments [article #130].
6. **Framing PDA as new narrows treatment options.** Focusing on PDA as a new and unique phenomenon/syndrome may limit access to effective treatments for anxiety and autism; understanding that PDA is caused by anxiety "может открыть более эффективные и менее стигматизирующие возможности лечения" (may open more effective and less stigmatising treatment options) [article #130].

The longread ends by pointing to "хорошее ревью и еще одно" (a good review and another one) as further sources; their titles and content are not given in the saved text [article #130].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- PDA lacks official diagnostic status and agreed criteria; whether it is a separate entity or part of the autism spectrum (or explainable by autism, anxiety and other conditions) is openly debated. The author nonetheless describes it as having distinctive patterns [msg #118, #130].
- (!) CONTRADICTION (tension within the channel): the longread criticises the frequent PDA recommendation of a "low maintenance" environment with minimal demands, arguing it brings momentary relief but long-term confirmation that anxiety cannot be controlled, and favours anxiety approaches such as exposure; the later PANDA post recommends minimising anxiety, reducing demands, choosing battles and insisting only when strictly necessary — though it also frames this as a basis for "постепенно сталкиваться с «требованиями»" (gradually facing "demands"). The channel does not reconcile the two explicitly [article #130, msg #142].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the longread's opening image contains only the word "no" in the saved transcription, and the two closing sources ("хорошее ревью и еще одно") are not identified in the saved text [article #130].
- The longread's alternative explanations are presented as positions held by "some researchers" or hypotheses (e.g. that ADHD and emotional instability describe PDA behaviour better than autism); no specific studies, authors or numbers are given [article #130].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the post says escalation can begin either when inconspicuous strategies fail or when others notice "something is off"; the mechanism of the second trigger is not explained [msg #119].
- The author ends the first post by inviting readers to share whether they have observed similar patterns in themselves or others — the description is offered as a profile to recognise, not a diagnostic tool [msg #118].
