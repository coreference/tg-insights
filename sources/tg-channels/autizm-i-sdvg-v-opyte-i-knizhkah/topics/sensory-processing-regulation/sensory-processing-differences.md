# Sensory processing differences in autism: senses, profiles and theories

## What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's explanations of how sensory processing works differently in autism: which senses exist (eight, not five), where in the processing chain autistic people differ, the two directions of difference (hyper- and hyposensitivity), how to map one's own pattern (a sensory profile, Dunn's four-profile model), two specific conditions discussed alongside autism (Irlen syndrome and gravitational insecurity), the brain-level theories the authors explored (predictive coding, habituation, disrupted connectivity), and a step-by-step method for overcoming discomfort that takes sensory differences into account. The channel's author argues this matters because we develop from birth through sensory processing and learning, so how the nervous system functions shapes who we become to a huge degree; that is why autism, like other neurodevelopmental conditions, "охватывает все сферы жизни человека" (*covers all spheres of a person's life*) and differs from other psychiatric diagnoses [msg #3]. Knowing your own sensory triggers lets you plan strategies that soften sensations and build a friendlier environment — "легче жить эту жизнь" (*easier to live this life*) [msg #22].

## Core ideas

- **Humans have eight senses**, not five: hearing, vision, taste, smell, touch, plus proprioception, interoception and the vestibular sense (see *The eight senses*) [msg #3, #22].
- **Autistic people register signals like everyone else; the difference is in processing.** Autism gives no super-vision or super-hearing; what differs is how the brain prioritises incoming information, so everything can seem equally important and overload follows (see *Four stages*) [msg #3].
- **Sensitivity goes both ways.** Hyper- and hyposensitivity are equally typical, can coexist in one person for different senses, and can shift randomly across life [msg #3, #22].
- **Many "random-looking" autistic traits are sensory.** Avoiding eye contact (a strong stimulus), rocking or spinning (vestibular stimulation), and sitting in twisted postures (proprioceptive input) have sensory explanations [msg #3, #56].
- **These differences are not specific to autism.** They also occur in ADHD and several other conditions; having them does not mean you have ASD [msg #3].
- **A sensory profile maps your pattern** and reveals cause-and-effect links to meltdowns and shutdowns; Dunn's model sorts people into four profiles by threshold and response style [msg #22, #225].
- **Brain-level explanations are theories, not settled facts.** Predictive coding, impaired habituation and disrupted connectivity are offered as hypotheses; the authors stress the research is mixed or unreplicated [msg #65, #180, #195].
- **Overcoming discomfort is possible but harder in autism** because reduced habituation and sensory sensitivity add to fear; it requires more gradualness, preparation, sensory and emotional regulation, breaks and self-compassion [article #180].
- **Gravitational insecurity is a neurological feature, not whims or an anxiety disorder**; it is much more common in autism and must never be treated by forcing movement [article #253].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### The eight senses
The channel's author's starting point: a person does not have five senses but eight [msg #3]. Катя Климочкина lists the same eight as the dimensions along which a sensory profile is drawn up [msg #22].
1. **Hearing (слух)** [msg #3, #22].
2. **Vision (зрение)** [msg #3, #22].
3. **Taste (вкус)** [msg #3, #22].
4. **Smell (обоняние)** [msg #3, #22].
5. **Touch (осязание)** [msg #3, #22].
6. **Proprioception (проприоцепция)** — "ощущение тела в пространстве" (*the sense of the body in space*); Климочкина glosses it as "внешняя телесная осознанность" (*external bodily awareness*) [msg #3, #22]. Lena Foer: it tells us about the position of the body in space [msg #56].
7. **Interoception (интероцепция)** — "ощущение происходящего в организме, типа усталости, боли и голода" (*sensing what is happening in the organism, like fatigue, pain and hunger*); Климочкина: "что происходит внутри тела" (*what is happening inside the body*) [msg #3, #22].
8. **Vestibular sense (вестибулярное чувство / вестибулярный аппарат)** — "ощущение движения в пространстве" (*the sense of movement in space*); Климочкина: the sense of balance and movement [msg #3, #22].

The author's personal illustration of interoception (kept because it carries the lesson): she wrote the post after running into her flat on the verge of a crisis, frantically heating lunch, "потому что забыла поесть раньше, не чувствовала голода, а потом оно как навалилось" (*because I forgot to eat earlier, didn't feel hunger, and then it came crashing down all at once*) — an example of weakly sensed interoceptive signals [msg #3].

**Proprioception, vestibular sense and interoception as a frame for identity.** While writing an online course on sensory sensitivity, the author quoted a book chapter on proprioception: "вместе с вестибулярной системой и интероцепцией (то есть ощущением, что происходит в теле), проприоцепция создает фреймворк для нашей физической идентичности" (*together with the vestibular system and interoception (that is, the sense of what is happening in the body), proprioception creates the framework for our physical identity*). Her reasoning: how we perceive ourselves through the body depends on how we sense the body and its movement in space; so a different perception of oneself in the body, and of the body in the environment and in motion, plausibly forms a particular experience of identity. She frames this as a note on the inseparability of autism as a neurotype from a person's identity, and says explicitly that she has "only thoughts" on what that experience is [msg #173].

### Four stages of turning sensory signals into knowledge
The author's model of how sense-organ information becomes knowledge of the world, in several stages [msg #3]:
1. **The signal reaches us** (сигнал доходит до нас).
2. **We register it** (регистрируем).
3. **We process it** (обрабатываем).
4. **We decide what to do with it** — sometimes consciously, sometimes not.

Where autism differs: problems perceiving sensory information are, in the author's words, one of the two core symptoms of ASD (the other concerns communication). **Registration** works the same in autistic people as in everyone else — "нет, аутизм не дает людям суперзрение или суперслух" (*no, autism doesn't give people super-vision or super-hearing*). What works differently is the **processing system** [msg #3].

Mechanism, "explained on the fingers": a neurotypical brain processing sensory information **sets priorities**. The partner saying he wants to break up — important. A crow cawing outside — unimportant. The face of the person opposite — important. A flickering bulb — unimportant. In autistic people this does not work that way: the crows, the flickering lamp, the voice of the partner who wants to break up and whatever he is doing with his face can all appear **equally important**, creating sensory overload "потому что очень много всего и нет способа сконцентрироваться только на одном, а остальное отсеить в белый шум" (*because there is too much of everything and no way to concentrate on just one thing and sift the rest into white noise*). "Мир просто обрушивается на вас весь сразу" (*The world simply collapses onto you all at once*). The author recommends a YouTube video that shows vividly how this feels, with the warning that it is itself overloading — watch with care [msg #3].

### Hyper- vs hyposensitivity (sensory issues)
**Terminology.** The author calls "сенсорная чувствительность" (*sensory sensitivity*) a deceptive phrase; the English "sensory issues", abstract as it is, is more accurate, because in autism stimuli can sometimes be felt **more strongly** and in a number of cases **more weakly**. Hyper- and hyposensitivity are equally typical [msg #3].

**Hypersensitivity (гиперчувствительность, повышенная чувствительность)** — definition by Климочкина: an excessive reaction to sensory information that may lead to meltdown, overload, distress, discomfort and even pain [msg #22].
- Coping direction: people try to **reduce** exposure — wear dark glasses, earplugs, eat blander food, wear looser clothing [msg #3]; a hypersensitive person may try to reduce stimuli and need a "сенсорной диете" (*sensory diet*) [msg #22]. (!) INCOMPLETE: the source does not explain what a sensory diet consists of.
- Examples (Климочкина): many loud sounds drive you mad; light seems too bright; smells are "ваш враг" (*your enemy*), especially sharp ones — not only perfume but food smells, washing powder and anything else; you prefer familiar foods and textures; it can be hard to keep balance, unpleasant to swing on swings or walk on stairs, and a car will certainly make you motion-sick [msg #22].

**Hyposensitivity (гипочувствительность, пониженная чувствительность)** — weak perception of sensory information, leading to a need to seek additional stimulation [msg #22].
- Coping direction: people want to **amplify** stimuli — listen to industrial music in a noisy club, dance under light shows, eat spicy and strong-smelling food, wear a corset [msg #3].
- Examples (Климочкина): you cannot stand complete silence and put on music or a podcast in the background; your little toe is tortured on every furniture corner at home because you keep bumping into things; it is hard to feel hunger, thirst, or the need to go to the toilet; you have a high pain threshold [msg #22].

**Mixed and shifting patterns.** One person can be hypersensitive to some sensations and hyposensitive to others. On top of that, a person's sensitivity can "выкидывать рандомные штуки" (*pull random stunts*) at different moments of life, "и это вообще нормально" (*and that is totally normal*) [msg #3]. The overcoming-discomfort longread shows both directions inside one situation: on the way to a job interview the noisy metro and the bright office light hit hypersensitivity, while an interviewer who speaks too slowly is labelled hyposensitivity [article #180].

**Sensory explanations of "random" ASD traits** (author): many ASD symptoms that look random are tied to sensory sensitivity. Looking into someone's eyes is a strong stimulus not everyone likes. Rocking or spinning is stimulation of the vestibular sense [msg #3].

**Not autism-specific.** All the described issues also occur in ADHD and several other disorders; having them does not by itself mean you have ASD [msg #3].

### Sensory profile across the eight senses
Presented by Катя Климочкина [msg #22].
- **What it is:** a tool that helps understand how a person perceives sensory stimuli from the environment and reacts to them [msg #22].
- **Purpose:** to explore where a person has sensory hypersensitivity (excessive stimulation) and where hyposensitivity (lack of stimulation) [msg #22].
- **Scope:** it is compiled across all eight senses — touch, taste, smell, hearing, vision, proprioception, vestibular, interoception (see *The eight senses*) [msg #22].
- **What it gives you:** it brings together in one place where you are hyper- and where hyposensitive; it gives information about triggers and the particular sensitivities of your sensory system, and shows cause-and-effect links — what exactly can lead you to a meltdown or shutdown [msg #22].
- **How it is used:** knowing your triggers, you can plan and introduce strategies aimed at softening sensory experiences and creating a more favourable environment around you [msg #22].

Alina Safi adds that interest in sensory features keeps growing; with the development of understanding of neurodiversity and individual differences in information processing people have come to understand better how to manage their sensory profile, and understanding it helps create a supportive environment and adapt everyday activities to individual sensory needs [msg #225].

### Dunn's Model of sensory processing (four sensory profiles)
Presented by Alina Safi, with a diagram [msg #225, img #225]. The model was developed by occupational therapist Winnie Dunn (Вини Данн) back in the 1990s to explain individual differences in perceiving and reacting to sensory stimuli. It rests on two parameters:
- **Sensory threshold** (порог сенсорной чувствительности) — high or low. Per the diagram, a **high** threshold means: requires intense stimuli to react; low sensitivity to the environment; slow to notice changes; needs bright, intense sensations. A **low** threshold means: notices even weak sensory stimuli; high sensitivity to the environment; reacts quickly to changes; easily over-excited [img #225].
- **Behavioural response** (поведенческая реакция) — passive or active. Passive strategies: "Не ищет и не избегает стимуляцию активно" (*does not actively seek or avoid stimulation*); active strategies: "Целенаправленно управляет сенсорной средой" (*purposefully manages the sensory environment*) [img #225].

Their intersection yields four profiles [msg #225, img #225]:
1. **Low registration — "Слабо регистрирующий сигналы сенсорный профиль"** (high threshold + passive). Means missing many sensory signals: a child may not respond when addressed, may not notice pain or temperature [msg #225].
2. **Sensation seeking — "Ищущий ощущения сенсорный профиль"** (high threshold + active). An active striving for intense sensations: children may spin, jump, touch different textures and seek loud sounds [msg #225].
3. **Sensitive — "Чувствительный сенсорный профиль"** (low threshold + passive). A fast reaction to weak stimuli: a child may react painfully to bright light, loud sounds or certain fabrics [msg #225].
4. **Avoiding — "Избегающий сенсорный профиль"** (low threshold + active). Active evasion of unpleasant sensations: avoiding crowds, refusing new foods, reluctance to get hands dirty [msg #225].

### Irlen syndrome symptom list and self-reflection criteria
Presented by Катя Климочкина under the heading "the perception syndrome that makes your head ache while reading and makes your eyes ripple from a screen" [msg #63].

**Definition.** Meares-Irlen syndrome, also known as Irlen syndrome or scotopic sensitivity syndrome, is a **perceptual processing disorder** affecting how the brain processes visual information. People with it have difficulty processing certain wavelengths of light, which can produce a range of visual and perceptual symptoms that interfere with reading, writing and other visual tasks and affect overall quality of life [msg #63].

**Cause (as described).** It is thought to be caused by a neurological anomaly affecting how the brain **interprets** visual stimuli, not by problems with the eyes themselves. The exact nature of the anomaly is not understood; it is supposed to relate to differences in the functioning of certain neural pathways or brain areas responsible for visual processing. Two hypotheses are given [msg #63]:
- heightened sensitivity to certain wavelengths, especially the blue-green part of the spectrum;
- deviations in how the brain processes visual information, making it hard to distinguish letters or words on the page, especially black text on a white background.

**How it shows up (8 manifestations)** [msg #63]:
1. **Visual distortions** — text or images look blurred, moving or flickering.
2. **Light sensitivity** — discomfort with certain lighting, especially fluorescent (daylight) lamps, screen glare or bright sunlight.
3. **Reading difficulties** — problems with fluency, comprehension or tracking text on the page.
4. **Eye strain and fatigue** — eye discomfort, headaches or exhaustion after long visual work (reading or computer).
5. **Depth perception problems** — difficulty judging distances or perceiving depth accurately.
6. **Reduced reading speed** — slowed reading due to visual discomfort or difficulty processing text.
7. **Higher error rate** — more errors when reading or copying text: skipping lines or words, repeating words, losing one's place.
8. **Attention difficulties** — trouble maintaining focus during visual tasks, leading to distractibility or restlessness.

**Relation to other conditions.** Irlen syndrome is often associated with dyslexia, ADHD and ASD, but Климочкина stresses it is distinct and is specifically a disorder of perception [msg #63].

**Self-reflection criteria (5)** — offered "for interest"; she says self-diagnosis will of course give nothing here [msg #63]:
1. Eye discomfort or strain while reading, headaches, eye fatigue or visual overstrain, especially during long reading.
2. Words or text seem blurred, distorted or moving on the page, especially black text on white or under fluorescent light.
3. Heightened sensitivity to bright light, glare or harsh lighting, causing visual discomfort or difficulty focusing.
4. Visual distortions or patterns; words or letters seem to jump, shift or swirl on the page, especially in high-contrast conditions or when looking at striped or patterned surfaces.
5. Reading is more comfortable, clearer or better understood with coloured overlays, filters or tinted lenses, which ease visual discomfort and improve fluency.

She notes she only discovered the syndrome recently herself [msg #63].

### Predictive coding account of autism (precision parameter)
Presented by Катя Климочкина in a post and a longread [msg #65, article #65]; also used in the habituation post [msg #180].

**1. The general theory.** Predictive coding says our perception of the world comes **from within**. The brain "generates" a model of the world that predicts what we are about to see, hear, touch, smell and taste — it "предполагает и создает образ" (*supposes and creates an image*). The sense organs' job is to **check** these predictions, making sure the internal model does not drift far from actual reality [msg #65, article #65].

**2. Beyond perception.** Proponents apply the theory also to emotions, cognition and motor control: we move a hand because we predict that we will move it, and the body acts so that the prediction comes true. Климочкина describes the theory as a nod to the Bayesian mathematical model, "где все хаотично, но со смыслом" (*where everything is chaotic, but meaningful*) [article #65].

**3. The autism hypothesis.** The predictive-coding theory of autism proposes that the autistic brain does not form accurate predictions, **or** that sensory information overrides these internal predictive models; therefore the autistic person is excessively sensitive to external information and unable to tune it out. It is hard to process social signals and communication in time to generate a fitting reaction because internal behavioural models are formed "недостаточно хорошо" (*not well enough*). Thus predictive coding could explain the social, sensory and other difficulties of ASD [msg #65, article #65].

**4. Autism as a different learning curve.** Predictive coding views autism as a difference in the brain's **learning curve**, where "learning" covers everything from making sense of a "Where's Waldo?" picture (hard for visual perception) to preparing for exams [article #65].

**5. The precision parameter — how learning normally works.** A parameter called "точность" (*precision*) sets the value the brain gives to mismatches between sensory information and our expectations [msg #65, article #65].
- When we learn something new, the brain **raises** precision and uses input to build and complete the model.
- When the brain decides the model is complete, it **lowers** precision, assuming further discrepancies are random variation it can safely ignore — following the rule "зачем стараться, если уже понятно?" (*why bother if it's already clear?*) [article #65].
- Modelling is **hierarchical and sequential**: in the visual system, for example, it starts with geometric details and moves on to global features and abstractions [article #65].
- Biologically the brain regulates precision by changing the proportions of chemical messengers such as glutamate, dopamine and noradrenaline. Subjectively we feel surprise, then the satisfaction of mastery, and finally boredom [article #65].

**6. What differs in autism.** The idea is that in autism the brain **re-tunes precision more slowly**: it stays tuned to details and finds it harder to generalise [msg #65, article #65].

**7. What it would explain.** Why autistic people often describe a feeling of over-arousal and constant surprise, why they prefer routine, and why problems understanding other people may arise [article #65].

**8. Supporting evidence (as described).** [article #65]
- In everyday life and in lab experiments autistic people are slow to tune out background noise and quick to pick up novelty in their surroundings — the impression is that people with ASD are less bound by prior expectations.
- Measurements of brain and skin responses to sensory input show they habituate with difficulty to metronomes or other repetitive stimuli.
- They are less susceptible to optical and multisensory illusions that trick expectations.
- **The face/house tone experiment:** researchers play a high or low tone, show a picture of a face or a house, and ask participants to press "face" or "house". At first the high tone predicts a house, later the low tone does. The relation is never perfect — just higher odds for one tone — so it is not obvious whether a break in the pattern is an exception or a new rule. Autistic people are slower at the task overall, but **notice faster that the pattern has changed**, suggesting they expect change — rather than clinging to a pattern — more than typical people do.
- Predictive coding also suggests delusions may arise when expectations are too weak and the brain over-adjusts, making a person draw grand conclusions from scant data.
- It is unclear whether the predictive difficulty lies in **forming expectations** or in the **senses overriding** those expectations; the experiments seem to lean towards the second option.

**9. Counter-evidence.** In some studies autistic people handle predictive tasks — habituating to repeated stimuli, responding to some kinds of illusions, recognising patterns unfolding over time — as easily as neurotypical people. The direction of causation is also unclear: instead of prediction problems disrupting social cognition, social difficulties may change how a person's internal models develop [article #65].

**10. Relation to other theories.** Predictive coding overlaps with other autism hypotheses. Like some of them, it suggests the autistic brain focuses too strongly on details and is slower to zoom out to see the whole picture. It also implies difficulty guessing other people's intentions — an instance of a struggle with predictions in general — which fits the theory that autistic people struggle with "theory of mind". (The article at one point writes "теория примитив кодинга" [sic], evidently meaning predictive coding.) [article #65]

**11. Practical implication.** If the autistic brain handles sensory information and expectations differently, people with ASD can learn to compensate: for example, coaching or CBT could help learn to shift attention from low-level details to higher-level ones, train attention and adapt models [article #65]. The article states it is based on articles about predictive coding in ASD, autistic traits and sensory prediction, and anticipation (not named) [article #65].

**12. Link to habituation.** Difficulty forming accurate predictions about the environment may make **repeated stimuli feel new or unexpected**, blocking habituation (see next framework) [msg #180].

### Habituation account of autistic sensory sensitivity
Presented by Катя Климочкина as a continuation of the longread on overcoming discomfort, because habituation differences make "overcoming" a hard task both for the person and, separately, for their nervous system [msg #180].
- **Definition:** habituation (привыкание, габитуация) is the reduction in the intensity of a response when a stimulus is repeated. The organism remembers and learns to filter familiar, predictable stimuli, freeing attention for new or significant ones. Example: after a while we stop noticing a strong smell in a room or a previously infuriating label on our clothes [msg #180].
- **Why it matters:** habituation supports **selective attention**, helping avoid over-stimulation by filtering repetitive, irrelevant sensory signals — "Привыкли к этикете и забыли про нее" (*Got used to the label and forgot about it*). In autistic people this process may be disrupted; some autistic people do not adapt to stimuli the way neurotypical people do [msg #180].
- **Findings in autism:** people with autism show reduced behavioural habituation (slowing of reaction time) and changes in the amygdala on exposure to repeated stimuli, in particular other people's faces. This points to **difficulty filtering irrelevant or non-essential stimuli** — e.g. heightened attention to repetitive visual stimuli (rotating objects, someone twirling a pen) and reduced attention to social stimuli (facial expressions) [msg #180].
- **Consequences:** when a person cannot tune out the clicking of a pen, the hum of an air conditioner or clothing labels, this leads to discomfort, distress, sensory overload and difficulty concentrating on other tasks. Repeated, even trivial stimuli feel… noticeable. This may coincide with the theory of **weak central coherence**, which proposes a detail-oriented processing style in autism [msg #180].
- **Alternative hypothesis:** impaired predictive coding — repeated stimuli seem new or unexpected, preventing habituation [msg #180].
- **Typical vs autistic trajectory, the shop example:** in typical development repeated exposure lowers reactivity over time and redirects attention to new or unknown stimuli — you enter a shop, it is noisy, you stay a while and it seems "quieter"; this shift supports learning and adaptation. In autism, ineffective habituation can lead to heightened sensitivity to environmental change, sensory over-arousal and a feeling of unpredictability — you enter a noisy shop, stay a while and get overloaded by the noise, so much that you do not want to return at all, never mind waiting to adapt to the volume [msg #180].
- **What to do:** repetitive behaviour can help create structure and cope with the anxiety caused by this unpredictability. "чтобы привыкнуть все равно нужно привыкнуть… просто дать себе больше условий для этого" (*to get used to something you still have to get used to it… just give yourself more conditions for it*); how exactly is laid out in the overcoming-discomfort longread (next framework) [msg #180].
- **State of the evidence:** there is no research that unambiguously describes habituation differences in autistic people; some studies find the differences, in others they look insignificant. It seems that even among autistic people there are many subgroups with different habituation profiles. Климочкина calls the topic very interesting and very complex and wants to keep exploring it [msg #180].

### Overcoming discomfort step by step while respecting sensory features (18 strategies)
Source: the longread «Преодолеваем дискомфорт при аутизме» by @autismis and Катя Климочкина, linked from the habituation post [article #180].

**Why overcome discomfort at all.** One important strategy of psychotherapy (and of life) is overcoming and tolerating discomfort from activities that trigger negative emotions (fear, anxiety, shame or guilt) or strong reluctance. The reason: this way we learn, in a safe and moderate form, to be where and do what matters to us but is hard (or sometimes unbearable). It will still be hard, but making it a bit easier is within our power [article #180].

**Why it is harder in autism.** One has to bear not only the discomfort itself (e.g. being very scared to go somewhere) but also the effects of sensory sensitivity (noisy, bright) along the way, and to account for a nervous system that affects how habituation happens and how reactions to stimuli change [article #180].

**Worked example — the bookshop job.** You want your first job in a bookshop but must pass an interview (scary); talk to a stranger (scary); possibly mask and make eye contact (exhausting) or smile (exhausting); answer strange questions (hard). To get there you ride a noisy metro (sensory hypersensitivity), the office has bright light (hypersensitivity), and the interviewer speaks too slowly (hyposensitivity). The authors note that even writing the scenario was painful; managing sensory sensitivity in the moment, so as not to have a meltdown or get so tired you recover for a week, does not make it easier when you also have to think on the spot [article #180].

**The balance.** Handling discomfort and sensory features is "хрупкий баланс, который можно достичь" (*a fragile balance that can be achieved*). Progress is gradual and is reached by combining sensory regulation methods, emotion regulation strategies and self-compassion. Preparation, a favourable setting and breaks are very important, as is knowing when to step back and try again later if the situation becomes unbearable [article #180].

**A. Preparation — "Кто хорошо готовится, тому и проще жить." (*Whoever prepares well has an easier life.*)** [article #180]
1. **Recognise triggers.** Understand your specific sensory triggers (loud sounds, bright light, strong smells, crowds) and emotional triggers (e.g. social situations, new surroundings). Knowing what to expect lets you prepare better.
2. **Gradual habituation.** If the stimulus is loud sound, start with quiet sounds and very gradually raise the volume. Example: first visit the bookshop in noise-cancelling headphones; the second time take them off and stay without them for 20 seconds; the third time stay a minute without them. "Постепенность - ключ к успеху" (*Gradualness is the key to success*). Because habituation to sensory stimuli is reduced in autism, this requires patience and even more gradualness.
3. **Start with familiar places or people.** Being in an uncomfortable situation is uncomfortable enough, so start small, with what is FAMILIAR and controllable. Example: if you practise visiting busy places to get the bookshop job, go with a friend who understands your needs and can encourage you.
4. **Set small, achievable goals.** Break big goals into smaller stages; rather than plunging into a hard situation, aim for smaller, more achievable steps that build confidence gradually. "Лучше сделать 5 шагов, чем облажаться на первом и слишком трудном" (*Better to take 5 steps than to fail on the first, too-difficult one*). Example: before the bookshop interview, maybe start by meeting friends there.
5. **Discomfort hierarchy.** List situations that cause discomfort, ordered from least to most unpleasant. Work through the list gradually starting with the least unpleasant; over time take on scarier ones, keeping sensory and emotional regulation strategies to help yourself.
6. **Calm down in advance.** Before entering a hard situation, do a sensory activity that gives a feeling of regulation or grounding — a few minutes of deep breathing, stretching, a calming scent; e.g. stand in the hallway and smell your favourite candle. This "calming ritual" before entering the "uncomfortable world" helps with sensory and emotional discomfort — a preventive measure.
7. **Create scripts.** Social scripts — short descriptive stories of what to expect in a specific situation — help psychological preparation. Practising how the situation may go makes it more predictable and less overwhelming.
8. **Visualisation.** Mentally rehearse or imagine yourself successfully handling the situation — both "how it will go" and "how I will cope if it gets hard". Imagine how you would handle potential sensory triggers and what steps you could take to stay calm. This can reduce worry about the unknown and increase self-efficacy.

**B. Self-help during exposure** [article #180]
9. **Regulate sensory sensitivity.** All your life hacks for making the world feel less bright, noisy and overloading belong here: reduce the intensity of stimuli **without avoiding them completely**. Noise-cancelling headphones, sunglasses, antistress toys or barefoot shoes can make sensations less overwhelming.
10. **Add something pleasant.** If you like a particular texture, smell or sound, include it in the process, balancing "the unpleasant with the pleasant". Example: listen to a favourite music genre in headphones in a crowd — a hard experience "outside" combined with soothing input "in the ears" can reduce overall discomfort.
11. **Practise mindfulness.** Deep breathing, progressive muscle relaxation or grounding exercises (e.g. count all the red objects around you) help bring attention back to the body and handle stress when the environment overdoes it.
12. **Focus on one sense at a time.** If a situation is hard because of many stimuli, focus on just one sense. Example: if the bookshop is noisy, bright and crowded, minimise distractions and focus only on hearing — sit in a corner armchair, close your eyes, take off your jacket, help yourself with a favourite fidget and listen to what is around you for a minute at first, maybe even less.

**C. Emotion regulation** [article #180]
13. **Cognitive reframing.** Rethink negative thoughts, challenge and doubt them. A thought like "Я не справлюсь" (*I won't cope*) is so scary and "heavy" that it is hard to ignore, and focusing on it makes it bigger; reformulating it more realistically — "Будет непросто, но я попробую и посмотрю что получится" (*It won't be easy, but I'll try and see what happens*) — makes it a little easier.
14. **Recognise emotions and feelings.** If recognising and naming emotions is available to you, do not push them away. Realising "я чувствую тревогу и беспокоюсь" (*I feel anxious and worried*) or "I feel depressed" can sometimes lower the intensity of emotions.
15. **Take breaks and recover.** Leaving the comfort zone to do something "important but scary" is very hard — even harder than you may realise; even a step towards discomfort is powerful, takes lots of energy and tires you. Giving yourself time to rest, relax and forget about it is as valuable as the overcoming itself, and matters long-term so as not to burn out and give up. Example: after 10 minutes in the bookshop with friends you surely need time in quiet and comfort — an hour or two, or maybe "денечек в одеялке" (*a little day in a blanket*).

**D. And also** [article #180]
16. **Track progress.** Tracking sensations and reactions while overcoming discomfort helps you notice progress, even if it is smaller than you expect of yourself.
17. **Be flexible.** Planning matters, but so does breaking your plans so as not to feel pressure. If you wake up with a headache, no energy or simply no desire to follow the plan, forcing yourself is a bad idea — drop it; adjust goals or step back if needed. Progress can be non-linear, and that is normal.
18. **Celebrate successes, however small.** Mark every small win — a few extra minutes in a busy shop, an extra second without headphones in loud noise, taking part in a hard social interaction. Positive reinforcement helps keep motivation.

**Closing caveat.** Coping with discomfort and fear while balancing sensory sensitivity is genuinely hard; many struggle with sensory and emotional regulation, which makes "out of the comfort zone" situations that trigger fear, shame or tension especially intense. Still, being in such situations can sometimes build resilience and the ability to run your life "as I want" — provided everything is done at a manageable ("посильном") level, thoughtfully and with these strategies [article #180].

### Disrupted connectivity theory (long-range underconnectivity + local hyperconnectivity)
While writing a course on sensory sensitivity in autism, the channel's author collected a list of **five** theories of how sensory sensitivity in ASD works at the brain level and chose to present the first, which is also one of the main theories of "autism in general". Her hedges: no research has fully confirmed it, and it will most likely bring no practical benefit in life, but knowing how autism works at the brain level is "просто огонь" (*simply fire*, i.e. fascinating) [msg #195]. The longread is by Lena Foer [article #195].

**Status.** The pathophysiology of autism is a dark and poorly studied area. Disrupted connectivity is today's leading pathophysiological model; it is called a theory because decades of neuroimaging studies have been able neither to confirm nor to refute it. The whole field of "what lies behind autism at the brain level" is still a big challenge, so one can speak only of hypotheses [article #195].

**Core claim.** Put simply: in people with ASD separate brain regions are **weakly connected to each other**, while **within** regions there are, on the contrary, too many neural connections [article #195].

**Origin of the two halves.** [article #195]
1. *Weak long-range connections:* the hypothesis dates back to 1988, when PET scans of autistic adults showed reduced signalling between frontal, parietal and subcortical areas. The next study on this came 15 years later and confirmed the original finding.
2. *Local hyperconnectivity:* suspected separately and in an original way — head circumference was measured in children with and without ASD and was larger in ASD. Investigating why showed an increased volume of superficial white matter, in fibres connecting neighbouring areas; hence the conclusion of local over-connectivity.

**Why it was historically important.** [article #195]
- It began a new view of autism: before it, the developmental pathologies responsible for ASD were sought in individual brain areas; disrupted connectivity instead sees autism not as dysfunction of one part but as a **systemic** issue involving the brain as a whole, in all its interconnectedness.
- It became the first "theory of everything" in ASD, incorporating earlier theories: explanations through theory of mind and executive dysfunction weave naturally into it.

**Consequences — two theories in one.** [article #195]
- **Reduced connectivity between areas** disrupts functions that require coordinated work of those areas.
  - *Social interaction:* while we chat about the weather with a colleague, the brain simultaneously processes the other person's facial expression, tone of voice, body language, social context and the actual words, then integrates them into a coherent hypothesis about what is going on — "Маша выглядит усталой, вчера был корпоратив, наверное, у нее похмелье" (*Masha looks tired, there was a corporate party yesterday, she's probably hungover*). This requires coordinated communication between brain parts; when signalling between them is disrupted, assembling a clear interpretation is hard.
  - *Executive functions:* the set of high-level cognitive abilities we need to structure information, plan, prioritise and remember what matters. They work in a "distributed" way across different areas, so insufficient connectivity impairs planning, organisation, memory and task switching (the source ends the list with "и так далее" (*and so forth*)).
- **Local hyperconnectivity** complements the picture: a huge number of connections leads to more thorough processing of information — in other words, getting stuck on it. Rigidity, heightened attention to details, inability to see the whole picture because separate parts stick out too much — all are, per the theory, consequences of local hyperconnectivity. On one side this gives strong attention to detail; on the other, the inability to abstract from details is the cause of **sensory sensitivity**, intense special interests, hyperfocus, and focusing during a conversation on the other person's mole instead of what they are saying and what is happening overall. "Гипердетализированная картина получается фрагментированной и не имеющей смысла" (*The hyper-detailed picture turns out fragmented and meaningless*).

**The catch.** Foer wrote "this is how things are" only for brevity — whether the theory is right is unknown [article #195]:
1. **Measurement problems:** methodologically, how to measure this connectivity has no clear and simple answer.
2. **Replicability problems:** one study shows one thing, another a completely different picture; the two halves of the theory are studied in separate research; and one cannot look at "connectivity in general" — studies look at connectivity between, say, the left hippocampus and the left fusiform and parahippocampal areas, and another study between something else. It is a huge amount of work.
3. **Current literature:** in the last five years papers saying "we looked, seem not to have found, but found something, but it differs for everyone" coexist peacefully with "hooray, we found it".

Her conclusion: it will take a good while longer for things to become clearer; meanwhile one can enjoy an elegant, all-encompassing hypothesis that "не множит сущности, а ровно наоборот" (*does not multiply entities, but exactly the opposite*) [article #195].

(!) INCOMPLETE: the other four of the author's five brain-level theories are not named in the post; predictive coding and habituation appear in other posts of this topic, but the source does not say whether they belong to her list [msg #195].

### Gravitational insecurity — neurobiology, differences from anxiety, signs and support
Post by the channel's author and a longread by Lena Foer [msg #253, article #253]. The post's image is captioned "My scary steps" [img #253]. The author says she planned a short post but fell into a rabbit hole of neurobiological details, more interesting than expected [msg #253].

**Definition.** Gravitational insecurity (гравитационная неуверенность, ГН; GI) is a fear of movement arising from dysfunction of the vestibular system, first described by Jean Ayres in 1972. People with GI feel **panic** during actions most find mundane: going down stairs, tilting the head back at the hairdresser's, lifting the feet off the ground on a swing. In people with ASD it occurs substantially more often than in everyone else [msg #253, article #253].

**Neurobiology — three links in the chain.** [article #253]
1. **Gravity detectors.** In the inner ear are the otolith organs, the utricle and saccule, which detect gravity. They contain calcium carbonate crystals lying on hair cells; when the head tilts, the crystals shift, bending the hairs and generating nerve signals about the body's position relative to gravity. In GI the brain cannot correctly integrate these signals, producing deep spatial disorientation.
2. **Weak amplification (VVS).** A 2022 study (Potegal, May-Benson et al.) identified a specific neural network linked to GI: the vestibular velocity storage system (VVS), a brainstem-cerebellar network that amplifies weak vestibular signals. In adults with GI the VVS amplifies vestibular signals much less effectively, so the vestibular system finds it much harder to distinguish movements, producing a constant feeling of instability.
3. **Route to fear.** Neural pathways connect the vestibular nuclei with limbic structures, including the amygdala, responsible for fear reactions. So panic during movement becomes a logical consequence of what happens in the brain.

**Four ways GI differs from primary anxiety disorders** [article #253]:
1. The fear is originally tied to vestibular-proprioceptive stimuli, not cognitive triggers (though those may be added later).
2. GI improves with vestibular rehabilitation; anxiety disorders improve with therapy.
3. GI shows measurable VVS abnormalities absent in pure anxiety.
4. GI usually starts in early childhood and stays stable through life; anxiety disorders usually intensify or go away.

**GI vs acrophobia.** GI must also be distinguished from acrophobia (fear of heights): people with GI fear a change of position even at minimal height (stepping off a kerb, lying down), whereas acrophobia requires at least some significant height [article #253].

**Link with autism (numbers).** In a sample of 842 children, pronounced GI was found in 14.3% of children with ASD versus 3.7% of children without ASD but with sensory processing disorders; another 7.1% of autistic children had mild GI. For comparison, prevalence in the general population of adolescents and adults is about 2%. The neuroanatomical basis of the link: cerebellar and brainstem abnormalities, consistently documented in autism [article #253].

**Eight behavioural signs in children** — research found these predict GI with 93% accuracy [article #253]:
1. Goes down stairs unsteadily/hesitantly.
2. Dislikes having the head tilted back (hair washing, at the dentist).
3. Dislikes lifts and escalators.
4. Puts both feet on each step instead of alternating.
5. Hesitates before getting on swings or roundabouts.
6. Dislikes being turned upside down.
7. Is afraid of amusement rides.
8. Has difficulty catching a ball (which requires visual-vestibular integration).

**In adults.** 16% report mild difficulties and 2% pronounced ones. Driving-related fears (bridges, motorways, hills, tunnels) correlate strongly with GI. Also: trouble with stairs, fear of slippery surfaces, avoiding amusement parks, stress at the hairdresser's and dentist's [article #253].

**What helps.** Ayres Sensory Integration therapy (ASI) is classified as an evidence-based practice for autistic children aged 4-12. Schaaf's RCT (2014) found that children after 30 ASI sessions achieved significantly better outcomes (effect size d = 1.2). Key principles [article #253]:
- Slow **linear** movements (back and forth, up and down) calm the nervous system, whereas **rotational** ones over-excite it.
- Start with the feet supported on the ground, on stable surfaces.
- Proprioceptive input (deep pressure, weighted objects) helps "ground" and prevents over-stimulation.

**What not to do.** The main contraindication is **forcing movement**. Do not coax a person with GI who shows resistance: coercion strengthens fear, creates negative associations that block therapy, and causes psychological harm. Treating GI as emotional weakness or a behavioural problem makes things worse. "ГН - не капризы и не тревожное расстройство" (*GI is not whims and not an anxiety disorder*) — it is a neurological feature with measurable dysfunction in vestibular circuits. Respecting the person's need for control while gradually building vestibular tolerance creates conditions for adapting to life with it [article #253].

### Proprioception and strange sitting positions
Lena Foer asks whether you like sitting in strange positions — toes curled, twisted up as much as possible — "Не вам одним!" (*You're not the only one!*). Proprioception tells us the body's position in space and in autistic people can be hyper- or hyposensitive. With **low** proprioceptive sensitivity you know less about where your body is and may, for example, often get bruises — and sit in strange positions. Mechanism: when you sit "like normal people", there is too little sensory data about body position; intensely twisted postures give the brain much more information about body position, and as a result you feel calmer and comfortable [msg #56].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The authors repeatedly hedge brain-level explanations: disrupted connectivity "not fully confirmed by any research", neither confirmed nor refuted by decades of neuroimaging, with measurement and replication problems [msg #195, article #195]; habituation research is inconsistent and there may be many autistic subgroups with different habituation profiles [msg #180]; predictive coding is presented with "may" throughout, some studies find no autistic difference on predictive tasks, and the causal direction (prediction problems → social difficulties or the reverse) is unclear [msg #65, article #65].
- (!) UNCLEAR: whether the predictive difficulty in autism lies in forming expectations or in senses overriding them; the article says experiments seem to lean to the second [article #65].
- The exact cause of Irlen syndrome is not understood; only hypotheses exist, and self-diagnosis is not meaningful [msg #63].
- Sensory differences are not specific to autism (they also occur in ADHD and in a number of other disorders) [msg #3]; Irlen syndrome is associated with dyslexia, ADHD and ASD but is a distinct perceptual disorder [msg #63].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the author states sensory perception problems are one of the "two main symptoms" of ASD [msg #3]; the source does not reference diagnostic criteria for this.
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the "sensory diet" concept is named but not explained [msg #22].
- (!) UNCLEAR: in Dunn's model the post's text describes the profiles mainly with examples from children; it does not say whether the same profiles are used for adults [msg #225].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the overcoming-discomfort longread labels an interviewer who speaks too slowly as "sensory hyposensitivity" without explaining the link [article #180].
- Gravitational insecurity statistics (842 children, 93% accuracy of eight signs, adult 16%/2%, Schaaf d = 1.2) are given in the longread without full study references beyond author names and years [article #253].
