# DBT for autistic adults: rationale, adaptations and evidence

## What this covers

This topic gathers everything the channel's authors — autistic DBT therapist(s) who run a DBT skills group for autistic adults — wrote about using dialectical behaviour therapy with autistic clients: the conceptual and clinical arguments for DBT over CBT, how common emotion dysregulation is in autism and what causes it, the role of alexithymia, the authors' own adaptations of the skills training, and the evidence from a 2024 UK conference, a 2026 Russian conference and the Strasbourg randomized controlled trial. It matters, according to the source, because suicidality, self-harm and emotion dysregulation are far more frequent in autistic people, while protocols with proven effectiveness specifically for dysregulation in autistic adults have been lacking [msg #2, #266].

## Core ideas

- **No therapy treats autism; therapy treats the problems autistic people have.** The author stresses that any therapy "in autism" is therapy of the difficulties autistic people face, and DBT's target is emotion dysregulation and related problems, not autistic traits [msg #2, #266].
- **DBT's premise fits autistic experience better than CBT's.** DBT allows emotions that are not mediated by thoughts, which matches sensory overload and communication burnout (see *DBT vs CBT premise*) [msg #2].
- **Emotion dysregulation is very common in autism** — four times more frequent than in the general population (England-Mason, 2020), 40–60% of autistic people per the 2024 conference — and it drives self-harm and suicidality [msg #2, #170, #266].
- **Alexithymia is the bottleneck.** Regulation starts with being aware of the emotion; alexithymia breaks the first step, and the Strasbourg RCT found DBT reduced dysregulation through reducing alexithymia [msg #170, #266].
- **Even unadapted DBT works, but adapted DBT is better**, and adaptations concern the form of delivery more than the content [msg #170, #193, #228, #258].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Six arguments for DBT for autistic clients (author's rationale, 2023)
Purpose: explains why the author — who was a DBT client, is autistic, is a DBT therapist and is interested in autism therapy ("Мы с темой “ДБТ для РАС” были созданы друг для друга" — *"The topic 'DBT for ASD' and I were made for each other"*) — believes DBT can be an excellent therapy option for people with ASD. Her one-line answer: the problems autistic people face can be solved well by DBT [msg #2].

1. **Conceptual argument: emotions without thoughts.** DBT differs from CBT in one key premise about where emotions come from (fully described in *DBT vs CBT premise* below). DBT's view fits sensory overloads and communication burnout much better. The author puts this argument first deliberately, while noting the next one is no less important [msg #2].
2. **DBT was built for chronic suicidality.** DBT was created as a therapy for chronic suicidality — for people who for a long time think about killing themselves or harming themselves. The author read about four large studies on suicidality in the autistic population and, generalising, says it is high, as is the frequency of non-suicidal self-injury. For this reason a large study of DBT for ASD was underway in the Netherlands at the time of writing (2023), which looked very promising to her [msg #2].
3. **Emotion regulation is the core of DBT.** Emotion regulation problems are four times more frequent in autistic people than in the general population (England-Mason, 2020). Episodes of aggression and self-harm are behavioural manifestations of these problems. Work on emotion regulation is essentially the main part of DBT: it teaches people to notice when emotions arise, to recognise and name them (the author reminds that alexithymia often comes "в комплекте" — *bundled* — with ASD), to reduce their intensity, and to regulate their behaviour under their influence so as not to harm themselves [msg #2].
4. **Crisis management fits meltdown prevention.** DBT gives a lot of attention to crises: how to recognise that a crisis is coming, how to prepare in advance and "где подстелить соломки" (*where to lay down straw*, i.e. cushion the fall). This matches very well with building a sensory profile in ASD and finding ways to avoid meltdowns before they start. DBT helps a person compile a "мануал" (*manual*) to themselves and to handle high vulnerability to external factors skilfully [msg #2].
5. **Clear, unambiguous structure.** DBT is built on clear instructions and unambiguous algorithms with minimal room for interpretation; it is straightforward and offers rules for dealing with oneself and with other people — in the author's words, the dream of a person who prefers everything to be orderly ("четенько") [msg #2].
6. **A social-skills block — with caveats.** Skills training includes a block on social (interpersonal) skills. The author thinks it is rather simplistic for ASD, but research says socialising skills of autistic people who completed DBT skills training increased. A further caveat: teaching the "form" (how to meet a new person, how to start a dialogue) without feeling the content is not liked by everyone as a concept [msg #2].

### DBT vs CBT premise on where emotions come from
- **CBT premise (as the author describes it):** all emotional reactions are a consequence of our thoughts — not the event but thoughts about the event cause emotions. Her examples with a falling cup: the cup falls → "какой идиот поставил чашку на край стола" (*what idiot put the cup on the edge of the table*) → anger; the cup falls → "it was my favourite cup and now it's gone" → sadness; the cup falls → "my cup broke and Masha's didn't" → envy [msg #2].
- **DBT premise:** this is not necessarily so. Some emotions are caused by thoughts and some are not. Example: "Я резко выбежала к краю обрыва - я испугалась" (*I suddenly ran out to the edge of a cliff — I got scared*) — no intermediate links [msg #2].
- **Why it matters for autism:** the second view fits sensory overloads and communication burnout. Example: the neighbour turns on a drill and "меня колбасит" (*I'm shaking/falling apart*); the author does not think "the neighbour is a jerk" or "poor me, forced to listen" — she just feels bad. This fits DBT and fits CBT worse. It may seem a small thing, but she asks the reader to imagine the total number of CBT therapists "вытрясающих из аутистов автоматические мысли там, где их нет" (*shaking automatic thoughts out of autistic people where there are none*) [msg #2].

### Three-stage model of emotion regulation
Presented at the 2024 conference "Adapting DBT for Neuro-Divergent Individuals"; the author had not heard of this model before. People regulate in three stages [msg #170]:
1. **Become aware of the emotion** — recognise what one is feeling.
2. **Form an intention to change it** somehow.
3. **Take purposeful actions** in that direction.

Why it explains autistic dysregulation: alexithymia, or difficulty understanding one's own emotions, is present in more than half of autistic people, so the problem appears already at the first step of the model and blocks managing one's state. The Strasbourg RCT later confirmed this, in the authors' reading: naming a feeling is "той самой ступенькой" (*that very step*) without which doing anything further with the feeling does not work [msg #170, #266].

### Biological and social causes of emotion dysregulation in autism
As reported from the 2024 conference, dysregulation is so common because of biological and social factors [msg #170]:
- **Biological factors:**
  1. Difficulties with social interaction — everything is constantly unclear, there is constant risk of error, uncertainty is very high.
  2. Rigid behaviour patterns — sensory overloads contribute, plus low tolerance of uncertainty and of disruptions to routines.
  3. Difficulties recognising emotions.
- **Social factors:**
  1. Lack of proper learning of regulation from adults.
  2. "наказывание за аутизм" (*punishment for autism*).
  3. Elevated risk of bullying and of further violent situations.
  4. Limited access to suitable therapy.
  5. Stigmatisation of autism by society.

### Four standard modules of DBT skills training
Kept in full in the authors' adapted program and used unchanged in the Strasbourg RCT [msg #193, #228, #266]:
1. **Mindfulness (осознанность)** — to be in contact with oneself and notice one's emotions and states better. [msg #193, #228, article #228]
2. **Distress tolerance (стрессоустойчивость)** — skills for acute and crisis states. [msg #193, #228, article #228]
3. **Emotion regulation (эмоциональная регуляция)** — to regulate difficult emotions and states (emotions, overloads). [msg #193, #228, article #228]
4. **Interpersonal effectiveness (межличностная эффективность)** — to feel more comfortable among people; "коммуникация, которая работает" (*communication that works*). [msg #193, #228, article #228]

### Autism-specific additions to DBT skills training (authors' adapted protocol)
Katya Klimochkina (clinical and social psychologist) and Lena Foer (psychologist), both DBT and CBT therapists and channel admins, built a skills group for autistic adults after doing extensive research on adaptations ("Мы провели большой ресерч по адаптациям прежде чем запускать тренинг" — *we did a big research on adaptations before launching the training*). Their rationale, taken from the 2024 conference and existing research, has three points:
1. Emotion regulation difficulties are very widespread in ASD. [msg #193, #228, article #228]
2. Even the basic classic DBT skills training is effective, gives a noticeable effect and helps people learn to handle themselves better. [msg #193, #228, article #228]
3. There are observations about which changes make the standard training more effective, and these concern the form of delivery of the material more than corrections to its content. [msg #193, #228, article #228]

The program exists in two formats. The first version (2025) had 21 weeks: 14 weeks of the standard skills-training protocol ("Нексью", the protocol they name) plus 7 weeks of additions. The shortened version described in the program longread runs 12 sessions over 12 weeks of 2 hours each: part of the sessions come from the standard DBT skills-training protocol (Нексью), and several weeks are filled with the additions; groups are capped at no more than 10 people and joining requires a preliminary interview with the leaders. The authors say they have good experience with autistic clients in other skills groups and outside groups too. The additions [msg #193, #228, article #228]:
1. **More time for getting acquainted, explaining rules, accommodations and the session plan** — a slower, more explicit start. [msg #193, #228, article #228]
2. **More sessions — and in the 12-week version "Больше времени и подробностей" (*more time and detail*) — than in standard training on studying one's own emotions**, because the alexithymia characteristic of ASD makes this harder. [msg #193, #228, article #228]
3. **Distinguishing skills use from masking.** People with ASD often try to look socially desirable, so the authors found it important to draw a line between actions done "так надо для других" (*because others need it so*) and "так стоит сделать, чтобы помочь себе" (*worth doing to help oneself*) — "делать для себя vs делать, чтобы понравиться" (*doing for yourself vs doing to be liked*). [msg #193, #228, article #228]
4. **Building a sensory profile** and teaching about meltdowns, shutdowns and sensory overloads and how to help oneself with them. [msg #193, #228, article #228]
5. **A session dedicated to unmasking** — kept both in the 21-week version and in the 12-week program described in the longread (the short Telegram announcement #228 does not list it). [msg #193, article #228]
All of this is built on top of the four standard modules, and delivered as visually as possible and as little abstractly as possible ("максимально наглядно, минимально абстрактно" — *maximally visual, minimally abstract*) — with pictures, memes and presentations [msg #193, #228, article #228].

### Comprehensive DBT with four treatment components (Strasbourg RCT)
The DBT tested in Strasbourg was comprehensive, with all four components [msg #266]:
1. Group skills training, 2 hours 15 minutes per week (standard modules: mindfulness, emotion regulation, distress tolerance, interpersonal effectiveness).
2. Individual session once a week.
3. Phone coaching between sessions.
4. Weekly consultation meeting for the therapists themselves.

### Six DERS subscales (as the authors explain them)
Purpose: the DERS (Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale) is used by the authors to measure change during DBT; for every subscale, the lower the score, the fewer the regulation difficulties. The authors describe the six subscales like this [article #258]:
1. **Non-acceptance of emotions (Непринятие эмоций)** — difficulty accepting one's own emotions: the person is angry at themselves for their own emotions, ashamed of them, or believes they «не должен так чувствовать» (*shouldn't feel this way*). These are secondary reactions to an emotion. [article #258]
2. **Difficulties with goal-directed behaviour (Трудности целенаправленного поведения)** — how much emotions interfere with concentrating, making decisions, doing things and solving tasks: «если мне плохо, то я ничего не могу делать» (*if I feel bad, I can't do anything*). [article #258]
3. **Impulsivity (Импульсивности)** — problems controlling behaviour under strong emotions: breakdowns, self-harm, abrupt actions or words; a deficit of impulse control in a "state of affect". [article #258]
4. **Emotional awareness (Осознанности эмоций)** — difficulties recognising emotions: "не понимаю, что чувствую" (*I don't understand what I feel*), confusion between bodily sensations and emotions, emotions recognised only "после" (*afterwards*). In the authors' words, it is about a deficit of recognition — whether I notice emotions at all. [article #258]
5. **Emotional clarity (Ясности эмоций)** — how "understandable" emotions are versus felt as blurred and mixed, how hard they are to name or separate from each other; it is about understanding which emotion exactly it is. [article #258]
6. **Regulation strategies (Стратегий регуляции эмоций)** — beliefs that emotions cannot be regulated, that «ничего не помогает» (*nothing helps*), plus the absence or shortage of effective coping strategies. [article #258]

The distinction between subscale 4 (do I notice an emotion at all) and subscale 5 (do I know which emotion it is) mirrors the alexithymia problem discussed in *Three-stage model of emotion regulation* [article #258].

### Authors' own DERS before/after observations, autistic vs neurotypical participants
Klimochkina presents a bar chart titled "Регуляция эмоций: до и после" (*Emotion regulation: before and after*), DERS subscale scores on a 0–30 axis for four groups: before, neurodivergent ("ДО нейроотлич"); before, neurotypical; after, neurodivergent; after, neurotypical. Approximate values read from the chart (before → after) [img #258, article #258]:

| Subscale | Neurodivergent before → after | Neurotypical before → after |
|---|---|---|
| Non-acceptance (непринятие) | ~19.5 → ~10.7 | ~20.6 → ~9.9 |
| Goal-directed behaviour (цель) | ~18.6 → ~11.4 | ~21.4 → ~18.3 |
| Impulsivity (импульс) | ~13.9 → ~9.2 | ~16.4 → ~8.4 |
| Awareness (осведом) | ~18.3 → ~12.7 | ~16.9 → ~11.4 |
| Strategies (стратегии) | ~25.6 → ~16.4 | ~27.4 → ~17.1 |
| Clarity (ясность) | ~13.2 → ~9.2 | ~12.4 → ~10.5 |

The authors' interpretation, subscale by subscale [article #258]:
1. **Non-acceptance:** the program effectively reduces secondary reactions (shame, anger at oneself) in both groups. Importantly, ASD does not hinder learning the skill of accepting emotions; the effect is, visually, even slightly more pronounced in autistic participants. [article #258]
2. **Goal-directed behaviour under emotions:** skills related to structuring, pausing and planning "ложатся" (*fit*) especially well for autistic participants. For neurotypical participants emotions continue to interfere more with activity (in the chart their score barely drops). [article #258]
3. **Impulsivity:** "Вопреки стереотипам, импульсивность не является ведущей трудностью при РАС." (*Contrary to stereotypes, impulsivity is not the leading difficulty in ASD.*) The autistic group's baseline here was lower than the neurotypical group's; the program effectively reduces impulsive reactions in both groups. [article #258]
4. **Emotional awareness:** autistic participants learn emotion-tracking skills fairly effectively; the authors call this an important argument against the idea that autistic people do not feel or are not aware of emotions. [article #258]
5. **Emotional clarity:** a skill that improves, but more slowly than acceptance or regulation strategies. The authors call this expected: clarity requires time, practice and linguistic mediation. [article #258]
6. **Regulation strategies:** the key effect of the program — participants with and without ASD gain a sense that tools for regulating their state are available to them. [article #258]

Overall: the strongest effects are in regulation strategies, acceptance of emotions and goal-directed behaviour — exactly the areas where structured, DBT-oriented programs are most effective, according to the authors. Klimochkina's personal conclusion: emotion regulation problems in ASD are about the lack of accessible and adapted strategies, and DBT provides them [article #258].

## Detailed insights

### What DBT is and who it was made for
DBT is an approach for clients with chronic suicidality, self-harm and, more broadly, disrupted emotion regulation, including strong emotional swings; it is most often used for people with borderline personality disorder. Recent research, the authors say, shows it can also be very useful for autistic adults [msg #193, #228, article #228].

### What emotion dysregulation is
Two definitions appear. At the 2024 conference: emotion dysregulation is when there are many emotions, the perception and/or expression of emotions is painful, they last long and they disrupt functioning [msg #170]. In the 2026 post: emotions hit a person quickly, strongly, for a long time and often — what would not be a trigger for others can become one for people with dysregulation. In autistic adults it is common and pulls self-harm and suicidal thoughts along with it [msg #266].

### The 2024 UK conference "Adapting DBT for Neuro-Divergent Individuals"
The author spent two days at this conference of the British DBT society. Her impressions [msg #170, #193, #228]:
1. "I told you so!" — a year earlier she had written that DBT looks like an excellent option for emotion dysregulation in ASD, and many experts there said the same.
2. 40–60% of people with ASD were said to have emotion dysregulation; she knew it was typical of autistic people but not to this degree.
3. Causes are biological and social (see *Biological and social causes*).
4. Alexithymia's large contribution was explained via the three-stage regulation model.
5. Surprisingly, even unadapted standard comprehensive DBT (and skills training alone) works well for ASD clients: emotion dysregulation, depression and suicidality decrease, including long-term. But adapting is still better than not adapting. Observations show the changes that make standard training more effective are less about correcting content than about the way the material is delivered.
6. She liked that autism was discussed not as a disorder that must itself be cured but as a neurotype that must be taken into account when working with autistic clients.

### Findings shared from the 2026 Russian conference (Katya Klimochkina)
Klimochkina presented "ДБТ для взрослых с РАС" (*DBT for adults with ASD*) at the conference "Аутизм. Врачи, пациенты, родители: вместе в интересах людей с РАС" within the Congress of Paediatricians of Russia; her section also included a talk on self-injurious behaviour in ASD, and colleagues spoke on masking, sensory features and comorbid AuDHD. Her summary of the research: in recent years a considerable number of good studies have shown high effectiveness of DBT for autism, and even more benefit when DBT is adapted in format. Benefits range from a low rate of abrupt dropout (pleasing for specialists) to large-scale reductions in suicidality and increased ability to manage emotions, both through new constructive strategies and through better recognising and being aware of one's state. This in turn led to reduced depression, higher quality of life, social functioning and overall wellbeing [msg #258].

She adds that she and Lena asked participants of their own programs — and, per the longread, also clients in comprehensive DBT — to complete a set of questionnaires to measure the dynamics of change over therapy, for example the DERS, one of the "basic" DBT questionnaires (the scale of difficulties in emotion regulation). She calls it "еще круче" (*even cooler*) to "add" their own results to the results of others' studies. In the longread she notes that the body of good studies on DBT for autism deserves a separate post of its own [msg #258, article #258]. Their own results are written out under *Six DERS subscales* and *Authors' own DERS before/after observations* below.

### The Strasbourg randomized controlled trial (2026 post)
**Context:** suicide mortality in autistic adults is, by various estimates, 3.7–9 times higher than in the general population, yet protocols with proven effectiveness specifically for dysregulation in autistic adults have been lacking [msg #266].

**Design:** conducted by the university clinic of Strasbourg with 63 autistic adults without intellectual disability. Diagnosis was confirmed with ADI-R, ADOS-2 and WAIS-IV. Inclusion required a high DERS score (36-item questionnaire on emotion regulation difficulties) and self-harm, suicidal thoughts or a suicide attempt in the last six months. Participants were randomised: one group started DBT immediately, the other waited five months on a waiting list while continuing usual treatment [msg #266].

**Sample:** mean age 29; 94% current suicidal thoughts; 59% self-harm; 81% taking psychotropic medication; 43% comorbid ADHD; 60% had received the autism diagnosis less than a year earlier; 48% women, 44% men, 8% non-binary [msg #266].

**Results:**
1. Emotion regulation difficulties dropped by mid-therapy; by the end the difference from the waiting list was almost 32 DERS points; the improvement held six months later.
2. Depressive symptoms decreased and quality of life increased, most strongly on the psychological wellbeing scale (life satisfaction and sense of meaning).
3. Anxiety and suicidal thoughts changed about equally in both groups.
4. Most interesting to the authors: the effect on dysregulation was **mediated by reduced alexithymia** — people first began to distinguish and name their feelings better, and through this regulation became more accessible. The authors see this as confirming the conference point about alexithymia.
5. Higher autistic traits on the AQ at baseline predicted stronger improvement at six months; masking, gender, age and borderline traits did not affect the outcome [msg #266].

**Limitations (as the post lists them):**
1. The control was a waiting list, so one can say "DBT is better than waiting" but not "DBT is better than another therapy"; the study authors say this themselves and propose an active control next.
2. All main measures were self-reports.
3. 10% of participants had comorbid BPD, so part of the dysregulation could come from there.
4. Waiting-list participants knew therapy awaited them, and this expectation alone may have raised their scores, blurring the difference on anxiety.
5. One RCT with 63 participants in one centre is good news, but more data are needed [msg #266].

**Conclusion:** the target of DBT is emotion dysregulation and what is linked to it; autistic traits were not targets, and the therapy was not "therapy of autism" [msg #266].

## Tools and resources mentioned

- **DERS (Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale)** — 36-item questionnaire; one of the basic DBT outcome measures; used by the authors to track their groups and as inclusion/outcome measure in the Strasbourg RCT [msg #258, #266].
- **ADI-R, ADOS-2, WAIS-IV** — instruments used in the Strasbourg RCT to confirm autism diagnosis and absence of intellectual disability; **AQ** — autistic-traits measure, a predictor of improvement [msg #266].
- **Strasbourg RCT article** — the original paper on DBT for autistic adults, https://doi.org/10.1159/000544717; the authors' verdict: a pleasant start, but more data needed [msg #266].
- **England-Mason (2020)** — source for the fourfold frequency of emotion regulation problems in autism [msg #2].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The arguments in #2 are the author's opinion ("почему мне кажется" — *why it seems to me*), written before the conference evidence [msg #2].
- The author herself thinks the DBT social-skills block is rather simplistic for ASD, and teaching social "form" without felt content is not acceptable to everyone [msg #2].
- Prevalence figures differ by framing: "four times more often than the general population" (England-Mason, 2020) vs "40–60% of people with ASD" (conference) — not contradictory but different measures [msg #2, #170].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the conference report (#170) says standard DBT reduces suicidality, and #258 reports large reductions in suicidality, while in the Strasbourg RCT suicidal thoughts changed about equally in both groups (possibly due to waiting-list expectation effects, which the post mentions only regarding anxiety) [msg #170, #258, #266].
- Strasbourg limitations: waiting-list control, self-report measures, 10% comorbid BPD, expectation effects, single centre, n = 63 [msg #266].
- The authors' own DERS data (#258) are observational: no group sizes, no control group, no statistics and no exact numbers are given — only a bar chart (values above are approximate readings) — and the data pool program participants with clients in comprehensive DBT. The authors' comparisons ("visually even slightly more pronounced") are by eye [article #258].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the longread does not say how many participants were in each group or how "neurodivergent" vs "neurotypical" was determined [article #258].
- The authors' finding that awareness and clarity improve fits the Strasbourg mediation finding (reduced alexithymia drives reduced dysregulation), though the authors themselves judge clarity the slower-improving skill and strategies the key effect [article #258, msg #266].
