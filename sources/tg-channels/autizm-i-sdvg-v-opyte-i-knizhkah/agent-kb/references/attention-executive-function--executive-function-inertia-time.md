# Executive function, autistic inertia, time perception and object permanence

## What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's explanations of executive function and its breakdown in autism and ADHD, the components of attention and time perception that suffer in ADHD, the not-yet-official concept of sluggish cognitive tempo, autistic inertia (trouble starting, stopping and switching) and what autistic people themselves say helps, object permanence in relationships and in conflicts, and a study-based argument that rest alone does not repair self-organisation. According to the authors, this matters because these difficulties are often misread as laziness, indifference or insufficient effort; knowing the mechanisms lets people "немного меньше грызть себя" (bite themselves a bit less) [msg #25, #263, #284].

## Core ideas

- **Executive function is the capacity to be one's own manager**, and up to 80% of autistic people have impaired executive function; it is also impaired in other neurodevelopmental conditions such as ADHD [msg #25].
- **Sluggish cognitive tempo** describes an "internalised", passive, foggy inattention with slow processing and low energy, overlapping with ADHD and autism but without hyperactivity or impulsivity [msg #29].
- **Time perception in ADHD** is impaired in at least three components (estimation, sense/monitoring, motor timing), it is not in the diagnostic criteria, and there are no universal behavioural fixes [msg #86].
- **ADHD attention** is impaired unevenly: sustained and divided attention suffer more than selective attention, and all three neural attention mechanisms work inefficiently [msg #224].
- **Autistic inertia** is the tendency to stay in the current state — unable to start, unable to stop, and treating switching itself as a separate task; it is not laziness or depression [msg #263].
- **Emotional object permanence** problems in ADHD make absent people "drop out" emotionally; a related dimension — keeping the value of a person in mind during conflict — can be trained [msg #216, #217].
- **"Can't get organised" is several different processes**, and rest restores energy but does not teach planning, organisation or impulse control [msg #284].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Nine skills of executive function
Lena Foer defines executive function as all the skills that let us manage different aspects of life — "наша способность быть себе менеджерами" (our ability to be our own managers): arriving on time for a meeting, putting the keys in their place when coming home, and a million other everyday things. The skills listed [msg #25]:
1. Planning.
2. Organising oneself, others and space.
3. Taking on tasks and doing them.
4. Allocating time.
5. Self-control.
6. Applying consistent effort.
7. Showing flexibility.
8. Reflecting on how our own thinking works (metacognitive processes).
9. Prioritising.

### Seven types of self-regulation linked to executive function
Lena Foer gives seven main types of self-regulation tied to executive function [msg #25]:
1. **Awareness of what is happening** — managing one's own attention.
2. **Self-control** — restraining oneself when one does not want to but has to.
3. **Non-verbal working memory** — holding information in memory to navigate plans and tasks.
4. **Verbal working memory** — remembering inner speech.
5. **Emotions** — using inner dialogue and images to regulate one's emotional state.
6. **Self-motivation** — prompting oneself to act when there are no external stimuli.
7. **Planning and problem solving** — finding new approaches and solutions.

How executive function problems can look [msg #25]:
- "временнАя слепота" (time blindness) — inability to plan and keep future events in mind;
- difficulty building chains of actions toward long-term goals;
- difficulty organising work and making schedules;
- difficulty controlling emotions and impulses;
- difficulty analysing and processing information.

Because of such impairment, even household tasks like clearing a table can look — and actually be — very hard [msg #25]. (!) INCOMPLETE: the post promises follow-up posts on how executive function works, how it fails and what to do; those posts are not part of this input.

### Characteristics of sluggish cognitive tempo (SCT)
Katya Klimochkina learned about SCT at a workshop by Masha Sergeeva on ADHD diagnosis strategies and problems. SCT (ВКТ, "вялый когнитивный темп") is a concept describing a set of attention-related symptoms; it is **not yet recognised as a separate disorder**. Its characteristics [msg #29]:
1. **Daydreaming** ("мечтательность наяву") — absorption in one's own thoughts (about everything) that detaches the person from the surrounding world; the mind wanders.
2. **Slow processing** — more time may be needed to process information, answer questions or make decisions.
3. **Easily confused** — people with SCT may become confused or disoriented, especially with complex or fast-changing information; changing plans are genuinely hard.
4. **Inattention** — resembles ADHD, but in SCT inattention is more internal, marked by clouded consciousness rather than restlessness; "При ВКТ внимание «пассивно»" (in SCT attention is "passive").
5. **Low energy** — frequently reported physical and mental tiredness and lack of motivation, which may not improve after rest.
6. **Social withdrawal** — preference for solitary activities and visible social isolation.

**What SCT, autism (ASD) and ADHD share** [msg #29]:
- *Concentration difficulties*: in SCT — internalised inattention, daydreaming, mental sluggishness; in ADHD — external inattention, distractibility and trouble sustaining concentration; in ASD — variable, possibly problems with selective attention or switching attention between tasks.
- *Executive function impairment*: planning, organisation, working memory and task initiation can be impaired in all three.
- *Processing speed*: slowed cognitive processing is linked to SCT, can be seen in some people with ADHD (especially on tasks requiring sustained attention), and processing-speed differences can occur in ASD.
- *Working memory problems*: a working memory deficit is a common feature of neurodivergence; working memory is temporary storage and use of information, and it affects tasks needing memory and attention — "а это - вся наша жизнь" (and that is our whole life).
- *Attention-related symptoms*: forgetfulness, absent-mindedness and mistakes happen to everyone, but are much more frequent in SCT, ASD and ADHD.

**What distinguishes SCT** [msg #29]:
- *Sensory processing*: sensory sensitivity is common in ASD, while SCT may involve a sensitivity related more to mental processing and attention than to sensory input.
- *Less impulsivity*: unlike ADHD, SCT is usually not associated with impulsive behaviour or rash acts.
- *No hyperactivity*: SCT does not involve the hyperactivity or restlessness that mark the hyperactive-impulsive subtype of ADHD.

### Three problem components of time perception in ADHD
Time perception is defined in the post signed by Alina Safi as the subjective experience of time passing and organising one's life by an internal clock. Its peculiarity is one of the ADHD symptoms **not included in the diagnostic criteria**, yet it occurs in most people with attention deficit. A study published in 2021 identified three problem components [msg #86]:
1. **Time estimation** — estimating the time needed to complete a task or reach a goal.
2. **Time sense** — monitoring time: how much has passed and how much is left. Research on time sense showed that children with ADHD relied on an internal subjective feeling of time without checking the clock, and misjudged how much had already passed.
3. **Motor time** — the ability to estimate and control the duration of one's movements and actions; people with ADHD often predict the time of their own actions inaccurately.

The author suspects there may be more than three characteristics. There are currently no universal behavioural strategies to improve time sense; calendars, to-do lists and timers are effective apps but cannot fully solve the time-perception problem [msg #86]. ### Attention classification in ADHD
Alina Safi's post and its diagram describe attention as a complex system of several components, with different types impaired to different degrees in children with ADHD [msg #224, img #224].

**I. By mode of control** [img #224]:
- *Exogenous attention* — activated by external stimuli, happens automatically and involuntarily. In ADHD: excessive reaction to external stimuli; difficulty ignoring irrelevant stimuli; quick switching to any new stimulus.
- *Endogenous attention* — controlled voluntarily, requires effort and intention. In ADHD: difficulty concentrating on long tasks; difficulty directing attention to the needed objects; getting stuck on one task or, conversely, excessive switching.

**II. By functional type** [msg #224, img #224]:
1. *Selective attention* — focusing on relevant information while ignoring distractors. Impaired **moderately** in ADHD: easy distractibility, difficulty concentrating in noisy surroundings, inability to filter out irrelevant information.
2. *Sustained attention* — maintaining concentration on a task over time. Impaired **much more strongly**: quick fatigue from tasks, falling performance over time, missing important signals. The post's example: a child can play video games for hours but cannot do 10 minutes of homework.
3. *Divided attention* — processing several information streams at once. Works **especially poorly**: cannot do several things at once, overload from multiple stimuli, problems with prioritising; children cannot listen to the teacher and take notes simultaneously and forget the previous task after switching to a new one.
4. *Attention switching* — flexibly moving focus between tasks or stimuli. Also impaired: getting stuck on tasks (e.g. on an interesting activity), difficulty starting new tasks, chaotic aimless switching between tasks.

**III. Mechanisms of attention (per the Perceptual Template Model)** [msg #224, img #224]:
1. *Stimulus enhancement* — increasing the "brightness" of the target stimulus; improves perception when the signal is weak.
2. *External noise exclusion* — filtering and blocking irrelevant information; improves perception amid strong interference.
3. *Internal noise reduction* — lowering "noise" in the nervous system itself; improves signal-processing accuracy at all levels.

In ADHD all three mechanisms work inefficiently: important signals are weakly amplified, interference is poorly filtered, and internal noise is raised. According to the post this explains why attention problems appear in any conditions — in silence as well as with distractions [msg #224].

### Autistic inertia — starting, stopping, switching, and what helps
The channel author (who says she knows the state from her own experience) describes autistic inertia: you have a task, you know how to do it, you have the time, and still cannot move, "будто между "я хочу" и "я делаю" кто-то вынул мостик" (as if someone removed the bridge between "I want" and "I do"); or the reverse — you are absorbed and cannot stop though hungry and it is time to sleep. Both are autistic inertia. The term was coined by autistic people themselves by analogy with school physics: a body at rest tends to stay at rest and a body in motion keeps moving until an external force acts; for people it concerns states — hard to start from rest, hard to stop on the go, hard to switch [msg #263].

The source study: a team led by Elizabeth Pellicano (Communications Psychology), including autistic co-authors, analysed 501 Reddit posts on the topic with almost ten thousand comments, aiming to understand how people experience the state. The author calls it the very first step, since academia previously did not know or speak about autistic inertia [msg #263].

1. **Not being able to start.** Many posters had taken their trouble starting tasks for laziness. People sometimes cannot "найти кнопку запуска" (find the start button) even for the simplest task — from washing the floor to a beloved hobby — regardless of whether it is boring or desired. Inside it feels like a barrier, as if "примерз" (frozen) or "заперт в клетке" (locked in a cage), while fully aware, which makes one even angrier. Hardest are multi-step tasks like cooking: unclear where to begin, the brain is flooded with information, and a stupor sets in [msg #263].
2. **Not being able to stop.** Not necessarily suffering: often it is a flow state where everything goes by itself, described as uplifting and healing, a "море кайфа" (sea of bliss). But the same inability turns against people: they could not break off to eat or sleep and were absorbed in one activity for days [msg #263].
3. **Switching is a separate task.** One participant put it: you cannot go straight "от мытья посуды к стирке" (from washing dishes to laundry) — you must first go "от мытья посуды к переключению задач" (from washing dishes to switching tasks) and only then to laundry. Switching itself is a task that must be started. That is why abrupt interruption hurts autistic people so much; being yanked out of a state is described as "вылететь через лобовое стекло" (flying out through the windshield) [msg #263].
4. **It is not laziness.** Laziness is a tempting simple answer but unconvincing: procrastination does not explain why one cannot start even something genuinely interesting. Nor does it reduce to depression — flow-state absorption looks nothing like it. Inertia strikes regardless of effort: one can want badly to start and still get stuck dead [msg #263].

**What helps, according to the people themselves** [msg #263]:
- *External push*: body doubling (someone simply nearby while you do a hard task), reminders, a sense of being accountable to someone.
- *Structure*: rigid routines, schedules, breaking a big task into small steps.
- *Time for switching*: set a timer with a margin, to have time to crawl out of one state and into another.

There is no universal recipe, but knowing the phenomenon is described and somewhat studied lets one blame oneself a little less [msg #263].

### Emotional object permanence in ADHD — manifestations and causes
Katya Klimochkina defines object permanence as the ability to understand that people, objects and relationships continue to exist and matter even when physically absent or not interacting with you. It forms in infancy: "вижу маму - мама есть" (I see mum — mum exists), and if I don't see mum she "hopefully" still exists. In ADHD there may be difficulties with **emotional** object permanence, especially in relationships: when a close person is out of sight, the brain struggles to hold their emotional presence, so it is as if they no longer exist [msg #216].

**How it manifests** [msg #216]:
1. **Out of sight, out of mind** — a friend you saw regularly moves away and you "forget" them; not from rejection or actual forgetting, but because their emotional presence leaves your focus; you "stop feeling" closeness due to physical distance and feel emotionally detached when the person is not right before you.
2. **Emotions on/off depending on contact** — lots of emotion and connection in person, but once apart the feeling of connection melts. This can confuse a partner, swinging 180 degrees from involvement to detachment.
3. **Dates, signs of attention, ordinary requests fly out of the head** — not from indifference but because ADHD working memory does not hold such things long.
4. **Long-distance relationships** — if meetings are rare and everything rests on messaging, it is probably "не ваш варик" (not your option); without physical presence keeping the emotional connection is hard, you forget to write, and relationships may gradually fade.
5. **Need for frequent confirmation of feelings** — without constant assurance of being loved or remembered (since you don't always remember someone is there), and without regular tokens (the author jokes: 100 peonies or a couple of Lego sets), anxiety may arise; even in stable relationships one wants to hear it again and again. Confirmation here is about restoring emotional clarity.

**Why it happens** [msg #216]:
1. **Working memory problems** — working memory is like a "ментальный стикер" (mental sticky note) holding important information briefly until crumpled; the brain doesn't hold what it doesn't see now, so loved ones "fall out" of attention without "reminders" — not because you don't care.
2. **Context-dependent attention** — the ADHD brain scans only what's in front of it; everything else "collapses" like hundred-year-old browser tabs.
3. **Impaired time perception** — you may think it's been "only a couple of days" since you wrote, but a week has passed; past and future feel vague and distant, the brain lives "here and now".
4. **Dopamine-driven motivation** — due to dopamine imbalance the brain struggles to "switch on" without novelty or urgency; even if you want to write, it may not feel important enough to act.
5. **Emotional vulnerability and fear of rejection** — when too much time has passed, shame, guilt or anxiety that the person is angry appear, and contact is postponed further. A closed loop: "Я забыла написать, теперь мне стыдно, и я продолжаю это о т к л а д ы в а т ь." (I forgot to write, now I'm ashamed, and I keep postponing it.) A small slip (not replying on time) causes guilt that can become big GUILT and lead to avoidance; the more time passes, the harder the step to restore contact, though the desire hasn't gone.

The author says this hit close to home and that she would try to record a video message to friends to break the loop [msg #216].

### Object permanence in conflict — four strategies
Continuing Katya's post, the channel author adds another dimension: the ability to remember what a person means to us and why we value them even when angry or in conflict — holding an integrated image of the person with both positive and negative sides, which she calls critically important for healthy relationships. Impaired object permanence occurs not only in ADHD but also, e.g., in borderline personality disorder and some traumatic experiences [msg #217].

What people with impaired object permanence experience during fights [msg #217]:
- A feeling that the close person was always terrible, nothing good ever happened, nothing unites you — even after 10 years together in harmony. Psychologists call this "эмоциональной амнезией" (emotional amnesia): literally forgetting all the good amid acute negative emotion.
- Conviction that there is, was and will be no relationship at all — complete devaluation of the bond and shared history, as if it were an illusion.
- Disproportionately strong emotions — every fight perceived as a potential end of the relationship.

Why it matters: when the information that we value the person and share a history is emotionally available, reconciling is easier and conflict hurts less; a realistic view holds that conflicts are a normal part of relationships. When the relationship seems to cease existing during a fight, it is exhausting, each conflict looks like a tragedy, and a person may act impulsively and regret it — breaking up or saying cruel things that cannot be taken back [msg #217].

The four strategies [msg #217]:
1. **Regulate emotions** ("первый совет ДБТ-терапевта на все случаи жизни" — the DBT therapist's first advice for every occasion). Use whatever in-the-moment distress-reduction techniques help you, to ease your state and not worsen the conflict: leaving the situation ("time-out"), slow calm breathing, going for a run, retreating into a favourite book — anything that lets you exhale and lower the emotional temperature.
2. **Create an "emotional reminder".** If in conflicts it's hard to remember why the person matters, prepare beforehand: write in a note exactly what will likely become inaccessible in a fight — concrete moments you're grateful for, qualities you value, shared plans and dreams. You may not fully believe it mid-conflict, but a seed of doubt in your negative perception is already good.
3. **Practise mentalisation.** Try even in conflict to keep the ability to see the other's perspective — e.g. mid-fight ask yourself what your opponent thinks and feels (it will be hard). This cognitive "bridge" helps not to see the loved one as an absolute enemy.
4. **Discuss it with your partner in a calm moment.** Explain that during fights it is sometimes hard for you to hold an integrated image of the relationship, and agree on "signals" or phrases that help both of you remember the value of the relationship.

The author's claim: object permanence "это не магия, а навык" (is not magic but a skill) that improves with practice; each time you keep the value of the relationship in view during conflict, neural pathways strengthen and it gets easier over time [msg #217].

### Self-organisation difficulties as separate processes (why rest is not enough)
Katya Klimochkina challenges the idea (often held by older generations about younger ones, or by zealous perfectionist control-seekers) that someone who constantly fails at daily tasks isn't trying hard enough, and that if tired they should just rest and try again. She bases her argument on a study of cognitive rehabilitation in adults with ADHD: a program targeting attention, motivation, organisation, impulsivity and anger management, with participants randomly assigned to rehabilitation or a control group (no intervention) [msg #284].

The finding as she presents it: what looks from outside like "не могу самоорганизоваться" (can't organise myself) may consist of several different processes [msg #284]:
1. You may understand perfectly what needs doing but lose the thread of the task.
2. You may start a task but switch to something else.
3. You may make a plan but fail to hold it in mind at the needed moment.
4. Sometimes impulsive decisions and emotional reactions add on, leaving even fewer resources for ordinary life.

A universal "rest" does not necessarily solve all of these: sleep and reduced load can restore strength, but do not by themselves teach planning, organising activity or managing impulsivity. This doesn't mean rest is useless or that a person with ADHD must urgently train all skills at once; rather, it is important to distinguish **exhaustion** from **difficulties that persist even when there is more energy**. The takeaway: if ordinary life demands disproportionate effort, the question may be not only how much you rest but which specific tasks are hard for you and what help each needs [msg #284].

## Practical playbook

- For time perception, calendars, to-do lists and timers help but don't fully solve it; Alina Safi invites readers to share what works for them [msg #86].
- To reconnect when object permanence has let a contact lapse, Katya's own move is to break the shame loop with a small direct action, such as recording a video message to friends [msg #216].
- Ask, for each recurring difficulty, whether it vanishes once you are rested (exhaustion) or persists (a skill or process needing specific help) [msg #284].
