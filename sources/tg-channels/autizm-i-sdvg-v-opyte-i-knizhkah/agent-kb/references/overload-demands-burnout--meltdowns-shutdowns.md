# Meltdowns and shutdowns: triggers, differences and recovery

## What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's series "Мелтдауны и шатдауны 101" (Meltdowns and shutdowns 101) by Katya Klimochkina (with comments by Lena Foer), a video recommendation showing what a meltdown feels like, and a later channel post on what to do in the hours after a meltdown. It covers causes (overload sources and vulnerability factors), the definitions of meltdown and shutdown and how they differ, everyday coping strategies, and a recovery protocol for oneself and for close people. [msg #17, #18, #19, #58, #279]

According to the source, meltdowns and shutdowns are "то, что может случаться, когда людям с РАС становится невыносимо жить" (what can happen when life becomes unbearable for people with ASD) and are "очень интенсивные и изнуряющие переживания" (very intense and exhausting experiences). The channel's later post explains why recovery deserves attention: "Про сами мелтдауны написано порядочно, а про то, что происходит следующие несколько часов, почти ничего" (Quite a lot has been written about meltdowns themselves, but almost nothing about what happens in the following few hours). [msg #17, #279]

## Core ideas

- **Overload is the root cause.** Both meltdowns and shutdowns are reactions to stress stronger than the person can "digest"; the stress comes from sensory, social, change-related, demand-related and anxiety sources, and is made more likely by hunger, sleep loss and overwork. See *Five overload sources plus vulnerability factors*. [msg #17]
- **Meltdown is outward, shutdown is inward.** A meltdown is loss of control expressed outwardly (screams, crying, aggression); a shutdown is loss of control toward withdrawal and switching off. A shutdown looks less intense from outside but can be just as exhausting. [msg #18]
- **A meltdown is not a tantrum.** It may look like anger, hysterics, panic or anxiety, but its cause is overload and the inability to "withstand" what has piled up; it arises from a combination of sensory overload, stressors and difficulties with emotional regulation. [msg #18, #19]
- **Coping is about knowing your triggers and arranging your life around them**: identify triggers, have a safe space, agree with close people to be left alone there, use self-regulation techniques, keep a routine and announce changes, and build support. [msg #19]
- **Recovery after a meltdown is its own phase.** Exhaustion, physical symptoms, memory gaps and almost always shame follow; shame slows recovery. Physical comfort first, no decisions, food and water, rest, the familiar; analysis later. [msg #279]
- **The most useful help is prepared in advance** — a plan made on a calm day, agreed with close people, because during and right after a meltdown access to one's own thinking drops. [msg #279]
- **Frequent meltdowns are a signal about the environment**, which demands more than the person can give; this is worth taking to a specialist who understands sensory and social load. [msg #279]

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Five overload sources plus vulnerability factors
Purpose: to understand what causes meltdowns and shutdowns. Overall, the cause is overload ("перегрузка"): both are a reaction to stress greater than the person is able to process ("более сильный, чем человек способен переварить"). For a person with ASD such stress can be caused by: [msg #17]
1. **Sensory overload** — too much sensory information that is too intense *for you* (the individual threshold matters). [msg #17]
2. **Social overload** — too much communication, or communication that is too complex. [msg #17]
3. **Sudden changes in anything**, for example in plans. [msg #17]
4. **Too many demands** about what needs to be done — especially when they have poured in from different directions and are unrelated to each other. [msg #17]
5. **Strong anxiety.** [msg #17]

**Vulnerability factors** sit on top of these immediate causes: if you have not eaten, have not slept and have overworked, you are more likely to be "knocked out" ("вышибет") than when you are fed and rested. The author ironically signs this as obvious — "(всегда ваш, Кэп)" ("always yours, Captain [Obvious]"). [msg #17]

The post is illustrated with a meme [img #17]: the text "CANNOT STAND LIGHT TOUCH OR LOUD NOISE" followed by "FULL BLOWN SENSORY MELTDOWN" — illustrating that light touch or loud noise alone can tip into a full sensory meltdown. [img #17]

The later post (#19) expands two of these sources with examples: [msg #19]
- **Sensory overload, example:** imagine being in a noisy, crowded place with bright lighting and strong smells — for a person with ASD this can be overwhelming, causing anxiety and irritability. Overload can also happen at home when something goes in an unaccustomed way, e.g. someone at home abruptly turns on music or switches on all the lights. [msg #19]
- **Emotional overload:** meltdowns are linked to strong emotions that flood and carry the person away; the emotion can be any — "good" or "bad", disappointment, anger or fear — as long as it is intense and all-consuming. Many people find it hard to recognise and express their emotions, and for a person with ASD it may be even harder, "но от этого эмоций меньше не становится" (but that does not make the emotions any fewer), which sometimes leads to meltdowns as a way of discharging accumulated stress. [msg #19]

### Meltdown vs shutdown distinction
**Meltdown (мелтдаун, срыв)** — a state of loss of control during which the person, as a rule, actively expresses themselves in some way. It can include, in any combination: [msg #18]
1. Screaming, shrieking. [msg #18]
2. Crying, sobbing. [msg #18]
3. Physical aggression — undirected (e.g. breaking something), directed at oneself, or directed at another person. [msg #18]

To others meltdowns can look like outbursts of anger, tantrums ("истериками"), panic attacks or anxiety, but their cause is overload — the impossibility of "withstanding" what has piled up — while the behavioural form of expression can vary. [msg #18]

**Shutdown (шатдаун, отключение)** — also a reaction to overload and a state of loss of control over behaviour, but toward withdrawal and switching off. It can look like: [msg #18]
1. Silence and the inability to start speaking, even if you want to. [msg #18]
2. Inability to communicate in any way at all. [msg #18]
3. Retreating to a quiet dark corner, away from whatever caused the shutdown. [msg #18]
4. Inability to move from the spot, to move at all. [msg #18]
5. Lying motionless somewhere in the foetal position. [msg #18]

Mechanism offered: a shutdown may be connected with the brain not understanding how to react, or with you being unable to react the way you want because the mind has lost the ability to process information and "слился" (has dropped out / given up). [msg #18]

**Summary of the difference:** a meltdown manifests outwardly, while a shutdown consists of internal reactions and behaviour. A shutdown seems less intense from the outside, but can be just as exhausting an experience as a highly visible meltdown. [msg #18]

**Meltdown vs ordinary emotional outburst** (#19): a meltdown differs from explosive reactions — emotional outbursts can happen to anyone, whereas meltdowns in autism are caused by a combination of sensory overload, stressors and difficulties in emotional regulation. [msg #19]

### Six coping rules for meltdowns
Purpose: ideas for "hacking the system" so one can cope even with such difficult states. The author frames them as shared ideas ("Делимся идеями"). [msg #19]
1. **"Знать врага в лицо" — know the enemy by sight (identify your triggers).** Called "the first rule of Fight Club". It is very hard to do anything without knowing what you are dealing with, so identify what exactly leads to your meltdowns — specific things such as loud sounds, bright light or crowded places. How: for some time, write down what exactly might have led to each meltdown, keep a record, and build your own sensory profile (the channel promised a more detailed post on sensory profiles). [msg #19]
2. **«Я в домике» — "I'm in my little house" (a safe space).** Everyone needs a safe place where they can go and know that everything there is calm and predictable. Create a safe, quiet place where you can withdraw in difficult moments, and equip it with things that help you relax — for example noise-cancelling headphones, stress-relief toys, knitting needles, or a Switch with Mario ready to go. [msg #19]
3. **Agreement with close people to be left alone.** If circumstances allow, try to agree with close people that you will not be touched when you are in your safe place with headphones on. If a person needs a time-out to calm down, it is important to give them that opportunity. Try to get across to close people — perhaps by letting them read the post — that a time-out and a safe environment are important and vital for keeping self-control; so if you are sitting in headphones in a dark room with your eyes closed, "пусть весь мир подождет, это право стоит уважать" (let the whole world wait — this right deserves respect). [msg #19]
4. **Self-regulation techniques.** Practise techniques that work for you and help regulate your emotions — deep breathing, mindfulness, or some activities/hobbies to relieve stress and switch over. "Не недооценивайте силу хорошей кружки чая и/или прогулки за ней" (Don't underestimate the power of a good mug of tea and/or a walk to get it). [msg #19]
5. **Routine.** Stick to a daily schedule: establishing a regular routine and being notified of changes reduces anxiety. Predictability creates a sense of safety and reduces sensory overload. Both you and the people close to you need to know this. Example: an announcement like «через 5 минут выезжаем на дачу» ("we're leaving for the dacha in 5 minutes") is far from suitable for everyone — Katya adds "(я бы не поехала - Катя)" ("I wouldn't go — Katya"). So take this trait into account: plan, discuss options, and give time to "принять информацию и начать жить согласно ей" (take in the information and start living according to it). [msg #19]
6. **Seek support.** Self-described as a "vanilla comment": create a support network of understanding friends, family or professionals. Meltdowns (and not only they) are hard, so if close people matter to you, sometimes they can just be nearby — "просто побыть" (simply be there). [msg #19]

### Self-care protocol for after a meltdown (community experience)
Context from the channel (#279): there are no clinical trials testing ways of recovering after a meltdown. What exists is qualitative research in which autistic people describe their experience, a recent review of eighteen studies on communication in distress, and accumulated community experience; the post is based on these sources. [msg #279]

**What happens afterwards:** after the adrenaline surge people feel exhaustion and heaviness, sometimes a headache and muscles aching from tension. Some have a memory gap, and shame almost always comes. Shame was what autistic teenagers talked about most in interviews: "мне было стыдно, потому что из-за меня класс не мог учиться" (I was ashamed because because of me the class couldn't study). Mechanism: a meltdown happens when the load has exceeded the capacity of the nervous system, and shame lies on top as an additional burden and in turn slows recovery. [msg #279]

What helps oneself, per community experience: [msg #279]
1. **Physical comfort first**, including lowering sensory stimulation: dark, quiet, warm, a heavy blanket. [msg #279]
2. **No decisions right afterwards.** Shame and guilt may push you to act, but it is better not to make any decisions straight away: don't have it out with anyone, don't write apologies, don't quit your job, don't delete accounts. "Все это подождет до завтра" (All of this will wait until tomorrow). [msg #279]
3. **Water and food, even if you don't want them — at least a little.** Hunger and thirst after a meltdown are easy not to notice. [msg #279]
4. **Lie down — or better, sleep.** Many are helped by a horizontal position, and sleep is better still. [msg #279]
5. **The familiar and predictable** adds comfort: special interests, a series watched a hundred times, familiar music in headphones. [msg #279]
6. **Postpone the debrief.** Analysing what happened is better put off; and even when you get to it, many find it easier to analyse in writing than out loud. [msg #279]

### What a close person can do (research-based)
From the studies the channel cites: [msg #279]
1. **A calm environment.** This is the most consistent factor found in the review of eighteen studies. [msg #279]
2. **Fewer words.** Asked what good things one can say to a person in a meltdown, one participant answered: "хорошо бы не говорить ничего и дать мне говорить" (it would be good to say nothing and let me talk). [msg #279]
3. **Agree in advance rather than figuring it out in the moment** — whether to touch or not, talk or stay silent, make eye contact or not. One of the studies lists this among working strategies: a plan made for the specific person and agreed upon in advance. [msg #279]
4. **Don't leave the person alone unless they asked for it.** This is another thing worth agreeing on beforehand. [msg #279]

### What makes it worse (from teenagers' interviews)
The teenagers interviewed were completely unanimous on this. Things that make it worse: [msg #279]
1. A commanding (bossy) tone. [msg #279]
2. A mocking tone. [msg #279]
3. Shaming, e.g. «ведешь себя как маленький» ("you're acting like a little child"). [msg #279]
4. Demanding an immediate explanation. [msg #279]
5. And separately — an insistent «успокойся» ("calm down"). [msg #279]

### Six shutdown patterns described by autistic adults (2026 study)
A shutdown often follows a meltdown: the body seems to switch off, speech disappears or comes with enormous difficulty, everything slows down. In a 2026 study the authors collected how autistic adults describe this state, and six recurring patterns emerged: [msg #279]
1. Freezing. [msg #279]
2. The feeling of a "frozen computer" ("зависшего компьютера"). [msg #279]
3. Withdrawing into oneself. [msg #279]
4. "Я перестаю успевать" ("I stop keeping up"). [msg #279]
5. Survival mode. [msg #279]
6. Playing a role (acting out a part). [msg #279]
(!) INCOMPLETE: the source names the six patterns but does not explain them further, and does not give the 2026 study's citation. [msg #279]

Key warning: from the outside a shutdown is easily read as "успокоился" ("has calmed down") or "дуется" ("is sulking"), and this is "самое вредное недоразумение из возможных" (the most harmful misunderstanding possible): the person has not gone anywhere and hears everything. [msg #279]

### Post-meltdown plan made in advance
"Самое полезное - то, что сделано заранее" (The most useful thing is what is done in advance). A plan for after a meltdown is best drawn up on a calm day, not in the midst of a crisis. Its contents, per the source: [msg #279]
1. What helps. [msg #279]
2. What gets in the way. [msg #279]
3. Whom you can call. [msg #279]
4. What to say to the people around you. [msg #279]
5. Where to go. [msg #279]
Why: during a meltdown and immediately afterwards access to one's own thinking drops, and no decisions get made there. [msg #279]

If meltdowns have become frequent, it is important to examine what is happening in the external environment — where it demands more from you than you can give. This is worth taking to a specialist who understands what sensory and social load are. [msg #279]

## Detailed insights

### What sensory overload feels like from the inside
Lena Foer notes there are many videos showing the world through autistic eyes; her personal top is a video by the British National Autistic Society about how a meltdown can feel. It shows very clearly how, in sensory overload, the world loses its integrity and falls apart into separate painful stimuli that it is impossible to abstract from and that cannot be pushed into the background, the way people normally do. She invites readers to share whether the video resembles their experience. [msg #58]

### Media representation of meltdowns and shutdowns
Katya and Lena consider the series "The Good Doctor" very questionable in its representation of autism in general, but say it gives a decent idea of what meltdowns can look like (they link a clip). No shutdown example could be found from the series on YouTube. Lena's aside: this is exactly why "The Good Doctor" is a bad series — it ramps up drama until viewers burst, and ramping up drama is easiest by showing an autistic doctor incapable of masking who screams, throws medicines and sometimes almost kills someone, "but only almost", and then turns out still to be a cool savant. The implied lesson: dramatic on-screen meltdowns are overrepresented, while the quieter shutdown is invisible in popular media. Katya says she will keep looking for shutdown examples in films and series and asks readers for recommendations. [msg #18]

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The channel stresses that no clinical trials exist on ways of recovering after a meltdown; the recommendations rest on qualitative studies, one review of 18 works and community experience — self-care items are explicitly "по опыту сообщества" (from community experience), and close-person items "из исследований" (from research). [msg #279]
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the 2026 study on shutdown patterns is not cited, and its six patterns are only named. [msg #279]
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the promised detailed post on the sensory profile is not part of this topic. [msg #19]
- The vulnerability-factor point is self-labelled as obvious ("Captain Obvious"), and the support recommendation as a "vanilla comment" — the authors' own hedges. [msg #17, #19]
- Framing nuance: #18 defines shutdown as a reaction to overload in its own right, while #279 adds that shutdowns often follow a meltdown; the two are compatible but differently emphasised. [msg #18, #279]
