# Boredom, boring tasks and mindfulness for ADHD

## What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's material on boredom in neurodivergent people (ADHD and, more tentatively, autism): how it is experienced, how it is defined, and its two types. It then covers a practical method for making boring tasks doable (acceptance plus gamification, and reframing "laziness"), and finally mindfulness for ADHD: what research reportedly shows, why practice is hard with ADHD, why it nevertheless helps, and six concrete ways to adapt practices. According to the source, boredom in ADHD is not a trivial annoyance but can be felt as physical and emotional agony that drives people to flee situations, so tools for bearing boredom and training attention matter. [msg #192, #194, #200, #204, #222]

## Core ideas

- **Neurodivergent boredom is the same kind of state as neurotypical boredom, but it comes more often, lasts longer and hurts more** — often physically. [msg #192]
- **Boredom is a cognitive feeling with two forms**: apathetic (passive, "nothing matters") and agitated (restless search for stimulation). ADHD tends toward agitated boredom (executive dysfunction, novelty seeking, low error sensitivity); autistic people may experience apathetic boredom (sensory processing, sameness that is "not yours", social disconnection). [msg #194, article #194]
- **Coping should match the type**: agitated boredom needs extra stimulation, apathetic boredom needs a light push — with different hacks for ADHD and autism. [article #194]
- **Boredom is not the same as hyposensitivity**: boredom is an appraisal-plus-emotion label, hyposensitivity is weak sensory registration. [article #194]
- **Not wanting to do boring tasks is normal; accepting that a task is boring is the first step** — it lowers shame and anxiety, which makes the task easier to look at, understand and do. Adding play to the task helps further. [msg #200]
- **Mindfulness practice is reported by research to improve attention, executive functions and ADHD symptoms in children and adults**, and is described as useful for ADHD for the same reasons it is hard: what we train gets stronger. [msg #204, #222]
- **Mindfulness has to be adapted for ADHD**: shifting focus, movement, micro-practices, gamification, rotation and body doubling. [msg #222]

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Five features of neurodivergent boredom (Reddit thread summary)
Katya Klimochkina read a Reddit r/ADHD discussion ("what does boredom actually feel like for…") and posted a free summary of its key points to start a discussion in the channel. These are the experiences of thread participants, not research findings. [msg #192]
1. **Neurodivergent vs neurotypical boredom** — the two are the same in kind, but with neurodivergence boredom arises more often and lasts longer. [msg #192]
2. **Strong pain** — boredom in ADHD is often perceived as physical and emotional agony, even reaching the bodily level. People describe it as «болезненное», «разочаровывающее» или «угнетающее» ("painful", "frustrating" or "oppressive/depressing"). [msg #192]
3. **Stimulus-based relief** — many people need constant external stimulation — background music, podcasts, conversation or movement — to avoid the boredom of under-stimulation. Without it the mind can feel lost or «несвязанным» ("disconnected"), even in the most gripping situations. Klimochkina adds her own question here: at this point the line between boredom and hypostimulation seems to blur — where is the border between boredom and simple lack of stimulation? (left open for discussion). [msg #192]
4. **Restlessness and anxiety** — people with ADHD feel a need to leave a situation where boredom is felt physically and emotionally, so there is a pronounced need to immediately find a new stimulus and "escape boredom". This can feel like agitation, anger or anxiety. [msg #192]
5. **Mental chaos** — the ADHD brain is trying to stay still while moving at the speed of light, like the Flash from DC, or running "a thousand miles an hour". Silence or lack of stimulation feels overwhelming and unbearable, so boredom pushes the person to run from it as fast as they can. [msg #192]

### Two types of boredom: apathetic vs agitated
After the Reddit discussion (which turned out emotional, "inside the topic and beyond it"), Katya Klimochkina and the channel wrote a Telegraph longread with the significant points; the post #194 summarises it and the full article text is part of the source. [msg #194, article #194]
- **Definition.** Boredom is a psychological state characterised by an unsatisfied desire for stimulation, difficulty interacting with the surrounding world, and a feeling of restlessness. It often arises when a person perceives a situation as monotonous, meaningless or uninteresting — when everything seems the same or unimportant. In this framing boredom is a cognitive feeling: «мы оцениваем ситуацию как скучную, поэтому она такой для нас и становится» ("we evaluate a situation as boring, and that is why it becomes boring for us"). The practical implication of this framing is that boredom is partly a matter of appraisal, not just of the situation itself. [msg #194, article #194]
- **Apathetic boredom («апатичная»)** — a passive state in which a person feels detached, unmotivated and indifferent. Inner voice: «Ничего не хочу и на все все равно» ("I don't want anything and I don't care about anything"). [msg #194, article #194]
- **Agitated boredom («ажитационная»)** — an aroused, restless state in which a person needs stimulation and struggles hard to find an occupation that brings satisfaction. Inner voice: «Хочу чем-то заняться, но не знаю чем, вот и ищу» ("I want to do something but don't know what, so I keep searching"). [msg #194, article #194]
- **Link to ADHD and autism (the article's conclusion).** Boredom is closely tied to attention regulation. People with ADHD are especially prone to agitated boredom because of executive dysfunction and novelty seeking. Autistic people, by contrast, may experience apathetic boredom, linked to differences in sensory processing and social withdrawal. The mechanisms behind each side are spelled out in *Why boredom is frequent in ADHD* and *Why autistic people get bored* below. [msg #194, article #194]

### Boredom-coping hacks matched to boredom type and neurotype (article #194)
Purpose: the article selects hacks according to what the brain needs — extra stimulation (agitated boredom) or a light push (apathetic boredom) — and separately for ADHD and for autism. It frames them as optional: use them "if you want to cope with boredom" — «иногда поскучать топ!» ("sometimes being bored is great!"). [article #194]

**A. Agitated boredom (restlessness, seeking stimulation) — with ADHD** [article #194]
1. **Work (or spend time) together with others and add elements of fun.** Doing things in company makes them more cheerful. The author's association: the dwarfs from Snow White had a song for gathering stones — «вместе как-то повеселее» ("somehow it's more fun together"). [article #194]
2. **Bring in novelty** — for example, alternate activities every 20–30 minutes, so the brain keeps receiving something new. [article #194]
3. **More "food" for the senses** — listen to music or work in a café, i.e. raise the background sensory input. [article #194]
4. **Add physical activity** — for example, for every 20 minutes of monotonous tasks add something fast-paced (the author jokes that this may be how the Harlem Shake appeared). [article #194]
5. **Structured spontaneity** — plan random spontaneous mini-adventures for the day; e.g. a reason to visit all the places within walking distance of home or work. [article #194]

**B. Agitated boredom — with autism (ASD)** [article #194]
1. **Predictable novelty** — add something new, with a clear structure, into an already familiar routine; example: a weekly activity "try something new" every Thursday at 13:00. Novelty is supplied but its time and frame are predictable. [article #194]
2. **Regulate the deep-focus mode** — use timers and environment-control tools (noise-cancelling headphones, special lighting) to help yourself focus as fully as possible on engaging tasks. [article #194]

**C. Apathetic boredom (detachment, no motivation, feeling stuck in a loop) — with ADHD** [article #194]
1. **Use external accountability** — create a reward system to motivate yourself to start and finish tasks. [article #194]
2. **Rearrange the space**, or even change the lighting — this can stimulate motivation. [article #194]
3. **Start with small, easy tasks** — e.g. a 5-minute game, quick doodling or solving a puzzle — to raise engagement for the coming "real task". [article #194]
4. **Make a "productivity" playlist** with music that signals to the brain that «пора работу работать» ("it's time to work the work"). [article #194]
5. **Race a timer, or use an app that awards points for tasks.** [article #194]

**D. Apathetic boredom — with autism (ASD)** [article #194]
1. **Start with a calming, familiar activity** before moving on to something new. [article #194]
2. **Break tasks into predictable stages** with clear visual or written guides. [article #194]
3. **Structured low-load days** — they allow rest but include small, purposeful actions to prevent completely "dropping out of life". [article #194]

### Gamifying boring tasks
Purpose: once you have noticed you are bored (see *Acceptance formula*), do something about it by turning the chore into a game. Klimochkina's examples: [msg #200]
1. Set a timer for 5 minutes and see how many T-shirts you can fold in that time — then see how many you can fold in the next 5 minutes (competing with yourself). [msg #200]
2. Try to wash a plate with one hand. [msg #200]
3. Try to vacuum standing on one leg. [msg #200]
4. The general concept: «добавляй игру в скучные дела!» ("add play to boring tasks!"). [msg #200]

### Acceptance formula (from unpleasant task to easier task)
Starting point: «НЕ ХОТЕТЬ ДЕЛАТЬ НЕПРИЯТНЫЕ И СКУЧНЫЕ ДЕЛА - НОРМАЛЬНО!» ("NOT WANTING TO DO UNPLEASANT AND BORING TASKS IS NORMAL!"). Paradoxically, accepting that a task really is boring and uninteresting can already help you start it. Acceptance is described as the first and most important step toward awareness: once we are aware we are bored, we can do something about it (for example, the gamification exercise above). The formula is a causal chain: [msg #200]
1. An unpleasant task = stress + anxiety. [msg #200]
2. If I acknowledge that the task is unpleasant — rather than that I am somehow "lazy" — the intensity of inner shame drops, and so anxiety drops. [msg #200]
3. The lower the anxiety, the easier it is to look at the task I wanted to avoid. [msg #200]
4. The more closely I look at the task, the more I start to understand it — it becomes clearer. [msg #200]
5. The clearer the task, the better I can predict the first and the next step. [msg #200]
6. The clearer and the more predictable the steps, the easier the task is to do. [msg #200]

### Reframing "laziness"
Every time you catch yourself using the word «лень» ("laziness"), try replacing it with something else, for example: «Я сопротивляюсь тому, что мне не интересно» ("I am resisting what doesn't interest me"), followed by the question «Как я могу помочь себе сделать это дело чуть интереснее для себя?» ("How can I help myself make this task a little more interesting for me?"). This links back to the acceptance formula (naming the task as unpleasant instead of blaming oneself lowers shame) and forward to gamification. [msg #200]

### Five difficulties of mindfulness practice with ADHD
The channel author (who taught, with a colleague, a course for psychologists on mindfulness practices in dialectical behaviour therapy, DBT, whose last session covered adapting mindfulness for clients with different disorders) lists what makes mindfulness hard with ADHD: [msg #222]
1. **Difficulty with voluntary attention** — attention jumps wherever it wants, and it is unclear how to hold it on one thing such as the breath. [msg #222]
2. **Hyperfocus on the interesting, distractibility on the boring** — observing thoughts, emotions or the breath feels like holding a plank; it is hard to endure. [msg #222]
3. **Impulsivity** — once you want to get distracted, it is hard to restrain yourself. [msg #222]
4. **Intense emotional states** from which it can be hard to keep an observer's stance. [msg #222]
5. **Need for movement**, in the hyperactive type. [msg #222]

### Three reasons mindfulness helps ADHD
The author's logic: it is useful for the same reasons it is hard — «То, что мы тренируем, укрепляется» ("What we train gets stronger"). [msg #222]
1. Training voluntary attention builds more ability to steer it. [msg #222]
2. Observing impulses during practice without acting on them makes it easier to develop that same ability in everyday life. [msg #222]
3. Observing emotions lets us notice that they pass and change faster than if we tried to suppress them. [msg #222]
Summary: attention, executive function and emotional regulation can be improved by practising mindfulness. [msg #222]

### Six adaptations of mindfulness practice for ADHD
For clients with ADHD, or for yourself if you are that client. [msg #222]
1. **Practices with shifting focus of attention.** Instead of holding one object, move between objects. Example: notice two thoughts, then two bodily sensations, then two emotions. Another good example is the **54321 practice**: look around and find five objects that share some feature (for example, five pink things), four sounds, three tactile sensations, two smells and one taste — «Вуаля - попрактиковали!» ("Voilà — you've practised!"). [msg #222]
2. **Mindfulness in movement.** A classic practice is very deliberate barefoot walking on the floor, observing the sensations in the feet, separately in their different parts. Bonus level: walking on different surfaces. This addresses the need for movement. [msg #222]
3. **Micro-practices instead of agonising full-length practices.** Example: take three deep, mindful breaths in and out between activities — while waiting at a traffic light or for food in the microwave. The author: it seems trivial but helps to ground oneself and come back to the present. Also from the MBSR therapeutic approach, the **STOP practice**: stop; take a deep breath in and out; notice the world around you and your own state; consciously return to what you were doing. [msg #222]
4. **Gamification**, such as giving yourself gold stars. The author's example: at a retreat in Thailand, going to an outdoor shower in the morning, her mindfulness practice was counting how many animal species were in the shower with her (insects and lizards — "but it was fascinating"). [msg #222]
5. **Rotation of practices** — lots of different practices and searching for new ones instead of slowly withering in a single one. [msg #222]
6. **Body doubling** — it is easier to coordinate and practise with someone than to try alone. [msg #222]
Expectation-setting: one should be prepared to spend some amount of energy, inspiration and experimentation to build mindfulness into daily life. [msg #222]

## Detailed insights

### Why boredom is frequent in ADHD (article #194)
- **Attention and self-control difficulties breed boredom.** When attention or self-control is hard, one is often bored. With ADHD («сюрприз-сюрприз», "surprise, surprise") it is hard to concentrate, and doing monotonous or structured tasks is hard. Hence boredom — especially the agitated, excitable kind — which pushes the person to look for something else. Sometimes the desire for new experiences, so as not to be bored, can lead to impulsive or risky behaviour. [article #194]
- **Executive dysfunction makes tasks less interesting — a loop.** Executive dysfunction worsens difficulties with doing tasks, making them less interesting and less stimulating "the way they should be", which leads again to boredom (and to abandoning the activity). [article #194]
- **Low error sensitivity feeds the loop.** "Agitated boredom" in ADHD is linked to low sensitivity to errors: mistakes are not noticed at once and not corrected, which further hurts task performance; tasks have to be redone and drag on, which creates more problems and even more boredom («*рукалицо*», "*facepalm*"). [article #194]

### Why autistic people get bored — for different reasons (article #194)
1. **Sensory sensitivity.** It can make the environment feel overwhelming or unexciting, producing a state felt as boredom when there is too much or too little stimulation. [article #194]
2. **Sameness and predictability.** They can calm some autistic people but upset others, especially if their interests or preferences are not taken into account. This is not boredom "directly", but if sameness is "not yours", it is close to the definition of boredom. [article #194]
3. **Social disconnection** can contribute to boredom. The article adds a simple-language definition: «Скука — это когда человеку становится грустно и неинтересно» ("Boredom is when a person becomes sad and uninterested") — which can happen if the person does not communicate with other people or is not doing something interesting. [article #194]
4. **Withdrawal rather than impulsivity.** Unlike people with ADHD, autistic people may be more prone to apathetic boredom, leading to withdrawal rather than impulsivity. [article #194]

### Boredom vs hyposensitivity (under-stimulation)
The comment discussion under #192 (where Klimochkina asked where the line between boredom and lack of stimulation lies) prompted the article to distinguish the two: [msg #192, article #194]
1. **Boredom** is a cognitive and emotional state caused by lack of stimulation, often leading to restlessness or dissatisfaction. It is a feeling — a complex of emotions and mental evaluations. We, so to speak, hang this feeling as a label on a situation when we call something "boring". [article #194]
2. **Hyposensitivity, or lack of stimulation** («Гипочувствительность или недостаток стимуляции»), is a sensory-processing disorder in which a person needs a high level of sensory input to register stimulation. [article #194]
3. **Conclusion (hedged with «Пожалуй», "perhaps"):** boredom is more linked to mental appraisal and a certain detachment, while hyposensitivity concerns weak sensory perception, which can lead to seeking intense sensory experiences — for example, loud music or a spiky ball. [article #194]


### How the mindfulness-for-ADHD question came up, and what research reportedly says
The channel author "almost by accident" attended a lecture series by the Buddhist teacher and monk Mingyur Rinpoche, in which he and neuropsychologist Richard Davidson discussed distractibility: in the modern world it is harder for everyone to concentrate. As an illustration, Davidson described an experiment in which today's children were given the same concentration tests as children 50 years ago, and today's children did substantially worse. They then discussed how mindfulness and meditation help people stay in the moment and sustain attention and concentration longer. Asked whether mindfulness works for ADHD, Davidson answered that, according to research, meditation helps people with ADHD improve concentration and thereby reduces the need for pharmacotherapy. [msg #204]

Because the answer was short, the author searched for studies herself and found "a whole bunch". In most of them, not only attention improves (in all of the papers) but all executive functions work better in people with ADHD who practised mindfulness. Studies she points to: a small study on children; a somewhat larger study with very young children (5–7 years); another study; and a meta-analysis of different ways of practising mindfulness in people with ADHD of different ages. In all of them improvements were noticeable. The conclusion of the meta-analysis (the last and largest) states that "the results suggest mindfulness-based interventions led to substantial improvement compared with the control group" (author's Russian rendering: «результаты говорят о том, что интервенции, основанные на практиках осознанности, привели к существенному улучшению по сравнению с контрольной группой»). [msg #204]

What the papers found overall, per the author: [msg #204]
- Mindfulness exercises lead to greater activation of brain areas responsible for memory and attention.
- These exercises reduce the severity of ADHD symptoms.
- The effect is seen in both adults and children.
She notes the studies are fairly recent and expects the field to develop rapidly. [msg #204]

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The five features of neurodivergent boredom are a free summary of a Reddit discussion — lived-experience reports, not research. [msg #192]
- (!) UNCLEAR: Klimochkina herself asks where the border lies between boredom and hypostimulation (lack of stimulation); the source leaves it open. [msg #192]
- The autism side is tentative: #192 writes "СДВГ (и РАС?)" (ADHD (and ASD?)), and #194 says autistic people «могут испытывать» (may experience) apathetic boredom. [msg #192, #194]
- The boredom-vs-hyposensitivity distinction is offered tentatively («Пожалуй, что можно сделать вывод», "perhaps one can conclude"), and the article notes boredom need not always be fought («иногда поскучать топ!»). [article #194]
- Davidson's claim that meditation reduces the need for ADHD medication is reported second-hand from a short lecture answer; the author's own literature search reports improvements in attention, executive functions and symptoms but does not itself quantify medication reduction. [msg #204]
- The author notes the research is fairly recent; the post gives no effect sizes or sample sizes for the studies. [msg #204]
- Expect to invest effort, inspiration and experiments to integrate mindfulness into daily life. [msg #222]
