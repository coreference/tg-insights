# Anxiety, PTSD and OCD in autistic people

## What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's posts on three conditions that frequently accompany autism: post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD, including complex PTSD), anxiety (generalised anxiety and especially social anxiety) and obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD). It covers how often they occur, why autistic people are at higher risk (victimisation, sensory sensitivity, routine disruption, rumination, detail focus and, above all, masking), how these conditions look different in autistic people, how treatment and self-help are adapted, how to observe oneself for possible OCD with an adapted Florida Y-BOCS table, and how autistic people with OCD tell compulsions apart from stims and special interests. According to the authors this matters because the rates are very high (PTSD in 32–45% of autistic people, anxiety reported by 94% of autistic adults), because diagnosis is harder when emotions are hard to name, and because confusing the conditions leads to the wrong treatment — either taking away stimming as a self-regulation tool or leaving OCD untreated [msg #28, #55, #71, #283].

## Core ideas

- **Autism is a major risk factor for PTSD.** The authors give PTSD prevalence of 4–5% in the general population versus 32–45% in autistic people; autistic people both experience more traumatic events and are traumatised by events most people would not find traumatic, and their PTSD runs a heavier course (see *Why PTSD is more frequent and severe in autism*) [msg #28, #55].
- **Masking breeds anxiety.** Constant vigilance and self-adjustment to avoid judgement, with others' reactions acting as feedback that updates an inner "rule book", form a closed loop that makes social anxiety almost inevitable; masking is also named as a key driver of anxiety disorders generally (see *Masking-to-social-anxiety loop*) [msg #62, #71].
- **Autism and social anxiety can be confused and can co-exist**, but differ in eye-contact patterns, social motivation, body language and how social cues are read, and in brain activity; recognising the nuances shows the targets for change "if there is a need for them" [msg #62].
- **Anxiety in autism is very common and hard to diagnose**; clinicians lean on somatic symptoms, and both mindfulness and CBT need adapting (concrete language, more behavioural experiments) [msg #71].
- **OCD is hard to diagnose because people rarely report it**; structured self-observation with an adapted Florida Y-BOCS table helps patients notice the pattern and seek help [msg #247].
- **Stims/special interests and OCD look alike from the outside but feel clearly different from the inside**: ownership, presence of a thought, time cost, emotions, effect on anxiety, controllability and reaction to disrupted routine all differ; OCD can also "hijack" a special interest. The distinction decides the treatment [msg #283].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### What PTSD is (as defined in the channel)
Katya Klimochkina's short definition: PTSD is a disorder that can arise after experiencing a traumatic event. Its symptoms include re-experiencing the traumatic event, avoidance of triggers linked to it, negative changes in mood and thinking, and heightened arousal and emotionality [msg #28]. Lena Foer's version: PTSD is a reaction to traumatic events that arises in some people and includes intrusive thoughts, images or nightmares about the trauma, a sense of threat that begins to follow the person across different contexts, and avoidance of trauma-related triggers. If the event lasted a long time or repeated, there may be more symptoms, and the PTSD is then called complex (комплексное ПТСР) [msg #55].

### Non-standard traumatic events reported by autistic people (5 categories)
Katya Klimochkina, drawing on the National Autistic Society's professional-practice page on PTSD and autism, writes that the events autistic people themselves considered traumatic differ from the "standard list of PTSD causes". The five categories:
1. **Abandonment or loss of a close one** — e.g. a family member or a pet.
2. **Sensory experiences** — e.g. a fire alarm. Lena Foer gives the same example: because of how the autistic nervous system is built, events that are "totally fine" for most people are more likely to lead to PTSD in autistic people, such as screaming smoke detectors during a fire [msg #55].
3. **Life changes** — e.g. moving to another school, routine changes that depend on the season, unpredictability in everyday life.
4. **Difficulty recognising social cues** — e.g. misinterpretation in communication leading to misunderstandings and conflicts.
5. **Events linked to one's own mental-health problems** — e.g. psychotic experiences.
[msg #28]

### Why the risk is higher: more traumatic events
Autistic people are more likely to experience traumatic life events, particularly interpersonal trauma such as bullying, physical and sexualised violence, because difficulty understanding social signals can interfere, or they may lack strategies for how to react in ambiguous situations — or, after them, how and to whom to go for help [msg #28]. Lena Foer frames it as: autism implies problems understanding social signals, which "automatically" means a higher risk of victimisation; bullying, physical and sexualised violence happen to autistic people more often than to people in general [msg #55].

### Seven factors that raise the risk of developing or maintaining PTSD in autism
Katya Klimochkina lists the reasons for the elevated risk of PTSD symptoms developing or persisting in ASD:
1. **Hyperfocus (Гиперфокус)** — autistic people may over-concentrate on particular details of the traumatic event, which leads to intense rumination and increased distress.
2. **Sensory sensitivity** — autistic sensory sensitivity can amplify reactions to trauma-related triggers, making the sensory aspects of the memory more intense.
3. **Communication difficulties** — expressing feelings and thoughts connected with the trauma can be hard for some autistic people because of communication differences.
4. **Misinterpretation** — social difficulties may lead to misreading trauma-related social cues, causing additional stress.
5. **Rigidity** — difficulty with changes to the daily routine makes it harder to cope with the disruptions that traumatic events cause.
6. **Repetitive behaviour** — repetitive behaviour such as stimming can intensify during stress, including in response to reminders of the trauma.
7. **Limited coping strategies** — because of difficulties with emotion regulation, some autistic people have limited mechanisms for coping with PTSD symptoms.
[msg #28]

### Why PTSD is more frequent and severe in autism (Lena Foer's 3 + 3 factors)
Lena Foer calls autism "огромный фактор риска для развития ПТСР" (a huge risk factor for developing PTSD) [msg #55].

Why it happens more often:
1. **More traumatic episodes** — social-signal difficulties raise victimisation risk (bullying, physical and sexualised violence).
2. **Ordinary events become traumatic** — due to the nervous system's specifics, events that are normal for most people (sensory experiences like screaming smoke detectors) are more likely to cause PTSD.
3. **Lowered endurance** — distress from changes in routine or from a ban on stimming lowers the bar of endurance and makes it more likely that some unpleasant event will trigger PTSD.

Why it runs a heavier course ("as if this were not enough"):
1. **Emotion regulation is already difficult** even without PTSD.
2. **A general tendency to rumination** — intrusive memories of the trauma become even more intrusive, so re-experiencing symptoms may be more severe.
3. **Detailed focus processing** — autistic people focus much more on details, which are then endlessly replayed in memory in PTSD.
[msg #55]

### Two findings from PTSD therapy in autistic people
Katya Klimochkina reports two significant findings from therapy of PTSD in ASD:
1. **Identity entanglement** — people described difficulty separating which parts of their identity were linked to their underlying neurodivergence and which to their later traumas. Many developed a particular interest in trying to understand their traumatic experience, and for many it grew into a special interest.
2. **Hard-to-communicate and dismissed experience** — some found it hard to understand or explain their feelings, which made communicating and talking about their experiences difficult. Others said that when they discussed the after-effects of trauma with others, it was not taken seriously, so this experience too acquired a negative colouring.
[msg #28]

### Masking-to-social-anxiety loop (2 mechanisms)
Katya Klimochkina notes that the prevalence of social anxiety in autism reaches 50% (citing a Spectrum News feature, "Unmasking anxiety in autism") and explains why. Masking helps a person fit in but can contribute to social anxiety: masking is about vigilance and adapting one's behaviour to avoid judgement or criticism, which over time can lead to heightened social suspiciousness. Specifically autistic masking contributes to anxiety because:
1. **Masking requires developing vigilance** — careful analysis of the environment and adapting oneself to it: body posture, tone of voice, language, behaviour strategies.
2. **Others' judgement becomes feedback** — "мы совершаем «неверный шаг», воспринимаем осуждение или критику и обновляем нашу «книгу правил»" (we make a "wrong step", perceive judgement or criticism and update our "rule book").

This is a closed loop in which, the author writes, it is hard to imagine how someone could avoid developing social anxiety while constantly tracking the signals of those around them [msg #62].

Lena Foer independently names masking as another important cause of anxiety disorders in autism: the feeling of being alien, the attempt to guess others' expectations and meet them, and regular evidence that this does not work together create anxiety and tension [msg #71].

### Four behavioural differences between autism and social anxiety
Katya Klimochkina stresses these are "скорей наблюдения, чем статистика" (observations rather than statistics) but worth taking into account:
1. **Gaze avoidance** — in social anxiety, people typically first establish eye contact and then look away, reflecting a strategy of emotional avoidance. Autistic people may be slower to establish eye contact at all, because of discomfort or a lack of motivation for eye contact.
2. **Social motivation** — in social anxiety people more often signal sadness and tension when socially isolated; autistic people may (or may not) feel comfortable with social non-involvement, which reflects different levels of social motivation.
3. **Body posture and social cues** — socially anxious people may be timid and attentive to others' body language, whereas autistic people may struggle to interpret social cues intuitively or show atypical body language. For example, autistic people may stand too close to or too far from others and feel discomfort when others stand close to them; socially anxious people more often keep their distance and speak in a quiet voice.
4. **Reading body language** — socially anxious people can read social cues intuitively; autistic people may struggle with this or rely on analytical processing. Highly masking autistic people may seem skilled at interpreting body language but can be severely drained by using these decoding strategies.
[msg #62]

### Five brain-level differences between autism and social anxiety
The author contrasts the two conditions region by region (the amygdala point cites a PMC article):
1. **Amygdala activity** — ASD: hypoactivity or atypical activity patterns, leading to difficulty processing emotional signals (= hard to understand how to interact). Social anxiety: hyperactivity, especially in response to perceived social threats, contributing to excessive fear and avoidance.
2. **Prefrontal cortex functioning** — ASD: differences affecting social communication and interaction abilities. Social anxiety: changes contributing to heightened self-reflection, rumination ("мыслительной жвачке", mental chewing gum) and worry.
3. **Insula activation** — ASD: altered functioning affecting interoception and emotional processing. Social anxiety: hyperactivity, especially in response to social evaluation or anticipated rejection, contributing to physiological arousal.
4. **Default mode network** — ASD: disruptions affecting self-reflection and strategic thinking. Social anxiety: changes contributing to heightened self-focused attention and worry (spinning negative scenarios) in social contexts.
5. **Sensitivity to social stimuli** — ASD: hypersensitivity or hyposensitivity to social signals, leading to sensory overload or difficulty understanding others' emotions. Social anxiety: heightened sensitivity to social-threat signals, leading to anticipatory anxiety and avoidance of social interactions.

Conclusion: the two disorders can be confused and can co-exist; recognising the nuances lets one see the targets for change, if there is a need for change [msg #62].

### Adapting anxiety therapy for autistic people
Lena Foer: anxiety is usually treated with varieties of cognitive behavioural therapy or mindfulness trainings, but for autistic people there are nuances:
1. **Mindfulness** — it is generally recommended to adapt practices by removing the endless metaphors and making abstract language concrete.
2. **CBT** — autistic people often have difficulty revising their thoughts and finding alternative explanations, so a good option may be to put more weight on experiments and other behavioural approaches.
[msg #71]

**Diagnosis.** Diagnosing anxiety alongside ASD can be difficult: communication problems or non-verbality, and difficulty identifying and describing emotions, complicate it. Clinicians therefore look more at somatic symptoms: motor restlessness, insomnia, tremor, unexplained pain, sweating [msg #71].

### Six self-help recommendations for anxiety in autistic people
Lena Foer lists popular self-help recommendations:
1. **Research your anxiety** — study how you feel it, what causes it, what intensifies it, what helps you cope, and what seems to help in the moment but then makes it worse again.
2. **Energy-level monitoring** — periodically through the day ask yourself "я еще не устала? как я себя чувствую? не нужен ли мне сейчас перерыв?" (am I tired yet? how do I feel? do I need a break now?).
3. **Use stimming and pleasant sensory stimuli as a resource for stabilisation.**
4. **Create and carry out a plan to limit unpleasant sensory stimuli.**
5. **Visually pleasant calendars in a visible place** — to structure the day and understand what follows what.
6. **Find your own favourite relaxation practices** — from yoga and breathing exercises to walks in the forest and a big house-cleaning.
[msg #71]

### OCD self-observation homework with the adapted Florida Y-BOCS table
Purpose: OCD is, in the words of the psychiatrist author of the forwarded post (Elena Urdina, channel NO STIGMA), "один из самых сложных диагнозов" (one of the most difficult diagnoses) to diagnose and treat. People rarely seek help on their own and do not complain about symptoms even to doctors; most often the disorder becomes known in therapy, when the therapist notices a recurring structure of anxiety. In OCD this anxiety has a specific form — obsessions and compulsions. So at the slightest suspicion of OCD she often gives patients homework: observe their thoughts, rituals and reactions so that patterns can be seen together. Sometimes she uses the Florida Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Florida Y-BOCS), which patients fill in at home; it structures the observations and shows how exactly the anxiety manifests. Because there is no time in an appointment to explain the scale in detail and it often provokes questions ("а что сюда писать?", what do I write here?; "а это считается?", does this count?), she spent 10 hours (calling it real hyperfocus) making a version adapted to be understandable and comfortable for patients, for self-completion (best filled in on a computer). She shares it so that OCD is talked about more — she calls it a very widespread and complex disorder, "в тысячу раз сложнее" (a thousand times more complex) than generalised anxiety or panic disorder — and so that people can suspect OCD in themselves and seek help [msg #247].

Rule for using it: be sure to see a specialist if while filling it in you catch yourself thinking "Вау, у меня тут много плюсиков" (Wow, I have a lot of pluses here) or "Да, это на меня похоже" (Yes, this sounds like me) [msg #247].

**How the table works (from the attached spreadsheet)** [file #247]:
- Definitions given at the top: OCD is characterised by repeated intrusive thoughts, images, impulses or ideas (obsessions) that usually cause worry or distress, and repeated mental or behavioural phenomena (compulsions) accompanied by a feeling that they must be performed, either in line with the obsessions or according to certain rules, to reach a feeling that the action is "complete". Obsessions are unwanted, distressing ideas, thoughts, images or experiences that arise and repeat against one's will; they may seem senseless or unpleasant and may contradict one's own views. Compulsions are unwanted urges to perform an inappropriate act or behave strangely; if the act is not performed, anxiety usually develops, so resisting is often hard. Example obsession: recurring thoughts of physically harming one's own children while knowing perfectly well one would never do it. Example compulsion: when the thought comes, immediately taking the child to another room, closing the door, putting sharp objects and knives out of reach and counting to 30 in one's head until the anxiety subsides.
- Why diagnosis matters (per the table): accurate identification distinguishes OCD from other anxiety or affective disorders and guides treatment; CBT is named the main and best-evidenced psychotherapy for OCD, and psychiatrists use specific pharmacological protocols for OCD that differ from those for depression or generalised anxiety.
- Columns: Section and Subsection/Category (used by doctors to see in which area OCD is most expressed); Item (symptom/action), giving the general character of the manifestation; "Currently" and "Previously" (important because past symptoms can return); Examples (only for orientation — they trigger associations so you recall similar situations; if your version differs, write your own); Notes. In Notes you can mark both obsessions and compulsions, record how long each lasts (e.g. "I thought about this for 2 hours in the morning and/or resisted the urge to do this ritual for 15 minutes"), rate the severity of anxiety for each entry from 1 to 5, and add similar or individual obsessions and compulsions of your own. This helps the therapist, the doctor and you track symptom dynamics and therapy effectiveness.

**Obsession categories and items in the table** [file #247]:
1. *Harm / offence-related:* harming oneself (e.g. "what if I take a knife and cut myself"); harming others; violent or frightening images (scenes of violence, blood, accidents, loved ones suffering); insulting or humiliating someone; doing some other unpleasant act (spilling coffee on a colleague, sending a private message to a group chat); acting on an impermissible impulse (e.g. hitting a loved one); committing theft; hurting others through inattention (not replying to a message, forgetting to call one's mother); being implicated in something terrible such as robbery or arson.
2. *Contamination-related:* bodily secretions (saliva, urine, faeces); dirt or germs; harmful substances (asbestos, toxic and radioactive waste); household chemicals (detergents, solvents); animals/insects; liquid substances and waste; catching an infection and falling ill; infecting others.
3. *Sexual content:* forbidden or perverse sexual thoughts, images or urges; involving children or blood relatives; homosexual content (the table notes to take the context of orientation into account); sexual acts towards others, including aggressive ones.
4. *Hoarding/saving* (not including collecting, keeping personally meaningful items or financial saving) — e.g. fear of not buying enough food or toilet paper.
5. *Religious content:* concern with sacredness and blasphemy; excessive concern with morality, excessive judgements of right and wrong.
6. *Need for exactness or symmetry:* linked to magical thinking (belief that misfortune will happen if things are not exactly in place) and not linked to magical thinking (fear of disorder, not finding things).
7. *Miscellaneous obsessions:* need to know or remember; fear of saying certain words; fear of not saying what one should; fear of losing things; intrusive non-violent images; intrusive words, melodies, sounds; intolerance of certain sounds or noises (e.g. finger tapping, someone chewing); lucky and unlucky numbers; special significance attached to colours; superstitious fears.
8. *Health-related:* concern about illness; excessive concern with a body part or aspects of appearance (dysmorphophobia).

**Compulsion categories and items in the table** [file #247]:
1. *Cleaning and washing:* excessive or ritualised hand-washing; excessive or ritualised bathing, showering, tooth-brushing, grooming; the same for house-cleaning and washing things; special measures to prevent contamination or remove dirt (gloves, wrapping door handles in tissues, separate dishes).
2. *Compulsive checking:* locks and appliances; safety of things for others; safety of things for oneself; that nothing terrible is happening; that no mistakes were made; checking driven by health obsessions (repeatedly taking temperature, pulse, blood pressure, inspecting oneself for symptoms).
3. *Repeating rituals:* ritualised re-reading or re-writing; ritualised performance of everyday actions (going through doors, getting up from a chair).
4. *Counting / orders / hoarding:* compulsive orders (instructions given to others, e.g. "wash your hands again", "check the window"); compulsive hoarding (excluding collecting, keeping meaningful items, financial saving).
5. *Miscellaneous compulsions:* mental rituals not related to counting or checking; constant list-making; urge to talk, ask or confess; urge to touch, slap, rub; blinking or gaze rituals; measuring (not checking) to prevent harm to self, others or terrible events; eating rituals; superstitious behaviour; trichotillomania; other self-harm (scratching, biting lips or cheeks, picking skin, hitting oneself).

### Seven inside-view differences between stims/special interests and OCD
Context: the post opens with the question "Как понять, у меня особый интерес или уже симптом ОКР?" (How do I know whether it's a special interest or already an OCD symptom?) — from the outside they really look alike, but from the inside, it turned out, they differ quite distinctly. They are confused because autistic repetitive actions and OCD revolve around the same things — certainty, control, routine; both involve discomfort with surprises and both build rituals. They also often co-occur: according to a meta-analysis, OCD is present in 11.6% of autistic children and adolescents, and autism in 9.5% of children with OCD, so the question "is it one or the other" often turns out to be "all at once". The source is a very small study: Hannah Long and colleagues interviewed 15 autistic adults living with both; it is the first work asking about the differences those who know them first-hand. The seven differences:
1. **Whose is it (ownership)** — stims and interests are described as part of oneself: "когда я стимлю, это как будто подтверждает, кто я" (when I stim, it is as if it confirms who I am). OCD comes "из головы" (from the head) and feels alien, even though the person understands perfectly well that it comes from themselves. One participant: "аутизм - это арена, а ОКР - лев. Ты и так на арене, тебе и так страшно, а ОКР - это лев, который уже случился" (autism is the arena and OCD is the lion. You are already in the arena, you are already scared, and OCD is the lion that has already happened).
2. **Is there a thought** — autistic repetitions come without a thought, "просто делаю, и все" (I just do it, that's all). Behind a compulsion stands a thought with, in a participant's words, "вывернутой логикой" (twisted logic); the person recognises its irrationality but still has to do it.
3. **How much it takes** — people do not consider their stims and interests time spent: "это просто то, как я живу" (it's just how I live). OCD demands constant active management.
4. **Which emotions** — stims and interests accompany the whole spectrum: some joyful, some anxious, depending on the situation. About OCD, not one of the fifteen participants named a single positive emotion — only fear, panic and stress. As one said: "куда меня ни помести, тревога останется" (wherever you put me, the anxiety will remain).
5. **What happens to anxiety** — stims and interests reduce anxiety and help regulation. OCD feeds it, causing anxiety both before and during.
6. **Control** — a person can stop their own repetitions, even when they don't want to. A compulsion cannot be stopped, although one wants to very much.
7. **If the routine is broken** — disruption of a purely autistic routine feels "неуютно", "странно", "что-то не так" (uncomfortable, strange, something's wrong). Disruption of a routine that OCD has got into leads to a meltdown; one participant described it as "разверзается ад" (hell breaks loose).
[msg #283]

## Detailed insights

### OCD can hijack a special interest
The least obvious finding: participants described OCD as something that "подкрадывается" (creeps up) and "вцепляется" (latches onto) already existing routines and interests. A special interest can become material for the obsession: in one participant, an interest in medicine and the wish to harm no one turned into intrusive thoughts precisely about causing harm. Another described how an interest that touched a strong emotion slides into fixation: "про особые интересы обычно говорят, что это просто хорошо, но у них есть и темная сторона" (people usually say special interests are simply good, but they have a dark side too). So the same subject of passion can be both a resource and a trap, in different periods [msg #283]. This parallels the PTSD finding that for many autistic people the effort to understand their trauma grew into a special interest (see *Two findings from PTSD therapy*) [msg #28].

### Why the distinction matters for treatment
The distinction is needed to know what to do next. With stims and interests, the direction is "дайте им место" (give them room): they replenish resources and regulate. OCD is treated with exposure with ritual prevention (экспозиция с предотвращением ритуала); if this is mistakenly applied to stimming, the person loses a working self-regulation tool. The error in the other direction is costly too: obsessions get written off as autism and left without help. Sorting this out on oneself is hard, and this is a case where it is worth going to a specialist who knows about both conditions at once [msg #283]. Relatedly, Lena Foer lists a ban on stimming among the distress sources that lower endurance and make PTSD more likely [msg #55].

### Consequences of anxiety in autistic adults
Lena Foer names the main reasons for the very high anxiety numbers: the complexity of social situations, specific sensory sensitivity, changes in routines, and difficulties understanding and managing emotions — plus masking. Anxiety means more stress, more meltdowns, more burnout, more avoidance and isolation [msg #71].

## Evidence and numbers

- PTSD: 4–5% in people in general versus 32–45% among autistic people (Lena Foer) [msg #55]; Katya Klimochkina gives the general-population figure as 4–4.5% (see Caveats on her wording) [msg #28].
- Social anxiety prevalence in autism reaches 50% (Katya Klimochkina, citing a Spectrum News feature) [msg #62].
- National Autistic Society data cited by Lena Foer: 94% of autistic adults say they regularly experience anxiety; about 40–50% of autistic people (adults and children) meet criteria for some anxiety disorder; 47% of autistic people fall into the "severe disorder" category by GAD diagnostic criteria; 59% of autistic adults say their anxiety level prevents them from functioning normally [msg #71].
- OCD–autism co-occurrence (meta-analysis cited in the post): OCD in 11.6% of autistic children and adolescents; autism in 9.5% of children with OCD [msg #283].
- Long et al. interview study: 15 autistic adults with both autism and OCD; none of the 15 named a positive emotion about OCD [msg #283].
- A systematic review of 31 studies found that repetitive behaviour in the two groups overlaps noticeably in intensity and content, and that targeted comparisons are scarce — more often total scale scores are compared [msg #283].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- (!) UNCLEAR: Katya Klimochkina writes that PTSD in autistic people is "на 32-45% выше, чем у населения в целом (4-4,5%)" (32–45% higher than in the population as a whole), which reads as a relative increase, whereas Lena Foer states 32–45% as the prevalence among autistic people versus 4–5% overall. The two posts also give slightly different general-population figures (4–4.5% vs 4–5%) [msg #28, #55].
- The autism vs social-anxiety differences are explicitly "observations rather than statistics" [msg #62].
- The stims vs OCD findings come from only 15 interviewees — "скорее набор цитат конкретных людей" (rather a set of quotes from specific people), a prelude to future research; the systematic review shows the groups overlap more than textbooks suggest [msg #283].
- The Florida Y-BOCS table is a self-observation tool, not a diagnosis; its author urges seeing a specialist if many items fit [msg #247]. The channel likewise recommends a specialist who knows both autism and OCD for sorting out stims vs compulsions [msg #283].
