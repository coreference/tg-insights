## Burnout: causes, warning signs and prevention

### What this covers

This file collects everything the deep mind channel taught about burnout: what it is (the result of chronic stress), where it comes from (stress load on the nervous system, trauma, wrong mental models, work culture, loss of a sense of impact), who is most at risk, how to notice it early, and how to prevent or handle it — personally, as a founder/leader and in conversation with one's boss. It also includes the author's own episodes of near-burnout and burnout, told as lessons. According to the source, burnout has become the number-one reason people quit jobs in developed markets / the USA, affects 44% of Russians in big cities, and is pushing even 24–25-year-olds "to rehab", so neither employees nor companies can afford to ignore it [#448, #567, #860].

### Core ideas

- Burnout is the result of chronic stress. It is a complex issue tied to stress and load on the nervous system; it is not solved by one tool (a therapist or a job change) but by rebuilding the whole system from several sides. Its single root cause in the ReST framing is too long or too intense stress [#448, #695, #860].
- Trauma likely sits at the foundation. Trauma sets the nervous system into a mode where cortisol is elevated by default and even small events cause stronger stress (see *Why burnout happens*) [#695].
- Wrong mindset is the main driver, and the right mindset is medicine. Beliefs that contradict physiological reality ("hold on, don't notice the pain", hyper-responsibility) make us overwork and ignore bodily limits [#266, #860].
- Prevent before, not after. Resource-supporting tools work best introduced before burnout; rest must be spread across the year, not saved for January [#266].
- Burnout is also a systemic, cultural problem. Mainstream business culture burns out its most productive employees and assumes they can be replaced; that assumption no longer holds [#567].
- Lack of impact can matter more than workload. For many people the stronger burnout factor is feeling they cannot affect, help or change anything; feedback and gratitude are a real antidote [#1738].
- Even experts burn out. Knowing a lot about stress does not exempt you from it; the goal is to learn to be alive, not perfect, and to care for yourself with whatever resource you have [#1147].
- Conscious choice restores strength. Feeling like the author of your life, rather than someone things "happen to", is a lasting protection in hard periods [#35].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### ReST™ — three steps to prevent burnout
Purpose / context. The author presents ReST™ as a "scientifically grounded" framework for preventing burnout, developed by Dr. Bowick ("доктор Боувик") from the Hopkins institute to give busy people an orientation and some system. deep mind uses it in companies with overloaded people. The author's framing: many leaders have "heard about the tiger" many times, are used to pressure and believe they cope well with stress, yet "каждый второй из них скорее всего истощится в ближайшие годы" (every second one of them will most likely become exhausted in the coming years) and will not be able to perform as usual. Burnout is growing fast statistically and has become the number-one cause of resignations in the USA [#860].

1. Detect — recognise the symptoms. Burnout is described as "очень коварно" (very insidious): until it fully manifests, we often do not notice that "наш поезд едет в эту сторону" (our train is heading that way). So do not wait for the destination; watch along the way for physiological signs that you are overloading yourself. Symptoms and signals named: insomnia, headache, muscle tension or pain, hair loss, eczema, addictions — and any other physiological manifestations of stress. What to do: these symptoms signal the need to slow down — sleep well or push deadlines back. Caveat from the author: once symptoms are already strongly manifest, no "magic pill" will help; you must work with the cause of burnout, which is "всегда одна — слишком длительный или интенсивный стресс" (always one — too prolonged or intense stress) [#860].
2. Restore routine — fix the routine. Chronic stress can be worked with by changing your own routine. When we start to burn out, everything overloads the nervous system, so we need to create more resources to support it. Make small changes in three main spheres: how we eat, move and sleep. Do what you can in the direction of any resource for body and mind. Author's examples of swaps: instead of sweets, add fresh vegetables; instead of scrolling, read a book; instead of a Zoom meeting, go outside or meet in a café. The author notes this is simplified and that a lot of structured information on it already exists; that detail is not included in this source [#860].
3. Master mindset — fix mental models. The author's claim: we burn out first of all because of a mindset that is incorrect from a biological and physiological point of view — when our picture of the world contains beliefs and convictions that do not match physiological reality. Limiting beliefs can push us to overwork and stop us from noticing real bodily limits and needs. Personal example: the author has a mental habit of hyper-responsibility, which "хорошо раскрывается в лидерских ролях" (unfolds well in leadership roles) — whether a high corporate position or being founder of deep mind; such habits make us work without stopping and without proper rest. How to change it: "Правильный майндсет — это лекарство" (The right mindset is medicine). Psychotherapeutic work helps rebuild it by healing deep fears, insecurity or dissatisfaction. Besides therapy, one can "load" a fresh mental model — the system of self-compassion developed by American psychologist Kristin Neff on the basis of the Buddhist concept of compassion. The author's hedged opinion: it "seems" this model helps people live happier and evolutionarily replaces the rather inconvenient mindset of self-esteem [#860].

#### Five-point mindset for not burning out and staying resourced (pre-autumn)
Context. Written in August 2020 (pandemic): holiday schedules and load cycles were disrupted, people wanted to catch up on what was missed, and nobody knew whether lockdowns, remote work or children at home would return in September. The author notes that uncertainty creates background stress even when we seem used to it. His recommended approach to "what to do" questions: always start from the top level — our general understanding of the situation and attitude to it — and only then move to details. The "mindset" ("майндсет") that can help not to burn out [#266]:

1. We are in stressful circumstances and the load for most people will stay higher than usual. This is part of objective reality that exists and has to be dealt with [#266].
2. An unusually large amount of inner resources was spent before autumn. If you have not managed to restore them, you now need to guard and replenish them as attentively as possible [#266].
3. "Pull yourself together, hold on, don't notice the pain" are destructive rather than useful attitudes. We are used to coping with hardship this way ("Slavik heroes и великий народ" — Slavic heroes and a great people). But from the standpoint of psychology and biology these attitudes lead to trauma and long recovery periods; we cannot work at all in that state, and trauma cuts our effectiveness and adequacy in life overall [#266].
4. Resource-supporting tools are better introduced before burnout, not after. Start paying more attention to your state now, not in November. Examples given:
   - restore your biorhythms and start getting enough sleep;
   - start a regular practice of mindfulness and body work, or increase the current one — with the condition that these practices must help you better understand and live through emotions; otherwise they will be of low effectiveness for preventing burnout;
   - take supplements that support the brain and nervous system (the ability to relax, focus, sleep well), plus ones that reduce chronic inflammation, because stress increases it — e.g. turmeric, alpha-lipoic acid, healthy fats;
   - build habits for concentrating better and offloading memory and attention [#266].
5. A vacation will help cope with the load. Practices can support us substantially, "Но отдых ничего не заменит на самом деле" (But nothing really replaces rest). Resting in January for the whole year is a bad strategy — "Так не работает" (it doesn't work that way). So it is good to go on vacation right now, to cope better with the worsening weather of early autumn, and to plan at least one more rest window in October–November. The author's own reference point: he usually flies away every 2–3 months; at the time of writing he had been stuck in Moscow for 4 months, and a couple of days in St. Petersburg was not enough for full rest, with about 7 projects scheduled two months ahead [#266].

#### Nine anti-burnout solutions for founders
Context. A summary of the author's lecture for the R-founders community (Russian-speaking founders going global); he says it extends to almost all leadership positions [#1197].

Why founders are at risk. Founders are constantly in a risk zone of high stress load and burnout:
- a lot of responsibility, uncertainty and interaction with others = constant *emotional* overload;
- many decisions and actions = constant *cognitive* overload [#1197].

Why it matters for the team. In 89% of employees engagement drops when their leader is under strong stress (figure given without a named source). Two reasons: the position of power — a leader influences the team disproportionately strongly; and the leader's state is "первая точка управления состоянием команды" (the first control point for the team's state). To assess one's stress state, use a questionnaire — ideally regularly (every month) to know the dynamics [#1197].

The nine solutions (listed by the author without further elaboration in this post; he says much of it was discussed elsewhere on the channel and invites requests to expand):
1. A support circle — friends, coaches, all kinds of mental-health specialists.
2. Focus on one task at a time.
3. Breaks every 1.5 hours.
4. Do not load two evenings in a row with events.
5. Set aside a separate day for deep work — no calls and a minimum of communication.
6. Do regular dopamine fasting — without information and stimulation.
7. Regularly practise self-regulation skills.
8. Put your state and sleep first.
9. Use adaptogens [#1197].

(!) INCOMPLETE: the post gives the nine items as a list only; how to do each (e.g. which adaptogens, which self-regulation practices) is not explained in this source [#1197].

#### How to talk to your boss about your state
Context. A reader (a manager stuck abroad behind closed borders, isolated from the team) asked how to communicate her state to higher management. The author calls it a very important question: constructive work on one's state leads to emotions and sensations, which many leaders of the old (still widespread) paradigm deny; he calls it "достаточно примитивно" (rather primitive) to see emotions as weakness or femininity [#271].

1. Understand why bosses react badly. Bosses often try to accuse you of weakness when you complain of stress and burnout "просто от незнания как действовать в этой ситуации" (simply from not knowing how to act in this situation): you bring a problem, and they have no algorithm of action [#271].
2. Bring the problem together with a solution. Unless your boss is a well-educated HR person or an advanced practitioner of mindfulness, come with both the problem and a proposed solution, especially on this subject. Examples of solutions: "Хочу в отпуск / уволняюсь / давайте запустим какие-то практики в компании" (I want a vacation / I'm quitting / let's launch some practices in the company). The author's hedge: "Возможно, так поговорить будет легче" (perhaps this way the conversation will be easier) [#271].
3. If the case is not hopeless, use arguments ("пули в арсенал" — bullets for your arsenal):
   - Rational efficiency arguments from articles on why employees' state can no longer be ignored and why rest matters for creative ideas — these may land with rational leaders, "if they think about efficiency" [#271].
   - Competitors' examples: practically all large companies that year launched or were trying to launch some form of psychological support for employees; the author says he knows this first-hand because deep mind provides such services to businesses, and cites PwC as an example one can find online [#271].
4. Own the responsibility. The responsibility to talk about one's state and deal with it lies on our side; managers are not psychotherapists. But a modern manager must understand he works with living people and must have basic knowledge of how they function [#271].
5. If the boss ignores reality, consider a different boss. The author frames it as business competition: advanced leaders who work on themselves and their team's state attract the best people; even in Russia and Belarus there are already many such companies and top managers [#271].

#### Three questions for a "temporary" job
Context. Reader question answered by Max Rodin for A-Teams: "I found a new job, nice, good salary, adequate team, but I see it as a transit point before a job in a cool place that meets all my wishes. Is that normal? How not to burn out while waiting for the dream job?" The author's answer: the problem arises not from the place being temporary but from how we perceive it [#1641].

Mechanism. "Выгорание рождается не из-за временности, а из-за отсутствия вовлеченности" (Burnout is born not from temporariness but from the absence of engagement). When the current place seems to have no value for the future, the brain stops switching on motivation and everything turns into "временную симуляцию жизни" (a temporary simulation of life) [#1642].

The three questions to ask yourself [#1643]:
1. Autonomy: what in this job can I do my own way?
2. Competence: what specifically do I want to learn here that will be useful in the dream job?
3. Connection: with whom do I want to build relationships that will remain after this stage?

Reframe of the employment relationship. Modern career is not about eternal loyalty but about a transparent contract in which both sides understand: the company gives environment and opportunities; the employee gives energy, competence and participation [#1644].

#### Conscious-choice reframe: authorship of your life
When to use. When you feel "весь этот жизненный геморрой не для меня" (all this life hassle isn't for me) and want to quit everything — for the author this happens during too-strong conflicts, when nothing works out for a long time, or when he cannot pull himself out of operational routine [#35].

The story behind it. In 2015 the author simultaneously launched a business (a two-storey bar) and moved from the Federation Council to the leadership apparatus of the Ministry of Agriculture. The Ministry was "hell" — very tense and unpleasant. Every day after work, around 8–9 p.m., he went to the bar to solve issues there too. He slept little, ate badly, hated everything around and "носил костюм с надменной рожей" (wore a suit with an arrogant face). He says it was emotionally hard first of all because he felt he had made no choice — career and business both "happened to" him at once. The bar's building was then demolished in Moscow's campaign against unauthorised construction; he flew to New York on vacation trying to cope with depression, and after the vacation resigned from the Ministry [#35].

The lesson / method.
1. Recognise that the resignation gave back strength because it was a conscious choice: "Оно не случилось со мной, а я случился с ним" (It didn't happen to me — I happened to it) [#35].
2. Rely on the feeling of authorship of your own life from then on [#35].
3. In a difficult situation, regularly remind yourself which choice you are making — ask: "Что я выбираю: влияние на свою жизнь или спокойствие и пребывание в потоке?" (What do I choose: influence over my life, or calm and being in the flow?) [#35].
4. Either choice is good; what matters is that it is conscious and suitable for you in the current situation [#35].
5. If you choose to influence, any difficulties become natural and more organic — part of the deal you chose [#35].

#### Returning agency through feedback and gratitude
Thesis. "Обратная связь и благодарность — это не простое социальное поглаживание" (Feedback and gratitude are not mere social stroking). They are a real way to return to a person the feeling that their contribution matters and changes something — and one of the most effective antidotes to burnout and to the sense of powerlessness, which the author links both to trauma and to loss of agency "в нашем разрозненном мире" (in our fragmented world) [#1738].

Why. We often think burnout comes from the volume of load, but for many people a far more significant factor (the author links a study) is *отсутствие отдачи* — whether you can really affect, help, change anything. Adam Grant's work as cited by the author shows that when people manage to see the concrete benefit of their work for others, their engagement and effectiveness grow — "not because they were motivated, but because contact appeared with what it is all for" [#1738].

Example (Wells Fargo). A top manager at Wells Fargo collected short videos of clients telling how a loan at a normal interest rate helped them get out of debt and feel solid ground again — not "успешный успех" (success-porn), but very human stories of relief and regained control. After watching, many employees had an inner turnaround: they saw meaning instead of process — that their work was not about numbers and papers but could be support for living people. The lesson: contact with the real effect of one's work can noticeably support motivation and resilience [#1738].

How to practise it.
1. Shift focus "от данности к чудесности и необязательности всего происходящего" (from taking things as given to the wonder and non-obligatoriness of everything happening). Much of what we have exists thanks to others, however much we would like to ignore it [#1738].
2. Drop the illusion that since everyone gets a salary, everything should just work. People recreate the world each morning by investing their lives in it; each of us chooses daily to work well or badly. The author's examples: a clean stairwell and a tasty coffee before work are real contributions of other people that might not have happened [#1738].
3. When something works for you — someone's texts on Telegram, a barista's attitude, a boss's mentorship — say so. Do it in teams, friend circles and family [#1738].
4. Make it a habit: agency can be returned to communities "очень простыми действиями" (with very simple actions); you only need to set the focus and build the habit [#1738].

Benefits named. Each time we acknowledge others' merits we ourselves feel gratitude and joy; we help the phenomena and processes we need to continue; even from a purely selfish perspective it is constructive — the team shows better results and "моему любимому бариста не уйти с работы" (my favourite barista won't quit). It protects communities from stress and burnout and directly affects whether those things keep existing in our lives. Closing line: "Ничего не само собой разумеется" (Nothing is to be taken for granted) [#1738].

Author's own case. He has many times thought of closing deep mind and finding more understandable work, but each time was truly stopped by the response from others. After a month of open educational events (including with world-known figures such as Otto Scharmer and Robina Courtin), he says it is not so much the stars as the audience — presence, comments, donations, telling others about their activities — that inspires the team: "Вы добавляете нам агентности и спасаете от всегда дышащего в спину выгорания" (You add agency to us and save us from the burnout always breathing down our necks) [#1738].

### Detailed insights

#### Why burnout happens: the mechanism
- Burnout is the result of chronic stress at work; the WHO recognised it as a disease, and (as of July 2021) in Russia it was supposed to start being officially treated from the following year [#448].
- Burnout is the result of chronic stress, when cortisol is often present in our blood. At the foundation of the system there most likely lie psychological traumas: trauma creates a mode of nervous-system functioning in which cortisol is elevated by default and stress is stronger even from insignificant events [#695].
- Because it is complex and tied to the nervous system's load, burnout is not solved by a single tool or action — "пойти к терапевту или поменять работу" (go to a therapist or change jobs) — but by rebuilding the whole system from different sides. The author says almost all texts on burnout miss this [#695].
- The worst situations happen when we decide to "выдержать" (endure) fatigue and ignore simple biological reality. Then we go down the path of insensitivity and become unfeeling people — and, the author says, many managers have already become that, having long forgotten what it means to feel. He asks not to blame them: they live hard lives, adapting them for years to the consequences of psychological traumas, and many will never have access to help tools (mindfulness practices, therapy, the embodiment approach) — and hence to a body not clogged by blocks and tension, to the real depth of life in its feelings, and to the joy of simply being present. He calls this sad and worthy of compassion [#271].
- Our society bears huge costs and losses because of such archaic attitudes [#271].

#### Who is at risk
- Higher risk applies not only to certain professions (as is often said) but also to certain ages: young men, and women aged 20–35 and over 55. Stress-reduction and burnout-prevention programmes should target them first [#695].
- Burnout symptoms are observed in 44% of Russians in big cities, according to research cited in the guide by Lara Granovskaya written for the Deep mind community [#448].
- In the author's practice in Russia they see burned-out people of only 24–25, who should still be growing in responsibility and moving organisations forward but are "уже пора в рехаб" (already due for rehab) [#567].
- Among the core 27–45-year-old employees "who actually crank out the product", the author says practically nobody is left in normal shape [#567].
- Founders and leaders are a risk group because of constant emotional and cognitive overload (see *Nine anti-burnout solutions for founders*) [#1197].

#### Burnout as a cultural and systemic problem
- The author describes today's work reality for many valuable employees as a loop: lots of stress at work → "хочется все бросить, но не бросаю – выгораю – бросаю –восстанавливаюсь – цикл еще разок" (want to quit everything but don't – burn out – quit – recover – one more cycle) [#567].
- Burnout is the main reason for leaving jobs in developed markets (per an Inc. article on why people quit), and the author sees no preconditions for change in the next 5–7 years [#567].
- He questions whether owners and key managers understand this, or still believe a burned-out person will be replaced by a fresh one — and asks where that person would come from if other startups and companies have exactly the same model and culture that does not put people's state first. In the classic big-consulting model, the young replaced the ever-tired, turning the wheels of a "not very ethical" business non-stop [#567].
- Widespread stress and burnout aggravate mental problems: e.g. in the last 2 years (as of 2022) people began suffering from PTSD more than ever in history, per a linked source [#567].
- Mainstream business culture and models push talent into rehab, freelance, new forms of downshifting, or advanced companies with a caring environment. The latter are clearly too few, partly because burnout itself appeared relatively recently — in the 1970s in the West — and most do not yet know how to handle it at whole-company scale [#567].
- Consequences: employees have to master new self-regulation skills and engage more actively in their own psychological development. For companies the situation opens either the collapse of business models successful for years, or the chance to attract the best people on the market — if they can create a great environment at all levels of the organisation [#567].
- Workaholism is widely not seen as a problem: the author regularly meets "полным непонимаем проблемности трудоголизма" (complete incomprehension of workaholism as a problem) at corporate workshops; friends who tirelessly do intense sports training or fly somewhere every 2 weeks treat it as the norm — a norm he says is not alien to him either. Only in the last ~4 years has he become much more careful with boundaries and is glad when he does less instead of piling on [#1440].
- Observed trend (2020): many of the author's clients were quitting jobs despite the turbulence and the fear of not finding new ones; tolerance for load and bad working conditions is declining and people cannot keep up the pace. The author says this trend was felt even before that year, but explicitly notes he had not seen current research and his team had not conducted any [#266].

#### The inside view: an expert burning out (2024)
- The author describes starting to burn out ("Начал подгорать"): for a month he had slept more or less one night and 2–5 hours the next; he lost 4 kg in a month; one morning he was very foggy at an important work meeting he could neither cancel nor run normally, and could not get out of the loop. His response: an open-ended vacation and dopamine fasting [#1147].
- Hidden suffering. As has long been clear from inside the psychotherapeutic community, most of us look fine outside but feel bad inside. Social media and routine small talk do not reflect the real state of things. Weakness and problems — especially mental ones, still heavily stigmatised — cannot be shown, as historically that was dangerous for survival, so shame, fear and devaluation "help" us hide them from others or even from ourselves. His image: too many of us are like goods from a skilled market trader — fine on the counter, rotten at home the next day. A friend told him "По тебе и не скажешь" (you can't tell by looking at you); his Instagram photos from California were still beautiful, and he uses a "professional kit" for making himself look normal after rough nights [#1147].
- What he did during the episode: lying awake, instead of counting sheep he notices his thoughts; then he does bilateral stimulation; before a meeting he stretches; every day he spends time in nature [#1147].
- Paradox of practices. He half-jokingly thinks practices and all his knowledge of human states are "evil": without them he would have simply broken down much earlier in hard circumstances and would have had to solve the issue more fundamentally, rather than just working a bit worse. (A warning that coping skills can mask the need for fundamental change.) [#1147]
- Experts are not exempt. There is an idea that if you teach others to manage their state, you must always be in a good one. But however much you know about stress and however well you regulate it, you still experience it: "Превзойти это получается, но лишь у мертвых" (Transcending it works out — but only for the dead). So ultimately all we can learn is to be alive, not perfect, and to take better care of ourselves in different circumstances with varying amounts of mental and physical resource [#1147].
- Sleep–dopamine spiral. Poor sleep negatively affects dopamine production; a dopamine deficit can lead to bad addictive decisions (his example: binge-watching a new comedy concert or a new documentary about the 1990s), which tightens the downward spiral further [#1147].
- Dopamine fasting is claimed to restore the depleted "muscle" of decision-making, help release accumulated stress and improve sleep quality; it includes a digital detox [#1147].
- He also notes that recording practices for others requires being in a suitable state himself, which he rarely was during this period [#1147].

#### Workaholism, ambition and health: Anya's story
- The author's friend Anya, a fellow trainer in the embodiment community, always worked "как паровоз" (like a steam engine) — running many programmes, "не женщина, а кремень" (not a woman but flint). The author understands work drive, especially in helping professions where there is meaning and a wish to help others. But in 2018, when they trained together in a programme for body-awareness and facilitation specialists, the "shadow" Anya worked with was self-destruction — a part the author believes most "steam engines" have [#1440].
- At the end of 2023 Anya developed ALS — a rare incurable disease (the one Stephen Hawking had) in which a person loses the ability to move, speak and breathe independently while remaining fully conscious. The author says science knows almost nothing about ALS but has identified one pattern: it affects people whose work involves heavy load. Motor-neuron connections between brain and body break down faster than one can realise or accept it; within about a year one of the most energetic people he knows became dependent on her family around the clock, with bleak prognoses [#1440].
- Anya now catches herself thinking that this is how the Creator stopped her from working and let her spend more time with family; her words: "Болезнь принесла исцеление от амбиций, которые отвлекали меня от сути, от моего истинного пути. Сейчас фокус очень ясен. Я не спешу. Я просто живу, наслаждаясь каждым совершенным днем." (The illness brought healing from ambitions that distracted me from the essence, from my true path. Now the focus is very clear. I'm not in a hurry. I simply live, enjoying every perfect day.) [#1440]
- Author's caveat: do not look for a direct cause-and-effect link between self-destruction through workaholism and the disease — we do not know for sure whether it exists — and he wants to avoid any hint of victim-blaming ("сама виновата" — "she has herself to blame"). At the same time, he says, the link is logical and possible. Since Anya was a very conscious and self-attentive person, the story may serve as a lesson to those of us who are less attentive [#1440].
- Lesson: such stories can turn society at least slightly toward simple joys of life, slowing down and remembering that "никакой успех, никакие амбиции, не стоят того, чтобы потерять здоровье" (no success, no ambitions are worth losing your health) [#1440].
- The author also reflects that for two years he failed to formulate a message of support to Anya — perhaps from lack of courage — and finally wrote it publicly; he believes kind actions do not require knowing someone personally [#1440].

- 44% of Russians in big cities show burnout syndromes, per research cited in Lara Granovskaya's guide; WHO recognised burnout as a disease [#448].
- 89% of employees show reduced engagement when their leader is under strong stress (no source named) [#1197].
- Burnout is the number-one reason for quitting in the USA / developed markets (author citing an Inc. article and general statistics) [#567, #860].
- Higher-risk ages: young men; women 20–35 and over 55 [#695].
- Burned-out people seen as young as 24–25 in the author's Russian practice [#567].
- Adam Grant's research (as cited): seeing the concrete benefit of one's work for others increases engagement and effectiveness [#1738].
- Burnout as a phenomenon appeared in the West in the 1970s, per the author [#567].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- (!) UNCLEAR: the ReST framework is attributed to "доктор Боувик из института Хопкинса"; the exact name and institution are as given by the author and not further specified [#860].
- The author's 2020 observation that people increasingly quit despite uncertainty is explicitly anecdotal: he had not seen current research and his team had not run any [#266].
- The ALS–workload link is explicitly hedged by the author: we do not know whether a causal link exists; it is "logical and possible", and he rejects victim-blaming [#1440].
- The 89% leader-stress figure is given without a source [#1197].
- Potential conflict of interest: the author's company sells psychological support and burnout-prevention services to businesses and programmes (e.g. the Less stress course); his "competitors are doing it" argument is based on that first-hand commercial experience [#266, #271].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the Restore routine step refers to "a lot of structured information" on eating, moving and sleeping that is not given in the source; the nine founder solutions are not elaborated [#860, #1197].
- Tension noted by the author himself: his practices let him keep functioning while burning out, which he half-jokingly calls "evil" because it delays more fundamental solutions [#1147].
