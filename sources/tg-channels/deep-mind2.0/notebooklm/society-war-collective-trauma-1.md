## Collective trauma, propaganda and society

### What this covers

This topic gathers the deep mind author's analysis, written mostly from 2021 to 2026, of how collective trauma shapes Russian society: why part of the population supports the regime, believes state TV and justifies aggression; how unmourned Soviet violence and the trauma of lost greatness were turned into propaganda; how chosen trauma and collective narcissism drove a cycle of symbolic "repetition" (Crimea, the war); how constant violence produces tolerance to violence and collective dissociation; and what individuals, teams and communities can do to process rather than suppress the experience. The author treats this as important because, in his words, the echo of the tragic events of the 20th century still strongly affects Russian politics, family relationships and work interactions, and because unprocessed trauma tends to reproduce itself as new violence [#664, #828].

This part covers two essays from August and October 2026. In the first, written the day after a restaurant was blown up near the author's home in central Moscow, he argues that the four years since 2022 are a collective trauma for Russians in the literal sense. He explains that powerlessness, not the force of an event, is what makes it traumatic. He shows how public powerlessness teaches everyone watching that they are "nobody", and how the resulting detachment is a defense that is now being broken open into fear and rage. The deepest cost, in his view, is the loss of agency, which is handed down to the next generation [#1876]. In the second essay, written during a month in Japan, he reads Japan's change from a brutal empire to a society of quality, politeness and kawaii as a nation turning its power inward. He argues that this works only once there is safety, and hopes Russia can make the same turn by choice rather than through collapse [#1942].

Relation to the existing topic file. The existing file already explains trauma risk through two conditions: violence that we cannot stop or leave (#664). It also covers dissociation as a defense with costs (#1021, #1028), continuous traumatic stress and shattered basic assumptions (#1856), and Germany as the model of collective working-through (#984). These posts extend that material. The cause of trauma is narrowed down to the impossibility of influencing an event. The new idea of *public* powerlessness is added. Agency is named as the main price, and the healing task after the regime is defined as mental. Update / partial contradiction: in #1860 (summer 2026) the author felt comfortable in Russia and did not see the accumulated anger that others reported. In #1876 (August 2026) he writes that rage has become a frequent feeling he picks up from the people around him, and calls this something new. The Japan post adds a positive counter-model to the "unprocessed trauma repeats itself" and wounded-narcissism frameworks (#601, #602, #1685). Pride can rest on quality of life instead of territory, but Japan, unlike Germany, has never fully worked through its war guilt [#1876, #1942].

### Core ideas

- The current social contract rests on trauma. The author sees the public processing of trauma (comedy, interviews) as social healing and as a sign of an era "beyond Putin", because today's social contract largely holds on trauma [#452].
- The war became possible through the trauma of lost greatness plus daily propaganda. People whose self-worth was tied to the country's greatness became "nobody" when the USSR collapsed; the regime built its support on promising to return that greatness [#601, #602].
- Collective wounded narcissism never takes responsibility, sees causes only outside, attacks the values of those who "don't respect" it, and is mobilized, not weakened, by sanctions [#602].
- Propaganda works like a totalitarian sect built on narcissistic trauma. Per psychotherapist Andrey Yudin, sects recruit via "narcissistic seduction", which requires an object of hatred; leaving usually happens only when narcissistic supply breaks, and rehabilitation takes about 2 years, so there are no simple scripts for talking relatives out of propaganda [#611].
- TV and state power are attachment figures. Belief in TV against available facts is explained by attachment theory: the state occupies the place of the caregiver who ensures safety [#626].
- Unprocessed trauma repeats itself. Unhealed violence of the past leads to violence today; the state exploits past traumas, and the current war will be the defining trauma of a generation [#664, #828, #984, #1856].
- Chosen trauma and reenactment. WWII memory became a "psychological prosthesis"; slogans like «можем повторить» are group reenactment; Crimea was a "successful rehearsal", but the war broke the myth [#1685].
- Tolerance to violence / dissociation protects people from overload but lowers empathy, solidarity and self-worth, and lets violence continue; in Russia it forms a collective "glass ceiling" for inner work [#1021, #1028, #1856, #1860].
- Don't shame yourself or others for the regime's actions; shame paralyzes, while anger and concrete guilt can be turned into action [#589, #1856].
- Healing happens in communities: recognition of state violence, collective memory, witnessing, solidarity and horizontal communities where violence is not the norm; resilience must shift from "I" to "we" [#828, #853, #1856, #1860].

- Collective trauma in the literal sense. The author treats what Russians have lived through over the last four years as a collective trauma "в довольно точном, а не метафорическом смысле слова" (in a fairly precise, not metaphorical, sense of the word) [#1876].
- Powerlessness is the core of trauma. What makes an experience traumatic is the inability to influence an event, not its strength. Watching others be powerless in public teaches everyone the same lesson [#1876].
- Detachment is defense, not approval. Outsiders often read Russians' detachment as indifference or support. The author says it is a psychic defense, and it is now being broken by repeated shocks into entrenchment, sudden awakening or rage [#1876].
- The price is agency. Agency forms slowly, is destroyed quickly and is passed on to the next generation as a basic setting. The main healing work after Putin is therefore mental: restoring subjecthood and dismantling the internalized habit of authoritarianism [#1876].
- A nation's power can be redirected. Japan shows that militaristic energy does not disappear when a nation gives up conquest. It changes course into the economy, quality, ritual and aesthetics. National pride can rest on quality of life instead of the size of territory [#1942].
- Safety comes first. A constructive inward turn is possible only where there is safety. Like an organism under threat, a nation that lives in threat mode spends everything on defense, sometimes on imaginary threats that serve politicians [#1942].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Trauma of lost greatness + daily propaganda ("How trauma made the war possible")
Purpose: the author's explanation (two-part post, March 2022) of why a minority still supports the regime and justifies aggression 30 years after the USSR collapsed [#601, #602].

1. Two groups. Generations formed after the USSR collapse spent their lives trying to integrate into the global world — to build relationships of acquaintance, good-neighborliness and mutually beneficial exchange. They created the "Russian service miracle" and products like Yandex and Tinkoff, which the author says form the basis of the modern economy and genuinely earn respect for Russians. For them it is hard to support a regime that blocks development and efficiency, and impossible to support open aggression in 2022, because it destroys all of the above; so they are leaving the country or staying because of relatives and children they cannot abandon [#601].
2. The core electorate. The "ядерный электорат" (core electorate) is the last pillar of the regime; it is no longer a majority but still makes the failing, barbaric aggressive policy possible. The author estimates it — explicitly "по моим ощущениям" (by my impressions) — at 20-30% of the population: people so frightened by the Stalinist "не высовывайся" (don't stick your head out) and so unable to move into individualist thinking that they are ready to join the official agenda and mentally justify aggression. They still live by the ideology formed in the USSR, centered on "безопасность и мобилизация" (security and mobilization) [#601].
3. The answer to "why": "Травма потери величия + беспросветная пропаганда день за днем" (the trauma of lost greatness + hopeless propaganda day after day) [#601].
4. Mechanism — self-worth delegated to the collective. From childhood their self-awareness rested on slogans that saturated the country: we are a victorious country, a great country, the largest country in the world. Such slogans give a person a support in the collective order and effectively delegate thinking about big processes to the collective. They tie a person's sense of importance not to their own qualities and abilities but to the country's image in the world, Olympic victories and joy at selling gas and wheat to the whole world [#601].
5. Collapse = becoming "nobody". If in such a coordinate system the external greatness is lost, who do you become? If you have not walked the individualist path and grown something inside yourself, you become "никем" (nobody), and the meaning of life is lost. This painful collapse happened in the 1990s: people of a great country were left in poverty and disorientation after the fall of an artificial order built on the endlessly reality-simplifying ideas of Marxism [#601].
6. Compensation by a strongman myth. A short period of unstable democratization was replaced by the rule of a man from the KGB, who built all his popular support on returning that meaning — on force in the style of «будем мочить в сортирах» ("we'll wipe them out in the outhouse"). The myth of a strong invincible leader and the return of former greatness is common populist rhetoric, which in Russia became "компенсацией травмы потери величия и значимости" (compensation for the trauma of lost greatness and significance) [#601].
7. Closed door to the world. A huge number of Russians who lost the external support of greatness had few chances to fit into the global world, understand how it works and accept its values. When the world, too, refused to accept these people because of Putin's behavior on the international stage (outrageous by today's consensus), there seemed to be no inner support for self-worth left — only the desire to bring back external greatness and breathe meaning into life again [#601].
8. The missed chance. The author says Putin historically had an excellent chance to create a new national idea of development and creation, to "вписать всех нас в отношения с миром" (fit us all into relationships with the world) and build a successful civilized country with a decent standard of living on the ruins of the Soviet experiment. But, probably because of a level of consciousness built on a simple force model that is "уже не в ходу в сетевом мире" (no longer current in the networked world), he could not do it, nor understand that he is not good for Russia in this unique period. The author's phrase: he could not do it "с сознанием обиженной трехлетки" (with the consciousness of an offended three-year-old), which turned into a propaganda system about insecurity and the need to prove something to someone by force [#601].
9. Collective wounded narcissism (part 2) — see the next framework [#602].
10. Consequence: critical thinking switches off. The regime rests only on a core of voters with this narcissistic trauma of lost greatness, which generates such emotions that critical thinking turns off and does not let them see facts in an era of total transparency — e.g. the fact that one country attacked another and that this is bad [#602].
11. Forecast: sanctions mobilize them. The worse things get for them, the more the trauma of disrespect is activated and the more offended they become at the West. When the shops are empty they will say «до чего довели страну либералы и внешние враги!» ("look what the liberals and foreign enemies have done to the country!"). And when Putin inevitably passes away and a "restorer" leader appears, they will remember how good life used to be [#602].

#### Collective wounded narcissism
The author's definition: "коллективный обиженный нарциссизм: мы вам –потому что вы нам!" (collective offended narcissism: we do it to you because you did it to us!) [#602]. Its features:
1. It offers no path of development [#602].
2. It never takes responsibility for its own behavior and is never wrong [#602].
3. If something goes wrong, the cause is outside [#602].
4. Application — the anti-LGBT campaign. The author argues that the "madness" of recent years about LGBTQ comes not from latent homosexuality or an objective danger of "decay", but from a deep trauma of not being accepted by others, the deepest fear of rejection, and the narcissistic trauma of lost self-respect in the form of collective greatness. Its logic: "Раз ты меня не уважаешь – я буду атаковать все твои ценности" (since you don't respect me, I will attack all your values). He adds that these people apparently do not understand how much health and efficiency the values of tolerance to diversity bring to a society [#602].

#### How totalitarian sects and their propaganda work (Q&A with psychotherapist Andrey Yudin)
Context and purpose. In March 2022 the channel published "Что с ними не так, или как работают тоталитарные секты" (what is wrong with them, or how totalitarian sects work), a longread built from posts and comments of psychotherapist Andrey Yudin and colleagues, compiled by deep mind in question-and-answer format. The post says Yudin and colleagues describe in detail the narcissistic disorder the channel wrote about in #601 (see *Collective wounded narcissism*), which in the author's view became the foundation of the current "общественного психоза" (public psychosis). The channel calls the material impressively deep: it answers many current questions and gives an orientation on possible actions and consequences [#611]. The article's framing: the creation of totalitarian sects, "в которых участников невозможно привести в контакт с реальностью" (whose members cannot be brought into contact with reality), happens precisely on the foundation of this narcissistic trauma in society; the text covers what sects are based on, how they function and how to pull people's minds out of them [#611]. The twelve points below follow the article's order.

1. Why Yudin refuses to write "how to talk to relatives who are victims of propaganda". Over the previous three weeks this was the most frequent request from major media; he declined three such requests alone. His reason: building contact with victims of propaganda is "сложнейший навык" (a most complex skill), in difficulty somewhere between psychotherapy of acute psychoses and deprogramming members of totalitarian sects. It takes years of serious training and practice, plus an understanding of exactly how propaganda destroys cognitive skills along many lines and how to restore them. And, as with sect members and drug addicts, without the person's own will (or power over them) you will not achieve much. Therefore, for someone without such skills, any instruction boils down to three options: do not discuss politics; do not communicate at all (if that is possible); or simply minimize harm from forced communication [#611].
2. Psychotherapist Irina Katin-Yartseva: why instructions give false hope. She comments that those who cheerfully write instructions on talking with loved ones "affected by propaganda" raise hopes in vain. From materials on totalitarian sects we know that people who fall under their influence are often initially vulnerable because of a hard life situation, poverty, mental disorders, depression, PTSD, or a lack of warm connections. Being in a sect traumatizes them further: not only are their loved ones horrified by the complete "замене" (replacement) of their personality — they themselves often cannot remember what made up their personality and reality "before". Even after a person is physically taken out of a sect, the sect stays inside, occupying most of their psychic space [#611]. The hooks sects use, which form dependence:
   - a sense of community [#611];
   - euphoric practices ("романтизация героизма и вотэтовсе" — romanticizing heroism and all that) [#611];
   - pumping up a feeling of being chosen [#611];
   - intensifying splitting: only we carry light and good, everyone around is evil [#611].
   Return to life requires long systemic rehabilitation — on average about 2 years — involving specialists, other people who have left the sect, and a significant contribution from loved ones. It is literally social rehabilitation: in a sect a person unlearns how to live in society. The same applies to children from sects, who come out with complex PTSD, a catastrophically distorted reality and social maladaptation. She adds that this does not justify those who approve of crimes or are indifferent to them; it is rather about the additional difficulties that will have to be dealt with "после" (after) [#611].
3. Can sect members be lured into a "good" sect of love of humanity and brotherhood using the same methods? No. A person becomes a sect member through "нарциссическое совращение" (narcissistic seduction) — a procedure in which a person's bright feelings are artificially directed at the sect and at themselves (while the person increasingly identifies with the sect), and the darkest ones — disgust, hatred, deep resentment — at the world surrounding the sect. If narcissistic seduction succeeds, the result is a typical sect member who "со стеклянными глазами" (with glassy eyes) justifies or denies any crime of the sect and looks at all who disagree as lost, feeble-minded fellow citizens. The catch: narcissistic seduction is impossible without an object of hatred, disgust and resentment. A sect whose values come from genuine (not fake) love of humanity and brotherhood could not attract a single member, because it would have no object for narcissistic seduction. And "Обьектом ненависти может быть только человек или группа людей" (the object of hatred can only be a person or a group of people) [#611].
4. Does a person need a narcissistic or traumatized core to begin with? Short answer: yes. A reader observed that the people in her circle who fell under propaganda are very similar: they were cruel to children in one way or another (silent treatment, devaluation, physical punishment for school grades), handle arguments poorly (it matters to convince, not to hear), refuse to admit mistakes, and are emotionally superficial — "семена упали на благодатную почву" (the seeds fell on fertile soil). Yudin's fuller answer: a sect needs people with both a narcissistic and a borderline core. Narcissists are the leaders and the "костяк" (backbone) of any sect, but they also need less ambitious people as rank-and-file members. These will more often be people with a borderline core — used as a magnet binding them to the sect — and at the same time a narcissistic trauma, used as "замочная скважина" (a keyhole) through which the lock of their personality is broken and opened with a lock-pick. The answer ends: in "my circle" those who fell under it were unstable people — for example young people, older people, often those who are cognitively rather simple; their self-reflection is generally weaker, but they cannot be called directly immature compared to others [#611]. (!) UNCLEAR: this last observation stands inside Yudin's answer but reads like the questioner's or a colleague's remark; the text does not say whose circle is meant.
5. Can the object of hatred be shifted constantly without reflection inside the sect? Yes and no. Most totalitarian sects that live long enough pass through several stages of growing hatred, and the object may change from stage to stage. The regularity: the later the stage, the closer the object of hatred. Yudin's illustrative sequence: first they fought imaginary evil spirits in the subtle world; then Orthodox saints (a little more material); then the Russian Orthodox Church itself (very close); then their own "traitor" adepts caught sympathizing with the Church — the object ends up inside the sect. This does not apply to non-totalitarian long-lived sects, although a narcissistic dynamic can always be traced in them too [#611].
6. How do people get out of sects? In the overwhelming majority of cases by being "spat out" by the sect itself, once all the adept's resources and health have been taken and psychoses and suicide attempts begin. In the rarest cases relatives manage to pull the person out before that point; then special psychotherapy and social rehabilitation are used [#611].
7. Must disappointment come first? No — disappointment is the next stage. First there must be a break in what is called "нарциссическая подпитка" (narcissistic supply) — positive emotional reinforcement from other sect members. This break can happen through expulsion or simply through the sect being liquidated. Then the person usually sinks into a very severe depression, and a gradual return to normal life begins [#611].
8. Must "country X" fall apart for 70% of its population to come to their senses? Yudin says it is not quite correct to compare a country with a sect. For the stated goal, what must fall apart is rather the pyramid of power created (for example) by Ivan the Terrible and his oprichniki in the 16th century [#611].
9. Dugin: sectarianism or psychosis? A reader described Italians interviewing Dugin on sharp political topics who could guess his answers in advance, and he repeated them word for word, unembarrassed, like a mantra. Yudin: geopolitical grandiosity had earlier been discussed as a symptom of perverse narcissistic psychopathology, and Dugin's ideas (of the chosenness of the Russian people) belong to this area; in the reader's terms it is "вытесненный в бессознательное психоз, спроецированный на произвольные объекты" (a psychosis repressed into the unconscious and projected onto arbitrary objects) [#611].
10. Testimony of a former object of a sect's hatred. A reader had been an object of hatred for a sect half a year earlier; at some point she realized she was helpless before "этой махиной" (this machine), that people seemed to have gone blind, deaf and dull; she fought and fought and then admitted defeat. Her first advice "from inside": she always lost to the sect because each time she could not imagine the degree of "отмороженности" (callous ruthlessness) of the sect's leaders — their baseness, virtuoso lying, turning the truth upside down in their favor, their coldness, lack of humanity and decency, pursuit of purely their own gain, egoism. This shocked her, threw her into a stupor and confusion: she could not understand how one can be so cruel. Yudin addresses her words "Я всегда проигрывала секте, потому что каждый раз не представляла себе степень отмороженности лидеров секты" (I always lost to the sect because each time I could not imagine the degree of ruthlessness of the sect's leaders) to everyone who writes "ну у большинства-то не так уж сильно промыты мозги..." (well, most people aren't that brainwashed...) [#611]. (!) INCOMPLETE: her advice is numbered "1." but no further points are given in the article.
11. Sect vs church. To the objection "replace the word 'sect' with 'church' and try to prove the difference", Yudin answers that many confuse them, but the mechanisms are absolutely not the same. The most obvious differences: a normal church has no deception in recruitment (present in any totalitarian sect) and no "эзотерический разрыв" (esoteric gap) — the teaching inside is not different from the teaching outside [#611].
12. Can two hating "sects" merge? Three conditions. Asked whether society B, which also hates person X, could join society A in one "sect" against X, Yudin considers this unlikely. For sects to merge, three things must coincide: (1) the source of narcissistic grandiosity (the feeling of one's own exceptionalism); (2) the object of narcissistic rage (hatred and disgust); (3) the source of paranoia. Points 2 and 3 are no problem — they often coincide across societies, for example contempt for the USA combined with fear of being enslaved by the West. The problem is point 1: most society-level "sects" draw narcissistic grandiosity from cultural myths (for example "традиционные ценности" — "traditional values" — or "истинные арийцы" — "true Aryans"), and each society usually has its own. If one society hates another, that a priori means it does not recognize the other as grandiose relative to its own source of grandiosity, so they cannot be stitched into a "суперсекту" (supersect). A temporary alliance of two sects to fight a common object of hatred is, however, possible [#611].

#### Attachment theory of belief in TV and state power
Purpose: to explain why, after the Bucha and Irpin atrocities — in the most documented war in human history, with thousands of testimonies and videos — some people still support such "liberation" and say "Это не мы", "это фашисты", "это постановка" ("it wasn't us", "it's the fascists", "it's staged"). The author compares this fundamental distortion of reality to the public clouding of mind in 1940s Germany. The distorted reality is still formed by television, and belief in TV at the expense of easily available facts can be explained through attachment theory [#626].
1. TV is a projection of power and a basic attachment. "Телевизор – это проекция власти" (TV is a projection of power), and it became the basic attachment for the post-Soviet person who was deprived of a great country and a collective idea [#626].
2. Self-sealing narrative. TV keeps presenting lies as facts while reminding viewers that the lies are actually everywhere else. With such a picture of the world one does not even want to go to the internet, because there are only enemies there turning someone against us [#626].
3. TV's power is positional, not talent. Television is strong not because its staff are unusually talented, but because it occupies the place of the one who should care for you and provide safety. Right in your kitchen it tells you what is happening in the world and works "for you". It is hard to believe that at that very moment you are being used — that you are not the goal but a means of implementing someone else's policy [#626].
4. Why the shift is so hard. It requires a difficult upheaval of consciousness, because it is hard for a person even to admit that the one providing care and safety is in fact a murderer and rapist [#626].
5. Power itself is a basic attachment. When we trust it, we gain a sense of safety and connection with "our own". It is very hard to give that up, because then it would turn out that we must orient ourselves in the whole situation alone, make fateful decisions, and that there is no one here who thinks about and cares for us [#626].
6. Healing (the author's two levers): (a) that the creators of propaganda themselves become horrified by it, "ведь для многих есть предел" (since for many there is a limit); (b) giving the viewer a sense of greater safety in other places, meanings and values [#626].

#### Four past traumas exploited by the state (and a predicted fifth)
Context: during the USSR millions of good people lived through terrible events, but collectively society did not give them their due afterwards: in Russian society tragedies were never named tragedies, state crimes were not condemned, and no memorial processes were created that would have made a repetition of inhumanity impossible. Psychologists, neurophysiologists and even historians who study trauma say "в один голос" (with one voice) that unprocessed traumatic material tends to reproduce itself through repetition of similar external events — unhealed violence of the past leads to violence today. A traumatized person typically feels isolation and lack of safety, outbursts of anger, resentment and aggression; without work on awareness and healing, their thinking becomes increasingly rigid with age, making it harder to understand the new and reducing the capacity for trusting partnership relationships. For these reasons the past traumas are maximally exploited by the state and create opportunities for the current politics [#664]:
1. War trauma makes it possible to fight again if people are shown "fascists" [#664].
2. Trauma of the Gulag, repressions and camps makes state arbitrariness and any state policy possible: people know at the level of feeling that the state will act lawlessly and nothing will happen to it [#664].
3. Trauma of domestic violence leads to tolerance of violence in society as a whole; violence and powerlessness seem normal [#664].
4. Trauma of loss of meaning after the USSR collapse, plus the impossibility for huge numbers of people to really integrate into the modern world, easily turns into wounded narcissism; with propaganda repeated for years it demands revenge (see *Trauma of lost greatness*) [#664].
5. Predicted fifth point: the author says one can predict with high accuracy that the current war will become the main traumatizing event for a whole generation [#664].

#### Two conditions that raise the risk of trauma
The author's rule: the risk of trauma increases when [#664]:
1. What is happening deeply displeases us and is in fact emotional or physical violence, even if we do not call it that in the moment [#664].
2. We cannot stop it and really exit the situation [#664].

#### Process, but not suppress
The author's "most important thing about the psychology of today's events" (May 2022). To step into a healthy future, a big job of awareness about all the past pain of our loved ones lies on us now; that pain is not quite past, since it so strongly affects the present, and now it all lies on the surface and is more visible than before. He recommends relying on trauma science, which was unavailable to past generations, and finding a personal strategy "process, but not suppress" — somehow "проживать и переживать" (living through and experiencing) unbearable events rather than suppressing them [#664]. The same principle is applied to communities in #853 (communities that help members live through rather than suppress stress) [#853].

#### Negative consequences of dissociation ("tolerance to violence")
Phenomenon. When people constantly face terrible events, mental adaptation and defense mechanisms can make them perceive these events as a kind of norm. Cynicism grows and forms of alienation from what is happening appear. Typical phrases: "Я к этому привык", "Этого следовало ожидать", "Это ужасно, но меня не трогает" ("I've got used to it", "that was to be expected", "it's terrible but it doesn't touch me"), or people simply "skip over" traumatic events. Others around may seem too sensitive — panicking, "weak" — or one may feel irritation at psychologists and deep mind for paying excessive attention to events when one should just work and move on. The author calls all this absolutely natural reactions to oversaturation with heavy information or to the helplessness that arises in especially painful situations, and asks whether it is characteristic of the post-Soviet space [#1021].
Function. This "черствость" (callousness), or dissociation, protects a person from excessive emotional stress, keeps psychological balance under traumatic conditions, and can temporarily help avoid powerlessness or despair so one can keep functioning in daily life. The author: "Cтоит быть ей благодарным! Она здесь, чтобы нам помочь!" (one should be grateful to it — it is here to help us) [#1021].
Cost. Dissociation lowers empathy, because alienation from one's own emotions that naturally arise on contact with violence makes a person less able to perceive them in oneself and in other people. That makes us less vital, creative and resilient and creates a specific worldview in which a person is isolated from others — every man for himself. In this sense, the author says, most siloviki (security officials) have long looked at reality in a particular way and are capable only of crude solutions [#1021]. The three negative consequences:
1. Reduced social solidarity. Empathy and compassion are key aspects of cooperation in society; their loss can lead to greater isolation and less personal and social resilience and health [#1021].
2. Spread of violence and intolerance. Lack of empathy can contribute to further growth of violence, since people become less inclined to resist for the sake of justice — often observed in contexts close to the body, at home and at work [#1021].
3. Lowered self-worth. Alienating part of the emotional spectrum reduces self-confidence and the sense of one's own value, undermining overall success and well-being [#1021].
Recommendations. For these reasons the author has recommended not neglecting therapeutic dialogue even when "все нормально" (everything is fine), while stressing the full legitimacy of not following his advice and finding one's own strategies — "Никто лучше нас не знает как нам жить" (nobody knows better than us how we should live). Besides the commonly mentioned therapy, self-help practices and personal boundaries, he adds a separate recommendation: study the nature of trauma — scientific information about trauma helps increase resilience and prevent loss of empathy, which is why the channel published many articles on psychological trauma [#1021].

#### Chosen trauma and reenactment cycle (post-Soviet Russia through trauma theory)
Post written on day 1418 of the war (January 2026): "Когда миф больше не лечит" (when the myth no longer heals) [#1685].
- No switch dates, but symbolic oversaturation. It is tempting to think history has switch dates on which society suddenly sees things anew; at the individual level we know it doesn't work like that, nor with collective trauma. But there are moments of symbolic oversaturation when the old myth stops performing its function of anesthesia and regulation — it does not necessarily collapse but starts to malfunction. The author thinks society is at such a point now [#1685].
- The overall reading. Seen through modern trauma theory, Russia's post-Soviet history looks not like linear degradation or evil intent (although it contains very concrete decisions by concrete people) but like a long, poorly processed traumatic cycle in which loss, shame and powerlessness were again and again replaced by grandiosity and repetition [#1685].
1. Collective identity threat. After the USSR collapse what social psychology calls a collective identity threat occurred. Not just a political regime fell — a large frame of meaning disappeared (more precisely, disappeared over decades), the frame in which one could endure suffering, deprivation and one's own limitation. The loss of status, future and symbolic greatness was left without the possibility of being mourned [#1685].
2. WWII memory as a psychological prosthesis. At this point the memory of the Great Patriotic War became less history than a "психологическим протезом" (psychological prosthesis): a ready narrative giving everything at once — moral rightness, a sacralized sacrifice and a simple picture of the world in which we are the winners, on the side of good. The problem is not the memory itself but that it gradually became the only source of collective legitimacy, for the state and for the identity of many people [#1685].
3. Chosen trauma (Vamik Volkan). In analytic group theory, Volkan's concept of chosen trauma describes a past that is not processed but kept as an emotionally charged core of identity: "Это не просто память о боли, а боль, превращённая в право" (not just memory of pain, but pain turned into a right) — a right to exceptionalism, aggression, moral superiority [#1685].
4. Reenactment. Slogans like «можем повторить» ("we can do it again") can be seen as almost clinically pure examples of what Volkan calls group-level reenactment: the trauma is not mourned but played out again, because that is how it once gave a sense of strength and meaning [#1685].
5. 2014 as a successful rehearsal. The events of 2014 can be seen as a moment when this symbolic mechanism temporarily worked: for a significant part of the population it gave a quick effect of unification, removed anxiety and shame, and restored a sense of rightness. Psychologically, "травма временно полечилась повторением" (the trauma was temporarily treated by repetition) [#1685].
6. Why the effect fades — collective narcissism (Agnieszka Golec de Zavala and related work). This research is not about healthy pride but about a fragile sense of greatness that depends on external recognition and is constantly accompanied by a feeling of humiliation. In such systems symbolic victories give a sharp rise in affect, but it burns out quickly and demands ever stronger stimuli. Therefore Crimea gave an effect but could not become a stable solution; repetition demanded more, otherwise the question of legitimacy in its various planes would have come back on the agenda [#1685].
7. Limits of repetition. Repetition is not infinite; it requires ever more violence against reality, and at some point a gap appears between myth and actual experience that symbols can no longer close [#1685].
8. The war as that gap. The war seems to have become exactly such a gap. It did not reproduce the past scenario and thus struck not only political calculations but the very psychic construction on which collective identity rested for decades: instead of a quick victory — protraction; instead of a sacred feat — a feeling of viscosity, exhaustion and incompetence; instead of pride — poorly articulated shame [#1685].
9. Symbolic overheating. That the war has lasted longer than the Great Patriotic War is not a point of sudden insight, but an important moment of symbolic overheating [#1685].

#### Analysis of witnessed state violence and three practices against isolation and silence
Case (July 2026, trigger warning: violence against children): on the evening of July 3 a raid was carried out at Kitay-gorod. Teenagers walking in the streets, including minors, were brutally detained — for hairstyles, clothes, for waving at the police car. They were then beaten and tasered; in one episode a detainee was forced under threat to beat another detainee; on release they were asked not to tell anyone. The author names his own reaction — horror, hatred, "и знакомое желание пролистать дальше" (and the familiar urge to scroll on) — and instead chooses an action: to become a witness who will not forget, and to analyze the event through the lens of trauma [#1856]. The post's photo shows memorial plaques on a building wall (each headed "here lived", each with a name, profession, birth year, arrest year in 1937-1938, execution date and rehabilitation year), with several neighboring plaques torn off, leaving only scraps of tape [#1856].

Why it happens [#1856]:
1. Warped mourning. Cultural historian Alexander Etkind called this state of society «кривым горем» (warped mourning): 20th-century terror is not recognized or mourned, and the past — for the collective psyche too — becomes past only through grieving. "Неотгореванное остается настоящим и требует повторения" (the unmourned remains present and demands repetition) [#1856].
2. A function in holding power. Arbitrariness that seizes people not for an act but for appearance, for freedom of expression, is "не сбой системы, а сообщение" (not a system failure but a message): there are no rules that can protect, only force. Forcing people to beat their own makes them not mere witnesses but strikes at what resists arbitrariness — solidarity. The request for silence is "textbook": Judith Herman, one of the first trauma researchers, showed that structural violence needs *silence* from the victim and *mere inaction* from everyone else [#1856].
3. Why children. The fear on which such a system rests must be inherited; a raid on those who have not learned to be afraid is an attempt to force fear back into the bodies of the next generation [#1856].

What it does to us [#1856]:
1. Shattered basic assumptions. Trauma breaks basic assumptions like «если я ничего не сделал, мне ничего не будет» (if I did nothing, nothing will happen to me). When one can be seized for a hairstyle, the psyche concludes that behavior cannot protect, and puts the body into permanent guard mode, which we later call anxiety or insomnia [#1856].
2. Moral injury in those forced to beat: what hurts is not what was done to you but what you were made to do. This shame is carried silently — exactly as is convenient for the perpetrator [#1856].
3. Continuous traumatic stress for everyone else — a term from psychologists in apartheid-era South Africa, when the "post" in PTSD never arrives. The nervous system responds with acceleration (overheating) or freeze, and on top a meaning-level defense arises — «сами виноваты» (they brought it on themselves). Blaming the victim is easier than admitting the arbitrariness of misfortune. A society that massively chooses this defense loses empathy, and a society without empathy becomes an environment where violence can last indefinitely [#1856].

What to do. Trauma lives in isolation and silence and heals through safety, mourning and restoration of connections; at the scale of society this is called witnessing, collective memory and solidarity. Three practices that counter isolation and silence and are applicable right now, in order [#1856]:
1. First, diagnose your state and work with it. Overheating and freeze require opposite things: overheating needs discharge and a long exhale; freeze needs warmth, contact and gentle activation. Therefore a universal "calm down" works nowhere [#1856].
2. Then a container instead of the feed. Rereading in circles is the psyche's attempt to close a loop that scrolling cannot close; so the topic is given a set time — one window a day of 15 minutes, not in the morning and not at night [#1856].
3. Then turn shame into guilt, and guilt into one action. Shame ("I live where this is possible and I stay silent") is total and paralyzing; guilt is concrete and can become action — write, tell, help, support [#1856].
Rule: it is useless to argue with facts with those who think the children are to blame — there one is dealing with a deaf psychological defense, not with consciousness [#1856].
Conclusion. The author does not know when the circle will open, but knows it rests on isolation and silence. The teenagers who told their stories performed a simultaneously therapeutic and political act: they gave the event back its name and denied violence its main demand. The least we can do is remain those who see and name: "травма сильнее всего там, где ее замалчивают" (trauma is strongest where it is hushed up) [#1856].

#### Japan's inward turn of national power
Purpose: the author's account of how a country that "80 лет назад выжигала все пространство вокруг себя" (80 years ago was burning out all the space around itself) became the country of Hello Kitty, silent trains, perfectly clean streets and politeness. He calls it one of the most radical transformations a nation has ever undergone [#1942].
1. The starting point was brutal. The empire Japan built in the first half of the 20th century was cruel in the most literal sense: the Nanjing massacre, experiments on living people in Unit 731, and hundreds of thousands of Korean and other women taken into military brothels. The author notes that the Japanese still speak about this reluctantly. The war took many millions of lives across Asia and about 3 million Japanese lives [#1942].
2. It began with catastrophe, not well-being. The author stresses that the transformation did not start because people inside the country were doing well. Well-being came later. First came catastrophe: Tokyo burned down in one March night in 1945, then Hiroshima and Nagasaki, capitulation and 7 years of American occupation. It was defeat that turned Japanese imperialism, militarism and harshness around, "и в этом нет ничего романтичного" (and there is nothing romantic about it) [#1942].
3. No revanche: renunciation became strategy. Many defeated peoples nurtured revanche for decades and then repeated the same mistakes of war and populism. Japan did something else. The renunciation of war was written into the 1947 constitution. Prime Minister Shigeru Yoshida made it a national strategy: the USA provides the country's security, and all the nation's energy goes into rebuilding infrastructure and the economy. Defense spending stayed around one percent of GDP for decades [#1942].
4. Energy changed course instead of vanishing. Without its usual outlet, militaristic energy "не исчезла, а сменила русло" (did not disappear, but changed its riverbed). From the mid-1950s to the early 1970s the Japanese economy grew about 10% a year, and by 1968 Japan was the world's second-largest economy. The same samurai perfectionism, capacity for self-denial and discipline that once served the army moved into Toyota's workshops and Sony's labs. It also went into the kaizen philosophy of endlessly improving small things, which later spread widely through the world's best business schools. The author feels this every day when he watches a station worker bow to a departing train: "колоссальная собранность целого народа, направленная на качество совместного бытия" (the colossal composure of a whole people, directed at the quality of shared existence), not at conquest and military frenzy [#1942].
5. Kawaii as a collective refusal to be menacing. Back in the 11th century, the court lady Sei Shonagon compiled lists of touching and cute things. The real explosion came in the 1970s, when Japanese schoolgirls began writing in a round childlike hand and Hello Kitty appeared in shops. Anthropologist Sharon Kinsella reads this cult of cuteness as a quiet protest against rigid hierarchy and heavy adult duty, "как коллективный отказ быть грозными" (as a collective refusal to be menacing) [#1942].
6. A repeated cultural pattern. Japan has done this before. After a century of civil wars came two and a half centuries of peace in the Tokugawa era. The samurai became officials, and warlike spirit dissolved into the tea ceremony, haiku and woodblock prints. The author concludes that this culture has a rare ability to fold aggression into ritual and aesthetics [#1942].
7. The shadow. Energy turned inward has its own shadow: deaths from overwork, about one and a half million hikikomori recluses, and a birth rate that has fallen to one of the lowest in the world. "Внутрь можно не только заботиться, но и давить" (inward one can not only care, but also press). Most importantly, unlike Germany, Japan never fully worked through its war guilt. Disputes over school textbooks and Yasukuni have gone on for almost eighty years. The author says the national transformation is unfinished and far from linear, and that this attracts rather than disappoints him [#1942].

- How to notice it. Because of active repression (вытеснение) it is hard to recognize, but a collective traumatic experience common to most Russian citizens exists and is being lived through now. It can be noticed by the change in the sense of speed and rhythm of time over the last 2-3 years or since the war began: time has merged into a single lump without the former division into periods [#846].
- Ripples. It is too early to say into which fears, humiliation, shame and other collective feelings the experience will form. But the ripples from the epicenter already cause accumulating fatigue, increasing rigidity and lower employee productivity in the Russian context [#846].
- The epicenter. People involved in the fighting and their close circles face, and will face, falling out of society through addictions, growing aggression and violence, PTSD and other mental disorders. Major work lies ahead on recognizing crimes and purely therapeutic work to reduce these consequences [#846].
- A positive process too. The author argues that just as world war strongly advanced the feminist agenda, war and the crisis of power can become the basis for a healthy transformation of social norms that seemed carved in stone. The loud, obvious failure of the authoritarian model is already starting to destroy the model of toxic masculinity and brutal machismo on which communication and power in many organizations — and personalist autocracy itself — are still largely built [#846].
- Labor-market evidence (as reported). Labor-market experts speak of a surge in demand for new leadership styles: shareholders more often want women in top positions, because "сейчас важнее не хард, а софт навыки. Одна система ценностей. Сплоченность людей. А женщина это лучше чувствует. И лучше цементирует команду" (now soft skills matter more than hard ones; one value system; cohesion of people; a woman feels this better and cements the team better). The author flags this as "сексизм и предрассудок, но он не мой" (sexism and prejudice, but not mine) [#846].
- Forecast. This can drive a further wave of cultural transformation in companies toward self-regulation skills, non-directive leadership, transparent and open culture and nonviolent communication; the trend toward soft and meta-skills will get extra fuel for the next decade. Perhaps it already reflects a societal shift in demand toward a genuinely democratic regime and working institutions, as the experience of where "strong hands" lead becomes an ever more vivid example of bad governance and a terrible scenario [#846].

#### Healthy communities as islands of a new culture
The author's thesis: if a society truly wants not to repeat its tragic aggression and to step on the path of connectedness, health and cooperation, it must work on healing traumatic experience. Until work on collective trauma has begun, it is critically important to create healthy communities that help members live through rather than suppress stress. Teams and horizontal communities unexpectedly take on a crucial social function: finding ways to stay in contact with today's difficulties and creating "островки культуры, в которой насилие не является нормой" (islands of culture in which violence is not the norm), cultivating support, openness and cooperation. From such islands a new public culture will emerge, capable of stopping the reproduction of past traumas [#853].

#### Blind spot "we've moved past it" and the reactor in the therapy room (2024)
- Why the author wrote about it: the topics seem obvious, they have been chewed over for two years, everyone "got it" — «проехали уже» ("we've moved past it"). Exactly here a blind spot typical of traumatic experience formed. These themes are the water we swim in and no longer notice its wetness because it hurts — an experience we are forced to deal with although we wanted something else [#984].
- The reactor. However much one wants to move past it, in a therapeutic space it is obvious that "там остается атомный реактор" (an atomic reactor remains there): as soon as a reliable container appears, a chain reaction begins and energy goes straight into these topics, bringing them out of the shadow. "Отброшено, но не прожито до конца" (discarded but not fully lived through) — not processed, not made conscious, not made sense of. The author calls this absolutely normal, since the processes are not finished and much cannot close yet, while to the rest we have adapted — "привыкли" (got used to it) [#984].
- New baggage on old. The new baggage is layered on the old, undischarged one. After the inhumanity of the USSR society never went through a healing process. In therapeutic circles people did bring out the family pain of the Gulag, but that work stayed rather "marginal" and very narrow; the "general" state narrative quickly began to deny or even forbid it, closing the possibilities for joint healing of the whole society. This, the author says, certainly created the soil for a new wave of violence [#984].
- What to do. Not repeat this mistake; stop hatred outward and within society as soon as possible; turn to what hurts together and openly. If we are living through a collective traumatic experience, we must treat ourselves in it and it in ourselves with more attention and compassion — "Не с цинизмом, характерным для привыкания, а именно с добротой" (not with the cynicism typical of getting used to it, but with kindness) and a deeper understanding of the experience's significance. We suffered in it even if we adapted successfully; we lost a lot — but have those losses been mourned? Without shared kindness, attention and openness it will take part of our vitality and leave sad imprints that enter culture as an anxious echo — a common burden of defensive patterns depriving people of flexibility and joy [#984].

#### The collective dissociative glass ceiling for inner work (2024)
The author's personal, explicitly generalizing reflection [#1028]:
- Since the war began he feels contradictory emotions working with the Russian audience: as a provider of a culture of mindfulness, he feels a dead end and a special "viscosity" in moving forward; the public demand for inner work and its form increasingly differ from the West. (He notes Vilya wrote about the same in the context of trauma work.) [#1028]
- Then vs now. The earlier boom of psychotherapy and mindfulness was tied to positive aspects: society discovered a new field where effort quickly paid off in transformation; therapy, body practices and meditation promised development, growth and new horizons. With war, emigration and civil split, tasks shifted to anti-crisis and containment, from which everyone suddenly got terribly tired and began to avoid topics that "cannot be changed" and the feelings tied to them [#1028].
- Two effects. On one side the long boom did its job: people now seek professional support in hard moments and give feelings much more due attention. On the other, a "коллективный диссоциативный стеклянный потолок для внутренней работы" (collective dissociative glass ceiling for inner work) has formed, since it is impossible to bear the reality outside and therefore part of its inner reflection [#1028].
- Why. Today it is as if one needs to be nearly Buddha or Jesus to stay open while facing the madness and contacting one's parts screaming in horror and incomprehension. It is more human to push it all away in the natural striving to survive and feel familiar comfort. By cutting off part of one's capacity to feel, a person can lose access to the inner vulnerability needed for emotional perception and serious consciousness [#1028].
- Society closing. Russian society thus closes, living either in depressing daily frustration or in an anesthetizing bubble of "normality" with less and less reality, with which a feeling being cannot be comfortable. Example: after a terrorist attack people come in and dive into work as if nothing happened over the weekend. The author calls this absolutely normal in a life where less and less space remains for normality and humanity — only in a circle of friends or at work — or where the psyche spends a lot of effort on avoidance, pushing activity and attention into the few places where safety and distraction are possible [#1028].
- Why inner work feels meaningless. Going to a coach, doing mindfulness, talking to a therapist has light in it, but for many there now lies a hard turn toward a huge heap of emotions with no clear way to handle them. Inner work won't make the war go away, end violence and lawlessness, or bring back safety — at least so it seems because of the habit of tightly linking external reality with the inner state, as our childhood happiness was tied to a candy. Hence "Внутренняя работа сегодня лишена смысла" (inner work today is devoid of meaning) — presented as how it seems, through that habit [#1028].
- The classic scenario. It is hard to live openly; easier to drink some wine, get busy, cut off feelings — "Классический сценарий коллективной травмы" (the classic scenario of collective trauma), a path society has taken more than once, each time leaving behind these periods of shared trance a special mood of cynicism, fragmentation and characteristic insensitivity, which was later sold as a special national strength and knowledge — something that always made the author uneasy [#1028].
