## Sleep, sleep debt and caffeine

### What this covers

This topic gathers the deep mind author's posts on sleep as the foundation of stress resilience and cognitive work: evening practices for clearing the mind, the effect of light on sleep and mood, two "external" strategies (Epsom salt baths and electrolytes), sleep debt (what it is, how to measure it, how to pay it off), the illusion of being fine on 5-6 hours, quitting caffeine and what he drinks instead, and insomnia understood as an anxiety-and-control loop. According to the source, sleep matters because even one poor night lowers resilience, there is no body system untouched by sleep loss, and the sleep-deprived lose effectiveness without losing confidence [#262, #740, #1700].

### Core ideas

- Sleep is the basis of stress resilience. At least 22% of people on the planet have sleep problems, and even one night of insufficient quality sleep lowers Resilience — the ability to respond steadily and well to challenges during the day — so we cope worse with stress and strong emotions [#262].
- Sleep and stress form a closed loop. Insomnia is a key symptom of elevated psycho-emotional strain; sleep deprivation raises amygdala activity (the stress response), and stress in turn causes sleep problems [#740].
- Sleep debt, not last night, predicts how you feel. Current wellbeing is the summed result of the previous two weeks of sleep; only paying off the debt really helps [#767].
- Short sleep degrades you without you noticing. After 3-4 days of restricted sleep people lose internal calibration of sleepiness; they feel adapted while their prefrontal functions keep degrading [#1700, #767].
- Caffeine masks fatigue, it does not give energy, and it creates a vicious circle (worse sleep, then extra caffeine); the author quit it and recommends quitting for poor sleepers and anxious people [#767, #1256].
- Insomnia is often an anxiety loop around sleep; trying to force sleep is itself the fuel of arousal, and progress comes from allowing sleep rather than controlling it [#1756].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Five practices to calm the mind and improve sleep quality
Purpose: the author names clearing the mind of the day's tasks and information as one of the important elements of quality sleep. After a large flow of tasks it is especially easy to get stuck in thoughts and emotions; they speed up brain activity, create tension in the body and can easily prevent a good night's sleep. The practices below target this [#262].
1. A short mindfulness practice right before sleep. The author links his own body-scan audio practice for this purpose [#262].
2. Finish work activities at least an hour before sleep (earlier is better). This creates a buffer between the stream of tasks and bedtime [#262].
3. Write a to-do list with all of tomorrow's tasks at the end of the day. The why: it frees the head from having to think about the tasks and remember them [#262].
4. Keep a gratitude journal before sleep. Write down at least 3 things you are grateful to life and the world for that day. The author calls it "Очень мощная штука" (a very powerful thing) and says information about its benefits and scientific grounding is easy to find on your own [#262].
5. Do progressive muscle relaxation (Jacobson method) while already lying in bed. The why: sleep depends strongly on relaxation, and this practice helps with it [#262].

#### Light hygiene and light therapy
Purpose: reduce the harm of artificial light and compensate for lack of natural sunlight, which the author links to sleep quality and mood [#324].
1. Recognize light pollution. The modern environment, especially urban and office, creates a lot of light pollution: we constantly look at blue screens, and the yellow (night) modes only partially filter harmful blue light; on top of that we are surrounded by unhealthy white light from all sides [#324].
2. Understand what blue light does. Blue light acts on the brain by raising nervous-system activity. This is sometimes useful during the day while awake, but it spoils life overall; even a small amount can affect the circadian rhythm and lead to poor sleep quality [#324].
3. Understand the cost of too little sun. An excess of blue light plus a deficit of natural sunlight ("привет, Питер и Москва" — *hello, St Petersburg and Moscow*) causes symptoms of depression and raises the risk of a number of other diseases [#324].
4. Use circadian-friendly lighting and light-therapy devices. This situation led to a special type of home and office lighting, circadian-friendly, and to light-therapy devices. Light built on this concept does not knock down circadian rhythms but helps waking and falling asleep, and does not harm mitochondria [#324].
5. Author's self-experiment (the applied protocol). A week after returning from sunny Turkey to Moscow he instantly noticed a drop in mood and negative thoughts; after analysis he attributed it to the climate change, since gloomy Moscow weather affects state "весьма и весьма сильно" (very, very strongly). In addition to therapy he added vitamin D to his diet (together with testing his level) and ordered a special lamp, under whose light he now has breakfast or works in the daytime. After one week he reports that his mood improved strongly, as did his sleep quality. Lesson he draws: even small but targeted investments can produce a big shift and affect the quality of all the work and projects he will do in November and December [#324].

#### Two external game-changers for sleep and stress
Purpose: the author's corporate stress program usually gives a list of key actions to normalize sleep, which readers may already know; these are two "external" strategies that have not spread widely enough but help quickly and often radically improve sleep quality [#740].

1 — Epsom salt (эпсоновая соль) baths [#740]
- What: warm baths with Epsom salt — something you can do today to relax, restore the nervous system and sleep better.
- When: shortly before sleep.
- Why: to replenish the body's magnesium deficit under high stress, change muscle tone and lower body temperature for deeper rest.
- It works quickly and can be done regularly.
- The author's claims: most of us are magnesium-deficient; stress further depletes magnesium stores; overdosing is "практически нереально" (practically impossible) because the excess is simply excreted.
- Other names the author gives: magnesium sulfate, magnesium powder, "epson salt", "MgSo2" (as the author writes it).
- Enhancer: you can add natural rosemary oil to the bath to strengthen the effect.

2 — Electrolytes (электролиты) [#740]
- Why: sleep disturbances are often caused by problems with the body's water balance. Adding a quality electrolyte solution to the diet on a permanent basis can help with a whole "bouquet" of tasks: reducing swelling, chronic dehydration, and speeding up lymphatic drainage.
- Expected effect: helps deepen sleep quality and reduce the feeling of constant fatigue, usually within a few days to a week.
- The author's routine: he drinks good electrolytes twice a day — in the morning right after waking, and a couple of hours before sleep with less water.
- Add-on: pay more attention to the quality of the water you drink, and add spring and mineral waters to daily intake.

Caveat stated by the author: if you have health problems, and especially blood-pressure issues, consult a doctor before using these recommendations [#740].

#### Measuring sleep debt
Definition: sleep debt (недосып) is the hours we owe the body based on personal sleep need; they accumulate over several days or weeks when we sleep less than our norm [#767].
1. Simple manual method: track your sleep hours and count how many hours over the last week you slept below 7 hours a day [#767].
2. Rise app: the app calculates sleep debt. The author has used it with a watch for several months and sees a correlation between his state and sleep debt, but has questions about measurement accuracy, "как и всегда с этими приложениями" (as always with these apps) [#767].

#### Optimal ways to pay off sleep debt faster
Rationale: "Капитан Очевидность и исследования" (Captain Obvious and research) show that to improve wellbeing and performance you must pay off the debt; nothing else essentially helps, although other things sometimes give temporary relief [#767].
1. Go to bed earlier than usual and within your melatonin window [#767].
2. Avoid screens before sleep, because they block melatonin and shorten sleep time [#767].
3. Sleep 7 to 9 hours in one stretch, per the recommendations of the US National Sleep Foundation. Other approaches — like polyphasic sleep or 15 minutes every hour "because Leonardo da Vinci slept that way" — do not work [#767].
4. Nap 15-30 minutes during the day [#767].
5. Give up caffeine and other stimulants, at least for a while [#767].

#### Quitting caffeine and the author's caffeine-free coffee routine
Purpose: for those who sleep poorly (like the author) or experience a lot of stress and anxiety, the author's advice is to give up caffeine and other stimulants "навеки веков лучше прямо сейчас" (forever and ever, better right now) [#1256].
1. Quit gradually. The withdrawal protocol for long-term use recommends doing it gradually rather than quitting abruptly [#1256].
2. Remove all caffeine sources, not only coffee. The author has for about 3 years almost completely avoided caffeine, including caffeinated drinks such as black or green tea, and keeps away from other stimulants like mate and matcha [#1256].
3. Switch to decaf. He drinks coffee every day but has completely switched to decaf; good-quality decaf is harder to find than regular coffee but possible [#1256]. The photo of his current kit shows Rarebird PX Coffee, Blue Bottle medium decaf and a craft matcha tin [#1256].
4. Optionally try paraxanthine coffee (Rarebird). In California he started drinking Rarebird, which contains paraxanthine instead of caffeine. The company claims to have completely reinvented coffee; the author says there is some truth in this — paraxanthine, which the body forms when processing caffeine, is placed directly into the beans. It is believed to also stimulate but with many times fewer negative effects, including on sleep, and to be excreted faster. Verdict: he likes it but still feels stimulation that is not always needed, so when he uses it he halves the portion and mixes it with decaf. He wonders how Rarebird works for people with ADHD, for whom caffeine is even indicated and makes life noticeably easier [#1256].
5. If you must have matcha, buffer it with hojicha. He sometimes cannot refuse good matcha; he has not seen it made without caffeine, and for a full glass he often "pays" fairly quickly. Mixing it with hojicha powder, which contains relaxing L-theanine in a good amount, gives a more balanced effect. Hojicha latte on its own has long been his favorite evening drink [#1256].

#### The insomnia anxiety loop
Purpose: the author's model of why insomnia persists; insomnia is often tied to a negative emotional cycle around sleep, to which we became more prone because of the sleep-hype context (from Huberman to trackers) [#1756].
1. I sleep badly, or something goes slightly wrong.
2. Anxiety arises, because sleep is so important / I want to improve my metrics.
3. I start controlling myself and the environment more strongly to "починить сон" (fix sleep).
4. The nervous system activates because of the control and anxiety.
5. "гудбай сон" (goodbye sleep).
The harder he tries to solve the problem, the less it works, and the more he gets upset and worsens it — not because he uses the wrong protocol or supplement, but because "сама попытка заставить сон случиться и есть топливо для возбуждения" (the very attempt to make sleep happen is the fuel for arousal) [#1756].

#### Four things that feed the insomnia fire
1. Constant control — checking the clock in the middle of the night, counting how long is left until the alarm and how many hours are already lost [#1756].
2. Clinging to crutches (костыли) — when a particular sound, pillow, mattress, supplements or ideal temperature become a mandatory condition for sleep [#1756].
3. Beliefs such as "я должен спать прямо сейчас" (I must sleep right now) / "мне необходимо ровно 8 часов" (I need exactly 8 hours) [#1756].
4. Trackers and data on which we start to focus heavily [#1756].

#### Breaking the insomnia loop: allowing instead of controlling
1. Stop solving the sleep problem directly; work with the anxiety around it. For the author one of the really big shifts was not new techniques, mattresses or trackers, but allowing himself not to worry about sleep — which he admits is, to put it mildly, very hard for him. He tried living with attention to sleep and his reactions but without total control, and this greatly improved the situation [#1756].
2. Drop the illusion of control and accept imperfection. Stop imagining yourself a god who can control life, and allow imperfection of everything, especially your own state and limits [#1756].
3. Use permission-giving self-talk. The author's lines: "Если не могу заснуть — это окей, почитаю корейский роман." (If I can't fall asleep — that's okay, I'll read a Korean novel.) "Если не посплю вовсе, то все равно буду нормальным." (If I don't sleep at all, I'll still be fine.) "Разбитость — это не конец света." (Feeling wrecked is not the end of the world.) [#1756]
4. Keep working on sleep and emotions, but without the goal of sleeping now. You can continue working both on sleep itself and on the emotions around it, but without an explicit or hidden goal of urgently falling asleep or sleeping perfectly. Move as far as possible from any catastrophizing, including anxiety about possible anxiety [#1756].
5. Measure progress counterintuitively. Progress is not "I finally slept well" but "I panic and worry less about sleep". "Сначала уходит страх, и потом приходит радость." (First the fear leaves, and then joy comes.) [#1756]

Scope and caveat from the author: he is not a doctor and this is not a universal recommendation; however, testing it costs no money, seems to carry no risk and can be done quickly. If insomnia persists after you have already fixed basic sleep hygiene, it is better to go straight to qualified specialists [#1756].

### Detailed insights

#### Sleep loss and the stress loop
Scientists have not found a body system that is not affected by lack of sleep. Sleep is directly tied to stress: insomnia is the most important symptom of elevated psycho-emotional tension, and a closed loop forms easily — a sleep-deprived person's amygdala activity (stress response) rises, and stress leads to sleep problems [#740]. In 2020 the author framed attention to sleep as necessary "to survive this autumn": to feel good and perform strongly after a stressful half-year [#262].

#### Why the sleep-deprived don't notice: awareness and caffeine
People who habitually undersleep and accumulate sleep debt show a sharp decline in cognitive function, yet they often do not feel sleepy and do not see a clear link to their abilities. The author illustrates with Dr Mark Rosekind's study of people after long flights: they rated themselves in excellent condition, but their reaction speed was 20% below normal [#767]. The author's explanation: we have no internal instrument for tracking our state except mindfulness (осознанность); it must be developed and wellbeing must be constantly tracked — explicitly not by external measurements. On top of that we constantly stimulate bodily insensitivity, for example with caffeine: "Кофеин не восполняет энергию, а блокирует информацию об усталости" (caffeine does not replenish energy, it blocks information about fatigue). It suppresses the sleep hormone adenosine, and the brain thinks we are fine when we are not [#767].

#### Chronic short sleep: the 6-hour illusion
The author calls Dr Van Dongen's 2003 study still the basic reference for understanding chronic sleep deprivation, and takes two key facts from it [#1700]:
- After 14 days, people sleeping 6 hours performed on attention and reaction speed like people after 24 hours without sleep; the group sleeping 4 hours a day performed like after 48 hours awake.
- In both groups, after 3-4 days the internal calibration of fatigue and sleepiness (Stanford Sleepiness Scale) is lost: the person stops feeling they are getting worse while objectively continuing to degrade. The brain adapts to the feeling of tiredness but does not adapt to the decline of prefrontal cortex functions.

Sleep is "базовая инфраструктура когнитивной работы" (the basic infrastructure of cognitive work). If we constantly sleep 6, or especially 4, hours, the areas of life loaded with complex functions suffer. Leadership example: we think we are pulling through, holding focus, the team sees no obvious failure, but we pay a hidden tax — more iterations and rework are needed for a result, more strategic mistakes happen, and the thinking horizon shrinks [#1700]. Sleep loss is dangerous not only through fatigue but because it reduces effectiveness without reducing confidence; hence "мне хватает 6 часов сна" (6 hours of sleep is enough for me) in most cases means "я забыл, как выглядит нормальное качество мышления" (I've forgotten what normal quality of thinking looks like) [#1700].

Parallel with chronic stress (which the author says his corporate programs discuss again and again): with both sleep loss and chronic stress we often feel the situation not as a crisis but as a new norm. The baseline of our state — what we are used to considering ourselves — shifts; the organism adapts to the problem and we do not realize we could feel differently and be productive at a quite different level. There are exceptions to the rule, but the probability that we are one is much lower than that we simply undersleep [#1700].

Closing reflection: the author's many years of struggle taught him to panic a little less when something goes wrong with stress or sleep and not to wind himself up further. Knowing scientific facts matters so as not to fool oneself or other people with superpowers that don't exist, but perhaps more important is to treat difficulties more lightly and not dramatize — easier said than done. Ultimately one can always look for *who exactly* is suffering from stress or sleep loss, so as not to find them and calm down [#1700]. (!) UNCLEAR: this last sentence appears to be a meditative self-inquiry pointer ("look for the one who suffers and not find them"); the source does not explain it further. He opened the post noting that, as often, he himself had not slept well that night [#1700].

#### Caffeine as an unregulated psychoactive stimulant
Personal history: in 2015 the author owned a specialty coffee shop and bar where top Russian baristas hung out ("кофеиновые годы", *the caffeine years*), but at some point those years led to a dead end of constant fatigue and irritability, and he clearly realized that living without caffeine is much better than with it [#1256].

His argument: looking at facts rather than the cultural narrative, there are quite strong psychoactive substances with serious side effects that culture does not stigmatize or even praises — for example "крайне токсичный алкоголь" (extremely toxic alcohol) and caffeine, which is aggressive for the nervous system and addictive. Caffeine is one of very few psychoactive CNS stimulants in the world with no restrictions or rules [#1256].

Mechanism (per the author): caffeine blocks adenosine receptors, reducing the feeling of fatigue and increasing alertness; it temporarily masks fatigue but does not remove its cause. It is eliminated slowly — 7-8 hours — and can affect state throughout the day and sleep quality even if drunk in the morning. Sleep-deprived people often use it as a compensatory tool, which creates a vicious circle: worse sleep → more caffeine → even worse sleep [#1256]. Sometimes a very small amount is enough to cause problems: for "slow metabolizers" even one morning cup can strongly affect night sleep [#1256].

#### The sleep-optimization paradox and orthosomnia
"Сон стал новым сексом" (sleep has become the new sex): Matthew Walker wrote a book about it read by millions, Huberman has released more than one episode, and sleep-specialized hotels at 500 dollars a night have appeared. Everyone wants sleep but few can afford it, because the culture of constant "включенности" (being switched on) and obsession with metrics is the complete opposite of normal rest [#1756]. The more we want it, the more problems we face: we look at Whoop and Oura data and try to push sleep scores to 100%, start controlling routine and move away from the coveted "отпускание" (letting go). In 2017 Northwestern University researchers introduced a special term for this — orthosomnia. As a result, almost every second person has some difficulty with sleep, while every third has insomnia symptoms [#1756].

The author's own insomnia began in his youth during very stressful work in a federal ministry, where he had to be reachable at any time of day and night and the level of importance was always turned to maximum [#1756].

Final lesson: from insomnia he learned that sleep, like sex, is better not forced but allowed to happen. It is an example of a reality that responds poorly to a linear, achievement-driven approach and teaches us to be in live contact with the process; applying the usual logic "поставь цель, оптимизируй процесс, получи результат" (set a goal, optimize the process, get the result) can sometimes ruin everything [#1756].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The author repeatedly hedges: he is not a doctor, the insomnia approach is not universal, and persistent insomnia after fixing basic sleep hygiene warrants qualified specialists [#1756]; people with health problems, especially blood-pressure issues, should consult a doctor before Epsom baths and electrolytes [#740].
- The claims that most people are magnesium-deficient and that magnesium overdose is practically impossible are the author's statements without cited evidence in the post; the author writes the formula as "MgSo2" [#740].
- The negative effect of white office light on mitochondria is presented as known from "a large number of studies" without specifics [#324].
- Gratitude journal: the author asserts scientific grounding but leaves the reader to find it [#262].
- Rise app accuracy is questioned by the author himself [#767].
- (!) CONTRADICTION (mild): #767 recommends constant tracking of wellbeing via mindfulness and uses the Rise app with a watch, while #1756 lists trackers and data focus as fuel for the insomnia loop; #767 itself specifies tracking "не внешними измерениями" (not by external measurements), and #1756 advocates attention to sleep without total control.
- Paraxanthine claims (fewer side effects, faster elimination) are presented as "считается" (it is believed) and as the company's claims; the author notes ADHD as an open question, where caffeine may be indicated [#1256].
- The Van Dongen exception: the author grants there are exceptions to the "6 hours is not enough" rule but considers them improbable for any given person [#1700].
