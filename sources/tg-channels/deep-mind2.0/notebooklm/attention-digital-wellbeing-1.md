## Attention, focus and information overload

### What this covers

This topic collects what the deep mind channel taught about attention as a resource: why information overload and multitasking make people feel worse and work worse, the concept of situational attention deficit (СОДВ) and its symptoms, a set of focus principles, workplace mind-wandering data, the question "do I have ADHD?" and how ADHD relates to nervous-system dysregulation and mindfulness, the information-management framework FAST, and a philosophical argument that scattered attention is an evolutionary step back. According to the channel, all deep mind projects are built on working with attention, information overload is one of the main requests clients bring, and the cost of ignoring human biology is lower productivity and poorer health [#240].

### Core ideas

- Attention is the organism's most valuable — and most easily depleted — resource. The author says that even from a neurobiological point of view, "внимание — самый ценный ресурс организма" (attention is the organism's most valuable resource); where attention is directed creates deep changes in any system, a person or an organisation. At the same time it is easily overloaded [#240].
- Overload produces an acquired attention deficit (СОДВ). Constant information stress and multitasking create a situational attention deficit whose symptoms become habits and persist even after the pace slows — e.g. mentally solving work tasks on weekends and holidays [#240, #709].
- Multitasking is a myth. Task-switching lowers accuracy and raises errors; "Одна задача - в один момент времени" (one task at one moment in time) [#240, #253, #1088]. Byung-Chul Han, quoted by the author, goes further: multitasking is a survival mode of wild animals, a regression rather than progress [#1370].
- Most attention problems are not ADHD. Adult ADHD is rare (2–4%), diagnosis is hard, and the same symptoms are far more likely to come from nervous-system dysregulation (trauma/chronic stress, 50–70% of adults) or from СОДВ (likely ~100% of heavy technology users) [#709, #1190].
- Attention constantly pointed outward costs health and happiness. Being lost in external objects (smartphone, work, other people) means losing contact with body, feelings and needs; research the author cites links this to poorer self-awareness, emotional regulation and happiness, while practices that turn attention inward reduce stress [#1121, #1227].
- Stress multiplies mind-wandering; mindfulness, sleep and community reduce it. Attention wanders 37% of working time on average and 2–3 times more under stress; daily mindfulness, one extra hour of sleep and connection with others each cut mind-wandering considerably [#822, #823, #824].
- Focus is trainable and can be organised. Focus is a skill improved by daily practice (results in 10–14 days), plus scheduling, removing distractions, external memory systems and a culture of focus; information inflow itself can be managed with a system such as FAST [#253, #803].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Symptoms of situational attention deficit (9-item self-check)
Purpose: a check-up of one's state, recommended at the beginning of the week, so that measures can be taken if needed. The scientific grounding is attributed to Dr. Hallowell's material on diagnosing ADHD [#244]. The symptoms of "обусловленного дефицита внимания" (conditioned attention deficit):
1. Hurry — rushing even when there is no objective need for it [#244].
2. Inability to stay with one thing for a sufficiently long time (more than 5–10 minutes) — whether a line of reasoning, a conversation, looking at a picture for long, or the text of a book [#244].
3. The habit of jumping from task to task, from idea to idea, and sometimes even physically from place to place [#244].
4. A general feeling of being overloaded with tasks, information and responsibility [#244].
5. Guilt and anxiety about unfinished tasks [#244].
6. A feeling of having lost control over one's own life, and a disturbing sense of having missed something [#244].
7. An obsessive, constant urge to check the phone [#244].
8. Frequent irritability — impatience, boredom, dissatisfaction; or, more strongly, unrelenting anxiety, despair, panic [#244].
9. Loss of the ability to enjoy simple things, pleasant moments and achievements [#244].

Why it matters: the author states that attention deficit leads to a fall in energy and productivity. Immediate advice if already on Monday the organism is stressed by information overload: do a small information detox and "больше подвигать тушкой" (move your carcass more — colloquial for: move your body more). A more fundamental guide was promised and given as the focus guide below [#244].

#### Ultimate focus guide — key principles of improving concentration
Purpose: an overview of the key principles for increasing concentration, presented as the remedy for overload and attention deficit. The rationale given: concentration and clarity of mind create a harmonious, calm satisfaction with life, which the author calls much more positive than sharp rises of euphoria and falls of pain [#253]. The source numbers the principles 1–7 and 9–12; there is no item 8 (see Caveats).
1. Deliberately develop the skill of concentration. Even 5 minutes of concentration training helps, but ideally work up to daily exercises of 25 minutes or more. Results become evident in 10–14 days if the technique is done correctly. The channel offers its own "pleasant concentration" (pleasure concentration) audio practice for this [#253].
2. Find and fix in the schedule your time of high productivity. This differs between people with different chronotypes (the author points to a Mann, Ivanov & Ferber book, whose link slug reads "vsegda-vovremya" — Always on time — to read about chronotypes and determine yours). Use this time for important, focused work; leave routine tasks and messenger replies for periods of medium or low productivity — for example, after lunch [#253].
3. Avoid multitasking. One task at one moment in time; otherwise it is ineffective [#253].
4. Set goals, study and articulate values. This is "мета-фокусировка" (meta-focusing): it directs us overall and keeps us from being overloaded with tasks and incoming requests that in fact do not need to be done at all [#253].
5. Work through anxiety and fears. These emotions, more than others, make attention jump from object to object. It is important to get acquainted with your individual manifestations of anxiety and fear, understand their causes, feel them through and talk them over with a therapist [#253].
6. Build a culture of focus around you. People will only know about changes in our habits and priorities if we tell them; otherwise they distract us without realising it [#253].
7. Avoid messengers, social networks and email in the morning, before doing concentration practices or before your high-productivity work period. "Клиповая информация и дешевый дофамин" (clip information and cheap dopamine) quickly spend the resources of the attention system and make concentrating harder [#253].
8. (numbered 9 in the source) Eliminate all possible distractions. Close the office door, remove any unnecessary objects from the desk and the room, switch off the phone. Thoughts that pop up can also be distractions: write them down somewhere rather than reacting with immediate action [#253].
9. (numbered 10) Keep discipline. However much you want cheap dopamine from checking social networks or messengers, do not do it during periods of focused work. Create and sustain the intention to live in a focused way [#253].
10. (numbered 11) Avoid having to remember tasks and things. Called very important because memory and attention are maximally connected. Today it is better to create external memory and attention systems: all tasks, deadlines and details are recorded wherever is convenient for us — in one place. For all things and objects we create their own places so we don't search for them all over the house each time [#253].
11. (numbered 12) Sleep normally, eat normally, avoid caffeine and other stimulants, check magnesium levels and other health markers. The author adds "no comments" except the fact that caffeine does not help concentrate but rather hinders it [#253].

#### 3 key actions to improve focus and concentration
Derived from the Potential Project mind-wandering data (see *Mind-wandering by weekday and time of day*) [#824]:
1. Daily mindfulness training. People who do daily mindfulness exercises versus those who do not: 28% vs 52% mind-wandering during the day [#824].
2. Sleep. Just 1 extra hour of sleep (the difference between 6 and 7 hours) reduces daytime mind-wandering from 41% to 35% [#824].
3. Communities. Connection with others, as opposed to loneliness, "pulls" the mind out of automatic wandering and lowers distraction from 48% to 30% [#824].

#### Founders' anti-burnout focus card (NYC Tech Week)
The author took part in a panel at New York Tech Week where serial founders, their coaches and doctors discussed burnout; the card summarises the "basic but main" advice on how to move forward without burning out. The six points are all attention-management rules [#1088]:
1. Focus on one thing at a time — "многозадачность — миф" (multitasking is a myth) [#1088].
2. Schedule time for checking chats and email, so as not to be constantly distracted [#1088].
3. Practise breaks during the day, for example mini-meditations [#1088].
4. Choose a day for deep work [#1088].
5. Arrange short periods of time without the phone [#1088].
6. Avoid context switching during the day [#1088].
The longer write-up of the panel (what helps once already burned out, how to combine results with enjoyment of work, how to come up with new solutions amid many urgent tasks) was published outside the channel and is not in the source. (!) INCOMPLETE: only the card's content is available [#1088].

#### F.A.S.T. productivity framework (Andrey Altshuler)
Purpose: a system for filtering and processing information so that "the head is clear and things go well" — i.e. organising one's digital environment to reduce information noise under information overload. Presented as a talk by Andrey Altshuler in the deep mind community (part 1 ~34 min, part 2 ~22 min); Altshuler is described as an expert in futures studies and decision-making methodology, ex-founder of the agency Signal, whose work is processing large volumes of information, separating signal from noise, systematising and mining insights [#803, #804]. The four components, as shown on the slide [#803, #804]:
1. Filter
2. Automate
3. Structure
4. Templetize [sic] (templatize)

#### Two more likely causes of attention problems than ADHD
Purpose: an alternative explanation for anyone tempted to self-diagnose ADHD [#709]:
1. Nervous-system dysregulation. MD and trauma specialist Bruce Perry holds that a child's attention problems are more often linked not to ADHD but to dysregulation of the nervous system. It often arises through traumatic experience in relationships with adult figures. Afterwards the child's attention jumps from object to object because of a hyperaroused nervous system that gets stuck in the need to scan the surroundings for danger. Trauma and nervous-system regulation problems are genuinely common — the source cites them as affecting 50–70% of adults (citing APA material) [#709].
2. СОДВ — situational attention deficit. Differs from ADHD in that it arises under the influence of living and working conditions and is not of genetic origin: it is attention overload, the organism trying to cope with the stress of information volume and multitasking. The author says this problem most likely concerns 100% of people who actively use technology and are overloaded with tasks and communication. The remedy pointed to is the focus guide above [#709].

#### Three conclusions on mindfulness and ADHD
Context: written under the supervision of a clinical psychologist. ADHD has long been a "big thing" in the West among neurodivergences, and experienced practitioners have reflected on it in the context of mindfulness — developing attentiveness and awareness through meditative exercises. The author summarises that discourse into three conclusions [#1190]:
1. One can meditate with ADHD and possibly reach high stages of meditative realisation. Research (a PMC article) showed mindfulness meditation to be both feasible and acceptable as a treatment for ADHD. Gabriel Gould writes from his teaching experience that he has repeatedly seen practitioners with ADHD reach the highest goals of meditation. So people with a genuine diagnosis can still use mindfulness tools and advance far along this line of development [#1190].
2. Mindfulness can substantially help with ADHD. Mindfulness meditation is gaining more support as a non-pharmacological intervention for adults with ADHD and shows promising results in improving symptoms. Studies emphasise that mindfulness training can improve attentional control through the repeated practice of focusing and returning attention after distraction [#1190].
3. ADHD certainly does not make practice easier, and it is important to adapt. As with any neurodivergence, one must study and understand the specifics of the person's existence and adapt to their reality. Hence special programmes appear and are researched, e.g. "Mindfulness for Adult ADHD", which help clinicians and mindfulness teachers account for the context of clients with ADHD. General recommendations: additional support for the setup itself — a precise time in the schedule, a comfortable posture and place, having a buddy or teacher. In terms of techniques: first of all simple practices that ground you in the body and create a sense of stability — mindfulness meditation, body scan, grounding [#1190].

### Detailed insights

#### Why attention overload is a problem (the mechanism)
The author's basic numbers: on average the human brain perceives 4 to 40 bits of information per second — "Это мало! И катастрофически мало в сегодняшнем информационном мире" (That's little! And catastrophically little in today's information world). Because the speed of change and the volume of information have grown significantly, active people end up in constant overload [#240].

Edward Hallowell, an American psychiatrist specialising in concentration, proposed the term "ситуационно обусловленный дефицит внимания" (situationally conditioned attention deficit, СОДВ) for this. СОДВ is the organism's attempt to cope with the stress of information volume and multitasking. Over time its symptoms turn into a habit, become fixed and show up even when the original stressful situation has passed and the pace of life slows — e.g. on weekends or holiday we may keep mentally solving work tasks, not allowing ourselves to rest [#240].

Three consequences of overload listed by the author [#240]:
1. We lose contact with actual reality. A brain under information attack cannot process it properly or evaluate novelty and originality. Instead of perceiving the new, we begin to think in convenient, ordinary, short fragments — stereotypes, slogans and fashionable expressions [#240].
2. We lose contact with the body and emotions; we tire faster, fall ill more often and burn out. When attention is overloaded, the throughput of the tactile (bodily) perception channel also decreases, so we feel our body worse or not at all. Therefore tension accumulates in it faster and emotions are not fully lived through. We get tired much faster [#240].
3. We cannot concentrate and make mistakes. In 1995 cognitive psychologists Robert Rogers and Stephen Monsell found that instant switching of attention from one task to another and back (multitasking) lowers accuracy, increases the number of errors and harms work quality. Many later studies "разбили в пух и прах миф о многозадачности" (smashed the myth of multitasking to smithereens). A person in this mode is ineffective [#240].

#### Why organisations ignore focus
Information overload is described as one of the key requests people bring to deep mind. Yet, the author argues, most companies still do not understand the importance of concentration and of creating a suitable environment for it, because traditional organisations arose in the era of factories and plants, where constant loading of a person as a simple tool of production was valued. The price of this ignorance of human biology is reduced labour productivity and health [#240]. In a later post the author argues the problem has become so deep that an individual employee or company cannot solve it alone — it must be solved jointly (illustrated by a company deploying a deep mind practice tracker for its employees to return attention to themselves) [#1227].

#### Information as a biological load
In a personal reflection, the author names the main challenge for himself and many people today as the amount of information passing through us — messengers, tons of content, people. "Всего стало слишком много" (There's become too much of everything), and every piece of information is processed by us, i.e. it requires a reaction and response of the nervous system. Hence information overloads us biologically and increases stress [#487].

#### "Do I have ADHD?" — the diagnostic caution
ADHD (СДВГ, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder) is defined as a neurological condition in which a person finds it hard to concentrate, behaves impulsively, experiences brain fog and remembers events poorly [#709].

The author observes that in the attention economy these problems have become so pressing that many of us develop an unconscious wish to diagnose ourselves with ADHD — it points the finger for all our unexplained difficulties at one specific disease for which there is even medication (which the author calls "крайне спорное", extremely controversial). As a result ADHD became a hype topic and everyone went off to take tests [#709].

Why this is not optimal [#709]:
- ADHD cannot be competently diagnosed with internet tests or videos. It must be done by two doctors at once: a neurologist (or neuropathologist), and a neuropsychologist or clinical psychologist and psychiatrist (!) UNCLEAR: the source says "2 doctors" but then lists three or four specialities.
- If your difficulties with attention and memory have been present since childhood, you definitely need to see these doctors, because ADHD progresses without treatment.
- Even doctors (not just bloggers) struggle to diagnose ADHD, since other mental disorders accompany ADHD in 80% of cases.
- Statistically ADHD is not very widespread in adults: only 2–4% of the population in developed countries.

The later post adds: given the 2–4% prevalence plus diagnostic errors, the probability of a wrong diagnosis is extremely high. Symptomatically, ADHD looks very similar to nervous-system dysregulation arising from psychological trauma or chronic stress, which affects 50–70% of adults. If ADHD is misdiagnosed, one will be treated with highly controversial amphetamine-group drugs, which not only often cause severe side effects but further dysregulate the nervous system — i.e. make the situation worse. Therefore, if you have started to think you have ADHD, the first step is to unload attention and memory and systematically address the therapy of stress and trauma and practices for self-regulation of the nervous system [#1190].

#### Mind-wandering at work: the Potential Project data
Colleagues from Potential Project shared research on mind-wandering in the workplace [#822]. The author's key figure: "37% рабочего времени внимание сотрудников блуждает" (employees' attention wanders 37% of working time), drifting to thoughts unrelated to the current activity [#824].

Scattered attention not only lowers productivity; it also makes us less happy and satisfied. The author cites Daniel Gilbert's clinical study, which found the average level of mind-wandering and also its link with dissatisfaction — titled, literally, "a wandering mind is an unhappy mind" [#824].

Knowing the daily patterns of mind-wandering can help plan schedules and tasks so as not to overload attention further. The author's reading of the data: the most distracted times are Monday and Tuesday mornings, and Monday and Friday afternoons/evenings, when the mind wanders about 45% of the time — better not to put difficult, focus-demanding tasks there [#824]. The chart's headline: "The least amount of mind wandering happens on Wednesday afternoons and Friday mornings" [#823].

Under stress and anxiety, attention wanders 2–3 times more than in calm moments. People in chronic stress concentrate worse and get distracted more. The chart shows the clear difference in attention quality between those who do stress management and those who don't, especially obvious at the end of the week [#824, img #822].

#### Where is our attention? Outward vs inward
In a reflective post the author checks where his attention actually is and finds it always somewhere "вовне" (outside): in the smartphone, laptop, another person, tasks, entertainment, distractions — as if life happened exclusively there, and the body, feelings and knowledge of one's own thoughts and reactions did not exist, left like abandoned outskirts of an "империи внешнего" (empire of the external) [#1121].

He asks whether one can for even one minute simply be attention "тут" (here) in one's own bodily sensations, perhaps noticing the subtle fact of the existence of attention itself, rather than only directing it somewhere while ignoring its source [#1121].

Observations he makes [#1121]:
- We usually return attention to ourselves only for a few seconds — when we close our eyes, lose the endless objects in front of us, and fall asleep.
- Being not "out there" is so unfamiliar that the mind either starts working hard, producing thoughts and images to keep being distracted by (which sometimes prevents sleep), or, not knowing what to do in the emptiness, we simply "switch off" into oblivion — as often happens in our first meditation experiences, which invite us perhaps for the first time in life to look at ourselves rather than into the distance.
- Sometimes attention turns closer to the body in serious crises — when we lose someone or our identity breaks down; the vanity and some meaninglessness of everything external becomes obvious and we are forced to turn to what remains and look for other supports.
He closes with open questions: can one learn anything about the world without knowing oneself, and how can one know oneself while always looking outward? Why is it hard not to split the world in two but to be both there and here — at least 50/50 rather than a few minutes? What knowledge and feelings are we trying to avoid by escaping into external objects, and is this good for us? [#1121]

The follow-up states the problem with smartphones and work: in them we constantly direct attention outward and lose contact with ourselves. What is wrong with losing attention in external objects 99% of the time? "Только лишь то, что от этого мы болеем и становимся несчастными" (Only that it makes us ill and unhappy). Contemplative traditions long considered being lost in external objects a kind of disorder or extreme poverty. When attention is always "there", we don't understand what we feel and begin to process and satisfy our needs, emotions and stress worse [#1227]. The research the author cites in support:
- A study (Fisher 2016) showed that constantly directing attention to external stimuli can reduce opportunities for self-awareness, making it harder to understand one's own feelings and needs and affecting stress management and overall emotional regulation [#1227].
- The famous study by Killingsworth and Gilbert (2010) in Science showed that a mind wandering among different external objects is an unhappy mind: living in distraction from one's own state and in defocus lowers happiness and satisfaction [#1227].
- From the other side: programmes aimed at returning attention "inward" through mindfulness and self-regulation practices (which deep mind has done for 5 years) show effectiveness in improving mental state and reducing stress (meta-analysis, Goyal et al., 2014). The author takes this as confirming that turning attention to inner experience helps reduce the negative consequences of external stimuli [#1227].

#### Scattered attention as an evolutionary regression (Byung-Chul Han)
The author quotes philosopher Byung-Chul Han (author of a treatise on the "burnout society"): increasing workload demands special methods of managing time and attention, which in turn affect the structure of attention. Multitasking as such a method is not evolutionary progress, not an ability only humans of the late-modern labour and information society could possess — rather it is a regression. Multitasking is widespread in wild animals: it is an attention-management method needed to survive in the wild [#1370].

The author's development of this idea [#1370]:
- "Наша эпоха боится не боли, а тишины" (Our era fears not pain but silence). We have developed an almost biological intolerance of pauses, slowing down and boredom, and fear emptiness as if something unbearable were in it — possibly a sign not of progress but of a step back on the evolutionary path. [#1370]
- Multitasking, scatteredness and constant connectedness are not signs of a developed consciousness but of an organism forced to constantly survive. In the wild, an animal must distribute attention among different background activities: while eating or mating, its attention must scan the background for danger, so it cannot sink into contemplation, which requires full focus. In the wild there is no contemplation, no slow sensitivity — only survival. [#1370]
- In the modern world "гнезда расслабления" (nests of relaxation) — moments of deep contemplative attention — are disappearing. With relaxation "уходит дар прислушиваться" (the gift of listening goes away), which is inaccessible to hyperactive attention; the "сообщество слушающих" (community of listeners) disappears. [#1370]
- The paradox: with sensor systems, iPhones and plans a year ahead, in the structure of our attention we become ever closer to the world of wild animals. Our attention does not deepen but scatters; we see more but live through less reality, which becomes a torn ribbon stitched from fragments of tasks, links and chats. Consciousness essentially lives in the mode of an "тревожного сканера" (anxious scanner). [#1370]
- What makes a human human is exactly the opposite — the capacity for contemplative attention. The person who creates the new, who creates culture, is one who can slow down; who can not merely survive but be present, befriending boredom and letting go of automatic perception. "Чистая суматоха на создает ничего нового" [sic] (Pure bustle creates nothing new) — it reproduces and accelerates what already exists. [#1370]
- Boredom is not a nasty emptiness to be got rid of quickly, but merely a quality of our attention, for which it is painful to stop jumping from object to object. Boredom does not stop perception; it becomes the threshold where we can change it, suspending habitual automatism and the regression into the consciousness of the beast. [#1370]
- The author links this to Theory U, which says the new emerges not from automatically loading patterns of the past but only from a state of presence that allows noticing and combining many more perspectives; he says returning nests of relaxation and creating new communities of listeners describes what deep mind does in its Resonance groups. [#1370]
- Conclusion: contemplation and listening may be the very essence distinguishing human consciousness from that of all other species. "Мы не можем вернуть их себе усилием воли, но можем перестать им мешать ускорением и многозадачностью" (We cannot get them back by willpower, but we can stop hindering them with acceleration and multitasking). We can allow ourselves not to know, not to be efficient, not to be productive — and in that begin to be again. [#1370]

### Evidence and numbers

- Human brain perception: 4 to 40 bits of information per second on average (as stated by the author) [#240].
- Information Overload Research Group: attention overload causes the loss of 25% of people's working time annually, costing the US economy $997 billion (as cited by the author) [#240].
- Rogers & Monsell, 1995: rapid task switching reduces accuracy, increases errors and harms work quality [#240].
- Focus training: even 5 min helps; ideal 25+ min daily; results evident after 10–14 days with correct technique [#253].
- Adult ADHD prevalence 2–4% in developed countries; comorbid mental disorders in 80% of ADHD cases; trauma/nervous-system regulation problems in 50–70% of adults; СОДВ likely affects ~100% of active technology users (author's estimate) [#709, #1190].
- Potential Project: 37% average workplace mind-wandering; 2–3× more under stress; mindfulness practitioners 28% vs non-practitioners 52%; 7 vs 6 hours of sleep 35% vs 41%; connected vs lonely 30% vs 48% [#824].
