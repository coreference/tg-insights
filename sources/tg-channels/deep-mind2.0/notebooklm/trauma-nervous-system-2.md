## Self-regulation techniques for stress and anxiety

### What this covers

This file collects every concrete, body-based self-regulation practice the deep mind channel taught between 2021 and 2026: breathing patterns, EFT tapping (alone and in a sequence with a meditation on "me, mine, to me"), grounding and centering, a somatic touch-and-movement practice, noticing "glimmers", short "autonomy clicks" for hyperactivation, a supported resting position, micro-movements for freeze, and group chanting (kirtan). For each it gives the mechanism the channel offers, the dose and frequency, and the safety caveats. According to the channel, changing the state of the nervous system is what lets us deal with the hardest emotions and resolves many symptoms — emotional breakdowns, strong stress, insomnia, addictions, unfocus, aggression and chronic fatigue — and such practices must be applied regularly, not just known about [#688, #1696].

### Core ideas

- Breath is a direct lever on emotion and on the nervous system. Inhale, breath-holding and exhale each do something different emotionally; breathing acts on the vagus nerve, and a lengthened exhale moves us toward relaxation (see *Somatics in 5 minutes* and the breathing methods) [#431, #444].
- Undischarged stress does not disappear. When stress really hits, we have no access to logic or good decisions; talking ourselves into calm almost doesn't work, and enduring or distracting leaves tension in the body that can become a permanent background of anxiety, irritability and insomnia. Working with stress is therefore not about ceasing to feel emotions but about training the nervous system not to get stuck in reactions and to return faster to safety at the level of physiology [#1696].
- Turning toward the feeling, not away from it. Fear "will not leave while we hold it back"; tapping addresses the disturbing feeling directly instead of fleeing into thoughts, work or addictions; grounding helps "digest" emotions instead of locking them in a box [#431, #889, #1022].
- Short and frequent beats long and rare for overloaded people. Autonomy clicks of 1–3 minutes "often do more than a long practice" for people with overload; short practices are recommended several times a day for at least two weeks [#1640, #1767].
- The nervous system reads bodily safety, not only events. We keep protective tension in the body even when the threat is gone; the nervous system orients by whether there is effort, support and safety in bodily experience — so supported positions and gentle micro-movements send it a signal that nothing needs to be held or controlled [#1704, #1743].
- Relaxation is a precondition for happiness and depth. Tension without relaxation creates pain; a tense body makes attention jump, perception superficial and decisions poor; "for happiness you need an exhale" [#1635].
- Glimmers are cues of safety, not toxic positivity. Noticing moments of safety and connection is itself a healing practice that builds resources without cutting off difficult experience [#1286].
- Practices must be scientifically grounded and ethical. Tools should be rooted in a modern understanding of the nervous system and the ethics of somatic work, recorded with clear goals and language and with awareness of the risk of retraumatization [#1696, #1767].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Somatics in 5 minutes — the three phases of breath
Purpose: use observation and conscious change of breathing for self-regulation in specific situations. The author lists three functions of breath [#431]:
1. Inhale — "Вдох помогает полнее проживать эмоции" (The inhale helps to live through emotions more fully). Breathing in opens us to feeling what is present [#431].
2. Breath-hold — "Задержка дыхания помогает отстраниться от эмоций и не чувствовать происходящего" (Holding the breath helps to distance oneself from emotions and not feel what is happening). Holding the breath is thus a way of blocking feeling [#431].
3. Exhale — "Выдох помогает расслабиться" (The exhale helps to relax) [#431].

How to apply (the author's worked example) [#431]:
1. Notice that your breath "caught" (дыхание перехватило).
2. Ask yourself: what is this reaction about right now? What emotions am I feeling?
3. Name the emotion — e.g., fear. Fear is unpleasant and we don't want to feel it, "but biologically everything is arranged so that it will not leave while we hold it back".
4. Instead of trying to flee from the fear, take a slightly deeper inhale and allow it to be in your experience.
5. Then make a conscious exhale to move from a collected (braced) state into a more relaxed one.

#### Breathing methods for activating the parasympathetic system

He claims breathing has a "wonderful" effect on the nervous system because it works directly with the vagus nerve, which he describes as running along the whole spine. He notes there are many breathing practices for completing the stress response and for general anxiety, and gives two for quick use [#444]:
1. "Breathing on 3 counts" (4-2-6). A calm, long inhale on 4 counts, a hold on 2 counts, an exhale on 6 counts. The point: "мы приходим к более длинному выдоху" (we arrive at a longer exhale) — the exhale is longer than the inhale [#444].
2. Deep nose inhale, slow mouth exhale with sound. Inhale deeply through the nose, exhale slowly through the mouth with a sound; with the sound we try to express relief and the letting go of tension as fully as possible. Pitfall (author's joke): don't do it without warning in an office next to colleagues [#444].

#### Two-technique sequence for fear and anger
Purpose: the author's own sequence for when one gets stuck in an intense version of fear or anger — emotions he calls frequent in today's world — and needs to change state quickly. He calls the two techniques "extremely simple and effective" [#889].
1. EFT tapping with trigger work — turning directly toward the disturbing feeling (described fully under *EFT tapping*) [#889].
2. Meditation softening the ego — "I, mine, to me" (я, мое, мне) — described below [#889].
Result claimed: if you do both practices in a row, anger and fear will stop disturbing us so strongly, and we can act from greater wholeness [#889].

#### "I, mine, to me" ego-softening meditation
Rationale: fear, anger and other unpleasant emotions are most directly connected with our focus of attention and the narrative at whose center "we, beloved" stand. The author quotes Wei Wu Wei, who explained the subtleties of deep Eastern teachings in the West in the 1960s: "почему мы несчастны? Потому что 99% времени мы думаем про себя и делаем что-то для себя" (why are we unhappy? Because 99% of the time we think about ourselves and do something for ourselves). Attentively noticing this pattern and letting it go weakens the impact of the emotional trigger and changes the state [#889].
Steps [#889]:
1. Sit in a meditation posture and, preferably out loud, repeat "я, мое, мне" (I, mine, to me), focusing on the sensations that arise in the process.
2. Do not evaluate or modify these sensations; simply notice them better and better, let them show, give them space.
3. Roughly once every 5–7 minutes stop the repetitions and observe the sensations from a wider field of attention — as if from the side, as is usual in observing the breath or other objects. Watch how the sensations change from being observed, give them room, let them go.
Meaning: a simple way to notice the natural egocentric pattern and free oneself from it a little; on the other hand, a spiritual practice exploring the hard question "who am I?" [#889].

#### Glimmer-noticing practice
Triggers vs glimmers. The author prefers viewing "trigger" from the standpoint of the nervous system, not only psychologically: a trigger is a stimulus that can instantly shift the nervous system from calm into the activation of defensive mechanisms; triggers are often linked to past traumatic experience. Working on ourselves, we can not only learn to be triggered less (losing resource in those moments) but also notice stimuli that support and create resource [#1286].
Definition. In 2018 clinician and trauma specialist Deb Dana drew attention to such stimuli and introduced the term glimmer (глиммер; the author suggests it can be rendered in Russian as проблеск, *a gleam*). Simply put, glimmers are subtle moments of presence and engagement with life that give us the feeling that everything is completely fine; we feel joy, pleasure, peace. They "calm" the brain's stress activity and send safety signals to the nervous system. They allow a healthy, non-avoidant switch from fixation on the difficulties of adult life into a more resourced state. From the nervous system's standpoint they are moments of evident safety and connection with the world — moments of regulated balance instead of the dysregulation (stress, anger, anxiety) habitual for many [#1286].
Individuality. Glimmers differ for each of us and can be external or internal. "Глиммер для одного — триггер для другого" (A glimmer for one is a trigger for another). Noticing glimmers is in itself a powerful healing and resource-giving practice [#1286].
Not toxic positivity, not distraction. Glimmers help balance the negative but should not be confused with toxic-positivity messages telling us to "focus on the good", which cut off an important part of direct experience; Dana stresses that this is not the point. They do not distract from problems the way alcohol or doomscrolling do; they create a sense of space and presence that ultimately helps gather resources and cope with difficulties better. Automatic social-media scrolling contributes to "disconnecting" from the body, so it cannot count as a glimmer — glimmers, on the contrary, return the sense of contact with the body and help feel safety at the bodily level. Unlike simple distractions or addictive behaviors used to muffle anxiety, glimmers make a person stronger and improve the state at the level of the nervous system, and so become a source of trauma healing [#1286].
Some universal glimmers [#1286]:
1. The feeling of warmth from the sun.
2. The smell of cut grass.
3. Contact with animals.
4. Watching your children.
5. A pleasant walk alone.
6. And, of course, self-regulation practices specially created to change the state of the nervous system.
How to practice: every day offers hundreds of glimmers — ask "which are yours today?" The practice means exploring your own glimmers and consciously working to see them more often; acting on the level of the nervous system, it will positively affect brain health, mood and general well-being [#1286].

#### Hyperactivation imbalance and "autonomy clicks"
Mechanism. The city and constant context-switching are, in the author's words, an ideal cocktail of stimuli to which the nervous system is forced to adapt: many people, noise, visual saturation, gadgets, fast tasks and constant switching, no personal territory, and changes of plans. The sympathetic system (activity) stays on longer than it should, and the parasympathetic (recovery) doesn't switch on in time. Its functions suffer: sleep and recovery, digestion, mood, the sense of safety. At some point a person can still "drive", but has long been out of optimal mode. The image card says: "Мы живём в ритме, для которого нас не проектировали" (We live in a rhythm we were not designed for) — constant switching, work with people and tasks keep the nervous system in "combat readiness" [#1640]. The author frames this as "December basics" for not burning out at the end of the year [#1640].

Hyperactivation imbalance — 7 signs [#1640]:
1. You fall asleep normally but wake up at 4–6 a.m. for no reason.
2. There is energy to act, but no energy to recover.
3. The head constantly seeks stimuli (content / food / coffee).
4. Inner space disappears — "external signals" all the time.
5. Rest becomes strange: you're tired but it is hard to relax.
6. Background anxiety, irritability or a sense of being lost appears.
7. You want to lie down, but reach for the phone.

What autonomy clicks are. Small pauses of 1–3 minutes in which we: (1) for a second stop adapting to the environment; (2) stop following external signals; (3) return attention inward; (4) feel the body without evaluation. The author finds the name "clicks" very precise: they are light switches of the nervous system from reacting into a state of presence. For people with overload such clicks often do more than a long practice. No mat, silence or solitude needed — they can be done in a café, on the street, in a lift, between tasks, in a queue, in a taxi, anywhere [#1640].

Five ways to do a click [#1640]:
1. Through the body (1 minute): stop; feel your feet; take 5 long exhales; relax the jaw; close your eyes for 2 seconds.
2. Through the breath (10 cycles): 4 seconds inhale, 6 seconds exhale, 1 second pause.
3. Through the gaze (2 minutes): choose 3 objects; look at them slowly, 30 seconds each; notice colour, shape, texture, distance; become aware of yourself not in thoughts but in physical reality.
4. Through a question (30 seconds): ask "Что мне сейчас реально нужно?" (What do I really need right now?) and wait for a sensation — don't look for the answer with your head.
5. Through micro-silence (1–3 minutes): sit; do nothing; put the screen away; just be.

Dose and expected changes in 1–2 weeks. If you do clicks 3–5 times a day, the tone of the whole system "may change" (the author's hedge), leading toward [#1640]:
1. falling asleep more easily;
2. fewer night awakenings;
3. a sense of inner space appearing;
4. background anxiety going away;
5. less craving for stimulation (content, sweets, coffee);
6. being alone becomes easier;
7. less irritability;
8. better mood;
9. breathing normalizes;
10. HRV rises;
11. a sense of an "inner core" appears;
12. decisions are made more calmly and honestly;
13. the sense of "I" under the layer of adaptation is restored.
Clicks help regain control of natural rhythms even if life stays just as saturated [#1640].

#### Supported pelvis and legs rest position (for hyperactivation)
Context: many people now live in hyperactivation — the nervous system almost constantly scanning for danger and engaged in coping, enduring, adapting; even when outwardly calm, background tension remains. The author expects that in coming years we will have to work with this state systematically, not episodically [#1704].
Principle: in approaches from polyvagal theory to Somatic Experiencing there is the idea that bodily we often keep holding protective tension even when there is no immediate threat; the nervous system orients not only by events and thoughts but by the presence or absence in bodily experience of effort, of a sense of support and safety [#1704].
The technique [#1704]:
1. When: add to the evening routine, or use at any moment of the day when overload is felt.
2. Position: lying down with full support of the pelvis and legs — e.g., a bolster or dense pillow under the pelvis and thighs. In restorative practices and body-oriented therapy such positions are used precisely because they remove gravitational load and reduce the need to hold anything with the muscles.
3. Duration and attitude: 3–10 minutes, with no goal, no "correct breathing" and no task to relax — just full physical support and no demands on yourself.
4. What may happen: in Peter Levine's understanding this creates conditions for lowering muscle activation and gently discharging accumulated tension. Sometimes this shows as slight involuntary contractions or micro-movements in the pelvis and lower back — what body approaches describe as trauma discharge patterns. Nothing needs to be deliberately provoked; it is enough to let the body be in the pose and not interfere.
Framing: "Это не переключатель и не быстрый нейрохак" (This is not a switch and not a quick neurohack) — rather a gentle signal to the nervous system that right now nothing needs to be done, held or controlled. The author says there are scientific data that under such conditions protective muscle tension decreases, breathing deepens by itself without effort, parasympathetic regulation increases, and the subjective sense of anxiety may decrease. Such bodily "islands of safety" are not a solution to all problems but an important element of basic nervous-system hygiene [#1704].

#### Micro-movement for freeze (30–60 seconds)
Freeze vs laziness. Freeze is the other pole of the same reality as hyperactivation (hypoactivation), and we habitually call it laziness. From outside they look the same: can't start, procrastinate, lie around, feel dull, as if "no fuel". But inside they differ: "Freeze — это не *мне не хочется*, а *мне небезопасно*" (Freeze is not "I don't want to" but "it's unsafe for me") — not a lack of motivation but a protective shutdown when the system sees no safe path to action [#1743].
Principle: as in polyvagal theory (Porges) and Somatic Experiencing, we bodily keep our defenses even without immediate threat; the nervous system orients not only by actual events but by whether its present experience includes support, backing and the possibility of *not* holding the defense [#1743].
Steps [#1743]:
1. When: any moment you feel everything is "stuck" and you can't move forward.
2. For 30–60 seconds add a gentle micro-movement with a quality of attention and kindness toward your sensations and yourself — not to "pull yourself together" but to give the system a minimal signal of life and safety. Options given: move your feet; gently clench and unclench your palms; slightly turn your neck; feel the support of your back; stretch.
3. Check: see whether at least 10% more access to the thought "I can start" appears. Sometimes this is enough to make it visible that the problem is not laziness but a state of unsafety in which the nervous system is stuck.
4. If it gets worse — stop. If panic, nausea, derealization or simply a sense of "too much" rises, "не нужно героизма" (no heroics needed). Stop. Look around. Find support in the sensations in the feet/back area. Sometimes the best step is not to push through but to give yourself time and space.
5. Meet the underlying need. Finding yourself in freeze is almost always a sign the nervous system needs support — sometimes obvious: sleep, food, warm water, fewer inputs, a pause from decisions — not as a reward for productivity but as a response to the need for regulation, without which clarity and will simply don't switch on.
Framing: not a switch or a productivity neurohack, but a gentle signal that nothing needs to be proven, held or controlled now, yet one can live and be in motion; sometimes such a signal opens access to unfreezing [#1743].

### Detailed insights

#### Kirtan: group chanting as multi-level nervous-system restoration
The author went to a kirtan — a deep meditative practice of group chanting — and argues it can be maximally useful for working with trauma and stress because the nervous system recovers on several levels at once: (1) coordinating voices with other people produces co-regulation of the nervous system; (2) voice vibrations act on the vagus, which he calls the longest nerve running the length of the spine; (3) through breathwork the body frees itself from stress; he adds there are "many more benefits" he does not detail [#647].
His experience (spring 2022, during the war) carries a lesson about what remains unprocessed: as soon as the singing began, tears flowed and his chest tightened. Although he regularly saw a psychotherapist, did sport and meditated, "my body and psyche do not know or understand how to live through war" — they had faced many difficulties and conflicts, but not the spectacle of terrible, unstoppable violence that one doesn't know how to place inside and live through. "все непрожитое догонит меня рано или поздно" (everything not lived through will catch up with me sooner or later). He wept for the dying, for soldiers, for those who steal or kill, for lost savings, homes and cities, for Russia, for disoriented friends and emigrants, for the leader's madness, and bitterly for his own powerlessness and sense of not coping with the world's complexity [#647].
The lesson he draws: at some point he realized that the warm voices around him were turning him toward all these feelings, gradually connecting him with other people and with life in its bright manifestation while he was lost in the dark one — and against that background suffering and his own difficult emotions became more manifest (more available to be felt). Singing with kind people means that even amid death, love and joy continue around. "Жизнь прорастет через любой бетон" (Life will grow through any concrete). By opening the heart toward the most terrible suffering, we find our own depth and strength to turn toward the darkest parts of the soul — deepest fears and old pain — which make us callous, unable to feel ourselves or others, and so neglected that they make us forget the sacredness of life. In those dark parts he finds powerlessness before life and awareness of the limits of his power over it; this, he says, is a step toward healing from the fear that makes us feel separate and stuck in war with others. His exhortation: look for life in yourself even when all around is death [#647].

#### Why deep mind built a neuro-regulation toolkit
The author's account: research by leading psychiatrists, physiologists and neuroscientists (Peter Levine, Bessel van der Kolk, Gabor Maté, Stephen Porges — spelled "Поджерс" in the post) led to new-wave therapeutic approaches (IFS, EMDR, DBT, Somatic Experience) and shifted psychotherapy toward working with the body, the nervous system and past trauma. Changing the state of the nervous system is what helps us deal with the hardest emotions and resolves many symptoms: emotional breakdowns, strong stress, insomnia, addictions, unfocus, aggression and chronic fatigue [#688].
The problem deep mind sees: most psychotherapy methods have not yet integrated these discoveries; they cannot teach nervous-system regulation skills because they don't pay attention to the body, don't know what different nervous-system reactions look like, and have no tools for working with them. The author states it is "proven" that talking is often not enough to feel better and move forward in relieving traumatic experience that shows up in today's behavior and perception. Mindfulness practices definitely help regulate the nervous system, but often don't work because of their complexity and the need for strict discipline. As a result people lack accessible, systematic ways to learn regulation [#688].
deep mind's response, per the post: adapting advanced, science-based neuro-regulation practices accessible to everyone into a single flow; the practices help quickly pull oneself out of emotional overload and fairly easily and consistently build subtle regulation skills; participants do them independently 10–15 minutes a day, whenever and wherever convenient [#688]. (The product name and purchase link are promotional and are not reproduced here as knowledge.)

#### Case: tapping after a motorbike accident (Zhenya Kozlov)
Framing by the author: when stress really happens (accidents, conflicts, sudden news, threat to loved ones, overload) the nervous system goes into survival mode: muscles contract, breathing becomes shallow, and inside there is a feeling of "as if I've been switched off" or, conversely, "I'm about to explode". People most often do two things: (1) try to talk themselves into calming down — which almost doesn't work; (2) simply endure or distract themselves hoping it passes. The story is "not about a miracle or healing from stress in 10 minutes" but an example of how one can correctly and carefully help the nervous system come out of shock after the event [#1696].
The case (shared with the author's permission): Zhenya, his wife and small daughter fell on their side in a not very strong motorbike collision; when he lifted his daughter her face was bloody and she was crying hard. At the hospital it turned out only her lip was cut inside. At home the wife put the child to bed while he walked in circles, unable to settle — the full shock reached him only at home: everything inside pulsed and his body was stone-like, "one big clenched lump". He remembered tapping, did the trigger-work practice and was noticeably released — "as if half the stress came out in 12 minutes of practice". After walking a bit more he repeated with the complex practice; subjectively, 70–80% of the remaining half went away. "The strongest stress passed almost completely in less than half an hour." Since then he has regularly used tapping for a couple of years in all kinds of stressful situations, and describes it as "always guaranteed relief" (his own testimonial) [#1696].
Lessons: the shock can arrive delayed, after the danger has passed; a trigger-focused tapping session followed by a fuller session can be stacked; the effect is reported subjectively by one user [#1696].

#### Using short practices with teams under daily stress (Dasha's notes)
In a forwarded post, Dasha (an HR leader whose team was handling a sudden crisis in autumn 2022) shares what she relies on: people and simple mindfulness exercises. She began using calming exercises more often herself and offered them to teams that work every day with other people's anxiety and problems. Under daily stress, short breathing and EMDR-type practices help — for example EFT Tapping. For teams, they combine these practices with a shared round of emotions and events of the day to relieve anxiety; personally, one can use such exercises in the morning and evening to give the body and nervous system regular support [#741].
Her second support is people: reading and listening to those whose views are close to her. She cites Marina Melia — professor of psychology, business coach, chair of the board of the children's hospice "Дом с маяком" — on how important it is to be where you are useful, especially where it is very painful and you have the strength to shine, and that solving hard problems is an important conscious choice: "До тех пор, пока ты жив, ты можешь решать в своей жизни сам" (As long as you are alive, you can decide for yourself in your life). Dasha's conclusions: "Свет запускается действием" (Light is launched by action) — action where you clearly understand what you are doing and why; for her, happiness and meaning are simply being useful and doing what she must; she urges readers who feel similarly to drive out doubts and help loved ones, friends and colleagues every day with warmth and a kind word. She also acknowledges that when everything becomes black and white, people often lack strength even to care for themselves [#741].

#### The "exhale": relaxation as a precondition for happiness
The author restates what he calls an obvious, often repeated but always relevant point. Participants returning from a Himalayan trip reported that their massage therapists said their bodies were more relaxed than ever; on the trip they both moved a lot and practiced mindfulness daily, returning attention to the body [#1635].
His own observation: after a good night's sleep — which made his attention less inclined to jump out of unpleasant bodily experience and escape into imaginary objects — he sat alone and started with the basics: detailed balancing of body and mind. These are preliminary attunement practices: going through a sequence of actions in the body, loosening inner chatter and relaxing the mind, all without losing alertness and clarity, without falling into a stupor or sleep. He holds that such practices, easy to mistake for something unimportant, can and should be done for years, especially in our modern situation, and for most of us are far more useful than everything else — especially dangerous ones like tantric "swinging" of some "energy" (his opinion). At the end of a half-hour sit he managed to relax very well while keeping clarity [#1635].
That evening, washing his hands and looking at the water, he exhaled so that the whole body relaxed following the air leaving the lungs; consciousness seemed to fill the body to every cell, and he felt pleasure. He wondered how often such exhales happen in his life per day. His observation: many of us live as if without them at all, probably allowing ourselves to relax and let go only when drinking alcohol or in deep dreamless sleep — in perpetual tension [#1635].
Consequences of living without exhales, as he observes in himself: he becomes a "sad person in every sense"; attention in a tense body constantly jumps because it is uncomfortable to be with one's own experience — "Напряжение без расслабления создает боль!" (Tension without relaxation creates pain!). It becomes much harder to see any issue or process in depth; perception becomes superficial, followed closely by quick conclusions, bad relationships and poor decisions. Beyond productivity there is subjective well-being: in perpetual tension, "in which even iron breaks", he feels unhappy, which he calls normal and logical. Conclusion: "Для счастья нужен выдох" (For happiness you need an exhale) — a relaxation in which attention can settle in the body and not run to the phone [#1635].

#### Short "moments of switching on" as an entry point to presence (Resonance)
deep mind shares a 4-minute attention and self-regulation practice from the opening pack of a presence-skills track in its group process Resonance, where getting out of the habitual mode of reactivity and automatism is a key task. At the very beginning, before longer practices, they give short moments of "switching on" that come easily to beginners and to the modern person overloaded with stimulation who finds turning attention to their present experience painful [#1767].
Why presence matters for leadership (Theory U): holistic leadership that helps act in new, nonlinear circumstances and get out of stuck states requires presence — the capacity to be maximally engaged in current experience and to withstand one's own emotions and those of other people. This yields important new information absent from the linear habitual view of tasks, which becomes a "game-changer". Presence is reached not through magic spells and affirmations, and not by itself, but through disciplined attention training and developing state-regulation skills. To stop permanently jumping out of one's body and real experience, one has to reduce stress, learn to contain difficult emotions and grow the capacity to be with any experience — all implemented at the level of physiology (the nervous system) through such mini-practices and training in real situations [#1767].
Dose: short practices suit both beginners and experienced practitioners; recommended several times a day — 3, 5 or 10 times, as comfortable — and for at least two weeks to understand what results the practice can give. The practice creates "a small island of regulation" and needs only 4 minutes [#1767].

### Practical playbook

Quick selector, based on the methods above:
- Breath caught / avoiding a feeling → deeper inhale to allow it, then a conscious exhale (Somatics in 5 minutes) [#431].
- General stress or anxiety, need a fast reset → 4-2-6 breathing, or the 4-6-1 breath click (10 cycles), or 3-minute Centering [#444, #730, #1640].
- Fear and panic → EFT tapping, 7 minutes; watch for a long exhale as the sign of shift [#588, #730].
- Stuck in intense fear or anger → tapping with trigger work, then the "I, mine, to me" meditation [#889].
- Acute shock after an event → trigger tapping (about 12 minutes), repeat with a fuller session if needed [#1696].
- Chronic overload, early waking, craving stimulation → autonomy clicks 3–5 times a day for 1–2 weeks; supported-pelvis rest 3–10 minutes in the evening [#1640, #1704].
- Can't start, "lazy", stuck → 30–60 seconds of kind micro-movement; if it gets worse, stop and find support in feet/back; meet basic needs [#1743].
- Building resources over time → notice glimmers daily; grounding 1–3 times a day for 2–3 weeks [#1022, #1286].
Frequency rules repeated across posts: EFT morning and evening or as needed; grounding 1–3/day for 2–3 weeks; clicks 3–5/day with effects expected in 1–2 weeks; short practices 3–10/day for at least two weeks; program practices 10–15 minutes a day [#588, #688, #1022, #1640, #1767].

### Evidence and numbers

- EFT tapping (studies cited by deep mind): the post says several studies have proven that EFT tapping reduces blood cortisol by 37–43%, anxiety by 40%, depression by 35%, post-traumatic stress by 32%, and increases happiness by 31%. The source does not name the populations or study designs; the claim is made in a promotional post [#688].
- Fitness-tracker data: one hour of yoga with attentive breathing: 0:59:42 total time, average pulse 88 bpm, 204 active kcal, 268 total kcal; the author reads the pulse graph as moving from arrhythmia to a calmer line (an n=1 personal observation) [#444].
- Zhenya's case: about half the stress released in 12 minutes of trigger tapping; 70–80% of the remainder after a second, complex practice; nearly full relief in under half an hour (subjective self-report) [#1696].
- Supported position: the author says scientific data show reduced protective muscle tension, spontaneous deepening of breath, increased parasympathetic regulation and possibly reduced subjective anxiety; no specific study is named [#1704].
- Autonomy clicks: expected effects include rising HRV, among the 13 changes listed under the method above; offered as possible outcomes ("may change"), not measured data [#1640].
- Corporate use: the author says the company Qiwi bought deep mind's practice set for all employees several years ago, and that people from Yandex, Avito and Sber use it [#1696].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- (!) INCOMPLETE: the EFT tapping instructions (points, phrases), the Centering practice, the grounding practices, Anna Titova's touch-and-movement session and the Resonance 4-minute practice are only in audio/video recordings that were not transcribed; the text conveys their purpose, mechanism and dose but not their scripts [#588, #730, #745, #1022, #1767].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the label "Дыхание на 3 счета" (breathing on 3 counts) is applied to a pattern with counts 4-2-6; "3" presumably refers to the three phases, but the source does not say [#444].
- (!) CONTRADICTION (minor): #444 gives a 4-in / 2-hold / 6-out pattern with the hold after the inhale, whereas the autonomy-click breath in #1640 is 4 in / 6 out / 1-second pause after the exhale. Both lengthen the exhale; the source does not reconcile them [#444, #1640].
- The breath-hold is described in #431 as a way of distancing from emotions and not feeling — i.e., a blocking mechanism — while the 4-2-6 breath includes a 2-count hold; the source does not discuss this [#431, #444].
- EFT is called "scientifically proven" and the percentage effects are cited in a promotional post for deep mind's own paid program; the author himself acknowledges in #1696 that the post is promo of his own system. The text gives no study details (populations, designs) [#588, #688, #1696].
- EFT/EMDR terminology is loose in the source: deep mind's tapping adds "a little bilateral stimulation from EMDR", and Dasha calls EFT Tapping an example of "EMDR-practices" [#588, #741].
- The author's hedges: the motorbike story is "not about a miracle or healing from stress in 10 minutes"; supported rest and micro-movement are "not a switch and not a quick neurohack" and "not a solution to all problems"; clicks "may" change the system's tone [#1640, #1696, #1704, #1743].
- Safety: if a micro-movement practice brings panic, nausea, derealization or overwhelm, stop and orient to the surroundings and to support in the feet/back rather than pushing through. Grounding techniques are chosen to be trauma-safe (no breakthroughs or disclosures), and deep mind's recordings are designed with the risk of retraumatization in mind [#1022, #1696, #1743].
- The author's opinion that preliminary body-mind balancing practices are more useful for most people than others, and that tantric "energy swinging" practices are dangerous, is stated without further argument [#1635].
- Kirtan's benefits (co-regulation, vagal effect of vibration) are the author's claims tied to a personal experience; he mentions further benefits without describing them [#647].
