## Relationships, family systems and parenting

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's thinking on relationships as the place where inner work becomes real: the evolution from role-based marriage and family toward conscious partnership and "pure relationships"; why a family system pushes back when one member changes, and what a person lives through emotionally while separating from it; how parents protect (or harm) a child's nervous system and what the child needs most from a parent; a relational contemplative practice (Golden Eyes); and how to support loved ones in a hard period. The author's recurring claim is that relationships, not hours of meditation or mastered theories, are the main indicator of whether a person or a community is actually developing [#1507, #1618].

This part adds two posts to the topic. #1878 is part 3 of the author's series "Чистые отношения" (Pure relationships); the existing file already covers part 1 (#1820: Giddens' pure relationships, Bowen's homeostasis and the "change back" reaction) and part 2 (#1841: the four stages of separating from a loyalty system). Part 3 turns to "parents with whom it is hard" and asks whether the widespread breakdown of adult children's relationships with parents is post-Soviet or universal; the answer is "both", and the post explains each layer sociologically. #1921 is a personal-experience post about couples therapy that extends the topic toward conscious partnership: how a couple finds the right therapist, what therapy gives (reducing helplessness, restoring agency) and Gottman's data on help-seeking delay and perpetual conflicts. According to the author, the closer a relationship, the more it influences life, well-being, understanding of self and others and movement toward goals, so tension accumulating there "can become fatal" [#1878, #1921].

### Core ideas

- Relationship forms evolve as adaptations. Marriage and the role-based family were built for conditions where people could not survive alone; today those conditions have changed, so partnership can move from survival and automatic reproduction to conscious partnership, and family ties shift toward "pure relationships" held together by what they give to both participants (see *Three functions of conscious partnership* and *Pure relationships*) [#221, #1820].
- The family is a system that defends its homeostasis. When a member changes, the system responds with a "change back" reaction; this is a systemic problem, not the personal failure of the one who changes (see *Family-system mechanisms*) [#1820].
- Separation from a loyalty system is lived through as loss and passes through stages - getting worse first, guilt without a crime, waves of bitterness and anger, and grief for the fantasy parents - which are the price of leaving, not signs of a mistake (see *Four stages*) [#1841].
- What protects a child from stress is warmth the child actually perceives, not the parents' self-assessment (see *Positive parenting*) [#888]; and the core of parenthood is being "for" the child as a separate living person (see *Be for the child*) [#1462].
- Relationships are the main indicator of inner growth: if our path does not make us more capable of realizing interdependence with parents, children, friends, colleagues and communities, other achievements are largely nullified [#1507, #1618].
- Real support means being near without simplifying or running away; most failures of support come from regulating ourselves through our own old pain strategies instead of being present with the other (see *Supporting loved ones*) [#1705].

- The rupture with difficult parents has two layers. It is a global process (family has stopped being an obligation) overlaid with a specific post-Soviet context (compressed modernization). This extends #1820's thesis that ruptures are a systemic problem rather than personal failure, now adding macro-sociological causes (see *Two layers of the rupture with difficult parents*) [#1878].
- The family has lost its survival functions, so tolerating destruction is no longer rational. Pensions, medicine and rented housing replaced what the family used to provide; what remains is either dry formality or a pure relationship that only lasts while it gives both sides something alive [#1878].
- A good relationship is not a conflict-free one. Per Gottman's research as cited by the author, about 69% of couple conflicts are perpetual and never solved; good relationships rest instead on trust, each side's authorship and the ability to hear both the other and oneself (see *Gottman's findings*) [#1921].
- Couples therapy reduces helplessness and returns agency. Close relationships accumulate a "bucket" of negative thoughts about the partner and zones of helplessness; therapy with the right specialist lowers the intensity of that helplessness and becomes a resource window rather than a consumed service [#1921].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Three functions of conscious partnership

Context and reasoning. In a post forwarded from the channel reconnect, titled "Sodom and Gomorrah on the way to conscious partnership", Maxim Timofeev describes how partnership is transforming. He observes that some people of his generation consciously choose to be childfree: working through the burden of their own childhood traumas, they do not feel they have enough resources, including psychological ones, to raise happy children. He calls this conclusion not devoid of common sense - it is easy to reach if you are in the now-mainstream field of modern science and work on your own healing from neurosis, trauma and emotional disorders. He also notes strong changes in the institution of marriage (a growing number of people see no point in it), growing openness of the queer community worldwide and a surge of interest in polyamory [#221].

From a fixed traditionalist worldview all this must cause gloom, fear and look like a devilish process of society decaying on the way to judgment day; from an evolutionary point of view it all looks natural: «Грешников в итоге не покарают, ибо не грешники они вовсе.» (The sinners will not be punished in the end, for they are not sinners at all.) [#221]

His definition: «Эволюция — это адаптация к изменениям и расширение сложности, многообразия и диапазона.» (Evolution is adaptation to change and an expansion of complexity, diversity and range.) Any living system either adapts to changes in its environment or dies. The institution of marriage ("what a terrible word applied to partnership", he adds) in its usual form was created when a person could not survive alone. Much later came the substantial emancipation of women and growth of their professional opportunities. Today people can live (even live well) independently of each other; single people provide themselves with a normal existence. Hence the natural question: why keep forms of relationship that have stopped performing their functions and were created under other conditions? This is a convenient moment for evolutionary adaptation and for changing the goals of partnership - to move from survival mode and automatic playing-out of biological reproduction functions into conscious partnership [#221].

The functions:

1. Healing from childhood traumas (first function). Relationships of support and care are therapeutic. Some partners can give each other the most important elements of health that they did not get enough of from their parents: belief in one's normality, support, acceptance [#221].
2. Joint growth and development (second function). It rests on the first, because belief in our adequacy and unconditional love are the main bricks of support for unfolding our potential in the world. If both partners choose constant development, each supports the other's process, and together they create a dynamic environment in which change is encouraged [#221].
3. A spiritual function - if the people feel such a need. With or without children, relationships can offer a person a place to transcend their own egoistic needs. In sincere care for another's development we serve our partner or children without expecting anything in return. This creates a point of applying effort toward something larger than the separate self and connects us with spiritual experiences [#221].

The author's conclusion (hedged: «Кажется» - *it seems*): at this stage of society's development there is more meaning in conscious partnership than in traditional marriage. It lets a person finally stop simply acting out social projections and automatic programs, pulls them outside other people's expectations of a "biological norm", allows them to realize themselves deeply according to their unique nature - and in addition to raise healthy, happy children. If humanity needs various experiments with intimacy on this path, they should have the right to exist [#221].

#### Pure relationships and the family-system mechanisms behind "change back"

Pure relationships. Sociologist Anthony Giddens described a historical shift to *pure relationships* - ties that are held not by roles, tradition and duty but by what they give to both participants. The author calls this a radically new model, while our ancestors' systems were shaped over centuries for a different one: roles, hierarchy, «так положено» (that's how it's supposed to be). "We are literally the first or second generation" making this transition en masse and facing its consequences as large-scale conflicts and ruptures in families [#1820].

Why it is not just "egoism". It is easiest to write such things off as generational quirks, egoism and Western values, but development is almost always adaptation to changing living conditions. The author says that 5-10 years ago this was not obvious to many, but today it seems to him personally a very clear fact that the role model with clear prescriptions becomes more maladaptive every day to a world of many-times-increased speed, complexity and uncertainty - it was literally built in other conditions and for other people. Whoever is adaptive to change, rather than closing off from it with psychological defenses, senses where things are going and rebuilds strategies for emerging realities; such a person is essentially forced to change the "norm", using appropriate tools - for example psychotherapy and development practices, which become a technical instrument for the transition. This leads to a more subject-like (agentic) and adult position, where you, not the role, family, God or state, are responsible for your life and emotions. It is a next level of freedom, often paid for with broken relationships with the family if the family is firmly stuck in the traditional model [#1820].

Such ruptures are a systemic problem, not a personal failure of those trying to live differently. "You simply wanted to live your own way" and got the reaction described by Harriet Lerner - «вернись обратно» (change back) - in the form of inadequate resentment, manipulation and accusations of betrayal; and if that does not work, other family members are mobilized. Behind this reaction stand well-described mechanisms [#1820]:

1. The family is a system, and the system protects itself (Murray Bowen). Bowen, founder of family systems theory, showed that a family functions as a single emotional organism with its own homeostasis. When one member changes - starts living differently, setting boundaries, leaving the habitual role - the system perceives this not as that person's development but as a threat to its own stability, and launches the change-back reaction: pressure, devaluation, resentment, mobilization of relatives. This is not the malice of specific people but «иммунный ответ системы на изменение» (the system's immune response to change). And it hardly depends on how precisely and calmly you explained everything [#1820].
2. Roles versus differentiation (Bowen). Bowen's key concept is differentiation: the ability to remain yourself while in contact with the family - «Не слиться и не сбежать — быть отдельным рядом.» (Not to merge and not to flee - to be separate while near.) In low-differentiation systems separateness simply isn't provided for: your decisions, money, things and life are perceived as shared, and any boundary reads as betrayal, because in the system's worldview boundaries between "our own" do not exist at all. That is why even a child's mildest "no" is read as a blow to the whole [#1820].
3. Invisible bookkeeping (Ivan Boszormenyi-Nagy). The author of contextual therapy described an invisible book of debts and merits that the family system keeps across generations. In such systems help is not a gift but an entry in the book. The terms of the entry are rarely voiced at the moment of help; they are presented later, when you decide to live not as expected. «Мы для тебя все, а ты...» (We [did] everything for you, and you...) is the voice of the bookkeeping, not of genuine relationship. In this way the system creates unconscious debts of the individual toward it, which a person can repay all their life without understanding why it is so hard to live freely. Therefore any attempt to simply accept care turns into a new bill [#1820].

#### Four stages of separating from a family loyalty system

Purpose. Part 2 of "Pure relationships": what a person goes through from the inside when separating from a family system. The author notes that psychotherapists and many texts on separation and psychological adulthood describe what to *do* - «ставь границы, не входи в треугольник, говори напрямую» (set boundaries, don't enter the triangle, speak directly) - but almost nobody says how leaving a loyalty system is experienced from within, and this gap often leads to painful U-turns halfway. So this text is not about boundary-setting technique but about the stages to be passed [#1841].

1. Stage one: it gets heavier, not easier. Setting boundaries, leaving triangulations and speaking directly really are the right actions and can give relief in the moment. But done consistently, the person may soon feel much worse than before - they end up in conflict and lose support. Why: triangulation and merging, for all their toxicity, performed a function - they connected us to the system, gave belonging and, paradoxically, took away part of the anxiety. While you're inside the game, you at least know the rules and your place. Leaving means losing that stability, and the body experiences it precisely as loss: «На физиологическом уровне сепарация почти неотличима от утраты.» (On the physiological level separation is almost indistinguishable from loss.) Insomnia, exhaustion and a feeling that things have become bad often appear. «Это не сигнал об ошибке, а цена выхода.» (This is not a signal of a mistake but the price of exit.) [#1841]
2. Stage two: guilt without a crime. The person did nothing wrong - set a normal boundary - but feels like a traitor. This is not conscience but the internalized voice of the system, sometimes spoken directly: «ты же сын, не обижай мать, мы же семья» (you're the son, don't hurt your mother, we're family). The system teaches us to feel guilty for separateness before we learn to walk, so often the first reaction to a healthy act is "I'm bad". The author calls this the most dangerous stage, because this is where people turn back: they take the guilt as proof that the boundary was a mistake. It is not; guilt is also part of the price of exit and passes with time [#1841].
3. Stage three: bitterness and anger. They come not on schedule but in waves, sometimes for years. Bitterness at how many years and how much strength went into a meaningless struggle and utterly unproductive conflicts; that support was absent when it was most needed; that time was lost; «Что все живы, но нет отношений.» (That everyone is alive, but there is no relationship.) This stage is unpleasant but more constructive than guilt, because more strength for useful action appears. Therapists say it is important not to skip it - anger must be lived through, not suppressed - and not to get stuck in it: anger that does not move turns into chronic bitterness, and then you risk repeating the very relatives who have been walking in circles of resentment for thirty years [#1841].
4. Stage four: grief. The main knot of transformation. Grief is not the worst stage but the one after which it gets easier, because you are grieving not the real parents but the fantasy of them - the ones who could have heard and accepted you. While this fantasy is alive, you are tied to the system by hope, and every contact becomes in some form a new bet in the game, a new attempt to get through. When the fantasy is mourned, something strange happens: the real family stops hurting so much - not because they change, but because you stop expecting what isn't in them. «Горе — это то, что отвязывает» (Grief is what unties), and in a sense this is why it should be welcomed [#1841].

#### Positive parenting and the child-perception rule

The author says that in recent years he feels increasingly uneasy around parents who shout at, ignore or devalue their children's feelings. With ever clearer data on brain and nervous-system development, we have come to understand that such behaviour differs little from physical violence and seriously affects a person's adult life [#888].

Evidence the author cites:
- A review he links: children who end up in a stress reaction but do not get support from significant adults may face a reduction in the volume of the hippocampus, which is responsible for learning and memory [#888].
- A "new wave of research" proves not only the harmful effect of various kinds of violence on human physiology but also the reverse: the positive parenting model can protect a child from the effects of stress [#888].
- A study (PNAS Nexus, linked) found that stress did not lead to reduction of brain tissue in children who reported more warmth from their caregivers. Positive parenting also changed behaviour: such children broke rules and were aggressive less often [#888].

Components of positive parenting as defined in the post: various warm and supportive methods such as (1) praise for a job well done, (2) emotional support, (3) tenderness; and among the most important practices, (4) teaching children to recognize and name emotions, and (5) helping them live through emotions without external evaluation and judgment [#888].

The child-perception rule: the key role in the effect on the brain belongs to the child's perception, not the parents' assessment of their own parenting. Where parents said they were supportive and positive but the child did not see them that way, researchers found no protective effect [#888].

#### "Be for the child" - letter to all parents

Genre and framing. The author presents "A letter to all parents" as a special genre of expressive writing and reparative statement - not born of a book but from inside, from a layer of experience that many seem to share. It is impossible without real experience and living figures, but should not be taken literally. It is written from the inner point where we all once were - small, vulnerable, open, dependent on someone's gaze. It is neither a complaint nor an instruction: if you are a parent and pain, guilt or defensiveness rises, treat the text as a reminder, not a verdict, because «Почти каждый из нас бывал и тем, кто не получил, и тем, кто не смог дать.» (Almost every one of us has been both the one who did not receive and the one who could not give.) [#1462]

The teaching, in the child's voice:

1. The child's trust is an unconditional gift. The child appears inside the parent - in the body, breath, eyes and reactions - is completely dependent and immediately, unconditionally trusts, not because the parent earned it or did something right, but simply because «вы — мои» (you are mine). This trust is the child's main gift, the gift of birth and existence, arising not in the head but in the body, intuition, where attachment lives [#1462].
2. The relationship is sacred and unique. It is not just closeness but something sacred: a relationship in which the parent is given the unconditional faith of a whole universe simply by the fact of the child's appearance. It cannot be bought or built by willpower; it is a real, rare, irreversible privilege - to become, for a time, someone's whole world [#1462].
3. Parenting is hard - and still irreplaceable. It requires patience, attention, endless effort; sometimes it is sleepless nights, loss of self, inability to rest; sometimes unbearable. But you will have no other relationship like this, and if the main thing is lost in it, no diligence can replace the loss [#1462].
4. The main thing is the faith that you are "for" me. Not for an image, a result or your own anxiety - «За меня живого.» (For me, alive.) [#1462]
5. One can be near without being "for". One can care, feed, provide, say the right words - and inwardly be on the other side: for control, for shame, for fear, for your own need that the child conform or compensate for something you lacked. The child feels it - feels when there is warmth and when warmth disappears because the child became inconvenient, noisy, unmanageable, shameful [#1462].
6. "Being for" is not indulgence. It does not mean pandering or removing boundaries or permitting everything. «Быть за — это значит не предавать.» (To be for means not to betray.) It means seeing the child as a separate, living person; not manipulating love when the child does not conform; simply staying with the child when they are alive, complex, not as you want [#1462].
7. Love that preserves the gift is not a feeling but intentions, words and actions: the choice not to disappear, to choose the real child rather than your ideas about the world, to stay with the child when fear, anger, shame or resentment rises inside you [#1462].
8. The parent's own history is the parent's work. It is hard, especially if no one gave this to you: if no one was for you, learning to be for someone else is almost impossible. But the child is not to blame for your history and can never sort it out; it is your work - and admitting this honestly matters so the child does not become a parent to the parent [#1462].
9. Parenting begins with seeing a separate life. It starts not with effort and upbringing but with the moment you see in the child not a reflection of yourself but a separate life, and love it so as to preserve the trust - the child's original knowledge that someone is truly for them [#1462].
10. Mistakes are survivable if "I am for you" remains. One may not know much, make mistakes, lose one's temper; but if the simple «я за тебя» (I am for you) remains at the base of the relationship, there will be something to return to [#1462].
11. Repair is possible. To be a mother or father means to be the beginning of the child's world, a world that at first trusts you unconditionally. Do not betray it; and if you have betrayed it, even for years, the sacredness of the bond can still help restore trust - if you really risk being for the child [#1462].

#### Golden Eyes practice (Dustin DiPerna, from Ken Wilber)

Source and context. The author heard this practice in an interview with Dustin DiPerna, who said Ken Wilber once gave it to him; it concerns view (воззрение) and relationships. DiPerna, per the author, is one of the notable contemporary practitioners, theorists and teachers of Integral theory. The author places him among his favourite thinkers on human nature and development who look at these questions holistically - synthesizing, integral psychologists such as Ken Wilber, John Welwood and Bruce Tift - and stresses that, unlike many psychologists, they all practise various, often advanced, contemplative methods and speak about the person, states and psychic structures not only theoretically but phenomenologically, from direct experience: «Это все меняет.» (This changes everything.) [#1507]

Framing principle. Explaining Integral theory in the podcast, DiPerna says that all frameworks and maps should be put in second place, and in first place - our relationships: what we arrive at there, how we *relate* to relationships, how we are present in them, how we affect others. The author considers this the most important focus when applying methods of inner work, and notes the thought recurs in Tift and Wilber and runs as a red thread through Welwood. DiPerna stresses that the more successfully we progress in practice, the more obvious our connection with others and our influence on each other become [#1507].

The practice. Every time you are in any contact with another person, you try to see them as more developed than you on two scales [#1507]:

1. More present than me (the scale of states in Integral theory). Looking at the other as "more present", you as it were acknowledge that their gaze, their breathing, their contact with the moment are deeper than yours. Even if they are objectively irritated or confused, within the practice you accept them as a bearer of greater presence [#1507].
2. More mature than me (the scale of growing up / development of structures of consciousness in Integral theory). Maturity here is not just age or experience but the capacity of consciousness - the ability to hold more perspectives and act from values, not only instincts [#1507].

Three possible results:

1. If the person really is like that, we become more open to the possibility of growing through them - we increase their chances to influence our experience and pass on part of their wisdom [#1507].
2. If not - the person is fairly stuck in an ego position and their structures of consciousness are not very complex - our view can open possibilities for them. Through such a view of reality we create relationships that hold the other in the vision of their greater potential. «Мы видим свежим взглядом, взглядом потенциала, которые не запаковывает, а распаковывает.» (We see with a fresh gaze, a gaze of potential that does not pack up but unpacks.) [#1507]
3. The final paradox: when we see greater presence and maturity in others, both begin to unfold better in ourselves [#1507].

DiPerna says this practice ultimately had a huge impact on his relationships. The author's takeaway: shifting the optics to see greater presence and maturity in the other trains us to step out of habitual defensiveness and builds respect, openness and vision of potential into contact; it works both ways, on us and on the other; and practices stop being about oneself in a vacuum and become about connectedness with something larger - which is precisely the development Wilber, Welwood and Tift write about [#1507].

#### Two layers of the rupture with difficult parents

Purpose and starting observation. The author notices the same conversation repeating in his circle in recent years: friends aged 30-40, often accomplished, reflective and many years in therapy, share in confidential talk genuinely hard stories about their parents. They are almost always heavy: someone leaves home and disappears; someone constructs non-existent facts and tells relatives fabrications; someone drinks. Conversations do not help at all, influence seems impossible, and any attempt to explain oneself turns into conflict. He set out to understand whether this is "our post-Soviet specificity or is it like this everywhere?" and found: «Оказалось, и то, и другое.» (It turned out, both.) [#1878]

Layer 1 — Global: "Семья больше не обязательство" (Family is no longer an obligation).

1. The phenomenon is global. Estrangement of adult children from parents is a worldwide process. Sociologist Karl Pillemer of Cornell conducted the first national study on the subject and found that 27% of adult Americans live in estrangement from someone close. "Even where there was neither the Union nor the nineties, families crack along the same seam." Lesson: the post-Soviet experience is not the whole explanation [#1878].
2. The usual explanation - psychologization of culture - is true but superficial. A language of boundaries and manipulation has appeared, and behaviour that used to be called «ну батя у нас такой» (well, that's just how our dad is) has come to be perceived simply as unacceptable. The author grants this is true, but says language does not come from nowhere: underneath it lies a change in the structure of society [#1878].
3. Historically the family was about survival, not feelings. For most of history the family was about land, inheritance, working hands and care in old age, not about feelings and deep relationships. Leaving it was impossible - outside the family you physically did not survive - so tolerating destructive relationships was rational. Under such conditions one could do literally anything and behave in any way, often up to physical violence, and the family would endure it [#1878].
4. Modernity took away the family's functions. Now people increasingly refuse to tolerate and simply leave. Modernity, and even more postmodernity, took almost all of the family's functions: the pension replaced children as breadwinners, medicine replaced care by relatives, renting replaced the family home [#1878].
5. What remains: formality or a pure relationship. In place of those functions there remains only the possibility of a dry formality or of a new form of relationship that Anthony Giddens called *pure relationships* (the author refers back to his earlier post on them, i.e. #1820/#1841 in this topic). Such relationships hold only because both sides receive something alive and emotional in them, and «быстро скукоживаются от насилия и абъюза любого толка» (quickly shrivel from violence and abuse of any kind). This sharpens the earlier definition in #1820 (ties held by what they give both participants) by stressing their fragility under abuse [#1878].
6. The shift is hard to accept. "We have quite unexpectedly found ourselves in a situation where the family has stopped being an obligation." Understanding and admitting this is extremely difficult, because for centuries people lived under a different arrangement. On top of this global process lies the post-Soviet context [#1878].

Layer 2 — Post-Soviet: "Сжатая модернизация" (Compressed modernization).

1. The concept. The term was introduced by sociologist Chang Kyung-Sup studying South Korea, which went from an agrarian society to high-tech in a single generation. As a result Koreans got one of the sharpest intergenerational conflicts in the world: 30-year-olds live in a world of KPIs and therapy, their parents in a world of survival and duty. A value transition that took 3-4 generations in Europe happened there inside one family [#1878].
2. The post-Soviet parallel. "We are similar here": the mass restructuring toward capitalism and Western professional culture began within the author's lifetime, roughly 2005-2015, when modern business centres appeared. The career practice of previous generations was much more built on connections and loyalty than on technologies of work. The author stresses «Это не упрек» (This is not a reproach) - that is how the world was arranged in Soviet Russia and in the 90s [#1878].
3. A radically different level of complexity, invisible to parents. Building a career and business in the modern sense has to be done completely differently: modern culture places different demands on skills, speed and talent, and "we find ourselves at a radically different level of complexity, which is a complete blind spot for our ancestors" [#1878].
4. First-generation students and first-generation professionals. American sociology has the notion of *first-generation students* - the first in a family to make a class migration through education. It is harder for them because nobody passed on the codes of the new environment. The author says he and his friends are often such «профессионалы первого поколения» (first-generation professionals), who do not fit the habitual norms of the generations before [#1878].
5. Effect on family dynamics. This, too, radically changes family dynamics: where previously one could tolerate the destructive behaviour of relatives, now one cannot, because of the circumstances of the environment in which we exist [#1878].

#### Criteria of a fitting couples therapist (from the author's experience)

Context. The author writes that this year things have been difficult with his meditation, but inner work and development did not stop: it is one of his most stable periods in sport (when he can, he trains almost every day and feels much stronger and more energetic), and there was a lot of therapy - he tried a new individual therapist, and he and Dasha went to couples therapy [#1921].

How they found the therapist. The path was thorny: they found a suitable specialist only on the third attempt, trying again and again with many doubts and much uncertainty, because the process was new to them and they lacked reference points. They kept going through specialists until they found, in this relationship with a third person, enough safety and their own agency (субъектность). Lesson implied by the account: do not stop after one or two unsuitable therapists [#1921]. The qualities they were looking for:

1. Safety and own agency in the relationship with the third person. The couple must feel safe and remain subjects (authors) of their own choices in the triad with the therapist [#1921].
2. Non-judgmental acceptance. On one side, the therapist can accept any oddities and choices without condemning them [#1921].
3. Support within a non-invasive but clear setting. On the other side, the therapist is a support «в неинвазивном, но четком сеттинге» (in a non-invasive but clear setting), and explores questions full of emotion for which easy solutions simply do not exist [#1921].
4. Not imposing ideas; separating oneself from cultural and gender constructs. In therapy in general, and in couples therapy especially, it matters that the therapist does not intrude into your life with their own ideas and can separate themselves from cultural, gender and other constructs that may influence a person. Example: their therapist often says «на меня сейчас влияет идея» (an idea is influencing me right now) before sharing any judgment, as if drawing attention to the fact that we are all human and the systems we grow up and live in act on all of us. The author explains why this matters: the freedom sought and found in therapy is to a large extent the freedom to choose what works and fits us as individuals, as couples or other relational configurations - the possibility of going beyond imposed ideas [#1921].

Result. Fairly quickly ("though one certainly had to get there") the hour-and-a-half sessions became not one more item in the calendar but another «окном ресурса» (window of resource) - a place where he does not just take something from the therapist, consuming her as a service, but where, he supposes, a mutual process of exploration and enrichment happens [#1921].

### Detailed insights

#### Relationships as the main indicator of inner growth

The author shares John Welwood's approach - a man deeply rooted both in contemplative traditions and modern psychotherapy who, at the peak of his thinking about how people develop, paid special attention to relationships. Welwood said relationships are the main indicator of whether we are truly advancing on our spiritual or therapeutic path [#1618].

Other metrics of development exist and different stages have different tasks: subjective well-being, flow states, the capacity for self-regulation, the ability to return from a traumatic response, even biochemical markers such as the level of inflammation, which may reflect how balanced our system is. But all of these are only parts of the whole. The further we develop, the clearer it becomes that a person does not exist outside interdependence. If our path does not make us more able to realize this interdependence - with parents, children, friends, colleagues, communities - we may be advanced in some line of development yet remain "like children", at a stage of deep immaturity that largely nullifies our achievements [#1618].

Then it no longer matters how far we have progressed in studying the theory of everything, how many years we studied with Tibetan masters, whether we did hundreds of tantra retreats or thousands of ayahuasca ceremonies. If we have not learned to be genuinely included in the networks of relationships that create us as persons - as neurobiologist Daniel Siegel brilliantly shows - and have not learned to show presence, attention and compassion in contact with others and support their growth, then however far we have gone outwardly, «мы всё ещё стоим у порога. Мы все еще избегаем настоящей работы, убегая во что-то другое.» (we are still standing at the threshold. We are still avoiding the real work, running away into something else.) [#1618]

The same point closes the Golden Eyes post: the main indicator of inner work is not the number of hours of meditation, therapy groups or mastered theories, but how we are actually present in relationships [#1507].

#### Maturity of the helping community

Looking in the mirror (the author links this to a recent discussion of IFS) means looking not only at the individual path but at the community that itself carries ideas of development. By this maturity metric, the author asks [#1618]:

1. Can we, in our relationships with each other, create a force that helps serve society better than we do alone?
2. Can we see allies in one another?
3. Can we refrain from judging at the threshold and from throwing our projections onto social media without checking and dialogue, and instead, if needed, help integrate what the other is missing?
4. Can we become a force that does not just talk about transformation but embodies it in its way of being?

These are not philosophical questions: answers begin with very down-to-earth things, like replying to each other's messages. His assessment: the community of helping practitioners grows and develops, but remains a mirror of the society it came from - fragmented, disunited, inclined to row alone, poorly cooperating outside its narrow circle, full of judgments of one another. One can speak of maturity - of standing on some step and being a reference point - only when the community learns to find points of contact inside itself and, despite differences, stay together. Otherwise the question stays open: «имеем ли мы право говорить о развитии, если не способны воплотить его не только в себе, но и между собой?» (do we have the right to talk about development if we cannot embody it not only in ourselves but between ourselves?) [#1618]

#### Supporting loved ones and receiving support

Asking for support as maturity. The author says he is in a difficult period and is asking other adults for support. This is counterintuitive in "our culture" but, from a position of maturity, quite adequate: adults seek help from adults, acknowledging the reality of interdependence, showing vulnerability and, paradoxically, moving forward in their tasks. This carries an adult picture of the world in which any life involves difficulties and connection with others. Support within a network of contacts is essential for the health of the whole network - yet most of us regularly make basic mistakes once it goes beyond actions and quick solutions [#1705].

Why support is hard. We usually find it hard to bear another's pain and helplessness - we have enough of our own. Loved ones are also harder to control. Inside, both empathy and a sense of confusion easily arise [#1705].

How we lose contact even while wanting to help (mechanism). Human experience is largely similar, but shades of suffering differ greatly, and we cannot understand many things we have not lived through. The "rather silly foundation" of failed support: I understand the person feels bad, and this quickly raises my own stories of pain. The real situation before me is replaced by a similar situation from my past where I had already developed survival strategies - and I hand those out, usually much faster than needed. We tend to simplify and automatically apply our learned pattern of handling pain: «соберись, обесцень, отрежь сложность, убеги» (pull yourself together, devalue, cut off the complexity, run away). Psychologically this is described through emotional reactivity, projection and defense mechanisms: faced with another's vulnerability, we often start regulating ourselves first, not the other. Support turns into advice, interpretation or an attempt to close the topic as fast as possible [#1705].

Effect on the person seeking help. This almost never matches their reality. They feel unheard and lonely; not only do they fail to find support, helplessness often increases - having taken another step, they just hit a new dead end. The author calls this a bad scheme in terms of the potential of our networks of closeness [#1705].

Benchmark example - incorrect support of the dying (Andrew Holecek). The author (with a content note that death is mentioned) recalls the American meditation teacher's examples of two extremes that regularly recur [#1705]:
1. The person already knows they are dying, while the people around act as if this is not happening.
2. The person is still here, but others start treating them or talking about them as if they were already gone.

These are examples of the strongest divergence between what one needs from others and what one actually gets, and they show the lack of understanding and skills in this area [#1705].

Why it will matter more. The less linear the world becomes, the more this gap matters: we understand each other's contexts less and less, while those contexts demand more resources and help. Examples of such contexts that are hard to understand without living them: falling out of technological progress, emigration, non-linear careers, new family models, constant relocation [#1705].

"One of the hardest skills of adult life - to be near without simplifying and without running away." The two forms of conscious support the author recommends [#1705]:
1. Be aware of your own patterns of handling pain, so as not to project them too directly and too quickly.
2. Learn to listen not in order to solve or fix, but to create a sense of presence - that the person is not alone and their experience needs neither to be evaluated nor hurriedly solved.

#### Sex Education as a model of a psychologized culture

The author treats the (then just finished) Netflix series *Sex Education*, about a school in a small British town, as an ideal of a future psychologized culture whose seeds already exist. From the first season the characters communicate in the model of direct nonviolent communication, openly showing their feelings and needs in many situations. This forms both a new cultural code and a special psychic reality for the viewer - triggering and, as it were, reassembling some of the viewer's own childhood situations [#931].

Imperfection as realism. Not all dialogues follow the classic NVC framework, and that is normal and true to life: this is how ecological communication looks in the real rather than ideal world. Living people often feel strong emotions, so until we are "black-belt masters of mindfulness" we will never fully free ourselves from imperfect impulsive reactions and equally imperfect communication. But the characters model behaviour for these habitual imperfect situations too: sooner or later they repair the consequences of their impulses - apologize for things left unsaid, poor conflict management or words said in emotion - and reach open, vulnerable communication [#931].

Therapeutic function. Because of direct communication and vulnerability the series has a therapeutic function. It is more noticeable to a person from outside Western culture, who sees a picture that is something of a fantasy, very different from post-Soviet reality. For contrast, the author says our model of culturally making sense of school can be reduced to Valeria Gai Germanika's "incredible" series *School* - a depressive realism of a quite different life, even an anti-utopia, opposite to Netflix's bright colours. Still, the therapeutic effect is universal: most of the lousy stories from our own childhood - from shameful incidents to bullying and even sexual violence - are worked through in a format of direct communication. When we hear from the screen the words we needed back then and see the support the kids give each other, a superficial but still therapeutic work happens. The series constantly gives examples of essential communication that can, by the mere fact of happening, change all subsequent development of a teenager or child [#931].

The five key elements of a healthy society in the modern pop-psychologized understanding, as the series models them [#931]:
1. Psychological literacy (awareness).
2. Openness and vulnerability.
3. Mutual support.
4. Inclusivity.
5. Agency (субъектность - being a subject of one's own life).

The author asks whether this ideal is achievable at all, but holds that every step toward it can reduce a great deal of suffering; and that the question shows which meanings and tasks modern societies address when they are not busy degrading their citizens with paranoia and conspiracy theories [#931].

#### Why invest in couples therapy

The author acknowledges that their spending on therapy is at its maximum ever - three therapeutic processes for the couple, "and that is a lot" - but, aware of his privilege of access to therapy and to great specialists, he is fully satisfied and sees much potential. His reasoning: the closer the relationship, the more influence it has on life, well-being, understanding of oneself and others, and movement toward goals. If tension and mutual misunderstanding accumulate in such close relationships - «а у всех нас есть ведерко или ведро негативных мыслей про партнера» (and all of us have a little bucket or a bucket of negative thoughts about the partner) - it takes a great deal of resource and can become fatal [#1921].

#### Zones of helplessness in a couple

Relationships often contain zones of helplessness. The author's three examples:

1. Someone waits for the other for years or decades and suffers because the partner is always late [#1921].
2. Someone wants a non-drinking husband, but he drinks, and there is no way to influence it [#1921].
3. The couple has a different sexual rhythm or temperament, producing a chronic mismatch that seems irreparable [#1921].

In such situations we can get tired and angry "as if to our very depth". Couples therapy, per the author, helps reduce the intensity of this helplessness, returning strength and agency [#1921].

#### Gottman's findings: help-seeking delay and perpetual conflicts

- About six years of delay. According to psychologist John Gottman's estimate, couples on average wait about six years from the appearance of serious problems before seeking help - "six years in which the bucket manages to fill up" [#1921].
- About 69% of conflicts are perpetual. The author says he was surprised to learn from Gottman's research that about 69% of conflicts in a couple are "perpetual": stable couples never resolve them, often because there simply are no normal solutions. Yet these can be good relationships, because they contain trust, the authorship of each side and the ability to hear not only each other but also oneself [#1921].
- Definition of a good relationship. "Good relationships are not those where all conflicts are resolved or where none arise at all." The latter, the author says, is the lot of dead or lifeless relationships [#1921].
- Relationships are mess. «Отношения — это месс, а не идиллия.» (Relationships are a mess, not an idyll.) The closer we are, the greater the chance of hurting each other, and more than once. Thanks to couples therapy, the author says, he now knows "we are not alone in our powerlessness" [#1921].
