# Willpower, self-control and habits: what the evidence says

## What this covers

One long post by the deep mind 2.0 author traces the 20-year life of the "willpower is a muscle" idea (ego depletion): the original 1998 experiment, the snowball of supporting studies and pop applications (identical founder breakfasts and T-shirts, decision fatigue, glucose, the Israeli judges), and the collapse after correction for publication bias and a large preregistered replication. It then covers the post-collapse picture — a possible threshold effect, Inzlicht's motivation/attention model, the failed "belief in depletion" idea — and the author's practical conclusion: high self-control comes from habits and fewer temptations, and real gains lie in intention, conative intelligence and attention training. According to the source this matters because people scold themselves for "weak will" possibly for nothing, and because careers and trainings were built for two decades around an effect that most likely does not exist [msg #1902].

## Core ideas

- **Willpower is not a muscle.** The theory that self-control is a depletable resource was treated as established fact for about 20 years and then did not pass verification: bias-corrected meta-analysis and a 36-lab replication both found an effect statistically indistinguishable from zero [msg #1902].
- **Science is a process of refinement, not a set of reinforced-concrete facts.** Smart, conscientious people built careers and trainings on an effect that "most likely" does not exist; the author adds "we are still in the sandbox" [msg #1902].
- **High self-control is mostly about not needing willpower.** People with high self-control face choices less often, because they have fewer temptations on their path and more habits in the right places [msg #1902].
- **Do not fall in love with explanations because they are scientific or beautiful — including this one.** The author explicitly extends the skepticism to his own post [msg #1902].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Rise and fall of ego depletion (timeline of evidence)
Purpose: a worked case study of how an attractive scientific theory spreads and then fails, used by the author to calibrate trust in "scientific" self-help [msg #1902].

1. **1998 — the original experiment.** Roy Baumeister seated hungry students in front of a plate of cookies and a bowl of radishes. One group was allowed the cookies, the other only the radishes; afterwards everyone got a puzzle that had no solution. Those who had chewed radishes (i.e., had to resist the cookies) gave up twice as fast — **8 minutes versus 19**. The conclusion "suggested itself": self-control is a resource and it gets spent. The theory was named ego depletion (истощение эго) [msg #1902].
2. **The muscle metaphor.** It came later, together with the promise that will can be used up or "pumped" by regular exercises. The metaphor perfectly explained evening breakdowns, a collapsed diet, and the inability to be a good parent right after negotiations — which is part of why it was so appealing [msg #1902].
3. **The snowball.** Hundreds of confirming experiments; a meta-analysis with a solid effect; side branches in all directions: (a) will "feeds on" blood sugar; (b) decision fatigue — the source of the identical breakfasts and T-shirts of founders; (c) the Israeli judges, whose share of approved parole (УДО) fell from 65% at the start of a session to almost zero before lunch; (d) Baumeister's bestseller. The theory moved from labs into trainings and entrepreneurial practice [msg #1902].
4. **2014 — first signs.** The same meta-analysis was recalculated with a correction for publication bias. The author explains the mechanism: journals print experiments that worked, while those that showed nothing stay out; the size of this skew can be estimated, and after correcting for it the willpower effect turned out indistinguishable from zero [msg #1902].
5. **2021 — the large replication.** The original experiment was repeated at scale: 36 laboratories, 3,500 people. The protocol was preregistered: everyone committed to publish the result whatever it was and not to tweak the analysis after the fact. Result: again statistically indistinguishable from zero [msg #1902].
6. **The side branches fall too.** The glucose version was not confirmed. The judges, it turned out, did not hear cases in random order — prisoners without lawyers came at the end of sessions, which explains the drop without any depletion [msg #1902].
7. **2025 — a partial finding.** Another study did find the effect, but only with an exhausting task. The author's reading: depletion may have a threshold, but possibly it is not about willpower at all [msg #1902].

### Inzlicht's motivation-and-attention shift model
Michael Inzlicht proposed a different model of what happens after long effort: nothing "runs out"; instead motivation and attention shift — the brain gets tired of "надо" ("have to") and is pulled toward "хочется" ("want to"). In the author's words this is a need, not an empty tank of will. It is offered as the alternative explanation for the threshold effect seen in the 2025 study [msg #1902].

### Alternatives to willpower (what the author says actually helps)
1. **Fewer temptations and more habits.** Per a newer line of research the author finds convincing, people with high self-control rely on willpower less; they stand before a choice less often because there are fewer temptations on their way and more habits in the needed places [msg #1902].
2. **Science plus radical empiricism.** The author suggests relying not only on science but on science together with the radical empiricism used in contemplative traditions (first-person testing) [msg #1902].
3. **Work with intention and conative intelligence.** According to the author, contemplative traditions long ago found that the real profit lies here [msg #1902].
4. **Attention training.** The author says what is lacking is stability of attention sufficient to move toward a goal without distractions, so attention should be trained [msg #1902].
5. **Environment design — works, but modestly.** Environment design also works, but more modestly than commonly thought; the author backs "скромнее" with a link to the Retraction Watch report on JAMA journals retracting six papers by food-marketing researcher Brian Wansink [msg #1902].

## Detailed insights

### The founders' identical breakfast as a downstream myth
The post opens with the popular practice of entrepreneurs eating the same thing for breakfast to avoid wasting willpower on trivia and save it for important decisions. The author traces this habit (and identical T-shirts) to the decision-fatigue branch of ego depletion, and closes: you may decide what to eat for breakfast and wear different T-shirts [msg #1902].

### The "it's all suggestion" turn (Dweck)
The author addresses trainees who explain any change of state after a practice as mere suggestion (самовнушение). The story had a "beautiful twist" for them: Carol Dweck found that willpower depletes mainly in those who believe it depletes — "checkmate". The author calls this tempting but untrue: a later check did not confirm the idea. The lesson is the same as the whole post: an elegant explanation is not thereby a true one [msg #1902].

### Self-blame for "weak will"
Given the evidence, the author says that if you scold yourself for weak willpower, you may be doing so in vain — the deficit is better addressed through habits, fewer temptations and attention than through effort [msg #1902].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- (!) UNCLEAR: the key sentence on high self-control is garbled in the source — "люди с высоким самоконтролем реже вообще меньше на нее опираются" (*people with high self-control less often at all rely less on it*); the intended meaning appears to be that they rely on willpower less, but the exact wording is broken [msg #1902].
- The author hedges the central claim: the effect "скорее всего" (most likely) does not exist, and the 2025 study did find an effect under exhausting tasks — so a threshold form of depletion remains open, with Inzlicht's model offered as one possible explanation, not a settled one [msg #1902].
- The claims about contemplative traditions (intention, conative intelligence, radical empiricism) are the author's opinion and are not backed by studies in the post; the author also applies his skepticism to his own text [msg #1902].
- The post contains typos ("мастшабе", "не подвердилась", "в воли" [sic]) that do not affect meaning [msg #1902].
