# Employee wellbeing and workplace mental health

## What this covers

This part collects what the deep mind channel (a consultancy and community that brings mindfulness and other science-backed practices into organizations) posted between April 2020 and November 2022 about the psychological state of employees: stress, burnout, depression, anxiety and quiet quitting, the corporate wellbeing trend, the limits of perk-based wellbeing programs, ESG as a vehicle for mental-health policy, and survey data used as evidence. The source treats employee state as a strategic management issue: in its view stress and low engagement destroy team effectiveness, productivity and retention, and the "radical breakthrough in effectiveness" lies in developing people and interpersonal interaction, not only in technology [msg #177, #414, #747, #753].

This part covers deep mind's posts from 2023 to 2026 on stress, wellbeing and mental health inside companies: what the channel's corporate programs (Less stress, Emotional balance, leadership programs) taught about reducing stress, statistics on workplace stress, the company-controllable drivers of employee happiness, resilience and resilience culture, wellbeing understood as "flourishing", emotional labour, the toxicity of performance reviews, a cross-country ranking of state care for employee wellbeing, and the emerging trauma-informed workplace movement [msg #806, #945, #988, #1027, #1043, #1059, #1131, #1346, #1419, #1442, #1723].

According to the source it matters because a bad work environment creates a lose-lose situation: lower engagement, higher turnover, lower performance and higher health, retention and hiring costs. Happy, resilient employees are more productive, quit and burn out less, and integrated cultures show better long-term results. The author argues that the next gain in organizational effectiveness will come not only from AI but from companies that stop wasting the "inner" resource of their people [msg #945, #988, #1131].

This part adds one post from October 2026, framed by the channel as a "корпоративный кейс deep mind" (deep mind corporate case) on "Как ведущие компании работают с состоянием сотрудников в 2026 году" (How leading companies work with employees' state in 2026). It gives market statistics on burnout, HR priorities and the state of corporate wellbeing spending, argues that the wellbeing market currently offers a poor solution, and describes deep mind's own two-month state-management program for leaders, its three components and its before/after measurement results. According to the source it matters because companies face a squeeze: burnout drives people out, HR's top priority is productivity, and with record-low unemployment there is no one to replace a burnt-out person [msg #1956].

Relation to the existing knowledge file: this post updates the earlier program evidence. Earlier parts describe Less stress (a one-month program, reported ~30% group stress reduction) and other deep mind programs; this post presents a different, newer "flagship" program aimed specifically at leaders, lasting two months at 1.5 hours a week, with a three-state measurement instrument and a 32% drop in the share of participants outside the stress norm. It also extends the earlier theses that wellbeing perks and lectures are insufficient with fresh 2026 market numbers on how few companies invest in, or trust, their wellbeing programs [msg #1956].

## Core ideas

- **Employee state is a business task, not a perk.** Deep mind positions mindfulness and other evidence-based practices as tools for solving business problems; its two focus problems in 2020 were stress management and team adaptation ("rebuilding the business") [msg #177].
- **Companies over-focus on external goals and ignore their inner state.** This produces distrust, disintegration, poor management and high stress from constantly defending one's own safety; employees spend most of their time hiding mistakes and building personal power instead of acting in sync for the company [msg #177].
- **External wellbeing care alone is the "infancy" of wellbeing culture.** Paying for sport, meditation apps and ergonomic chairs does not help a person who suffers in contact with their manager or in their tasks; one must work directly with the inner state (see *Three components of working with employees' inner state*) [msg #414].
- **The direct manager is the main lever.** Honest conversation about feelings, plans and friction, and giving and asking for feedback, is presented as one of the most effective ways to start real work on employee state [msg #414].
- **Stress, then burnout, then quiet quitting form a chain.** Chronic stress leads to burnout; quiet quitting is described as post-pandemic burnout amplified by unclear goals, fewer growth opportunities, weak company support and bad (authoritarian) bosses [msg #435, #753].
- **The response has two prongs:** change the circumstances that cause stress, and adapt through self-regulation skills plus company processes that support those skills and co-regulation at the workplace [msg #747].
- **A meditation booth is not a substitute for culture.** Self-regulation requires skills trained with a real mindfulness teacher, and an overwork culture leaves no time for booths; culture, environment and processes must change [msg #435].
- **Wellbeing is becoming strategic and institutional** - evidenced by corporate strategies (Magnit), company-wide time off (Nike), dedicated mental-health leadership roles (Gazprom Neft) and ESG's governance/social agenda [msg #231, #469, #489, #573].

- **Stress is reduced through regular bodily practice, not theory alone**; theory is still needed for a systemic understanding (see *Seven lessons from three years of Less stress*) [msg #806].
- **Mindfulness is the "queen of all skills"**, but most people today first need simple neuro-regulation tools before they can sustain a meditation practice [msg #806].
- **Workplace stress is massive, costly and leader-contagious**: 89% of employees disengage when their leader is highly stressed [msg #945].
- **Companies can directly move five happiness factors**: mental health, healthy atmosphere, recognition/feedback, flexibility, financial confidence [msg #988].
- **Resilience is a trainable skill with five components** and must be developed jointly by employee and company, starting with top management [msg #1027, #1043].
- **Genuine care is not protection but creating a place where people flourish** — meaning and growth, not benefits alone [msg #1059].
- **Emotional labour is a "second job"** that drains attention into self-protection; integrated cultures that value the inner dimension perform better long term [msg #1131].
- **Performance reviews stack three powerful stressors** (social evaluation, financial risk, career/identity risk) and are neither ethical nor trauma-informed by modern standards [msg #1419].
- **State-level care shapes people's psychological baseline**; Russia ranks 43rd of 60 [msg #1442].
- **Trauma is a business issue, not a niche clinical one**; trauma-informed practices (TIP) built on six principles improve conditions for everyone [msg #1723].

- **Burnout and productivity demands now collide.** Burnout is widespread and causes people to leave, while HR's number-one priority is productivity and labour is scarce, so companies are expected to deliver more with people who cannot easily be replaced [msg #1956].
- **The current wellbeing "solution" is poor.** Most companies say wellbeing is important, but few actually invest, fewer still rate their programs as effective, and budgets are not growing; the market is full of vendors nobody believes and products with nothing to show afterwards [msg #1956].
- **A systemic, low-time-cost program for leaders can shift a whole group's state.** deep mind claims its comprehensive program (skills, technology, team/culture) took only 1.5 hours a week and cut the share of leaders outside the normal stress zone by about a third (see *Three components* and *Before/after results*) [msg #1956].
- **Under tight budgets HR should buy measurable results.** The author's conclusion is that HR should choose products that deliver results in numbers and real benefit to the group [msg #1956].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Deep mind thesis on managing employee state (10 theses from the HR conference talk)
Purpose: the channel's programmatic statement, presented at the All-Russian HR conference in April 2020 (early COVID crisis), about why and how organizations should manage employees' state. The ten theses in order [msg #177]:

1. **What deep mind does.** It brings mindfulness practices and other practices "confirmed by science and research" into organizations to solve business tasks. Its current focus is two tasks: (1) stress management and (2) team adaptation, i.e. rebuilding the business [msg #177].
2. **Forecasting via sociological regularities.** Forecasts are very hard because experts hold diametrically opposite views on every question, but one can rely on sociological regularities. One such regularity: "Кризис ускоряет объективные процессы" (*A crisis accelerates objective processes*). Trends of recent years may change shape along the way but will most likely intensify. Examples of such trends: flexibility and distribution of teams, rising stress, remote work, speed of change [msg #177].
3. **Management attention to the person is itself a trend.** It showed up over the previous 2–3 years in global wellbeing programs being actively pushed down into corporations from the global level. The author gives two causes: the world's growing complexity ("people burn out and need pills for it") and, what matters more, the very process of organizational development [msg #177].
4. **Inner goals after outer goals.** Having done a lot of work on external goals (product, business processes), companies realized there are inner goals too: the place where business processes originate is the human being, so it would be good to work with them. Key claim: "Радикальный прорыв в эффективности лежит не столько в области технологий, сколько - в развитии человека и межличностного взаимодействия в командах." (*The radical breakthrough in effectiveness lies not so much in technology as in the development of the person and of interpersonal interaction in teams.*) [msg #177]
5. **A new paradigm.** A whole paradigm of approaches, tools and methods has already appeared for this evolutionary task, and when organizations are viewed through it, their problems look somewhat different [msg #177].
6. **The key problem diagnosis.** (The detailed problems were on a conference slide not transcribed in the source - (!) INCOMPLETE.) The author's generalization: companies are excessively focused on external goals while ignoring their inner state, which leads to distrust, disintegration, poor management and high stress from constantly protecting one's own safety. The pressure is such that most of the time employees hide errors and imperfections and build personal power and safety, instead of synchronized team action for the good of the whole company [msg #177].
7. **The payoff.** If this is addressed, team integration increases - a direct step toward resilience and the ability to come through the crisis successfully [msg #177].
8. **The opportunity for HR.** The crisis is a chance to pay more attention to the question of WHO is in the organization and to strengthen HR, renaming it along the way to People management. Leaders are now more open to tools that work directly with the person - mindfulness, embodiment, Theory U. It suddenly became more obvious that a company is not an office but people [msg #177].
9. **Start with stress.** In a state of stress ("and it will only get worse from here") access to the rational mind is, to put it mildly, limited, even though it may seem the opposite. This hallucination of clarity is a consequence of adrenaline addiction or psychological trauma. Stress also destroys team effectiveness, which the author says research confirms. Remedies named: mindfulness practices in the corporate environment, body work and psychotherapy [msg #177].
10. **Human-centered companies survive crises better.** Collins's classic research found that truly enduring companies resort less often to layoffs and restructuring in a crisis; such companies are often human-centered (give substantial weight to employees) and do make breakthroughs even in crisis. Hence it is useful to look at the situation not only in survival mode but also in opportunity mode [msg #177].

### Three components of working with employees' inner state (Vlad Koroteev)
Purpose: guest author Vlad Koroteev (happiness manager at rbk.money, deep mind community member) argues that to raise a person's happiness and satisfaction "необходимо непосредственно работать с его внутренним состоянием" (*it is necessary to work directly with their inner state*), and spells out what that means [msg #414]:

1. **Build tools to analyze people's states in teams** and monitor and analyze that state as often as performance - "лучше чаще" (*better, more often*) [msg #414].
2. **Investigate the causes** of the emotions and feelings that arise in people [msg #414].
3. **Change culture and business processes** in light of understanding what state people are in and what influences it [msg #414].

Starting point: "Один из самых эффективных способов запустить реальную работу над состоянием сотрудников в компаниях — начать разговаривать." (*One of the most effective ways to launch real work on employee state in companies is to start talking.*) That means talking sincerely and with interest with the team about feelings, plans and friction, and giving and asking for feedback. This matters especially in power relationships, because the direct manager has the greatest influence on a person's job satisfaction (Gallup is cited for this). Such communication requires developing new skills and a non-trivial evolution of company culture [msg #414].

### Four consequences of absent manager–employee conversation (Vlad Koroteev)
Without such communication, employees in teams [msg #414]:
1. **Lose self-confidence.** This also harms the company, because insecure people make more mistakes, and the wish to assert oneself leads to a substitution of goals [msg #414].
2. **Consider their work unnecessary**, because they see nobody is interested in it [msg #414].
3. **Do not understand how and where to develop**, so motivation falls and turnover rises [msg #414].
4. **Do not share important information about problems in the business** [msg #414].

Where it bites hardest: these difficulties arise more often in middle management and senior specialists. They have complex, abstract tasks, and their direct managers (often C-level) have little time for timely feedback. Koroteev suggests (hedged, "Возможно" - *perhaps*) that well-tuned communication at this level should become the first point of effort on the way to more happiness in companies [msg #414].

### Four business consequences of workplace stress
The channel states that companies increasingly recognize that stress is proven to cause large losses of money and productivity, through four effects [msg #435]:
1. Engagement decreases.
2. Productivity falls.
3. Employees more actively look for a new job.
4. Employees show up at the workplace less often (absenteeism).

### Two-pronged response to workplace stress
Responding to 2022 data on mass anxiety in Russian companies, the channel proposes [msg #747]:
1. **Act on circumstances:** "Текущий уровень стресса требует от нас каких-то действий, направленных на изменение обстоятельств." (*The current level of stress demands some actions aimed at changing the circumstances.*)
2. **Adapt:** develop new self-regulation skills, and build company processes that support those skills and "корегуляцию прямо на рабочем месте" (*co-regulation right at the workplace*).

The channel also points to three levels of its own help, by situation: when you need to help yourself and a friend; when you need to support a team; when employees are ready to learn (the links themselves are logistics and dropped). Anti-pattern named in the same post: organizations try to cope by identifying spreaders of alarming rumors and banning political discussion at work - "Конечно, это решения, которые толком не способны повлиять на ситуацию." (*Of course, these are solutions that are not really able to affect the situation.*) [msg #747]

### Quiet quitting - definition, amplifying factors and organizational responses
Definition: "Скрытое увольнение [Quiet Quitting] — это работа на минимальном необходимом уровне, когда делаешь базовые обязанности, но не прикладываешь усилий и не стараешься достичь результата." (*Quiet quitting is working at the minimum necessary level, when you do your basic duties but put in no effort and do not try to achieve a result.*) [msg #753]

Scale and trend: the channel says we are living through the lowest workplace activity of the past decade; engagement began falling in late 2021, and up to 50% of US company employees are now in quiet quitting (Gallup). Engagement and enjoyment fell most among people under 35 working remotely [msg #753].

Cause: the trend is directly linked to the consequences of prolonged stress in recent years - "post-pandemic burnout" - amplified by three factors [msg #753]:
1. Uncertainty of work goals.
2. Reduced opportunities for learning and growth.
3. Insufficient support from the company.

Bad management as a further driver: it is not only general pandemic fatigue but poor management. A 2020 study (cited via HBR) found harsh managers have 4 times more subordinates working in quiet-quitting mode. A toxically authoritarian management style produces a sense of injustice, being undervalued and meaninglessness of work [msg #753].

Russia-specific forecast (author's view): this is the trend now unfolding in Russia, where some companies are experiencing a crisis-driven rollback in management culture; this will intensify quiet quitting and further redistribute talent toward ethical companies and leaders [msg #753].

How organizations respond (author: this significant "objective process" will certainly lead to further change in work culture and evolution of management) [msg #753]:
1. **Culture of care and wellbeing plus self-regulation skills:** leading companies increasingly build a culture of care and wellbeing and develop employees' self-regulation skills that help adapt to high stress.
2. **Leaders' subtle skills,** e.g. the ability to build trust - because that quality lets a leader get good results while preserving relationships and supporting the team's state (HBR cited).
3. **Meaning and living values:** companies look for ways to shape their activity around greater meaning and living values, not only around status and money.

### ESG and its employee-wellbeing dimension
Definition given: ESG in the broad sense is a company's focus or set of actions directed at sustainable development, split for convenience into three directions [msg #489]:
1. **E - environment:** responsible attitude to the environment.
2. **S - social:** high social responsibility.
3. **G - governance:** high quality of corporate governance (emphasized by the author).

The author's observation from RBC's first ESG conference: in Russia, ESG will for a long time be filled with everything related to ecology and consumption; corporate governance is not yet understood - "это все еще какой-то сферический конь в вакуме" (*it is still some kind of spherical horse in a vacuum*), hard to measure and hard to transform. The topic is much broader; according to Anne Finucane, VP Bank of America, it includes [msg #489]:
1. Social contracts with employees: how they are treated in the company and how well they are paid.
2. What childcare leave is offered and the whole parental-leave procedure for female and male employees.
3. What the company does in the area of mental health.
4. What the company's management methods are.

Context and outlook (author's view): COVID forced the global energy transition and with it ESG took off again in the corporate world; it is an important trend to watch "даже в РФ" (*even in Russia*) despite rising energy prices. It is a new challenge for Russian companies, but millennials and younger employees already hold many senior positions, and for them this topic is part of their values and interests - they are the ones who can seriously help companies with ESG transformation [msg #489].

### Millz Mood Index (Deloitte)
Described in the Deloitte 2021 survey attached by the channel: an index that gauges respondents' mood and gives an annual snapshot of millennials' optimism that the world and their place in it will improve. It aggregates five questions into a 0–100 measure, allowing comparison year to year and across regions and demographic groups within a year. The five components [file #541]:
1. Economic situation.
2. Sociopolitical situation.
3. Personal financial situation.
4. Environment.
5. Impact of business on wider society.

### Seven lessons from three years of the Less stress program

1. **Studying the scientific theory of human functioning matters.** Many articles and books exist, yet few people understand clearly and systematically the program's core topics — stress, emotions, mindfulness, self-care. So theory remains a needed component [msg #806].
2. **Stress decreases through practice, not theory alone.** The body is what reacts to novelty and danger, so teaching/re-teaching the body is the priority task for lowering stress. Retraining happens when we steadily and regularly *do* something with the body differently from the habitual contraction and tension (the author emphasizes "**делаем**" — "we do") [msg #806].
3. **Mindfulness is the queen of all skills.** It is described as an absolute game changer that affects everything we do, how we see the world and how we think. Sustainable change — necessary if we want to change the stress level in a system — is launched by insight and its development, and insight is impossible without mindfulness [msg #806].
4. **Most people need neuro-regulation practices to access mindfulness training.** Mindfulness remains elitist and inaccessible for most: the current state of people's nervous system and attention/memory systems makes a daily 15–20-minute mindfulness practice simply unworkable. Before meditation, simpler, targeted self-regulation tools are needed: bilateral stimulation, EFT, and rapid carbon-dioxide balancing through breathing. 6. **Stress reduction happens through step-by-step work across different life areas, not through one thing.** Inner work — self-study, mindfulness and self-regulation — is the most important and needed thing for a modern person, but an adult approach to stress necessarily includes outer changes too: relationships and boundaries, consumption of food and information, movement and rest. 7. **Teams that talk about their state and learn to regulate it work better.** When people say how they feel, they share something deeply human. Humanity creates trust and integration, which the author calls proven conditions for effective work [msg #806].

### Emotional labour and the integrated culture
**The "second job".** Today most people at work are constantly busy building personal safety and increasing their own resources. The author calls this a second job in which attention and presence are spent not on creation and collaboration but on control and social games. Employees try to hide their emotions, weaknesses and "imperfections". Because of this disintegration of goals — the personal can differ greatly from the team's — everyone suffers: organizations get mediocre results, and people get tired, consider corporations cesspools and burn out, instead of the flourishing available to all of us in teams [msg #1131].

**Why it happens (systemic cause).** People usually play by the rules of the system rather than being bad in themselves. Here they act this way because of the culture typical of the current developmental stage of most corporate systems: a culture of perfectionism and achievement focused entirely on the external — metrics, KPIs — and completely ignoring the inner dimension, which is no less real and important. The author notes this is exactly the same pattern of striving for safety and resources, only at the level of whole organizations [msg #1131].

**Example with its lesson.** The author knows of a well-known company where a manager can completely wear out ("укатать") a team and still receive a bonus and recognition if the result is achieved. The company gets results again and again and considers this order normal — "it works!" The author calls it madness and an example of a distorted world picture that sustains mediocre culture and closes many possibilities for society's wellbeing, because it contains no full information about what an alternative work culture would look like [msg #1131].

**Integrated culture (интегрированная культура).** The alternative: a culture where the inner is no less important than the outer — where you must not only achieve the goal but also preserve the team, because creating it costs a lot of resources, and because we are living people who deserve creation, not burnout [msg #1131].

**The concept of emotional labour.** The second job can be described through emotional labour, introduced by sociologist Arlie Hochschild: already 30 years ago she pointed out that employees must manage their emotions to meet company expectations and standards — suppressing negative emotions and displaying positive ones even if insincere. There are now meta-analyses on its real negative consequences for the person and the company: constant emotional labour leads to burnout, depression and lower job satisfaction, and to higher stress levels in the company and staff turnover [msg #1131].

**Alternatives exist — Patagonia.** Patagonia is given as an organization that successfully integrated inner and outer dimensions: oriented to external results but emphasizing employee wellbeing and sustainable business practices, with a culture in which employees can express emotions and care for their wellbeing without fear of judgment. ### Performance review as a 3-in-1 stress trigger
**The positive side.** Performance reviews became a symbol of transparency and fair resource distribution in tech and product companies, replacing archaic practices of nepotism, opaque decisions and subjective sympathies — a shift the author welcomes. Research he cites confirms performance-based evaluation reduces favoritism and increases the sense of fairness [msg #1419].

**The problem.** A disturbing pattern: reviews come with a sharp rise in stress and anxiety, affecting productivity and atmosphere. Through the author's lens this is due to overlapping triggers — the review "like Nescafe" combines three in one [msg #1419]:
1. **Social evaluation.** Receiving an assessment of one's contribution and "social value" in the group activates deep patterns of fear of rejection or exclusion. In the modern understanding, social rejection activates the same brain centers as physical pain [msg #1419].
2. **Financial risks.** Reviews are often tied directly to bonuses and next-period salary, yet positive expectations are held by very few: per a McKinsey study the author cites, only 10–20% of employees get a raise based on review results [msg #1419].
3. **Career and identity risks.** Reviews serve as a way to decide on layoffs, and job loss is one of the strongest stressors, threatening identity and health [msg #1419].

These three add up to an intense stress load that knocks employees out of optimal working state for several weeks or even months [msg #1419].

**Reviews raise effectiveness in one place while dramatically lowering it in another** [msg #1419]:
- Per Deloitte estimates, in most modern IT companies 40–60% of employees show signs of burnout during the review period [msg #1419].
- Teams under high stress load lose 20–40% of productivity [msg #1419].
- Chronic stress damages cognitive functions, worsens memory, and reduces the ability to learn and think flexibly [msg #1419].

**Ethics.** How does a review take the person's life context into account? If an employee lived through the death of a loved one, a serious illness or a personal crisis during the period, will that be considered? Usually not — "KPIs remain KPIs". This inability to "see the person" behind the numbers creates a risk of ethical distortion and lowers loyalty even of the most motivated employees; the company openly views the person not as a living partner but as a function, a cog, "a slave of the system" — a view the author calls disgusting and important to talk about. On average, reviews in large companies cannot be called ethical or trauma-informed by modern standards; rather, they become traumatic for many employees, negatively affecting their life trajectory for a long time. "Cherry on top": by the author's field observations, reviews at this stage are of very doubtful objectivity, with many distortions [msg #1419].

**The turn needed.** Without accounting for a person's state, resources and the wider set of perspectives making up a living person's life, evaluation becomes a "stress steamroller" that grinds people down and ultimately destroys the very effectiveness it seeks. The key shift is to see both effectiveness and the person — the most important challenge for new leadership. A sustainable high-performance culture must work with a broader set of perspectives than now; modern HR approaches increasingly seek contextual, adaptive evaluation systems, but such models are still rare in practice [msg #1419].

### Five-level trauma-informed workplace maturity model
From the "Managing Trauma in the Workplace" report, shared as an image. 1. **Level 1 — Trauma-unaware.** Trauma is not seen as relevant to the workplace; responses are reactive and compliance-driven. Culture/leadership: leaders do not view trauma as relevant to workplace functioning; culture prioritises performance and compliance, with silence or stigma around distress and mental ill health. People practices: no training for managers on trauma-informed support; minimal awareness of psychological safety, wellbeing or relational support. Systems: HR and governance policies lack reference to trauma or flexible response; no defined process for trauma disclosure, adjustment or recovery [img #1723].
2. **Level 2 — Trauma-aware.** The organisation begins to explore the relevance of trauma. Culture/leadership: senior leaders begin to acknowledge trauma as a workplace issue; early champions or advocates emerge. People practices: some managers receive basic trauma-awareness or mental-health training; initial wellbeing or DEI briefings begin referencing trauma-related concepts. Systems: one or more HR policies reviewed for alignment with trauma principles; staff feedback mechanisms begin to capture psychological safety [img #1723].
3. **Level 3 — Trauma-sensitive.** Trauma-informed understanding starts to shape policies, training and culture. Culture/leadership: leadership starts modelling inclusive behaviours and acknowledges the emotional impact of work; conversations about psychological safety are encouraged. People practices: manager training includes skills for recognising distress and responding supportively (e.g. REACTMH — the OCR reads "REACTH"; the image shows REACTMH); staff experience of trust, belonging and psychological safety explored via surveys/focus groups. Systems: updated HR policies include trauma-informed language and adjustment options; a trauma disclosure process is introduced and tested; peer support or reflective practice piloted in high-risk teams [img #1723].
4. **Level 4 — Trauma-responsive.** Trauma-informed values are embedded into wellbeing, mental health and inclusion strategies. Culture/leadership: trauma-informed culture embedded in leadership expectations and values; leaders actively review decisions through a trauma-informed lens. People practices: most managers trained and confident in providing support during or after distressing events; adjustments offered proactively and equitably; lived-experience input into policy and support offering. Systems: key people processes reflect trauma-informed principles; governance includes trauma-informed reviews; uptake and impact of supportive offers monitored and evaluated [img #1723].
5. **Level 5 — Trauma-informed and healing-oriented.** Trauma-informed practices are embedded into people strategy and aligned with broader organisational values and leadership behaviours. Culture/leadership: trauma-informed values embedded across leadership behaviours, wellbeing, inclusion and people strategy; senior leaders model psychological safety and relational leadership, and trauma-informed thinking is reflected in how the organisation sets priorities around people and culture. People practices: staff report high levels of psychological safety, trust and inclusion; peer-led and manager-led support models embedded across teams; equity lens applied to trauma-informed training and support. Systems: trauma-informed design embedded across systems (e.g. recruitment, DEI, health and safety, hybrid working); policies co-created or reviewed with those impacted; data informs continuous learning and adaptation; external recognition or sector contributions shared [img #1723].

## Detailed insights

### Why the wellbeing trend exists (channel's reading)
The channel repeatedly reads corporate moves as confirmation of a "global trend on working with employees' state". Amazon's meditation kiosks for tired employees are framed as another confirmation of that trend and "очередное признание медитации как научно доказанного и ключевого способа улучшить благополучие человека" (*another recognition of meditation as a scientifically proven and key way to improve a person's wellbeing*) [msg #435]. In 2022 the channel says that people's state is becoming the most important challenge and increasingly falls into the zone of strategic management [msg #747]. Gazprom Neft opening vacancies for heads of mental health and well-being is offered as a sign that, while "некоторые все еще не верят в стресс и считают, что выгорание — для слабаков" (*some still do not believe in stress and think burnout is for the weak*), even a large oil company is creating such roles [msg #573].

### Happiness management and the limits of perk-based wellbeing (Vlad Koroteev)
A happy person burns out less often and is more engaged and productive, so happiness matters for companies, not only individuals. Companies have long cared about performance management; "happiness management" is only gaining momentum. Most modern companies already organize external wellbeing activities and see them as part of overall strategy: paying for sport, access to meditation and psychotherapy apps, changing the office environment, supporting remote workers. Koroteev calls this the "младенчеством культуры благополучия" (*infancy of wellbeing culture*): focus only on external care often fails because a person suffering in contact with their manager or with their tasks is hard to help with "бесплатными приседаниями, приятным голосом в Headspace или эргономическим стулом" (*free squats, a pleasant voice in Headspace or an ergonomic chair*). The remedy is the three components and conversation described in the framework above [msg #414].

### Amazon meditation kiosks - an ambiguous signal
The channel's verdict is two-sided. Positive: it confirms the trend and recognizes meditation. Negative: Amazon's corporate culture and business processes lead to overwork, so employees simply have no time to sit alone in booths; and, in deep mind's experience, self-regulation requires skills developed together with a real mindfulness teacher, not just an app audio recording and a booth. Conclusion: beyond educational programs for systematic skill development, companies must create the right culture, environment and processes. "Нам надо научиться не уставать насколько это возможно и вовремя проявлять заботу о своем состоянии." (*We need to learn not to get tired as far as possible and to take care of our state in time.*) [msg #435]

### Depression replacing acute stress as the dominant state (2021)
In 2020 deep mind worked mainly with the stress response, defined as the body's reaction to novelty. By early 2021 the "average" state had shifted and they dealt more with depression. The author notes that 2021 "coming-outs" were not about sexual or political preferences but public admissions of complete lack of energy, absence of desires and suicidal thoughts. Causes named: the direct effect of COVID on the nervous system and the collapse of habitual ways of life - inability to travel, go to the office, meet people freely [msg #398].

Cost: depression costs the US economy $100 billion a year, including $44 billion from lost employee productivity. In Russia such things are not yet counted and few people in organizations think about it, but the phenomenon and its consequences do not disappear. Some studies (in Norway, which the author notes is similar to Russia in climate) now address not depressed individuals but whole teams' susceptibility to such states [msg #398].

Cultural barrier: "Сложность прохождения этого вызова связана с повсеместным непониманием природы ментальных расстройств в нашей культуре." (*The difficulty of meeting this challenge is linked to the widespread misunderstanding of the nature of mental disorders in our culture.*) Fighting depression, a person most often cannot get adequate support from family and friends, which aggravates the problem at all levels. Resources pointed to: an academic article on managing team depression and an online depression self-test (the Beck Depression Inventory, judging by the link) [msg #398].

### Country-level care for employee wellbeing
The platform Remote.com built a ranking of 60 countries in which employees' mental health and wellbeing are among state-level priorities (forwarded from the channel "Чистые Когниции"). Criteria: length of paid leave, parental (maternity) benefits, wage levels, healthcare accessibility, safety and the general happiness level of the population. In 2025 the top 3 were New Zealand (leading for the third year in a row), Ireland and Belgium. The top ten also included Germany, Norway, Denmark, Canada, Australia, Spain and Finland. At the bottom of the 60 are Egypt, the USA and Nigeria. Russia ranked 43rd [msg #1442].

Commentary by coach and RANEPA lecturer Darya Timchuk [msg #1442]:
- **Culture and history drive state care.** In Northern Europe the idea "happy employee = productive employee" is built into the mentality, whereas in Russia it is still believed that a burned-out person is someone who did not try hard enough [msg #1442].
- **Care lives in details, not only in parental leave or pay.** Examples: some companies hand out ice cleats ("кошки") during heavy ice so employees reach the office without injury; in Dubai this summer a four-day week was introduced for state-company employees because in 40-degree heat people need more rest [msg #1442].
- **Economics matters**: when a state has resources it can afford to care for people [msg #1442].
- **State care and people's psychological state are directly linked.** As a coach she sees clients from different countries arrive with different baseline "security packages": in countries with more state support people set goals more boldly, accept change more easily and come with development requests; clients from less-supported countries more often ask how to cope and where to find strength [msg #1442].
- **Russia belongs to the second type.** Russia has many holidays, 28 days of vacation and parental leave, but what matters is not only how many days you are given but how you can use them: do people have money to rest, can they relax, what vacation is it if the manager can ask you to "stay in touch"? Mental health is still perceived as a fashionable thing rather than a necessity [msg #1442].
- **Positive trend.** More companies include psychological help in voluntary medical insurance (ДМС), introduce flexible schedules and employee support programs, and — most importantly — wellbeing increasingly sounds in the public agenda; people more often say they want to work in normal conditions, which is already a step toward change [msg #1442].

### The 2026 squeeze: burnout versus productivity
58% of employees have personally faced burnout, and half of them left their company because of it. At the same time HR's priority number one for 2026 is labour productivity, named by 72.8%. Unemployment is at a historic low, so "заменить выгоревшего человека уже некем" (there is no longer anyone to replace a burnt-out person), while greater results are expected. The source does not name who produced these surveys or for which country [msg #1956].

### Why the wellbeing market under-delivers
The wellbeing topic has been pushed for several years; unlike the recent past, 85% of companies now consider it important, but only 37% actually invest in it and only 27% rate the effectiveness of their own programs highly. Wellbeing budgets are not growing. The author's summary: the problem is important, but companies do not much want to pay for it. The market is full of suppliers who are not trusted and products after which there is nothing to show; "Платить за лекцию про баланс больше никто не хочет" (No one wants to pay for a lecture about balance any more). This extends the earlier channel position that external perks and one-off inputs do not change employee state [msg #1956].

## Evidence and numbers

### Workplace stress, anxiety and burnout
- Gallup (as cited): even before the pandemic, 76% of American workers showed some degree of burnout [msg #435].
- 33% of Russians name stress as the main cause of illness (RBC Trends, as cited) [msg #435].
- RBC HR Lab survey of 1.4 thousand employees of various Russian companies (October 2022): about 80% experience constant anxiety or periodic panic flare-ups - more than half are in a stably anxious state and another 27% periodically have panic attacks. Effects on productivity: 52% have trouble concentrating on work because of the negative psychological background; 49% report falling motivation; 35% report slower task completion [msg #747].
- Depression costs the US economy $100 billion per year, $44 billion of it in lost employee productivity [msg #398].
- Quiet quitting: up to 50% of US employees (Gallup); harsh managers have 4x more quiet-quitting subordinates (2020 study cited via HBR) [msg #753].

### Deloitte 2021 Millennial and Gen Z Survey - methodology and global findings
- Methodology: the 10th annual survey polled 14.6K millennials and 8.2K Gen Zs (22,928 respondents) in 45 countries; fieldwork 8 January – 18 February 2021. Millennials were born January 1983 – December 1994, Gen Zs January 1995 – December 2003. For the first time Gen Zs were surveyed in all countries, including 25 geographies where only millennials had been surveyed, so Gen Z year-over-year comparisons are not always possible [file #541].
- Environment: during the pandemic health care and unemployment topped millennials' concerns, but environment remained a priority (#3 for millennials, #1 for Gen Zs). About 40% believe more people will commit to environmental action after the pandemic, but 60% fear business' climate commitment will become less of a priority [file #541].
- Inequality: 69% of millennials and 66% of Gen Zs think wealth and income are unequally distributed; nearly a third have supported politicians who want to reduce income inequality; roughly 60% say legislation limiting the executive–employee pay gap would significantly help, as would requiring a livable wage; more than half say universal basic income would help [file #541].
- Discrimination: one in five feel personally discriminated against "all the time" or frequently because of an aspect of their background; 6 in 10 Gen Zs and 56% of millennials say systemic racism is widespread in general society; they believe individuals and activists do the most to reduce it, while education, legal system, government and business fall short of their potential [file #541].
- Stress: almost half of Gen Zs and four in 10 millennials feel stressed all or most of the time; about two-thirds often worry about their personal finances; family welfare is a main stress cause for millennials; job/career uncertainty is top for Gen Zs [file #541].
- Workplace mental health: about a third (millennials 31%, Gen Zs 35%) have taken time off work due to pandemic stress, yet about 40% did not feel comfortable disclosing the reason to their employer; about 40% feel their employers did a poor job supporting their mental well-being during the pandemic [file #541].
- View of business and loyalty: less than half of millennials (47%) and Gen Zs (48%) think business has a positive impact on society - the first time below 50% since the survey began in 2012; 62% of millennials agree businesses "have no ambition beyond wanting to make money" (down four points from 2020). More would like to leave their employer within two years: 36% of millennials and 53% of Gen Zs, vs 31% and 50% in 2020 [file #541].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- (!) INCOMPLETE: in #177 the list of key organizational problems is "on the slide", which was not available; only the author's generalization is preserved [msg #177].
- The stressed person's sense of rational clarity is attributed to "adrenaline addiction or psycho-trauma" - an author's claim without cited evidence; "research confirms" stress destroys team effectiveness is likewise asserted without a named study [msg #177].
- Conflict of interest: deep mind sells mindfulness programs for companies; several posts frame trends as validating its offering (Magnit, Amazon) and end with its service options [msg #231, #435, #747, #753].
- Amazon's kiosks are interpreted both as validation and as tokenism; the channel holds both views explicitly [msg #435].
- (!) CONTRADICTION: the channel's "more than half of both generations want to leave within 2 years" does not match the attached report's 36% (millennials) / 53% (Gen Zs) [msg #541, file #541].
- (!) UNCLEAR: many Russia-specific chart values in the Deloitte PDF are scrambled in transcription; only clearly labelled figures are given as fact, the rest are marked as reconstructions [file #541].
- The Norwegian team-depression research is presented with the author's own hedge ("отдельные исследования" - *separate/individual studies*), and Koroteev's suggestion to start with middle management is hedged with "Возможно" (*perhaps*) [msg #398, #414].
- The forecast that a rollback in Russian management culture will redistribute talent toward ethical companies is the author's prediction, not data [msg #753].
- In #747 the RBC figure "about 80%" is composed of "more than half" stably anxious plus 27% with periodic panic attacks [msg #747].

- Effectiveness figures for deep mind's own programs (30% stress reduction per month, Avito 15%, 90% application, TAPCLAP 50%) come from the provider's own surveys and posts that also promote its programs — a conflict of interest the reader should note; the source does not describe measurement methods [msg #806, #945, #1043, #1346].
- (!) CONTRADICTION: 30% stress reduction per month for Less stress vs 15% reduction in psycho-emotional tension at Avito — not reconciled [msg #806, #1043].
- Performance-review statistics (McKinsey 10–20%, Deloitte 40–60%, 20–40% productivity loss) are presented without detail on study scope; the author also says objectivity concerns come from his "field observations" [msg #1419].
- Deloitte's £51bn and the claim that trauma is "probably" the main hidden cost factor are explicitly hedged in the source ("вероятно") [msg #1723].
- The author's endorsement of Patagonia includes a personal disclosure of brand loyalty [msg #1131].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the resilience post refers to a diagram ("см. схему выше") that was not transcribed; the components are taken from the text [msg #1027].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the six TIP principles are named but not individually explained; REACTMH is not expanded [msg #1723, img #1723].
- (!) UNCLEAR: parts of the Ipsos chart's men/women ranks are not reliably legible in the transcription [img #988].

- (!) UNCLEAR: the "−32%" is a relative drop in the share of people outside the norm (60% to 41% is 19 percentage points); the post's paraphrase "стресса стало на треть меньше" (a third less stress) equates fewer people out of norm with less stress overall, which the data shown do not measure directly [msg #1956, img #1956].
- The market statistics (58%, half leaving, 72.8%, 85%, 37%, 27%) are given without sources, dates or geography [msg #1956].
- Conflict of interest: the results come from deep mind's own measurement of its own paid program, in a post that promotes it; the questionnaire, sample size and absence of a control group are not described. Earlier parts already flag the same issue for Less stress figures [msg #1956].
