# Impermanence, suffering and meeting experience

## What this covers

This part collects the channel author's (Max, deep mind) contemplative teaching on how to relate to pain, suffering, fear, grief, death and the constant change of everything — including oneself and other people. It covers the formula that pain times resistance equals suffering, surrender as an act of self-compassion and maturity, the Stoic tools of controlling interpretations and separating what is in one's power, remembering death through a Tibetan dying practice, Bruce Tift's idea that we organise life around avoiding core fears, sitting with one's strongest fear on retreat, the "free-fall" relationship with uncertainty, grief as a doorway to compassion and preciousness, and a contemplative critique of Sapolsky's determinism. [msg #684, #739, #869, #873, #876, #944, #1002, #1049, #1184, #1255, #1270, #1272, #1277]

According to the author, the stakes are high: running away from pain ("psychic anaesthesia") does not free us but produces constant background anxiety, wastes energy on defences, and closes the heart; meeting what is actually here instead yields resilience, wisdom, compassion, aliveness and a sense of the preciousness of life. [msg #684, #873, #944, #1272]

Part 2 gathers the channel author's personal-contemplative essays on impermanence, death and suffering: the Tibetan notion of bardo applied to everyday transitions, memento mori and Andrew Holecek's teaching that all life is preparation for death, the practice of "simply being" with what one does not want to feel (via Bruce Tift and John Welwood), impermanence felt through a grandmother's aging and a changed childhood yard, an impermanence contemplation at the Boudha stupa in Kathmandu, and Tift's thesis that life is a process, not a problem to be solved. A forwarded post adds a personalist view: transience does not mean insignificance. According to the author, seeing impermanence and staying with experience teaches letting go, reduces suffering created by clinging and by the problem-solving frame, and brings one to "life itself" rather than an image of it [msg #1299, #1379, #1787].

## Core ideas

- **Suffering is a fundamental property of life; avoiding it is impossible.** Even in a privileged bubble one will fall ill, lose something and die. All we have is the choice of how to be with pain. [msg #873]
- **Resistance multiplies pain into suffering.** Turning away from pain puts us in constant conflict with a reality that is by nature shaky and changing. Dropping resistance leaves the factual pain but removes the unnecessary layer of suffering about it (see *Pain x resistance = suffering*). [msg #873]
- **Surrender is maturity, not defeat.** Capitulating the infantile reflex of looking away from pain is the bravest process and leads to more, not less, action to reduce suffering, plus natural compassion for others (see *Wisdom of surrender*). [msg #873]
- **We organise life around core fears.** Following Bruce Tift, the author holds that nearly all our actions are made to avoid meeting our deepest fears, at the cost of much of our psychic energy; reducing the fear changes how we see problems and solutions. [msg #1049, #1272]
- **Remembering death makes life more alive.** Consciously turning towards death and dying, rather than suppressing it, gives a measure for every action, raises responsibility and, paradoxically, makes the world look more beautiful. [msg #944]
- **Impermanence includes people and the self.** Everyone, including oneself, is a fluctuating stream of energy; grasping for a stable identity is like clutching water. Relaxing into this reveals presence itself as what does not disappear. [msg #1277]
- **Grief connects.** Direct contact with the sadness of impermanence, especially shared openly, gives fearlessness, compassion and the felt preciousness of everything living. [msg #1184]
- **Real engagement is meeting what is, not producing special states.** The task of a conscious life is the present moment as it is, meeting beauty and suffering, fears, cruelty and injustice with an open heart. [msg #869, #1002]

- **Nothing is stable; only the flow can be felt.** Life contains no stable objects; everything dies and is born again, and the only reliable thing is the impermanence of all things itself. This can frighten but can also give hope [msg #1299].
- **Clinging to the familiar creates suffering.** Life constantly invites us into a new world; holding onto the known, choosing an imagined safety, makes transitions (bardo) hard [msg #1299].
- **All of life is preparation for death.** Following Holecek, the process of dying will be determined by the state of mind we trained during life; living well and dying calmly require learning not to fear death [msg #1334].
- **Simply being is the hardest and most essential task.** Staying with what is, without filters or escape, is the most direct bridge to life, because life is built around avoiding core fears (Tift) [msg #1379].
- **Do not flee into spiritual ideas.** Impermanence can itself become a hiding place from being touched by life; relative truth and real human feelings must be respected (Tsoknyi Rinpoche) [msg #1393].
- **Impermanence clarifies, it does not cancel life.** Awareness of impermanence helps both to let go of and to value relationships more strongly [msg #1632].
- **Life is a process, not a problem.** Per Tift, suffering is created by the frame of treating life as a task to solve, not by life's content [msg #1787].
- **Transience is not insignificance.** The forwarded post argues the fleeting human person may be what the universe exists for [msg #1281].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Pain x resistance = suffering (Shinzen Young)
**Purpose:** a reframe of pain so one can be more resilient and happier; the author presents it as a variant of the reframing of suffering he called for in an earlier article on difficult states. [msg #873]

**The formula:** the American meditation teacher Shinzen Young gives the definition «боль умноженная на сопротивление = страдание» (*pain multiplied by resistance = suffering*). [msg #873]

1. **Pain** — the factual, unavoidable component. "No one has yet managed to avoid pain"; tomorrow one will get sick, lose something, and at some point die. Pain stays as long as one lives, "because I am human". [msg #873]
2. **Resistance** — the intuitive, understandable urge to escape pain at any cost, which the author likens to a permanent "escape from Shawshank". This very urge becomes the cause of suffering: by turning away and resisting, we keep finding ourselves in conflict with the reality of life, which is by nature fluid and constantly changing, and we suffer from those changes. [msg #873]
3. **Suffering** — the product. If resistance is removed, one is left only with the factual pain, and the "unnecessary part of suffering about this pain" falls away. [msg #873]

**The reverse also holds:** by meeting suffering we can "befriend" one of the fundamental properties of existence. When a person stops running even for a minute, he can encounter the very essence of what is happening; traditions call this gaining wisdom or knowing the truth of life. In this way suffering can give wisdom and depth of view. [msg #873]

**Cultural obstacle:** pain is unpopular even amid the wave of "new sincerity". The author expects a couple of dozen people to unsubscribe just from the title, because it reminds them of something negative and triggers pain around which avoidance mechanisms are built. Nobody sells or wants to buy pain; instead there is a naive belief that success will rid us of pain — in "the magical world of unicorns" a successful person supposedly has everything pleasant and does not suffer. Hiding the negative is characteristic of the modern culture of narcissism and "toxic success", and the author calls it "completely infantile". [msg #873]

### Wisdom of surrender (capitulation of infantility)
**Purpose:** what meeting pain actually requires. [msg #873]

1. **Meeting pain presupposes capitulation.** Eckhart Tolle called it the "wisdom of surrender" (мудрость сдачи). It is wise because it skilfully overcomes the weakness that makes us infantilely avert our eyes from pain. [msg #873]
2. **It is not capitulation before life, but capitulation of our infantility.** It can become the bravest process in life and lead to greater activity in reducing suffering, because only with such capitulation do we begin to see the facts — how we ourselves suffer and how much suffering there is around us. [msg #873]
3. **Surrender to reality is an act of self-compassion.** Dropping resistance leaves only the factual pain and removes the needless suffering about it. [msg #873]
4. **It is a key part of growing up.** Without meeting the facts it is impossible to truly take responsibility on oneself and for oneself. Ultimately, the author says, pain and suffering — through taking responsibility for them — can pull us out of the shell of self-absorption, as Chögyam Trungpa taught. [msg #873]
5. **It opens compassion for others.** Trungpa's student, the American psychologist Bruce Tift, pointed to a powerful expansion available when the infantile position is capitulated: at some point natural compassion for others arises. We see that others are in pain and how they unconsciously increase it by resisting life. Thus suffering can help us mature and develop a compassionate position. [msg #873]

The author's personal note: despite enormous luck and privileges, there is still much pain and suffering in his life; pain will be there while he lives. "But sufferings, like good teachers, only until the moment they still have something to teach me." [msg #873]

### Three Stoic ideas for hard times
**Purpose:** an essential excerpt from Sergey Sukhov's lecture for the deep mind community (an 8-minute video plus a short text), addressed to "those who left, those who stayed and everyone else" (October 2022). [msg #739]

1. **Control interpretations.** Usually we act automatically: the senses bring an impression and we find ourselves already in some action. The Stoics, like the Buddha, were among the first to discover that between these two steps a moment of choice is possible, which lets us not follow our impulses. [msg #739]
2. **Understand what is in your power.** The Stoics found that 99% of worries concern things we can barely influence. So it is important to divide everything that happens into two groups: what is in my power and what is not. This grounds a person and lowers anxiety in difficult times. [msg #739]
3. **Be ready for the worst.** First think about the worst and imagine what you will do if everything goes badly. The Stoics build these negative scenarios not to frighten themselves but to be prepared in advance. [msg #739]

### Three capacities for transforming challenges
**Purpose:** concrete strategies for turning challenges into joy and getting out of a permanent chain of obstacles ("Mastery of passing through challenges"). [msg #1049]

**Preliminary distinction — two kinds of challenge:**
- *Challenges point to a need for external resources.* Often the stress of a challenge simply shows a zone where hard skills or other resources needed for the task are lacking. Then we can be grateful to anxiety as a pointer, and probably really should build the skills or obtain the resources. [msg #1049]
- *Challenges stimulate inner growth.* Just as often — and for modern leaders perhaps more often — challenges demand a change of viewpoint and skills of handling the very person going through them (oneself). Wisdom from inner-work approaches and subtle skills can give the capacity for mastery in complex situations. [msg #1049]

**The three capacities:**
1. **View: life is not problematic.** Advanced contemplative traditions "flip the chessboard" and change the most fundamental rules of the game. Tantra — which here has nothing to do with popular trainings — means *transformation* and teaches it skilfully. It proposes to stop seeing life as a problem altogether and to love everything that happens to us. Most daily difficulties are not problems at all when seen from a wider perspective; those that truly seem to be problems can be used to know oneself and the world, and then they deserve respect and we start being grateful to them. Example: Ram Dass spoke this way about his stroke, which significantly limited his freedom but opened new facets of interdependence and acceptance. Further reading: Lama Zopa Rinpoche's *Transforming Problems into Happiness* (tantric view) and Pema Chödrön's *When Things Fall Apart* (Buddhist approach). [msg #1049]
2. **Self-regulation.** Stress from serious challenges literally "switches off" the prefrontal cortex, depriving a person of part of rational and creative thinking. If we learn to regulate our state in such moments, they become part of our training in mastery over ourselves — "the most important of all trainings". Moments of calm where they were previously unthinkable give an incomparable sense of freedom and strength. Developed self-regulation skills completely change our ideas both of challenges and of ourselves. Reading: *Self-Reg: How to break the stress cycle and successfully engage with life*. Training options named: on one's own through the author's Emotional Balance programme, or in a team through Less Stress. [msg #1049]
3. **Friendship with one's core vulnerabilities.** Challenges cause stress because they create risks, and we judge as unsafe whatever is connected to our deepest personal fears. Such fears form from childhood and in adulthood produce quite irrational behaviour that can strongly limit us. To master difficult situations, one can focus — in therapy, meditation or coaching — less on the problems, more on the places inside where core fears arise around them. Practice shows that when the strength of the fear decreases, a completely new understanding of both the problems and the ways to solve them appears — again because we are in a more regulated state. On organising life around avoiding core fears, the author points to Tift's *Already Free*. [msg #1049]

### Tibetan practice of the stages of dying (memento mori)
**Purpose:** consciously touching death and the process of dying so that life is lived without regret and fear. The author led friends through "a traditional Tibetan practice through all stages of dying" on a Monday evening; the post carries a trigger warning for those in grief or sensitive to the topic. [msg #944]

**Why it is needed — the diagnosis:**
1. Modern culture tries to avoid everything anxious and unpleasant. In the materialist paradigm the goal of life becomes satisfying needs and pleasure — including in the form of self-disclosure and self-realisation, which the author says are often a hidden or subtler form of the same hedonism and egocentrism. So with death all meaning ends, and emptiness, loss and pain arise; this looks frightening and bad to "the average materialist". [msg #944]
2. This setup generates enormous tension and anxiety: something inexplicable and bad is happening ahead and around us. Since we can do nothing constructive about it, we try not to notice, push anxiety away, turn to the "good" and ignore the "bad". By the laws of the psyche this pattern leads not to liberation but to constant background anxiety and attempts to compensate for it. [msg #944]

**The contemplative alternative:**
1. In Buddhism one "rides the waves of reality" rather than ignoring it. The natural process of dying, unquestionably part of every life, is given an important place; it is an unusual, definitely peak experience, and conscious contact with it can qualitatively change our life. [msg #944]
2. **Remembering death is the adult way of dealing with reality.** Reality is such that death can come at any moment — "It is as it is". To approach death without regret and without fear (which may seem a useless idea now but will certainly not seem so later), one must measure and evaluate every action precisely by the moment of the final outcome. [msg #944]
3. **Dying without fear and regret is the true unfolding of life's potential**, unavailable to most because they constantly run from death. Even just thinking that you want to die without regret leads to reflecting on how to live your days right now and gives a different level of responsibility for your actions. For example, the author suggests that by deceiving, killing and using other people we will not get peace at the very end. [msg #944]
4. **Paradoxical result:** conscious turning to death and dying leads to greater aliveness. The participants said that after the practice everything around looked more beautiful and alive, and to the author they appeared with more spark in their eyes. [msg #944]

**Caveat on doing it alone:** the practice (a recording) can be done independently only if you have therapeutic support and experience of coming out of stress/traumatic reactions. For non-Buddhists it starts at minute 17 of the recording. (!) INCOMPLETE: the stages of the practice themselves are in the recording and are not described in the text. [msg #944]

### Sitting with the strongest fear (presence without fixing)
**Context:** on a holistic retreat on a tropical island (more "sanatorium" than meditation retreat, because these years he must do "many dances with a tambourine" just to recover enough to get to practice), an intense first-day practice catalysed emotional opening and brought up the author's "main fear", with which he had to stay alone for some time. He then had psychotherapy almost every day ("doing therapy like going to the shower"), read John Welwood, and meditated. [msg #1272]

**Why fear matters (Tift):** Bruce Tift says we all organise our whole life around such fears — 99% of actions are performed so as not to encounter them — growing many neurotic defences and depriving ourselves of a large share of psychic energy that goes to maintaining "these fortresses around what is really frightening". [msg #1272]

**How he practised (in the short hours of meditation):**
1. Remember what sitting is like and relearn sitting with the strongest fear. [msg #1272]
2. Do **not** try to work it through, calm it or drive it away. [msg #1272]
3. Simply be present without "the tiresome split" into experience and the one who observes it. [msg #1272]
4. Just be with the experience — not collapsing into it, but not suppressing it either. [msg #1272]
5. Notice the relief of stopping, even for minutes, the habit of constantly trying to create pleasant experience and avoid the unpleasant — a habit the author calls as energy-consuming as it is foolish, given its absolute impossibility even in the medium term. [msg #1272]

**Result:** "So now we are friends with fear. It is still here, but my presence is also here. We can sit and walk together on the island." He has more energy and sees everything more beautifully and brightly. He grants there are objective reasons, but attributes it rather to a brief calming of the mind, which lets him see beauty in the depth of contact with the world instead of staying in the "flat and stuffy little world" of unsuccessful pursuit of hedonistic pleasure and avoidance of pain. There is nothing bad in that pursuit, he says, as long as we do not confuse it with what can actually give a somewhat stable result in the form of genuine well-being. [msg #1272]

**Welwood on the closing heart** (quoted by the author): the heart closes most strongly when we do not allow ourselves to experience our experience but evaluate, criticise or try to change it. We often believe something is wrong with us if we are angry, strongly needy, feel dependence, loneliness, confusion, sadness or fear. We accept our experience only on special conditions — "if I feel this way, something is wrong with me… I can accept myself only if my experience matches my idea of how one should feel". Since we keep setting these conditions, we stay alienated from ourselves. [msg #1272]

### Free-fall mode (stepping into the unknown)
**Core thesis:** at some point the author felt that life is "not an endless search for stability but the art of bravely falling into the abyss of the unknown". [msg #876]

**The story and its lessons, in order:**
1. **Trigger — value dissonance.** A year before turning 30, after exhaustingly organising his company's participation in SPIEF and "all that cheek-puffing", he flew to a birthday in Tbilisi. His work had him socialising with men aged 40–70; the wide circle gave many opportunities, but a background feeling of slight nausea was usually present. On the way to dinner they stopped to slaughter a ram; he felt he could do nothing to prevent a death right before his eyes. ### Bardo — the transitional state between the departing and the coming
Purpose: a lens for any ending or transition (moving away, the end of a day, after any major event) that helps one both "die well" and "live well" [msg #1299].

1. **Recognize that you are in bardo.** The author, leaving a small island he had visited often over 10 years and where he lived for several months, calls the departure "a small death, a transitional state." In some traditions such moments are called bardo — the gap between what is leaving and what is coming. Whether we like it or not, we are in bardo all the time: every night we die to this day and wake into a new one; after any important event comes a period when the old has gone and the new has not yet formed [msg #1299].
2. **See what makes bardo hard.** Andrew Holecek, a known contemplative and researcher of death who taught at Naropa, says automatic habits and a lack of understanding of the nature of things make staying in bardo — and the natural transition into a new state — more difficult. We find it hard to leave "this world" because we are too used to it: it is all we know, and even if it is terrible, the familiar creates a feeling of comfort, safety and coziness. Stepping into the new world is hard because we do not know what awaits in the unknown [msg #1299].
3. **Notice the daily cost of clinging.** The author thinks this habit of holding onto the familiar plays a cruel joke on us every day and creates much suffering: where it is long time to let go and move forward, we cling to the familiar and choose an imagined safety. He remarks that many politicians currently exploit this, leading people into a past that no longer exists [msg #1299].
4. **Make contact with the flow.** Farewells (here, to the owner of an Italian restaurant who has been on the island ~20 years, whom the author watched age from energetic adults into grandparents and whose empty COVID-era restaurant has since expanded twice) let one feel the current of life: something leaves, something stays, and the author has changed too. He describes such moments as transpersonal experiences that evoke not simple feelings like irritation at a colleague but truly complex ones — sadness, tenderness, gratitude, wonder, mystery and joy "in one bottle" [msg #1299].
5. **Let the flow teach letting go.** Contact with the current teaches release because it carries everything away and again brings something fresh; nothing can be grasped and held. Everything will certainly change [msg #1299].
6. **Live forward.** Understanding bardo helps not only to die well, finishing what should be finished, but to live well: not fearing change, being in motion, going into the new world even without knowing what it will be. In a sense this is what living for real means, even if one meets fear, discomfort and sadness on the way [msg #1299].

The author's Austrian-Thai meditation teacher laughingly admitted she now does less formal practice: the main thing is to watch one's mind; if you do, life itself becomes a beautiful teacher and you learn to let go, since everything leaves anyway. ### Simply being with experience — "What do I not want to feel right now?"
Purpose: the author's lifelong core practice ("the most important training apparatus, lifelong") and the starting point of real meditation or psychotherapy [msg #1379].

1. **What "simply being" means.** Being with what is already in experience — body sensations, emotions, thoughts, moods — without adding interpretations on top, without escaping into explanations, without pulling toward the pleasant and without suppressing the painful; in short, not mixing experience with something extra, as we usually do. "Simply" refers not to ease but to the uncomplicated nature of the act [msg #1379].
2. **Why it is hard and why it matters.** It may sound modest but is one of the hardest and most essential human tasks. Solving it steadily, again and again, in the author's view makes us a person — not a set of learned reactions and correct actions, but a living being awakening from the ordinary sleep of automatism and only then being born into full life [msg #1379].
3. **The mechanism (Bruce Tift).** Tift says a person's whole life is built around avoiding core fears. Externally, investing unreal amounts of energy and resources into "building" a life, we often only compensate for what we cannot bear inside: feelings of insignificance, guilt, anxiety, loneliness, helplessness. Most of us spend years, sometimes decades, learning to avoid this encounter — running and worrying endlessly so as not to be alone with what is inside, not to feel how frightening it can be simply to be. This is an endless race, "for you cannot get drunk on food" (compensation never satisfies) [msg #1379].
4. **Address the root.** The running can be stopped and the painful knot untied only by turning to the root cause. So staying with one's experience without filters and without leaving becomes not a meditation technique or a nice phrase but the most important, most direct bridge to life — not to its pretty image, but to life happening in the body, feelings and thoughts, right now and never at another moment [msg #1379].
5. **See before changing (John Welwood, quoted).** Before we can really change anything, we must see, feel and understand what is, not our habitual version of reality. This is hard: we are blinded by hopes and fears, habitual ways of feeling and perceiving, favorite beliefs and opinions. So if we want to turn the personality into a path, the first step is to promise ourselves to perceive ourselves as we are, despite fear of possible discoveries. Welwood's heading: "Чтобы стать человеком, надо родиться дважды" (To become human, one must be born twice) [msg #1379].
6. **Practice daily, gently, in all conditions.** Perhaps the most valuable human skill is to approach the "apparatus" of radical openness to experience every day, little by little, again and again; to learn to be with what is without being distracted from oneself; to stay softly in what is already happening — when bad and when good, in wealth or poverty, defeat or success, health or illness, youth or old age. Here, in contact with the real, happens our first birth as a being present in life [msg #1379].
7. **Understand it as courage.** This is an act of genuine courage — not what an unhealthy culture calls masculinity (control, domination, endurance at any cost, which the author calls "soaked in weakness"). Genuine courage rests on serious inner strength that allows one not to run away, not to devalue, not to hide behind actions, roles and achievements. It needs no loud roar or visible feat but ultimately makes us a person — not by passport or job title but by presence and the capacity to be alive [msg #1379].
8. **Ask the question.** This is the hero's journey Campbell described analyzing the wisdom traditions of all great cultures, which begins and continues forever with one simple question: "Что я прямо сейчас не хочу чувствовать?" (What do I not want to feel right now?). A sincere search for the answer makes meditation or psychotherapy real and will take one further than any ecstatic, spiritual, authentic, fashionable or unfashionable retreats, trainings and teachers [msg #1379].

### Contemplative critique of Sapolsky's determinism
- **The book's claim.** Sapolsky's new book again states that humans have no free will: all decisions and actions are predetermined by genetics, neurobiology and environment; behaviour results from a complex interaction of biological and ecological factors we do not control. [msg #1270]
- **What the author appreciates.** Neuroscience "once again plays on the side of the contemplative approach". Though Sapolsky does not write about it, the book shares key contemplative ideas — recognising interdependence and the illusory nature of an autonomous "I", renouncing condemnation, and the necessity of empathy. It also gives a modern explanation of karma: in both Sapolsky and the traditional understanding of karma, behaviour and state result from accumulated conditions, not free choice — "karma in neurobiology". [msg #1270]
- **The systemic narrative.** Sapolsky contributes to the growing systemic scientific narrative developed by Gabor Maté, Otto Scharmer, Daniel Siegel and other scientists (the source names only these three): many individual problems cannot be "isolated" in the individual and are in essence symptoms of systemic imperfections, so they must be solved through systemic change. Example: an individual's addiction becomes a symptom of society's ill health (Maté's *The Myth of Normal*). Despite right-wing political rollbacks, the author thinks this narrative will very probably develop and have far-reaching consequences — from management changes to treating disease to building a new justice system. Although Sapolsky focuses on material causes (neurobiology, hormones, environment), his approach is humanistic and free of cynicism — another brick from science toward a more humane and conscious society. [msg #1270]
- **Critique 1 — consciousness ignored.** As B. Alan Wallace (meditation teacher, Stanford PhD, Sapolsky's Stanford colleague) often points out, Western scientific reductionism — especially easy to fall into in neuroscience — ignores the nature of consciousness as primary reality. The contemplative approach shows consciousness has properties of substantial weight; thanks to this a person can go beyond conditioned habits through developing mindfulness. This stays outside the author's (Sapolsky's) attention. [msg #1270]
- **Critique 2 — relative freedom through practice.** Although an ordinary person is under the influence of the deterministic conditions the book describes, mindfulness practice allows developing freedom from them. This "freedom" is not absolute, but it offers the possibility of authentic expression — which clearly contradicts Sapolsky's determinism. [msg #1270]
- **Critique 3 — the "biological automaton" idea.** Though Sapolsky seemingly refutes it himself (but many authors and relaying bloggers really believe it), such determinism multiplies the bad reductionist idea that a human is a biological automaton defined by biology (or by history in Harari): he empathises not because he consciously chooses but because oxytocin was high. In Sapolsky too it ends up as: not the person committed the crime, but his brain. Such ideas simplify reality, swap causes and effects, diminish agency and ignore the existence and nature of consciousness, juggling only stimuli and responses. [msg #1270]
- **Verdict.** Sapolsky gives "a cool map of a human" who knows nothing of developing mindfulness and does not practise it; the map ignores the practitioner and omits him also in analysing needed social changes. The book is useful in debunking important misconceptions and deserves respect, but reflects a materialist, reductionist worldview limited by neurobiology. Made contemplatively informed and integrating an understanding of consciousness, it would show a more holistic picture — acknowledging conditioning while leaving the individual more agency and significance of personal ethics, and "sold under a less loud title". [msg #1270]

The trap: "Так сладко в идее о непостоянстве укрыться от того, чтобы быть тронутым жизнью" (It is so sweet to hide in the idea of impermanence from being touched by life) with all its changes, which are not always joyful. Seeing reality as it is contains much pain — everything constantly falls apart and dies; we are fleeting guests who cannot truly hold onto things, career, reputation, children. The body, which from some point we try with all our strength to keep from changing, is the closest daily teacher of this law. With tears welling up, he understands why people want a firm narrative, some support against the all-pervading instability of life [msg #1393].

Two escapes from direct experience: some are tempted to deceive themselves with the illusion of solid reality, joyfully deifying materialism; others, after reading a few books on Zen or meeting "the only true spiritual teacher," unable to bear the power of direct experience, retreat into spiritual ideas — "все иллюзия, все пустотно, меня не существует" (everything is illusion, everything is empty, I do not exist) [msg #1393].

Tsoknyi Rinpoche's correction: during in-person meetings in Malaysia in April he several times said a phrase and fell silent: "Нельзя обесценивать относительную истину. Относительную истину надо уважать" (One must not devalue relative truth. Relative truth must be respected). The author's application: hold contact with instability but do not flee into clever wisdom away from immediate human experience. "Я человек и мои чувства такие реальные!" (I am a human and my feelings are so real!) He feels and respects his grandmother's aging, the changes in the yard and in himself, and that this moves him to tears. This life, truly relative in its impermanence, is a genuine reflection of something greater [msg #1393].

Learning old age: his beloved grandmother leaves the house with more difficulty and forgets words a little. He tries to learn old age from her now, because our decisions and choices risk being very mistaken and superficial if we do not see the whole picture of human life. "Никто не избежит старости." (No one will escape old age.) [msg #1393]

Crying as the start of practice: sometimes, to live through it, one must simply truly feel and truly cry. "Если ты еще по-настоящему не плакал, то практика еще не началась." (If you have not yet truly cried, the practice has not yet begun.) [msg #1393]

### Death in our culture vs elsewhere
Demographer Carl Haub calculated that since 50,000 BCE about 108 billion people have walked the planet, including 7 billion living now — so about 100 billion have already died, and we will certainly be among them. Despite this natural, universal process, death in our culture is mysterious, avoided and triggering: try talking about death at a social party and you will quickly find yourself outside society. In the mainstream death is felt as weakness and final defeat, "and this is a big problem" [msg #1334].

Contrast: a couple of years ago the author sat among cheerful Nepalis at sunset at the Hindu temple Pashupatinath in Kathmandu, where, as in Varanasi, bodies are burned and remains sent down the river. Every evening a crowd gathers and with joy and lightness sings, walks and so sees off the dead into some other state. He explicitly does not call this good or bad, only very different from how we treat death [msg #1334].

Mechanism of the cost: we do not understand and fear death, so we hide it under the rug in each day. This hinders, if not completely cancels, our ability to befriend a natural part of life and thereby better understand and live life itself [msg #1334].

Case — Dasha's care-home work: at 23, right after the psychology faculty of Moscow State University, Dasha worked in the best Moscow care home for the elderly; for many months she spent time with people "on the final stretch," and every week a patient died. The author (affected tangentially) says this shaped them both strongly: it forced them to learn much truth about life we usually ignore and gave an amazing sobriety about how valuable each second of human life is and how important it is how we spend them. As a result both work on what matters — helping people — rather than on what looks good [msg #1334].

### Practice in chaos rather than in comfort (Boudha)
Being at Boudha feels like a leap into the Middle Ages, though with ever more modernity: technology, global tourism and money leave no place a chance of staying unchanged. The medieval, past or present, is not romantic — scorching sun, dust, poverty and disease, belief in dubious magic and myths. Arriving at 8 a.m., one passes a line of beggars and disabled people, who in the US are isolated in separate neighborhoods and in Russia removed from sight entirely; here one meets this inevitably and touchingly. Spitting sounds become background, without disgust; dogs foul everywhere. He loves the place: not always pleasant, but it always knocks down arrogance a little and removes needless crusts of the mind's preoccupation with modern problems. A Tibetan woman with a censer on metal chains fumigates the stupa with juniper and sings a song that pierces him; two hours later in the same spot a Nepali man decides to clip his fingernails ("Ok.") — the sacred and mundane side by side [msg #1632].

He likes practicing in the style opposite to how it is now taught in the West: no quiet room, beautiful cushion or pleasant incense; everything is extremely intense, with smells, sounds, movement and many repellent objects. It is an amazing chaos in which many people direct intention and attention to non-egoistic actions, and this is felt and supports more strongly than a comfortable cushion of organic buckwheat husk. He likes finding himself in this thousand-year faceless stream — finding himself at his own center not in conditions isolated from the world but where he is maximally placed in it [msg #1632].

### Life is not a problem to be solved (Bruce Tift)
Context: Tift taught at Naropa University before moving to California (Naropa, where the author and Dasha spent much of 2025 while Dasha trained in PAT, was co-founded with the involvement of Ram Dass, Allen Ginsberg and Chögyam Trungpa; its teachers included Ken Wilber, John Welwood and Aroana Hayashi). Years ago the author was stunned by Tift's statement in his book that life is not a problem to be solved; it contradicted the paradigm he had lived in for years and freed him from many habitual strategies and a substantial share of suffering [msg #1787].

1. **Where the feeling that life is a problem comes from.** Western psychology and culture are built on the logic of solving: something is wrong → find the cause → fix it → it will get better. We apply this logic to ourselves constantly — to emotions, patterns and relationships. So anxiety becomes a problem, conflicts too, pain necessarily so. Even spiritual search is often built the same way: "I am not where I should be; I need to get there" [msg #1787].
2. **The frame creates the suffering.** Tift says suffering is created by this frame itself — not the content of life but the way of relating to it [msg #1787].
3. **The position of lack.** When you treat your life as a task to be solved, you are always in a position of lack: something is always wrong and you need to become a bit better, more healed, richer or freer. In this paradigm happiness is always somewhere ahead — after the next problem is solved. ("Ha-ha," the author adds.) [msg #1787]
4. **Life as process.** But the problem does not get solved, because life is not a problem — it is a process. It does not move toward a final point where everything becomes right; it simply continues with all its intensity, unpredictability and pain, which cannot be avoided. Realizing and accepting this takes us into a completely different plane [msg #1787].

### Transience and the significance of the person (forwarded post)
A forwarded post from the channel Malakhoverse uses an analogy: the Higgs boson lives in a collider for practically no time — ten to the minus twenty-second of a second, a number the writer says does not help imagine the duration — yet the boson, or rather the Higgs field it derives from, plays (at least in the physicists' *Mundus Imaginalis*) a decisive role in forming the material world. Likewise with humans: changeability, fragility and fleetingness — our lives are vanishingly brief on the scale of the material world — do not indicate the person's insignificance and randomness. Rather, we may be what the whole universe exists for: an enormous collider needed so that, for cosmic instants, the flicker of reality itself can appear somewhere [msg #1281].

The writer holds that when personalists say the person is the paradigm of being, this is not only a poetic image but an analytically precise and most plausible ontology: reality has a deeply personal nature, and its self-disclosure begins with us and in us. Whatever the mystery of the world, both it and its answer are hidden in the human being. We are only beginning to know ourselves, and if we do not destroy the planet and do not prefer manic oblivion to openness to the gift of being, we can become witnesses, participants and narrators of many unexpected stories that, once lived, turn out to have "always already been here" true and familiar [msg #1281].

The writer's main practice on this journey: to develop the ability to see in every person — regardless of qualities, merits, weaknesses and vices — the heart of reality itself, infinitely precious, holy and unique, and to relate to them (the person and reality) with all love and reverence [msg #1281].

## Tools and resources mentioned

Воспоминания* (Tightrope Walker: Memoirs)** — freely available memoir of the Russian mathematician and philosopher (author of *The Probabilistic Model of Language* and *Spontaneity of Consciousness*) who spent many years in Stalin's camps for his views.
