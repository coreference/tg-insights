# Spiritual materialism and pitfalls on the inner path

## What this covers

This topic collects the channel author's (deep mind) critique of how inner work — psychotherapy, meditation, tantra, retreats, psychedelic ceremonies — goes wrong in modern culture: when it is shopped for, collected for status, valued by intensity, or used as an escape from reality (spiritual bypassing, "Bali avoidance", "resort consciousness", "everything is already perfect"). Against these traps it sets out what the author sees as the actual conditions for transformation: honest motivation, commitment to a specific relationship or practice over time, simplicity and a beginner's mind, contact with the shadow and integration with ordinary and collective life. According to the source, this matters because otherwise people invest huge resources for years and never reach the "fruit of practice" — essential transformation [msg #488, #762, #1224, #1339].

## Core ideas

- **What goes in is what comes out.** The result of inner work is determined mainly by the motivation one brings; going to practice for entertainment, hype, status or novelty yields no sustainable transformation (see *Motivation check*) [msg #488, #1224, #1339].
- **Consumption is the main modern avoidance.** Spiritual materialism today looks like shopping for and collecting practices; it is not harmless because it leads away from essential change, though it also normalizes practices (see *Shopping model*, *Consumer avoidance*) [msg #762, #1339].
- **Systematic commitment, not variety, produces change.** In therapy the proven factor is a stable alliance within a specific, long-lasting relationship; in meditation, regularity. Collecting many experiences without forming a path leads nowhere [msg #762, #916, #1224].
- **Intensity is not value.** Measuring experience by "wow" is a bias imported from the experience economy; slow, gradual processes are often more effective, while chasing intensity risks retraumatization [msg #1521].
- **Spiritual bypassing cuts off parts of reality.** Using inner work to escape personal tasks, social and political reality, pain and conflict — whether in "adult sandboxes" like Bali, in resort-like communities, or in slogans that "everything is perfect" — is avoidance, not maturity [msg #1292, #1337, #1511, #1614].
- **Real progress shows as simplicity and humility.** Success is becoming simpler, fresher, a beginner again, aware of how much lies ahead — not inflating into "the main expert" [msg #916, #1523].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Shopping model of development tools (and its two benefits)
Purpose: to name one of the most widespread models of relating to development tools today. The author observes that ten years earlier people did not go en masse to psychotherapy, Vipassana and tantra; now all of this has become fashionable and commercialized "both on the side of professionals and on the side of clients." In the shopping model we treat development tools as fun, consume them and make them part of our status. Transformative experience has long been included in the status system of many circles and strata: for example, if I belong to "successful" entrepreneurs (or try hard to), I am practically obliged to constantly consume development experiences and be "informed" in meditation, ayahuasca, tantra — the author adds the mocking rhyme "фигантре" ("shmantra") — and be saturated from every side [msg #762].

The shopping model has only two benefits, according to the author:
1. **Normalization.** Consumption reduces the strangeness of practices and makes them a norm for the whole society. The author calls this "very good" and says that is why he does not criticize the model radically [msg #762].
2. **A path to real work for some.** Years of blind and relatively meaningless "poking around" lead some people to begin systematic work on themselves with qualified professionals [msg #762].

The cost: grabbing a bit here and a bit there, one is unlikely to practice correctly or transform at all. The author's appeal: remember the history and meaning of development practices — they are "precious and sometimes very labour-intensive discoveries of other people" who share them for the sake of the transformation behind them; do not buy them from those who want **only** to sell, and do not practice shopping if you want to understand something about yourself and truly get rid of dissatisfaction and fear [msg #762].

### Motivation check ("what goes in comes out") and the four key questions
Purpose: the foundational test to apply at any stage of the path — "especially when you are already 'experienced'" [msg #1224].

- **The principle.** The author calls "что на входе – то и на выходе" ("what goes in is what comes out") the cornerstone principle of any inner work: the result is substantially determined by motivation; no stable result or transformation comes if we go to practices to be entertained or to hype. He adds that this is "not moralizing" [msg #488]. In #1339 he says the phrase is a saying of the helping professions [msg #1339].
- **Why.** "Motivation completely shapes experience": with very small probability can we get more from practice than was initially laid into our motivation. If we approach the most serious transformation tools — tantra, meditation, psychedelics, depth therapy — for consumption (fun, pleasure, pampering the ego with uniqueness and novelty), we will probably miss their whole transformational potential and cannot assemble powerful tools and experiences into a path that bears fruit [msg #1224].
- **Tradition backing.** It is "not by chance" that the Tibetan tradition, which the author calls one of the most powerful systems of psychopractice, pays enormous attention to motivation. B. Alan Wallace calls this conative intelligence — the ability to discern one's intentions and sustain stable motivation and aspirations; it is "the force behind our desires and motivation," without which all other kinds of intelligence lose direction. The ability to create the needed motivation defines the depth of change [msg #1339].
- **The four key questions** that, according to the author, remain central in real work; replacing them with another motivation automatically creates a barrier on the path [msg #1339]:
  1. *Ради чего я вообще здесь?* (What am I here for at all?)
  2. *От чего я постоянно убегаю?* (What am I constantly running away from?)
  3. *Чего я больше всего боюсь?* (What do I fear most?)
  4. *За что я так сильно цепляюсь и почему?* (What am I clinging to so strongly, and why?)
- **Practical consequence.** Results depend most seriously not on the therapist, shaman or coach, not on how many cool people they have helped, not on the fee — far more on what we bring and with what motivation. So many people need to stop viewing these processes as services like a spa or a cinema and move to the position of a researcher of one's own experience in "ultimate honesty and openness" — constantly taking more healthy responsibility and going where one least wants to go: deepest fears, unpleasant moments in relationships, inconvenient facts about reality [msg #1339].

### Four non-growth motives for consuming inner practices (consumer avoidance)
The author completes a cycle of posts on avoidance with what he calls probably the main avoidance of modernity — **consumer avoidance**, which is what Chögyam Trungpa called spiritual materialism, now appearing as consumption of inner practices: psychotherapy, tantra, "teleski" (body practices), retreats and psychedelic ceremonies. Modern inner work turns into a catalogue of states, stories and realizations we collect, "as if buying artifacts in a shopping mall." Often this is done not for real contact with oneself but for [msg #1339]:
1. maintaining a special status;
2. a sense of belonging to some group;
3. improving mood and relieving anxiety;
4. or simply "not falling behind."

He does not want to demonize the dynamic but says it is not harmless: it leads further away both from the essence of inner work and from essential changes that actually move one toward well-being. In this culture we come to inner work as consumers, expecting someone outside to fix us, give us cool tools, understanding or simply create an incredible experience. But in inner work there is no "make me feel good" button — only a path, "sometimes quite ordinary, boring and sad" [msg #1339].

**"It's just a stage, you need to play it out"** — the author says he often hears this and calls the idea itself a form of intellectual rationalization as a defense: not every stage automatically leads to maturity, especially without contact with pain and a turn toward the shadow [msg #1339].

**Why consumption is unconstructive — Immunity to Change.** The author says Harvard professor Kegan's Immunity to Change approach (practiced in the author's Resonance program) explains this: systemic avoidance strategies are self-sustaining until their root — lying in fear and self-identification — is uncovered. Even with a strong desire to change we may unconsciously hold old strategies because they protect us from facing real "problems"; all the more so when we have no wish to change at all and came for something else. So we "work on ourselves" for years but remain in the same neurotic patterns, merely swapping some ideas about ourselves for new ones in which we are already very "worked-through." One can be entertained through breathwork or gather mindful parties as much as one likes, but there is no real request for growth there; in consumer avoidance it is motivation that suffers first [msg #1339].

### Self-inquiry for the pull toward intensity
Purpose: what to do if you notice in yourself a pull toward "the more powerful, the better." The author says this is not a reason to blame yourself but an invitation to inquiry that can be done right now by asking [msg #1521]:
1. What exactly am I looking for in intense practices — contact with aliveness, a sense of control, confirmation of my own courage?
2. Can I give myself the right to a slow, soft process in which the work does not look spectacular but gradually changes the foundations of my life?

The underlying model (bias, causes, harms, alternative) is set out under *Intensity is not the value of an experience* below [msg #1521].

### Three inner actions that turn travel into practice
Context: one need not limit oneself to formal practice (psychotherapy, meditation, other developmental activities); any action can become growth practice. For the author, three things work best: relationships, business and travel. Extending work on oneself beyond therapy sessions and morning sitting "on the cushion" is called informal practice; it is "not extreme and not always neurotic," and growth depends on it no less than on planned training in any method. To avoid the neurosis trap, one can strive to make everything a path of inner transformation simply because it is beautiful: perceiving literally everything lived as an opportunity to study oneself and reality makes life more symbolic and deep — "a beautiful form of living." Anything that softens the ego can become practice. When he travels (explicitly not sitting on Phangan or Bali, where tourists who ignore context and consequences have "swept local culture to zero"), his clinging to his own importance, problems and self-images softens, the heart opens to compassion and the joy of live encounters, creating powerful conditions for growth in a short time [msg #777].

The three inner actions [msg #777]:
1. **Intention.** "I don't travel as a consumer." The consumer role does not support development because in modern culture it puts me in a higher position and immediately creates blinkers that block the next steps. Instead he reminds himself of the role of a guest and tries to be a friend to everyone he meets.
2. **Attention or presence.** "Observe — observe — observe," as in Otto Scharmer's U-process (which deep mind uses for transformation in teams and organizations). Look at the world with a beginner's eyes, with curiosity and genuine interest; open to the pain, of which there is much around; really be in the experience rather than in one's judgments and thoughts. This is the most important step for the world to touch us, so that we grow from experience rather than "just running up the number on the age counter."
3. **Ethics, or noticing causes and consequences of my actions.** Ask: How does my presence affect the place? What remains after me? These questions help one serve others and become a co-participant in the world rather than its destroyer — e.g., you start carrying all your rubbish out of the mountains, or you consider local prices rather than your habitual ones so as not to create excess greed and destroy the culture of hospitality.

Result: when I stand on an equal footing with any culture, attend to what happens and try to leave places and people in a better state, "magic and transformation" can begin. The magic comes from witnessing unique circumstances that are not always comfortable, understandable or pleasing; this view makes travel moments complete and changes something inside, which reflection can then make conscious [msg #777].

Example: the author lived five days side by side with a porter from a Nepali village who works for 15 dollars a day, walks in a torn T-shirt and is raising 4 sons. Observing him, the author noticed he could not remain untouched by the porter's reality; this very simple encounter made him seriously think about how much money he really needs and whether he could use his time better to affect injustice and poverty at all. Perhaps in practice he cannot, but this itself shifts him toward more compassion, "and therefore maturity and depth [hope so]" [msg #777].

A later, mostly personal post (#1524, excluded as a travelogue) adds the author's own observation that intense travel experience seems to widen his "inner landscape" and make it hard to narrow himself back to one culture or fixed worldview, while constant movement makes deepening and routine (practice, systematic reading) much harder.

### Resort consciousness — definition, examples and conclusions for communities
Trigger: the author was invited to an entrepreneurs' community focused on personal development whose rules said "politics, religion, social conflicts, national differences — leave them at the door, we don't discuss them here." This reminded him of the taste of resort consciousness often found in neo-spiritual spots like Burning Man, Phangan or Bali, and widespread today in lightworker and transformational communities [msg #1511].

**Definition.** "*Курортное сознание* — воззрение, стремящееся создать что-то обособленное от испорченности обычного мира или проблем, которые мы не любим, боимся или не понимаем как интегрировать." (Resort consciousness — a view striving to create something separate from the corruption of the ordinary world or from problems we dislike, fear or don't know how to integrate.) Inside a container a space of lightness is created where "miracles and magical transformations" are the norm and any sharp corners are smoothed out and pushed into the shadow [msg #1511].

**On the one hand, it works.** People worldwide are very tired of news overload, social battles and polarization; at the "resort" they get an oasis of unity, a place to recover. The community becomes a resort for the psyche: a soft beach where one can take off one's armour and briefly forget the ordinary world. Such experience can be deeply touching and transformative [msg #1511].

**But it works one-sidedly and unsustainably.** When "inconvenient topics" are systematically cut out of the field of awareness, a habit of bypassing reality and a strategy of avoiding complexity form; harmony is kept in the moment but the whole construction becomes fragile. In integrative psychology this can be called a form of spiritual bypassing: instead of meeting the "shadow" — pain, conflicts, social differences — the community learns to live as if they don't exist [msg #1511].

**Examples from counterculture** [msg #1511]:
1. **Esalen, California, 1960s.** A center of humanistic psychology conceived as a laboratory of the new human, seeking a way beyond Western rationality. But soon it became clear that behind the facade of idyll lay disputes over power and meanings.
2. **Burning Man.** Its declaration of freedom and "radical inclusivity" in practice runs into questions of privilege, money and ecology; the desert event easily becomes an arena for conversations about who has access and who stays outside.
3. **The New Age movement** (Osho and many other gurus and communities). Mass enthusiasm for practices and a promise of harmony — but with it, superficiality and vulnerability to commercialization and guru manipulation. Resort consciousness became a product on the spirituality market.

**Beyond the resort.** Resort consciousness is useful as a temporary refuge — it lets one recover and breathe freshness — but if one gets stuck in it, it turns into a glass dome: beautiful, but limiting and fragile. Transformation requires not only light but readiness to enter shadow zones; in Theory U terms, to go down the U: see blind spots and conflicts we usually try to bypass. Then movement toward the future becomes sustainable [msg #1511].

**Is every container resort consciousness?** No. Containers that avoid the resort trap invite perspectives and conflicts inside rather than pushing them out, and "always — **always!**" pay great attention to integrating the experience with ordinary life: how what happens in the container connects with personal and collective challenges outside it [msg #1511].

**Four conclusions for communities** [msg #1511]:
1. Resort consciousness creates a resource and a feeling of safety — in reality limited, because ordinary problems don't disappear but are repressed.
2. It can be an entry point for many people seeking an experience of unity and restored contact with oneself and with other people.
3. But it becomes a trap if avoidance turns into the norm and is reinforced by community norms.
4. Communities that want to be alive and sustainable must be able to hold both light and shadow in the space — opening to difficult topics without destruction. Otherwise the resort becomes an illusion that sooner or later cracks under reality's pressure, "as already happened with Esalen and Osho."

### Psychological container vs "just a container" (Bali avoidance)
After living a month on Bali, the author describes "Bali avoidance" — less about Bali than about a psychological strategy, both personal and of whole communities [msg #1337].

- **Origin of the concept.** In the 1980s therapist John Welwood introduced spiritual bypassing — escaping into spirituality as a way to avoid solving key developmental tasks. He saw whole communities build spiritual practice not from the logic of genuine wholeness but from psychological defenses [msg #1337].
- **The observed dynamic.** In places more comfortable than a megacity it is easy to get lost in an eternal party and self-discovery without real goals. Many say Bali is like "a pioneer camp for adults": adults behave like teenagers — playing at paradise or building their own little kingdom, dressing in funny costumes and floating on unicorns, or wanting endless service that turns into being waited on [msg #1337].
- **Why it pulls (the post-Soviet childhood).** Behind the post-Soviet beauty on Bali the author keeps recalling a shared childhood: the pretentious-faced "Masha" with whom he ran around a shabby entrance hall with grey snow and sad poverty, who later moved to Moscow and worked in cruel toxic teams. It is no surprise we are drawn to a world of comfort and warmth where the body is a source of pleasure rather than an instrument, where one can finally "finish eating" the part of life that could not be had back then [msg #1337].
- **Pain of the past also shows as self-cruelty.** A local massage therapist told him she is afraid specifically of post-Soviet women: they demand the strongest pressure and complain to her boss if they don't suffer. The same pain shows in the striving to escape pain altogether — to create a beautiful life, land in the lightness of being, or become someone special [msg #1337].
- **Adult sandboxes.** The author empathizes and "sincerely loves these children" who live inside him too, but it is easy to confuse their wishes and our own inner process with some special energy of a place. Bali / Phangan / Burning Man become adult sandboxes — special containers where missed childhood joy is possible in adult life; we live a bright but simplified miniature of life: no boredom, no boundaries, no pain [msg #1337].
- **When it becomes a problem.** Problems arise when the container helps one escape reality instead of coping with it. We cling to toys instead of growing parts and feeling boundaries. Dissolving into one's own inner process shows up as a magical interpretation of the outer world that replaces real individuation — endless searches for one's purpose, seeing signs and energy flows [msg #1337].
- **The distinction.** A *psychological container* turns us toward ourselves and can restore resources to be more present in our own experiences while holding the boundaries of reality — "this is the avoided and most important psychological task." *Just a container* easily leads into a trap: warm sea, hedonism and the party lull the wounded child but cannot truly nourish it. Then the search for happiness and comfort becomes flight that never leads to well-being; play becomes a drug, and no adult appears next to the inner child — the child alone takes over the whole stage and gets stuck in play. Disappointment is inevitable because tasks neither get solved nor evaporate; hence many find that the island in the end "doesn't accept" them [msg #1337].
- **The shadow of the critique: judgment.** Many readers will feel disgust at all this "infantilism." But "we aren't triggered by what doesn't hook us": often in disgust lives the same longing child who was never given a voice, and to keep it at a distance one must urgently condemn the expression of its freedom. That too is flight — not into warmth but into control and rigidity [msg #1337].
- **Integration.** Integration is neither discarding sad Moscow life to dwell in eternal youth, nor keeping oneself constantly in an iron grip of formality, doing everything "correctly." In both cases important parts are suppressed and we get stuck in a one-sided strategy that leads away from whole living [msg #1337].

### Two levels of truth against "everything is already fine" reductionism
The author sometimes hears people in therapy or practice say the world is perfect, all is fine, nothing needs changing, saving or protecting, since everything is a manifestation of Great harmony. It sounds like reaching an important point of acceptance, but in his view it is often an extreme view — comforting, especially when tired of struggle or disappointed in social systems, but not truly integral: it creates an illusion of liberation, as if one could just let go, stop suffering and let everything be. More often it is "a pit of reductionism" we fell into along the way — reducing life's complexity to a convenient slogan that comfortably cuts off pain, chaos, injustice and with them the need for participation and responsibility. "Everything is already good" then becomes a psychological defense that helps not to feel reality's complexity under the guise of wisdom [msg #1614].

Arguments the author gives [msg #1614]:
- Contemplative traditions that holders of this view rely on do not teach inaction. If the Buddha had thought the world already perfect, there would have been no need to tell people about his psychological discoveries; on the contrary, he acted actively from understanding of interconnectedness and compassion.
- Rhetorical tests: if the world is already whole and perfect, why help a child in pain? Why tell the truth if a lie is also part of the harmony? Why create at all if "everything already exists"? The view leaves a person in a refined, often beautifully intellectualized form of non-participation, detachment and cynicism justified by clever words.
- Such views are dangerous not only for the detachment they create — they can justify any violence.

**Pseudo-systemics.** A related idea popular among "systemic therapists": "Все члены системы уже стоят на своих местах, всё так, как должно быть. Нечего исправлять — достаточно просто видеть" (All members of the system already stand in their places, everything is as it should be. Nothing to fix — it's enough simply to see.) Or: "Система пользуется нашим вниманием. Если боремся с ней, то лишь питаем ее" (The system uses our attention. If we fight it, we only feed it.) It sounds deep, but this "analysis" of the unconscious often hides magical thinking. Phenomenologically everything has causes and context, but it does not follow that everything is ethical or healthy; acknowledging that trauma is part of a system does not mean it should stay unchanged. If a mother beats a child and we say "it was needed for the evolution of the lineage," we replace compassion with observation and renounce responsibility by justifying events with "the system" [msg #1614].

**The view from later developmental stages** opposes such reductionism by distinguishing levels of truth [msg #1614]:
1. on the absolute level — everything is one;
2. on the relative level — suffering is real and demands a response.

Contemporary Tibetan masters such as Tsoknyi Rinpoche or Dzongsar Khyentse also stress that understanding emptiness without a stance of truly vast compassion becomes poison; non-duality without ethics is "another form of sleep, only more sophisticated." The world is indeed whole but not finished: it unfolds in every moment, and we are its organs of perception and action; compassion, creativity and readiness to notice pain are not a system error but its way of evolving. Therapeutic and spiritual achievements become such when they stop being a refuge that shields from others' suffering and instead open us to it, letting us hold more of the world's complexity without breaking and while actively participating. From there, understanding that the world is perfect on the absolute level becomes the most powerful call to act to relieve suffering around us. The same scheme covers "I am perfect, everything is fine with me": if the understanding of deep perfection is not supplemented by an objective view of areas for growth, it more likely reflects our narcissism than a next stage of integration [msg #1614].

## Detailed insights

### Inner work as status, leisure and spectacle
The author, who says he has seen hundreds of projects in the field and thousands of people practicing, finds it obvious that development practices have long become a symbol of social success or of belonging to certain groups; for many they have become fun leisure; and now inner work is also being turned into a "шапито" (circus/sideshow), where people pay to gawk at the inner worlds of others [msg #488].

### Commitment beats variety: therapy alliance and regular meditation
The author says he is increasingly interested not just in sharing development methods and tools but in helping people build a truly sustainable, results-producing practice — one in which we first become aware of our tasks and then solve them; he notes this is rather atypical today [msg #762].

When the deep mind community discussed the effectiveness of therapy, the first thought was the familiar one that the therapist's personality matters. The author says he believes in it, but this hypothesis does not find much scientific confirmation. What has been shown, he says, is systematicity — commitment to **a specific therapeutic relationship** over a more or less constant and long period. Then a reliable alliance arises, which the author calls a proven key factor of successful therapy (he links a research paper) [msg #762].

The same applies to meditation: grabbing here and there, we are unlikely to practice correctly or transform even a little. The author declines to cite studies, saying one can google "a million" showing that the more systematic and regular the practice, the more significant the changes in brain and behavior [msg #762].

### Practice without study (Lobsang Tenpa)
In a talk at deep mind community, Lobsang Tenpa said the main problem in the space of contemplative practices is excessive emphasis on practical application without study. Looking at the Russian-speaking space, "it would be hard to throw a stone and not hit some tantric retreat" where everyone is liberated at once, after which one must immediately go to popular business trainings. There is a lot of this, and it is connected, among other things, with not taking the time to google "what was actually meant by tantra in the traditional historical context?" [img #773].

### The "pseudo-perfectly worked-through" client
The author predicts — a future "that has already arrived" — that helping specialists will increasingly meet *pseudo-ideally worked-through* clients. They come to consultations and programs not to solve inner tasks but to write about it on their blog and tell their crowd; they are closed to growth by the thought that they already know everything since they have tried and understood a lot: they have meditated in caves, done tantra on Bali, moved their eyes in EMDR, talked to their parts in IFS, released terror in IMTT. Ayahuasca is "pop," they want peyote "and only natural"; Vipassana is boring, they look for slots in "post-punk dynamic meditations" from a fresh reincarnation of Osho and "the fifth coming of AI." What can a psychologist, trainer or mindfulness teacher surprise them with? They themselves are thinking of running trainings and already tell others from the height of their experience what to try next on the spiritual path [msg #916].

Mechanism and nuance: the more popular inner work becomes, the more the narcissistic part of clients in a narcissistic society will colonize the field, considering it simple, understandable and their own. The author says this is not a purely negative process — it complicates specialists' work in places and distorts the real goals of inner work in society, but life flows naturally, and statistically over time pseudo-ideal clients do become authentic people, with good education even helping practitioners, sometimes founders of new projects at the junction of inner-process experience and business or other expertise. "Perhaps deep mind itself is such a project" [msg #916].

What helps, both specialists and those simply on the path: tuning in to the goals of the path rather than attention to the number of different experiences and the coolness or "worked-through-ness" that is supposed to result. Quantity of practice does not mean result, especially if the practice leads to boredom and pride; many people "practice" Zen, Vipassana and even Dzogchen for years without essentially moving anywhere or actually practicing these methods [msg #916].

The author's criterion of success (which he invites readers to think about together): success comes when, as a result of many experiences or just one practice, we do not inflate the ego to the size of the world's main inner-work expert but, on the contrary, become simpler and reach a genuine freshness of perception, a beginner's view. With such a view natural stability, strength and authenticity arise, and we understand how much lies ahead. This understanding seasons one's own imperfection with compassionate humility: we not only strive to transcend neurotic reactions and traumas but also accept them, making friends with habitually banished weakness and strangeness, without falling into despondency over them. Hence the self-diagnostic: if I find myself competing with course leaders and confidently telling everyone about my hundred thousand meditations "moving my hair in slow motion," my main work on myself is probably still ahead [msg #916].

### The collector who squandered his riches
At a retreat in San Francisco attended by Dasha (the author's partner), there was a man over 65 who seemed to embody a collection of unique inner-work experience: years at Esalen, studied with Osho, Trungpa and Naranjo, lived in legendary ashrams, tried all psychedelics — and this retreat too was hard to get into. He caused cognitive dissonance: whenever he opened his mouth he shared a past genuinely worthy of admiration (personal contact with Trungpa alone created many spiritual leaders), as if from such teachers one could become filled with wisdom or a teacher oneself. But even at this retreat he only poured out stories of his experiences, losing what was happening in the present — "as if he isn't there, only the loud names remain" [msg #1224].

Lesson the author draws: he sees many stories likely to end the same way. People invest huge resources in diverse experience — popular coaches, dozens of retreats, years of flying to ayahuasca, then peyote — collecting it into their "шкатулку" (jewel box), certainly learning something about themselves and the world, but essentially unable to assemble any path, never reaching what Buddhism calls the fruit of practice: essential transformation, both a large personal change of the whole person and the most important liberation from the prison of automatism and ignorance of the nature of reality, in which most people will exist until death. He writes, he says, not from judgment or snobbery but for general analysis and a freer choice of path [msg #1224].

Context: in the author's post-Soviet society there are almost no examples of people who have done inner work seriously for the last ~40 years; experienced practitioners exist but are literally a handful, from generations shaped by peculiar historical circumstances — several Russian masters of inner work who long ago opened up transpersonal things told him personally they were under KGB surveillance in the 1980s. That is a different background from today's people who, like this man, have access to deep tools [msg #1224].

Mechanism: transferring their habitual patterns and consumer culture to this field, people collect tools into the box of their uniqueness and pleasure; inner work is consumed as a separate kind of refined product — "Походом в мишлен уже и самого себя не удивишь" (a trip to a Michelin restaurant won't surprise even yourself anymore). This motivation very often stays unconscious, because in the experience "everything is powdered with meaning, spirituality!" [msg #1224].

**The hardest trap** is not ignorance — when we don't know why we need it and are simply having fun; there is little understanding but also few illusions. Far worse is thinking that by collecting this "soup" we are doing genuine development. Then we are truly closed to the demands the path makes on us in order to allow us onto it, and we risk ending up like this man, who had enormous wealth in his hands but seems to have squandered it entirely, leaving nothing but memories and "quite empty appearances" [msg #1224].

### Intensity is not the value of an experience
In inner work — from meditation retreats to psychotherapy — the author often sees a tendency to rate experience on a scale of "how powerful was it"; without a "wow" feeling many think the process "didn't land" or "didn't work." He calls measuring depth by intensity a widespread trap on the path [msg #1521].

- **Consumer culture of emotions.** We live in a capitalist experience economy where a product is valued by the strength of feelings it evokes; marketers sell not goods but emotional response — delight, fear, awe — and the more it moves us, the higher its "value." Psychologists, the author says, call this *hedonic intensity bias*: a perceptual distortion where an experience's value is measured not by real usefulness but by sharpness of emotion. This creates an "economy of thirst." Neuroeconomic research he cites shows the dopamine system quickly habituates to novelty, so ever stronger stimulation is needed to get the same "rise" [msg #1521].
- **Market distortion.** We automatically transfer these patterns to inner work; together with social-media information bias this distorts the market: calm, gradual and **often more effective** processes stay in the shadow, though they are what solve the tasks for which people turn to inner work at all [msg #1521].
- **Psychological background.** The craving for strong sensations is sometimes linked to the trait Cloninger called *novelty seeking*: people high in it find routine hard to bear, take risks more easily and seek new stimuli. And, as the whole field of trauma research shows according to the author, behind the drive for intensity there is often early stress or unstable attachment: Bessel van der Kolk shows that a nervous system used to "swings" may confuse calm with emptiness and reach for new adrenaline surges — even in therapy or meditation. Then depth of work is replaced by the search for "peak experiences": new techniques, extreme retreats, "breakthrough" practices [msg #1521].
- **Why not seek intensity.** When a process is designed for emotions, the main principle of real inner work disappears: it must go at the rhythm of the specific person, according to their resource and current stage. When intensity becomes the goal rather than a tool, unnecessary retraumatizations happen more often and the result slips away — quick insights without integration, emotional hangover, and the feeling that "nothing changed" although momentary impressions were vivid [msg #1521].
- **What transformation actually looks like.** It rarely resembles fireworks; more often it shows as quiet building of boundaries, rhythm and habits; as returning to the same practice day after day; as the ability to tolerate pauses. In this "boring" consistency the nervous system restructures, support appears and a long-term effect arises. Often precisely giving up the race for intensity opens the door to the strong changes we need [msg #1521].

### A non-ecstatic container as a counter-example (Metamorphosis, Bali)
In a recommendation post the author describes wanting, on Bali, to shield himself from the comfort and outer images of narcissistic communities, sales, info-products and the new wave of pseudo-spirituality in Ubud "which has mixed too densely with capitalism and gone too far from simple work on oneself." Amid this noise he singles out the Metamorphosis process, founded and run by Sasha Konstantinov and Karime Kuri from Mexico — a cosmopolitan couple with MBAs from MIT and Oxford who three years earlier turned to designing an ideal container for transpersonal experience that, through a cycle of techniques and catalysts, helps a person relax deeply, regain contact with life and set up many mirrors for reflection [msg #1012].

Characteristics as described: a 4-hour group process, hard to describe because of its transpersonal modality which fills the container differently each time; an individual journey within a group, with facilitators and various catalysts from meditation to music and breathing, where each stage arises from the previous one, carefully helping inner experience open layer by layer. After more than 100 processes over 2 years for participants from around the world, the author finds it increasingly interesting both in its balanced, well-worked structure and in the facilitators' quality of presence, which gives a warm example of contact with the inner child within the boundaries of an adult; this presence facilitates careful reunion with one's own spontaneity and ways of bringing it ecologically into adult life [msg #1012].

The lesson relevant to this topic: what the author values is that it is "экологичен и не экстатичен" (ecological and not ecstatic) — no breakthroughs, languid touching, trance singing or sweat; it can take one deep beyond the habitual but with a clear, self-directed shift of gears "on one's own ship." He states that many parasitize on ecstasy and all kinds of "going beyond limits and boundaries," disregarding both the risks of retraumatization and the absence of long-term benefit. His caveats: the facilitators still "sin" with quite ambiguous elements of Ubud neo-New-Age culture, which time may correct; he also values that it is held in their home in the fields of Ubud, in three languages, and feels completely non-commercial [msg #1012].

### Spiritual bypassing of the political and social
The author connects his own biography with understanding large systems: at 17, preparing for a social-studies exam for Moscow State University, he felt old wise books alone wouldn't suffice and bought and read whole magazines weekly (Vlast, Newsweek, Expert, Kommersant); in 2005 he read a Newsweek article on Putin building the "vertical," which felt abstract. University gave a base in sociology, political science and psychology, but his understanding of big systems stayed unclear. The real shift came near the end of a career as a department director at a state corporation after the Federation Council and work in a federal Ministry's management team — years of direct experience before theory and practice formed a picture, which fully took shape only when he left all the systems and began to look at them from outside again. Now, in a different identity, he watches politics not as a separate reality but as a relative context defining our environment — "how much everything was changed, for example, by the terrible war!" [msg #1292].

The teaching: from the viewpoint of many contemplative traditions, perceived reality is distorted by an individual isolated view; practicing, we learn to see more clearly and understand the relativity and changeability of seemingly solid phenomena. But even someone who regularly contacts a truer understanding of reality, beyond everyday fuss including all politics, does not stop having a human, relative existence. Here one must distinguish healthy mindfulness from a rarely discussed aspect of spiritual bypassing. Mindfulness is the ability to see the world as it is and to be fully present in one's experience, whatever it is. But there is no isolated experience of a separate individual: according to modern neurobiological research the author cites, our mind itself forms intersubjectively — through connections with others — and when these connections break, depression arises. So genuine development practice — depth therapy, meditation, or an informal but sincere attempt to be more present in life — cannot be only about an isolated inner world, because it does not exist [msg #1292].

What does exist is avoidance: using inner work to escape real personal tasks and difficult topics with phrases like "всё иллюзия" ("everything is illusion"), "это не имеет значения" ("this doesn't matter"), "главное – мой внутренний мир" ("what matters is my inner world"). Then we literally cut off parts of reality [msg #1292].

In a more mature view gained through genuine inner work, we can see the reflection of large processes in life, giving them neither too big nor too small a place, and also see the reverse — how we ourselves create these processes, as Otto Scharmer's Theory U tries to show; perhaps the consequence of inner work then becomes manifest positions of activism and solidarity. We often suffer not only from lacking a deep perspective on life but from refusing to see big processes, reducing them to personal experiences: sometimes we feel bad not because we didn't meditate enough but because we work in a toxic system or a war happened. Hence an integral, nothing-avoiding view matters not only for philosophers but for everyone seeking sustainable happiness and freedom [msg #1292].

### Material vs spiritual: the Yakovlev debate
Vladimir Yakovlev, founder of the Kommersant newspaper, set out a view on the spiritual and the material, reposted by an entrepreneurial community. Its theses, as the author summarizes them [msg #1523]:
1. Renouncing the material for the spiritual does not always lead to development: Yakovlev spent 34 years in various spiritual activities without achieving anything significant and now believes the spiritual path gave him no more development than pursuing material goals could have.
2. True development happens through realizing simple desires: money, recognition, influence, sex, beauty.
3. Development can be measured by material indicators: the world really reflects inner growth through outer well-being, if the growth is real.

The author's response: he finds Yakovlev sympathetic, but feels that entrepreneurs especially — used to figuring everything out themselves and having built something successful — are more prone than others to not understanding their own ignorance of a subject. Progress on the spiritual path is said to show, among other things, in eventual simplicity: the Dalai Lama always calls himself a simple monk and does not recount how many retreats he did. Lack of such simplicity and humility acts as a bias that prevents correct conclusions — seeing that humanity has accumulated whole storehouses of structured experience, so we approach the question not in a vacuum but in an already developed context. The result is a classic story: someone did something unsystematic, but since they did it for 30 years, thought they understood everything and went to tell others [msg #1523].

The author says he knows spirituality "only by hearsay" but was lucky to meet several excellent teachers, and it seemed to him that in matters of professional spirituality the most important thing is to be aware of one's amateurism and to rely productively on professionals who have spiritual realization — a verifiable and significant achievement in the line of development of spiritual intelligence. He recalls B. Alan Wallace saying at a retreat that there are people who think they practice Zen, or Vipassana, or Dzogchen — for one, two, ten, twenty years. In the format in which most people do spiritual practices today, the author thinks there is no path — and no path means no fruit of the path; Yakovlev's conclusions, he suggests, are what that format produces [msg #1523].

How others would answer, according to the author [msg #1523]:
- **Ken Wilber** would say the idea that only realizing material desires develops is a reduction of development to the horizontal; claiming only they lead to genuine growth confuses development with compensation of deficit; such thinking signals being stuck at an individualistic, pragmatic, success-oriented stage.
- **John Welwood**, his colleague, would add that condemning spiritual goals as "escape" reflects unprocessed disappointment. Spiritual goals can indeed be a way to escape pain and frustration — but material goals can serve the same function, especially if success is used as protection against vulnerability. What matters is not which goals you choose but which traumas stand behind those choices.
- **Tsoknyi Rinpoche**, at the author's latest retreat in Malaysia, repeatedly stressed respecting the material — but, the author supposes, would not reduce all development to it. A sum of money says nothing about inner development — ethics, compassion, wisdom or freedom from suffering — and these do not automatically bring money and apartments either.

Conclusion: in the world of spiritual professionals this dichotomy Yakovlev tries to resolve seems not to exist. Desires are not the enemy; material goals are not a sin; but their intrinsic value is relative, all the more so to reduce all development to them. Really holding this perspective in one's experience requires serious, systematic inner work, "which few people do" [msg #1523].

## Practical playbook

- Before and during any practice, run the *Motivation check* and its four questions; re-check especially once you feel "experienced" [msg #1224, #1339].
- Choose one therapist / one practice and stay with it over a long, relatively constant period rather than sampling many [msg #762].
- Do not buy practices from those who want only to sell [msg #762].
- Study the traditional context of a practice (e.g., what tantra meant historically) before or alongside applying it [img #773].
- If a process "didn't feel powerful," don't conclude it failed; use the *Self-inquiry for the pull toward intensity* and favor slow, regular, unspectacular work matched to your resource and stage [msg #1521].
- When judging a retreat or container, check: does it invite perspectives and conflict inside, avoid ecstatic "breakthrough" designs, and attend to integration with ordinary and collective life? [msg #1012, #1511]
- Self-diagnostic signs that one's main work is still ahead: competing with course leaders, recounting countless meditations, feeling "already worked-through," judging others' "infantilism" with disgust, using "everything is illusion / perfect" to avoid pain and responsibility [msg #916, #1292, #1337, #1614].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The author repeatedly hedges that he is not moralizing or judging: "Ps. Это не морализаторство" (*Ps. This is not moralizing*) [msg #488]; he writes "not from judgment or snobbery" [msg #1224]; he does not want to "demonize" consumer dynamics [msg #1339]; and he acknowledges that in a free society everyone may live as they like [msg #1339].
- He grants consumption real benefits (normalization; a path to real work for some) and that pseudo-worked-through clients statistically often become authentic people or practitioners [msg #762, #916] — so the critique is not absolute.
- (!) UNCLEAR: in #762 the author says he believes in the therapist's personality as a factor while noting it lacks scientific confirmation; this is his stated opinion vs. the evidence as he reports it.
- (!) UNCLEAR: the claim about meditation research is asserted without specific citations ("you can google a million studies") [msg #762].
- The opinions attributed to Ken Wilber, John Welwood and Tsoknyi Rinpoche in the Yakovlev debate are the author's conjecture of what they "would say," not their actual statements [msg #1523]. The author also says he knows spirituality "only by hearsay" [msg #1523].
