# Rest and recovery: vacations, year-end and weekends

## What this covers

How the channel author understands real rest: why the habitual year-end crunch ("push through, then rest") damages health, decisions and relationships; what fatigue is biologically; the types of rest beyond sleep; how to design a winter vacation (the sandwich model rest-retreat-rest, dopamine fasting, retreats); how to make weekends restorative; how organizations treat vacations and sabbaticals; and why treating rest as another project fails, while letting go works. The author stresses that fatigue is not something to "just endure": chronic fatigue is a real biological state that lowers quality of life and leads to illness, and unrestored people — especially leaders — make worse decisions [msg #27, #288, #979].

## Core ideas

- **The year-end crunch is an ineffective, repeated absurdity.** Pushing hard in November–December to "rest afterwards" depletes the body so much that the vacation itself is eaten by recovery; it also causes mistakes, damaged relationships and a slide into bad habits. The author interprets it as an unconscious defense mechanism against fear, anxiety and guilt (see *Four costs of the year-end crunch*) [msg #27, #76].
- **Fatigue is biology, not a nuisance.** It involves immune, inflammatory, oxidative and nitrosative reactions, bioenergetic and neurophysiological deviations, hormonal changes and changed muscle tone [msg #27, #288].
- **We tire mostly from emotions, not from tasks,** and from our neurotic behavioral patterns; real rest comes from honest acceptance of our state and from stopping those patterns [msg #115].
- **Sleep is not the only rest**: there are physical, mental, emotional, spiritual, sensory and social rest; pick the most deficient ones [msg #232].
- **Recovery needs time and a switch.** Recovery usually starts only about 2 weeks after deprivation from work stress; without catalysts and clear boundaries, weekends and even 1–2 weeks of vacation pass without any rest [msg #976, #1051].
- **Rest cannot be "done" as a project.** Scheduling recovery activities with goals and discipline keeps the nervous system mobilized; real rest arrives when we let go and stop interfering with recovery [msg #1388].
- **Rest is a business and ethical choice.** The author chooses to build business so that December is calm, accepting lost short-term opportunities, because building something sustainable is counterintuitive [msg #776].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Four things to do right now at year-end
Purpose: to be — not merely appear — more effective at the end of the year, instead of following the "drag it to the finish, then drop" strategy [msg #27].
1. **Lower your ambitions for the rest of the year.** Yes, some things will not get done or implemented, but you will be calmer and healthier [msg #27].
2. **Arrange a short rest.** Go away for at least a couple of days. The author argues we can afford it if we want to, and offers a test question: what would happen if you really got sick right now? The tasks would wait [msg #27].
3. **Move: go to yoga or the gym.** Movement completes the stress cycle and releases suppressed emotions from the body. According to the author, incomplete stress and emotions are the main cause of fatigue and burnout [msg #27].
4. **Support yourself with meditation.** Practices that deliberately help to accept bodily sensations and release tension work especially well. (The author offered the channel's own "anti-burnout" audio recording for this.) [msg #27]

The post ends with the author's line «Берегите себя. Вы у себя одни.» (*Take care of yourselves. You only have yourself.*) [msg #27]

### Four costs of the year-end crunch
Context: in one week several strong people publicly declared they were "done" — including meditation and embodiment teachers, people who feel their body well and understand stress and fatigue. The author draws three conclusions: (1) we still don't understand burnout practically — the biology is clear, but how to organize activity accordingly is not; (2) the load on any active person is now too high; (3) the year-end is the time of peak load. Closing many issues in one month and then going on vacation sounds good; the problem is that this crunch is habitual and very ineffective [msg #76].
1. **"We'll strain now and rest later" — but we won't rest.** In the crunch we deplete the body's reserves, and it needs at least several weeks of recovery just to get back to the point where rest can begin. Hence many people get no rest at all, even if they go away and don't work for 10 days [msg #76].
2. **Acting depleted, we make more mistakes and worse decisions.** The author's example: tired and hurried, he parked badly (something he normally doesn't do) — the result was 4 hours and 10 thousand (rubles) instead of a 10-minute trip to the pharmacy. We seem to do a lot but then redo even more [msg #76].
3. **We damage relationships.** Christmas and New Year are time for family and friends, but overload makes us emotionally unstable, more irritable, and we behave inadequately with those closest to us [msg #76].
4. **We collapse into a cycle of bad habits.** Tired and worn out, the author starts making unhealthy choices instead of healthy ones: a TV series instead of exercise and meditation, then bad food, until he ends up "in a complete mess" that needs a separate effort to climb out of. He feels November and December are the months of the greatest damage to health [msg #76].

**Why we repeat it (the author's hypothesis):** the crunch is the playing-out of an unconscious defense mechanism. We overwork to relieve tension from fear, anxiety and guilt: fear of death, felt every time something ends; anxiety about our results and slowly resolving issues; guilt over mistakes and failures. We repeat the cycle every year because we do not know other ways to cope with these emotions and reach a state where we can truly release tension for a while. In #27 he similarly wondered whether it is a cultural trait of "solving everything at once in fire" or an unconscious fear of death at year's end — the result is chronic fatigue in January, i.e. borrowing from one's health [msg #76, #27].

**What to do instead:** to really stop burning and start living, become aware of this behavior as a defense mechanism, then work through the deeper meanings we are running from: (a) why we do our work; (b) how to cope with dissatisfaction with life without turning away or distracting ourselves from it; (c) how to live with the uncertainty and dangers of life [msg #76].

### Two conditions under which we really release tension
The author states we truly release tension and give the psyche rest in two cases [msg #115]:
1. **Honest acceptance and awareness of our state and emotions.** We tire most not from tasks but from the emotions those tasks provoke. In the city we are used to ignoring fatigue and cannot always express anger, fear or anxiety; these do not disappear from the psyche and lie as a heavy load on our shoulders [msg #115].
2. **Temporarily stopping our habitual neurotic behavior patterns.** The author claims that in industrial/post-industrial society neurosis, or a deep psychic split, is a problem for 99% of people. To adapt to this split, the psyche builds defenses that we reproduce daily as compulsive behavior: we stress-eat, worry, get angry, plan, ruminate on the same thing endlessly. Neurosis and psychic defenses are a colossal energy drain. «Одно мгновение остановки шаблона - ценнее целого месяца «на автомате».» (*One moment of stopping the pattern is more valuable than a whole month "on autopilot".*) [msg #115]

The author's personal practice: for many years he has started his winter vacation with a 5–10-day meditation retreat. This especially helped him recover after hard 24/7 work in government service. Retreats are hard to do in December, but this format changes all subsequent vacation days at the root, and the practice gives a deep understanding of how tension and its release work. He notes the topic matters especially for leaders, who tend to overwork, many of whom have long forgotten mental clarity and bodily energy, while people and companies depend on the quality of their decisions [msg #115].

### Six types of rest beyond sleep (Sandra Dalton-Smith)
Source: a podcast on rest deficit with Dr. Sandra Dalton-Smith, released by Dave Asprey (described as one of the Californian biohacking pioneers; in English). The post title says "5 types" but six are listed [msg #232].
1. **Physical rest** — physical relaxation [msg #232].
2. **Mental rest** — switching the brain into a passive mode without active perception and problem solving; also deeper rest from constant thoughts [msg #232].
3. **Emotional rest** — the possibility to be open, vulnerable and authentic with other people. It is important to have someone with whom we can allow ourselves to be like that; a coach or therapist can fill this role [msg #232].
4. **Spiritual rest** — feeling part of something bigger, connected with others. Without it we start feeling life's superficiality, meaninglessness and loneliness [msg #232].
5. **Sensory rest** — reducing the volume of information attacking our senses, which are often overloaded during the day; some we use practically non-stop. Track which channels were attacked most and deprive them for rest. Deprivation (floatation) tanks, retreats and mindfulness practices help especially well [msg #232].
6. **Social rest** — rest from interaction with people [msg #232].

How to use it: mindfulness supports physical, mental and spiritual rest, and in some cases sensory and social rest too. Dalton-Smith suggests not stressing if not all types are realized; choose the most deficient ones and give them time. The author's conclusion: «Фактически всегда путь к качественному отдыху и энергичности лежит через изменение образа жизни.» (*Practically always the path to quality rest and energy lies through changing one's lifestyle.*) [msg #232]

### Key blocks for fast resource recovery
While designing a three-day restorative retreat program (run by 4 different trainers, placed in October — when the weather becomes oppressive, the New Year break is still far away, and the whole country suddenly starts working hard), the author identified the blocks that work together for quickly regaining resources [msg #288]:
1. **Mindfulness practice** — the most important base, because without sensing one's state "from inside" one cannot work with it effectively [msg #288].
2. **Body practices** — simpler than pure mindfulness for most active people in a big city, because tension, stress and emotions have a bodily nature, and working with them through the body is easier and faster [msg #288].
3. **Social practices** — "the foundation of our resource"; the author is always charged and moved forward by experiences of trust, empathy and live, interested presence with other people [msg #288].
4. **Digital detox** — restores our attention system and frees the whole organism from constant information stimulation [msg #288].

The author's lesson from the background: after December 2019 he wrote a review article (on VC) about rest that moves us forward, but «понимать - не значит делать» (*understanding does not mean doing*) — the problems persisted the next year, so readers asked for the practices to be gathered into one sequential, beginner-friendly scenario [msg #288].

### Two steps to switch and really rest on vacation
Problem: many work the last days before New Year "like biorobots" to finish everything and go on vacation. Body and psyche are used to active mode — producing cortisol and adrenaline, tensing muscles, switching attention from task to task. Without help switching out of this mode, the vacation can bring emptiness and strong anxiety, constant reaching for the phone, and the body never really rests from information and tasks; the vacation itself can become frustration and new stress [msg #548].
1. **Do a dopamine fast.** Give your receptors freedom from constant stimulation. For at least one day (more is better, "but we have trouble with that") do not use the phone and computer **at all**. Do not stimulate yourself with series or food. No sex. Do not distract yourself from your own sensations and accumulated emotions. Face the most unpleasant ones; look for pleasant ones. All difficult experiences can definitely be endured if you observe them openly and don't collapse your attention into them. Remember «кто вы без дел» (*who you are without tasks*) and what you want besides achievements [msg #548].
2. **Leave mental space for the body.** All ethical and safe practices — massage, dance, sport — help switch the organism from biorobot mode into normal mode [msg #548].

Reframe for achievers: if, after a constant flow of tasks, this seems not serious, treat these actions as important tasks for recovery for the next year; nobody knows what it will be like, but rest will increase adaptability and mental clarity [msg #548].

### Sandwich model of vacation (rest-retreat-rest)
Purpose: a long winter vacation that really restores, especially for helping professions, which "digest" a lot of live content and emotions during the year, raising burnout risk. The author takes a deliberately long vacation at this time of year and has used this model for a long time [msg #976].
Structure: **rest → retreat → rest.**
1. **First rest (a breather before the retreat).** Why: jumping straight into a retreat after a loaded work period is quite stressful — there are many accumulated unmet needs, and natural resistance to the retreat arises [msg #976].
2. **Retreat in the middle.** Why: jumping into rest without a retreat means, for most of us, rest risks not even starting. «Восстановление стартует обычно примерно через 2 недели депривации от рабочего стресса в ситуации без сильной новизны, конкуренции и ответственности» (*Recovery usually starts about 2 weeks into deprivation from work stress, in a situation without strong novelty, competition and responsibility*). If we are used to overworking, stress-provoking thoughts about work have become a deep habit that follows us to the beach, mountains or dacha; if in parallel we keep deciding, delegating, reading about work, full recovery is unlikely. At the retreat one becomes aware of the habitual thoughts brought along and of emotions and stress stored in the body. Mindfulness practice multiplied by dopamine fasting, which calms automatic impulses, «работает как активированный уголь для души» (*works like activated charcoal for the soul*): it cleanses and prepares the ground on which we can for a while stay in a state of completed stress response, without constantly jumping attention, habitual thoughts and emotional imbalance [msg #976].
3. **Second rest.** Then the actual rest can happen on the cleansed ground (the source does not detail this phase separately) [msg #976].
Verdict and caveats: hard to implement, but works very effectively and brings a lot of joy. It is a privileged option, available to the author thanks to his team and the understanding that resource must be created. Other strategies exist — find what works for you. Yet he sees few examples of truly restorative rest among friends who fly out of megacities to the sea for a couple of weeks, changing climate and time zone and lowering stress with substances [msg #976].

### Four stages of successful rest (a self-check)
You can test your rest strategy: successful rest takes a person through four stages: **fatigue → relaxation → recovery of strength → activation.** To move to the recovery stage you must recognize and accept fatigue and free body-mind from accumulated tension. If it works, after rest you should feel a surge of strength that can be channeled into constructive activity. If there is no strength, either change the strategy or deal with deeper problems: chronic fatigue syndrome, burnout or something else [msg #976].

### Four responses when weekends do not restore you
Background: we are automatic creatures moving by inertia. Without a habit of relaxing and special rituals, when rest arrives we continue mentally and physically doing what we did before. Weekends then "just pass as if they never happened" while we work latently — thinking about tasks, checking, writing something here and there. The author sees cases where people cannot rest over a weekend or even 1–2 weeks of vacation: the organism cannot, without extra help, complete the stress cycle and enter recovery — there is no habit or skill of rest. To really use weekends we often need special **rest catalysts** that help body-mind realize it is not in the usual routine, followed by some time to get used to the new state. Periodically throwing in something habitual — usual responsibility, even micro-load — greatly slows the speed and depth of switching, because it returns triggers of the usual tense, engaged state into the psyche [msg #1051].
When he finds it hard to use weekends for their purpose, the author:
1. **Looks for comfortable, working catalysts to switch the nervous system:** a banya (sauna) with an ice plunge, nature, longer, more intense meditation. Any activities without strong dopamine release (as when watching series or eating pizza), and with serotonin release, as with gently bringing attention/movement into the body. Better not to use alcohol for switching: its cost and effect often exceed the benefit, and the organism develops no new skills for handling stress and accumulated emotions — though he treats those who have no other tools without judgment and with compassion [msg #1051].
2. **Treats it as a signal of blurred boundaries and draws them more clearly:** he creates a **container for rest** — a space without novelty, responsibility and social competition. For most of us this means ultimately putting the phone far away and postponing really all tasks and decisions. He feels anxious about leaving risky tasks unresolved over a weekend, but lets them go for a couple of days, knowing he will benefit from the rest and decide better later with a clear head [msg #1051].
3. **Treats it as a signal of high stress load that the body needs help with:** perhaps he has been in stress for long, or strongly, without even noticing — we get used to everything and the state starts to seem normal. Noticing "rest without rest", he can check his stress load (the channel's stress test) and follow recommendations for his specific level. At the late stages there are contraindications for "rest": trying to visit several museums in a day or going on a long trek are bad ideas at the exhaustion stage [msg #1051].
4. **Takes it as a reminder of the importance of pauses and relaxation on weekdays too,** including in the middle of tense moments [msg #1051].
Conclusion: patterns repeat what we have. The idea that you can work all your life and then travel carefree, or plough nonstop now and rest at the weekend, is a myth in most cases [msg #1051].

### Attention focuses for restorative rest (4 principles + 5 activities)
Source: the author's Telegraph note «Фокусы внимания для восстанавливающего отдыха» (*Attention focuses for restorative rest*), written for readers after he himself failed to disconnect fully in the first week of his vacation. Purpose: what to focus attention on during a vacation so that rest actually restores. It has two parts — «Несколько принципов» (*A few principles*) and «Несколько занятий» (*A few activities*) [article #979].

**Principles**
1. **Complete disconnection from tasks («полное отключение от дел»).** Called the most important action, one worth approaching with enthusiasm. Why: even if we handle only a couple of work tasks a day instead of the usual 30, we stay in daily contact with our stressors — the triggers of stress — and this prevents truly switching and releasing tension. How: when trying to disconnect completely, build into the attempt from the start a kind attitude to the strength of one's own inertia; the author admits that for him disconnection usually does not succeed on the first try [article #979].
2. **Digital detox («диджитал-детокс»).** Stopping the flow of information at first causes something like drug withdrawal — bodily discomfort and a feeling of anxiety. But within a couple of days it unloads the dopamine system and returns strength and freshness to the mind. (Lesson: expect the initial discomfort; it passes in about two days.) [article #979]
3. **Planning and flexibility («планирование и гибкость»).** If rest is not planned, it is easy to get stuck on habitual rails and start looking at work chats. But this planning differs from work planning: it should include both specific desired activities and enough room for spontaneous ones [article #979].
4. **More of what you want / less of what you must («больше того, что хочется / меньше того, что надо»).** On vacation you can shift the balance towards "I want" if it has been disturbed, and so nourish the inner child, whose needs are often postponed during working days. Caveat: as a rule, small and symbolic actions towards the inner child are enough — «есть сладкое 7 дней не обязательно» (*eating sweets for 7 days is not necessary*) [article #979].

**Activities**
1. **Contact with nature.** Walks in a park, hikes or simply time in the fresh air support psychological recovery. According to the note, nature positively affects reduced anxiety, speed of recovery from illness and increased life expectancy (no specific study is named) [article #979].
2. **Movement and the body.** Physical activity releases endorphins, helps relax muscles and relieve bodily tension. Body and mind are closely connected, so when the body feels better, the mind relaxes too [article #979].
3. **Meditation, self-regulation.** Mindfulness practices help lower stress levels and restore contact with needs that were pushed to the background during the period of active work. (The note offers Deep Mind's own meditation and self-regulation practices, noting a VPN is needed to access them from Russia.) [article #979]
4. **Time together with others.** Time with family and friends can be restorative if you do not overload yourself with social obligations. This can be combined with nature, movement or meditation — spending time together in those ways. The note cites "a study" according to which physical activity brings more benefit when a person exercises in a group (the study is not identified) [article #979].
5. **Conscious doing-nothing («сознательное ничегонеделание»).** Time when we refuse any activity and simply observe the world around us helps to get pleasure on a subtle level — from simplification and the cessation of stimulation. In these moments you may have to face discomfort from the unfamiliarity. But if you observe it and go further, you are rewarded with a new state: perhaps noticing important needs, finding greater calm and simply feeling the so often lost «ощущение бытования» (*sense of being*) [article #979].

### Rest as a project vs letting go
Problem: driven people now know the toolkit for switching and recovering — banya and ice plunges, forest bathing, body practices, breathwork, meditation — and even know it works at the nervous-system level ("not magic but physiology"), teaching the nervous system not to get stuck in sympathetic mode and to be more often in a state where recovery is possible. Yet we still hit high stress or symptoms (sleep and attention problems, illnesses that all point to initial overload and stress) [msg #1388].
- **What "rest as a project" is:** by habit we treat fixing our state as a project and add tasks to the calendar: worked, now banya or gym to complete the stress stored in the body. This is understandable logic and can help — especially if basics are not covered, e.g. we don't move at all or don't sleep enough hours [msg #1388].
- **Why it fails:** it often reinforces the very approach at the root of the problem. A rest day looks like a set of rest activities — eat right, then gym, then banya, then massage — "we're not working, but we still get tired of resting". This is a functional attitude to oneself — planning, goal setting, KPI, control and discipline — which itself leads to high stress, exhaustion and eventually burnout. Its internal bug: it activates the same nervous circuit as work mode and keeps us mobilized. You can lie in a warm bath "doing relaxation" while the body stays active and cortisol does not fall [msg #1388].
- **Evidence cited:** research by Harvard Medical School and neuropsychologist Judson Brewer, summarized in *The Craving Mind*, shows (per the author) that attempts to control relaxation and recovery can backfire and raise stress by activating the default mode network — the brain mode responsible for mental noise and tension rather than presence and calm [msg #1388].
- **What works instead:** real rest happens when we let go. Special actions are often needed to help a stuck, hyperactivated nervous system switch, but after that we drop goals and tasks and simply live — not making a special effort to recover but «перестаем мешать восстановлению разворачиваться самостоятельно» (*stop interfering with recovery unfolding by itself*). Deep rest «не достигается, а приходит» (*is not achieved but arrives*). From this perspective even meditation stops being a task one "must do" and becomes minutes of presence, silence and joy, with flexibility, freedom and effortlessness, contact with oneself and the world, and calm joy [msg #1388].

## Detailed insights

### Fatigue as a biological state
The author repeatedly rejects the idea that fatigue is something to tolerate. In #27: fatigue is a very complex biological reaction including a wide range of immune, inflammatory, oxidative and nitrosative reactions, bioenergetic and neurophysiological deviations; as with chronic stress, we usually don't realize how ineffective "drag it to the finish" is. In #288: when we feel tired, a "bouquet" of biological reactions arises — hormonal changes, increased inflammation, changes in muscle tone; constant fatigue means lower quality of life and depressing illnesses. He sees constant fatigue in his circle as "a sign of the times", suggests we are still adapting to a new world, and calls for talking more about rest and load and finding ways to live in the city without sacrificing ourselves to external goals. His own example: for two days he could not work normally, was tired and wanted nature; he did only the most important tasks, "gave up, let go" and tried to recover, knowing the whole team's results depend on his state — and he reminded his team that their upcoming retreat would succeed first of all if they were in good shape, and only secondly if logistics and program were well assembled [msg #27, #288].

### Workaholism as culture and personal responsibility
The author calls workaholism and rest deficit among the most harmful things in modern work culture, as if created by people who understand nothing about productivity and human biology; this ignorance produces losses in companies, mental disorders and mass burnout. Understanding modern science, overwork, multitasking and chronic fatigue at work can be seen as wrong in the same way as rudeness to a client or embezzling a company budget. But there is also much individual responsibility: on top of outdated corporate culture, Russia — "rich in military feats" — developed a national work pattern of heroism in reaching goals, "lying down on the embrasure" for incredible results. Often we don't know how to stop doing things, and associate "rest" only with sleep, vacation or weekends [msg #232].

### Vacation reality in organizations; sabbaticals
- Sber's ("Gref's sabbatical") initiative allowed employees unpaid leave of up to one year and three months a year of remote work; by agreement with the manager, an employee needing a career time-out can study, launch a startup, write a scientific work or just rest, while the company keeps a valuable employee. The author's verdict: a good initiative towards flexibility and adaptability, useful for the Russian state economy as a precedent and role model, but in reality few can afford an unpaid sabbatical, especially in such a hierarchy [msg #441].
- His case from a federal ministry (6 years before the post): he found colleagues who had not taken vacation for months and even **years** — surreal after digital agencies. Their "real-virtual" reasons: low pay during vacation, falling out of context and political games, risk of losing one's position. Some later moved to Sber and similar companies, so the sabbatical mindset still needs work — on both sides: even the news article is written in a parental tone ("Sber **allowed**"), as if employees were serfs [msg #441].
- In #979 the author cites that 57% of Russian employees are forced to, or choose to, work during vacation. When employees don't rest it is lose-lose: current load and stress without matching recovery reduce productivity, and everyone loses. This especially concerns top positions: unrestored leaders start doing their main job — making decisions and directing people — poorly, though they cannot always notice the change. He himself could not fully disconnect in the first week of vacation, which he started before New Year; he does not scold himself or the team for being unable to disconnect, but adapts by shifting vacation dates slightly forward and turning to "attention focuses" that charge one for restorative rest — written out in full as *Attention focuses for restorative rest* [msg #979].

### Building a business with a calm December
Three years before #776 the author's company set a rule: no corporate projects after 15 December. The rule was never kept, and they always regretted it. Case: at the end of 2020 they cautiously took on a retreat for Qiwi top managers; the "cup of fatigue" overflowed towards burnout, half the team caught COVID and suffered another 10 days in Moscow quarantine; the money earned went into slow "rising from the ashes" rather than reinvestment. In 2021 he again "failed December", resolving issues until New Year, and then not very successfully tried to recover during a month of vacation (a practice he has long used at any job). In 2022 they finally progressed: no usual activity in December except "mindful corporate events" (facilitating year results, resourcing before holidays) and routine; part of the team started vacation early to handle end-of-month processes in good shape and then rest again in January; he worked in light mode for two weeks, mostly studying Dzogchen, before leaving for a reset in the Himalayas [msg #776].
His reasoning: in ordinary business logic, calm work and rest are not counted as achievements; time for oneself is opposed to business — a "factory-smelling" idea that employees bring value when they work, not when they don't. Founders and key teams often merge with the business so much that everything "non-useful" is thrown overboard, shrinking the world to achievement in every breath and draining a person's depth. He admits not working is possible only by refusing work — giving up opportunities, slowing short-term growth, not earning some money — but chooses to do business this way, believing that creating something sustainable is counterintuitive. Counterintuitiveness is the key growth principle in meditation and therapy, so it should apply to business too: to get more tomorrow, do something that seems scary today [msg #776].
Adizes (as quoted): if we don't take time for ourselves, modern life will simply leave us without it. Hence the author suggests treating nourishing time that connects us to simpler or deeper values as the most important time: plan and prioritize it, put it in the calendar, and where possible make it sacred — untouchable. His mantra: to be able to share something, you must create something for yourself; otherwise, existing only in the modality of "outward" and "for the sake of", what person does one become? This matters especially for helping professionals: without time for their own focused practice, reflection and rest, they start only taking from clients, lose ethics and fool both themselves and the client. His wish: if not two weeks, then at least 5 real minutes in a goal-free mode [msg #776].

### Self-care beyond pampering
Typical answer from colleagues: "I'm fine, all normal. Sure, I'm loaded with tasks, haven't slept properly for weeks and haven't had a vacation for half a year, but I manage." Circumstances stretch us, we dream of escaping on vacation, but the time never seems right. Self-care is pushed everywhere as images of jacuzzis, manicures and lush travel. The author's definition: self-care is the mindset and skills of caring for oneself and one's needs (taught as a module in some of their corporate state-management programs); it lies more in taking responsibility for oneself — consciously managing different zones of one's wellbeing and state. It includes dedicated time to understand who we are and what we need, and commitment to fulfilling the needs necessary for happiness, health and full presence in life. He wants "more than normal" for all of us — for colleagues to answer «Я лучше, чем нормально» (*I'm better than normal*) — and says we have that choice [msg #1117].
