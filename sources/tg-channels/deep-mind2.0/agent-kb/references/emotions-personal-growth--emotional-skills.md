# Emotional skills: accepting, naming and working with emotions

## What this covers

This file collects what the deep mind channel teaches about emotional intelligence as a practical skill: why emotions are a necessary part of good thinking and leadership, how to accept and name an emotion in the moment, how anger can be suppressed, erupted or used righteously, what the "coping by force" model costs individuals and states, how to sequence work with experience (Letting be → Letting go → Letting in), why accepting all emotions should be followed by cultivating constructive ones, how to befriend protective parts ("beautiful monsters"), how to share emotions ecologically, and how psychotherapy, secular mindfulness and the contemplative path treat emotions on different levels. According to the author, emotions are important for quality decisions, leadership and simple joy in life, and real effectiveness ("high performance") depends on balancing them — healing past emotional traumas and building skills to use emotions wisely [msg #184, #809].

This part covers three posts by the author of deep mind 2.0. The first uses Nick Cave as a role model: someone who feels and expresses emotion, including grief, and is still strong. The second shows how to turn complaints about others into responsibility for your own disturbing emotions. It draws on Bruce Tift and on research by Barrett, Duke University and Lieberman. The third separates guilt from regret and describes a morning practice of reaching out to people from regret rather than guilt. The source's reason for caring: this is what makes us grow up, brings more peace and stability, and leads to "strong connections and healthy relationships" instead of isolation in being right or in being bad. [msg #1693, #1786, #1808]

## Core ideas

- **Emotions are part of rational thinking, not its enemy.** Strong emotions do impair thinking, but losing access to emotions impairs it too; emotions are "condensed past experience" that steer thinking. Detail in *Emotions in decision-making* [msg #184, #809].
- **The essence of EQ is in-the-moment awareness and conscious choice.** Not blocking emotions, living them freely, and making choices that take the information "from" emotions into account, instead of reacting to them or from them. Detail in *Three-step method for accepting an emotion* [msg #258].
- **Acceptance is a learnable skill most of us trained the opposite of.** Since childhood we are taught to suppress emotions, so adults have to relearn; a full healthy emotional cycle lasts only seconds if not held back by non-acceptance [msg #258].
- **Anger is a natural, often healthy function.** Both suppressing it inward and erupting it outward are ways to stop feeling it; righteous (individual or collective) anger against injustice is a sign of health [msg #427].
- **"Push through by force" is one coping model among several, and an expensive one.** It turns natural strength into perceived weakness, wastes energy, breeds cruelty, kills empathy, and finally creates the very instability it fights [msg #672].
- **Three strategies cover all successful work with experience**: Letting be, Letting go, Letting in — in that order, with awareness as the base meta-skill [msg #755].
- **Accepting all emotions is step 1; step 2 is cultivating constructive ones.** Emotions have functions, but anger, envy, anxiety and self-preoccupation do not lead to wellbeing; the author argues people can transform patterns to the point of almost no longer feeling anger [msg #969].
- **Self-violence and not-feeling reinforce each other**, and violence toward oneself is eventually carried over to partners, children and employees; non-violence is the deepest path of self-knowledge and healing [msg #798].
- **Therapy, mindfulness and contemplative practice work on different levels.** Therapy teaches being with emotions and building a healthy self; contemplative practice teaches not becoming them and questions rigid selfhood; mixing the levels without distinction creates problems [msg #1684].

- **Your reaction is always yours.** Dissatisfaction with others, especially partners, is a chance to take responsibility for your own disturbing emotions instead of blaming others. This does not mean others never act badly. See *Complaint as a compass arrow*. [msg #1786]
- **We push the cause outward automatically.** When anxiety, shame or loss of control comes up, we don't stay with the feeling. We run through an automatic cycle that ends with a conclusion about the other person. [msg #1786]
- **Naming the feeling is the first step, and it is turned toward yourself.** Naming what you feel moves you from reactive to more conscious mode. That has to happen inwardly before any conversation. [msg #1786]
- **Disturbing emotions are a sign of a living relationship, not a bad one.** This is Tift's position, set against the romantic-love idea that a relationship is defined by the happiness it gives. [msg #1786]
- **Guilt and regret are different things.** Guilt puts the guilty person at the centre. Regret faces outward: it sees the other person and the consequences, and takes responsibility. Acting from regret rather than guilt builds connection. [msg #1808]
- **Sensitivity does not cancel strength.** The author holds up the artist archetype (Nick Cave) as a role model in which feeling and strength exist at the same time. [msg #1693]

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Emotions in decision-making (five-point model)
Purpose: to replace the popular but, in the author's words, scientifically wrong belief that emotions interfere with reason ("эмоции мешают разуму" — "emotions interfere with reason") [msg #184].
1. **Strong emotions really can interfere.** They can create neural noise and deprive the prefrontal lobe of the ability to maintain working memory. That is why constant emotional discomfort often weakens mental abilities and makes people agitated and impulsive. Emotional outbursts also cut off access to common sense and lead to automatic, unconsidered actions [msg #184].
2. **The other, more important side.** Research at the University of Iowa showed that we make fundamentally bad decisions not only under strong emotions but also when we have no access to emotions at all: if the connection between the amygdala and the prefrontal area of the brain is damaged, thinking becomes distorted. The author repeats this finding in 2023, adding that ignoring emotions lowers decision quality because it reduces the amount of information we have [msg #184, #809].
3. **Emotions participate in logical thinking as much as the rational mind.** They are systems of condensed past experience that push thinking in the right direction and simplify decisions [msg #184].
4. **New paradigm: emotions are important for quality decisions.** We do not need to get rid of them, suppress them or replace them with reason; nor treat them as an evolutionary imperfection or take the "chauvinistic" position of attributing them to women. The author calls all of this "простое невежество и на самом деле слабая позиция" ("simple ignorance and actually a weak position"), because by denying emotions we also deny our responsibility for working with them [msg #184].
5. **Real effectiveness / high performance is about finding balance**, which has two parts: (a) healing oneself from past emotional traumas that trigger uncontrolled and not quite adequate bursts of feeling; (b) developing the skills to use emotions wisely — for example the ability to be aware of, name simple and complex emotions and understand the purpose of their appearance [msg #184].

### Three-step method for accepting an emotion
Context: the author criticises most corporate EQ programs for being theoretical and run by trainers without real skills in working with emotions — comparable to learning swimming or sex theoretically: "you'll be shown photos of water or people, told that water is wet and people are warm, and that's the whole swimming training". Everyone says "accept your emotions", but how to do it is unclear because for years we trained the opposite habit. Learning takes years or a lifetime and consists of many skills; acceptance is one of them [msg #258].

1. **Become aware of your experience in the moment.** First notice that something is happening to you right now. Notice what thoughts are spinning in your head and what sensations are located in the body; try to detail them as much as possible, explore and study them without any evaluation. Author's example: "There is some tension in the chest and heaviness in the hands. It seems dark-coloured, pulsing and moving a little. Unpleasant, and I want to get rid of it." Why it matters: the author says most EQ programs do not understand what mindfulness is, skip this most important step, and therefore fail in his eyes [msg #258].
2. **Name your main current emotion, or mark it as an image in the body.** Ask: what name can be given to the emotion that most creates the current experience (thoughts, images, bodily sensations)? Either name it or notice it as an image. Example: "Кажется, что это гнев, который напоминает черный мешок" ("It seems to be anger, which resembles a black sack") [msg #258].
3. **Directly accept it and stop obstructing its free living-through**, by consciously choosing to relate to it from one of the following states:
   - **Interest / Curiosity / Beginner's mind.** Simply look at it with interest: what is it, how does it show up? Look with enthusiastic interest, as at a miracle or a "wow" event. Look completely without prejudice, as if seeing it with fresh eyes for the first time ("which is true"). Example: "Wow, this black sack is also in my legs. Wow, it moves fast. Wow, it pulses strongly" [msg #258].
   - **Researcher of the emotion's meaning.** Ask yourself or the image: why did the emotion come? What need is hidden behind it? What does it want from me? What message does it carry? How does it want to take care of my wellbeing? Example: "Anger came as a sign that this person is violating my boundaries. I don't like the situation. I need to change it and tell this person to stop" [msg #258].
   - **Hospitality / Gratitude / Joy.** When we understand the emotion arises for an understandable reason and is trying to help us, we can stop fighting it. We can rejoice that we have evolutionary mechanisms that wish us well, "even if in such a strange form". Consciously allow all sensations characteristic of the emotion to be in the body and mind. The easiest way to feel this is to consciously invite the sensations to settle throughout the whole body and take up all the space they need. Be hospitable to these sensations, thank them for trying to help you, be glad you have them — "you are a living person" [msg #258].

Outcome and rationale: this is how one can feel in practice what a full and healthy cycle of living an emotion means. The cycle itself lasts only a few seconds if not delayed by non-acceptance of some sensations. Because we are taught from childhood to suppress emotions, we have to relearn as adults through such exercises. When we understand the emotion's message, our ways of acting in situations change strongly — in a direction very different from habitual strategies, toward health and happiness [msg #258].

### Three formats of anger
Context: reading social-media comments on any topical issue shows that people in Russian society are angry a lot and actively, and many are tired of it. People with developing political and social consciousness often gain access to collective experiences and even collective anger, which, according to Jungian psychoanalyst Clarissa Estés, surface in the psyche again and again. The author says it matters to understand the nature of this anger and how it is expressed, because constructive handling of anger leads a person or society to health or illness, forward or backward [msg #427].

1. **Format 1 — "Никогда не гневайся" ("Never be angry").** We dislike something but endure and stay silent; we "must" stay calm, not create conflicts, stay nice. This suppresses anger by distancing from it so as not to feel pain and discomfort. A variant in self-development and practitioner circles: if others hurt you and you get angry, you simply do not practise well enough. The author says this misreads non-violent religious traditions and classes virtually all unpleasant emotions as "shadow", without separating the shadow from a healthy emotional reaction to circumstances [msg #427].
2. **Format 2 — Erupting anger.** The same suppression, but in the other direction — outward; this is what we see in comments. By erupting anger we try to suppress the emotion as strongly and quickly as possible by attacking an external object that opposes our picture of the world: waving fists and writing rude comments to get rid of emotional pain quickly (usually without realising it, just acting). Because the aim is to defeat the external object fast, no dialogue and no conflict resolution happen [msg #427].
3. **Format 3 — Righteous anger.** From the standpoint of Jungian psychoanalysis and of the physiology of emotions in general, anger is a natural function, and collective anger, grief or resentment exist. Feeling them is a healthy psychic reaction, and it is equally natural to turn them against injustice to bring about change for the better. Collective rage, like individual rage, is a teacher pointing to signals important for our system; it should be used as motivation to seek and give support, to engage the group and individuals in dialogue, and as a reason to demand accountability, progress and improvements. For a healthy psyche it is adequate to respond to disrespect, injustice and trauma with sharp anger — so if it hurts you when people are jailed for posts, "это свидетельствует о вашей нормальности и здоровье" ("this testifies to your normality and health") [msg #427].

The unhealthy reaction common to formats 1 and 2 is to neutralise anger so as to stop feeling it and therefore not strive for development and change. The author adds that many media exploit the misconception of format 1 to spread the myth that if a group in a country is dissatisfied with the existing order, it is because of that group's egoism, immaturity or illness — and not because of a real situation of violence and injustice that causes a healthy reaction and requires dialogue and change [msg #427].

### Five costs of the "coping by force" model
Thesis: our next step in development often lies in areas that evoke negative feelings in us — disdain, rejection, anxiety or fear. The author notes that resilience psychology has become fashionable, yet some people show no interest in it although most of us regularly and painfully experience instability. Achievement culture and the traumatised post-Soviet culture tend to ignore these parts of human experience and use a model of coping with instability through force — "собраться/ держаться/перетерпеть/справляться/сражаться" ("pull yourself together / hold on / endure / cope / fight"). We forget it is only one model with its own price, and may not know or believe that other models are more effective [msg #672].

1. **Part of our natural strength starts to be perceived as weakness.** The human state — including sensations, emotions, stress and trauma reactions — is seen as weaknesses that get in the way [msg #672].
2. **We spend a lot of energy suppressing subtle needs instead of working with them.** Since in this model weakness must be overcome by force, the "force" becomes bodily contraction meant to create tension so as *not* to feel unaccepted sensations. To keep them out of the conscious field we must constantly maintain defences on the physical and psychic levels [msg #672].
3. **We produce cruelty toward ourselves and others.** Constant contraction and tension create physical and psychic discomfort and further entrench the model of force and endurance. We want to turn away from any weakness quickly because it unconsciously reminds us of our unmet but unaccepted emotional needs, and of all the uncomfortable tension we constantly hold [msg #672].
4. **We lose empathy, and with it access to relationship models built on trust and cooperation.** Since the model requires constant suppression of feelings and turning away from everything to do with one's state, these fall more and more into a blind spot; we stop seeing subtle and important parts of human reality and lose the ability to build deep relationships and systems where real cooperation is possible [msg #672].
5. **We create instability, because neglecting weakness eventually makes the model itself weak.** Personally, important subtle needs are ignored — hence the stories of years of effective work after which a person falls ill and re-evaluates all their values (i.e. this model). State systems or organisations built by people with this mindset sooner or later start tearing apart from contradictions; through rigidity from constant tension and cruelty they do not learn and integrate novelty poorly. To resolve the natural conflict and preserve itself, such a system, lacking any other model, uses violence, starts wars, jails people: unaccepted elements must be suppressed by force, "as it has been habitual to do for years with oneself" [msg #672].

Way out: more often notice what you want to skip over or assess with disdain, and look exactly there — that is probably where the next level of effectiveness, complexity and resilience lies. This requires genuine leadership — the ability to go against the mainstream and treat "weakness" with kindness and acceptance, to then find strength in vulnerability, and to build far more beautiful states and organisations on humanity, trust and cooperation instead of war and violence [msg #672].

### Three strategies for working with experience (Letting be, Letting go, Letting in)
The author claims the path to greater wholeness, satisfaction and the development of complexity of consciousness runs through just three kinds of practice, which summarise all successful strategies of working with experiences. Sources listed: Rick Hanson's *Neurodharma* and Kristin Neff's *Self-Compassion* [msg #755].
1. **Letting be.** Simply stay with what arises in experience: accept, feel and possibly even explore phenomena. When we just stay with sensations they may change, but we do nothing to influence them or modify our current experience. The author attached a 17-minute guided Letting be practice from his "Emotional balance" program [msg #755].
2. **Letting go.** Consciously release painful or unpleasant sensations: relax tension in the body, question obsessive thoughts, step back from desires that cause suffering to ourselves and others [msg #755].
3. **Letting in.** Cultivate what helps and develops us — various qualities or skills, e.g. becoming more resilient, attentive, compassionate and grateful [msg #755].

Order and logic: it is a sequence in which learning to *simply be* with experience is the key step we always start with. It contains the main meta-skill or base process — awareness, defined as "знания того, что есть в опыте прямо сейчас" ("knowing what is in experience right now"). Without awareness we can neither release tension nor cultivate useful qualities. In difficult situations, Letting be is often the best strategy available — we are aware of our fear or anger without making the situation worse by additional reactions to them. But it is not the whole practice: it is not enough to just be with thoughts and reactions; we need to learn to develop them. At some point while staying with experience we can learn to let go of the unnecessary — relax the body more, help the natural flow of feelings unfold and complete restless thoughts — and then let into the freed space what will support us and let us change, for example self-compassion. Rule: check that your meditation or mindfulness system includes all three steps [msg #755].

### Step 2 of emotional development — accept all emotions, cultivate constructive ones
Purpose: to explain "why 'bad' emotions still exist" and where the biggest potential of emotional development lies [msg #969].
- **Step 1 (the established scheme).** Modern psychology recognises that all emotions have functional meaning and teaches not to divide them into good and bad. This helps people understand and manage reactions by exploring emotions and the needs behind them; the task is then simple on paper but sometimes takes years of practice — learning to fulfil these needs ecologically, without harm to oneself and others. The author says this scheme is included in most deep mind corporate programs because it builds the foundation of emotional healing and growth [msg #969].
- **The obstacle.** The psychotherapeutic approach that helps us evolve toward accepting all emotions is especially useful in the first steps away from suppressing or flaring up unwanted emotions ("девочки не злятся, мальчики не плачут, не хочу быть тревожным" — "girls don't get angry, boys don't cry, I don't want to be anxious"). But having gone a bit into acceptance and discarded society's imposed hierarchy of good and bad emotions, we often throw the baby out with the bathwater and stay in a limited scheme of nihilism where everything matters equally and there is no difference. Everyone has the right to feel anger; it need not be considered bad, suppressed or dumped on others. But it should not be equated with, for example, compassion or romantic love — these are very different states in sensation and results. Having learned not to suppress anger but remaining an often-angry person, we subjectively feel not very happy and get ill more often [msg #969].
- **Step 2.** "*Мы учимся принимать все эмоции и культивировать те, что ведут к ощущению большего благополучия и здоровья.*" ("We learn to accept all emotions and cultivate those that lead to a sense of greater wellbeing and health.") Anger, self-preoccupation, envy and anxiety do not lead there. EQ development does not end with recognising an emotion and identifying needs; a person can also learn *skills* to bring themselves into an emotional state that helps unlock potential and feel satisfaction [msg #969].
- **Where the methods come from.** Historically this was the work of the contemplative approach, which used remarkable methods of transforming emotions and created technologies for developing constructive states: kindness, compassion, gratitude. Over the last 30 years these have been moving into and being supplemented in modern, increasingly hybrid psychotherapy schools — e.g. CFT (compassion-focused therapy), which relies heavily on mindfulness and compassion skills, i.e. cultivating certain states and emotions. CFT develops a compassionate stance — the ability to sympathise with oneself and others and to receive sympathy from others; one way is reformulating self-blame into self-correcting ideas, extending compassion to oneself and others [msg #969].
- **Healthy ranking of emotions.** The author stresses the idea of healthy ranking of emotions and cultivating constructive ones: important needs lie behind anger, envy and anxiety, but by themselves these emotions lead to illness, unhappiness and bad relationships. We can not only learn to recognise and express needs but consciously choose to change our personality — developing states and emotions that turn into health on all levels. Through such work a person can not only learn to live anger ecologically but at some point practically stop experiencing it, fully transforming emotional schemas and patterns. In the author's opinion this idea is still strongly underestimated or completely ignored by the psychotherapeutic community, which thereby understates possible human potential [msg #969].

### Beautiful monsters — befriending protective parts
Context: written from a retreat with Tsoknyi Rinpoche (whose book with Daniel Goleman, *Why We Meditate: The Science and Practice of Clarity and Compassion*, combines contemplative depth with neuroscience). The author's point: deep mindfulness practice is not only about training focus or lowering stress; it unfolds development on different levels and contains methods that can be considered therapeutic, which are being studied and increasingly integrated into classical therapy [msg #1366].
- **What a beautiful monster is.** Tsoknyi's name for parts that are not-very-helpful strategies of adaptation to trauma. Not a metaphor of evil, but parts of the personality that look good, even dignified, yet are built on fear and ultimately cause us suffering. Our perfectionism, independence, caring or calmness may be not an expression of maturity but a defensive construction behind which stands deep anxiety about the need to survive at any cost: "красивый снаружи, но стоящий на страхе внутри" ("beautiful outside, but standing on fear inside") [msg #1366].
- **Worked example — "The perfectionist who never rests".** Outside: successful, organised, always in control, works with full dedication, never allows herself weakness, gets a lot of praise. Inside: a deep fear of being "not good enough"; an early feeling "меня полюбят только, если я буду идеальной" ("I will only be loved if I am perfect"); this fear unconsciously runs her whole life — rest causes anxiety, and any mistake causes shame and self-punishment. Why it is a monster: it destroys body and psyche and cuts off living spontaneity and joy [msg #1366].
- **Other common monsters:** the spiritual seeker who has renounced emotions; the caring rescuer; the independent loner [msg #1366].
- **Why they are a problem.** Monsters exploit us. When such a pattern activates — and many of us may be substantially identified with it — we perceive reality in a rather distorted way, anxiety captures us and we act without attentiveness and kindness to ourselves. We lose flexibility and adaptiveness in action, and also the ability to feel and be fully alive and vulnerable [msg #1366].
- **Method: being with parts, not fixing them.** Rinpoche suggests not fighting these monsters and not trying to improve them, but noticing, befriending and loving them. Love — which we first must learn to show to ourselves and our parts — is the main fuel of healing and transformation. Love shows as the ability to **simply be together** with difficult parts: not suppressing them, not trying to fix them, and not fully merging with them as we may be used to. Instead, see the monster's form, motive and suffering, and acknowledge that it too is part of a sentient being that feels, loves, suffers and wants contact with the world [msg #1366].
- **Related practice — Feeding Your Demons** (adapted by Tsultrim Allione, a western student of Chögyam Trungpa). Instead of expelling difficult parts ("demons"), you invite them, look them in the eye, give them form, sensation and voice — and then feed them. You ask the demon: "Что тебе на самом деле нужно? Как ты можешь получить это без разрушения?" ("What do you really need? How can you get it without destruction?") [msg #1366].
- **Application and result reported.** This way we can heal our parts on our own outside a therapeutic relationship and become more integrated. The author reports that in the previous month they applied exactly this practice in therapeutic work — in a psychedelic-assisted therapy session — and it helped make progress with preverbal trauma. Tsoknyi Rinpoche's "handshake with the monster" practice exists as an English text and video [msg #1366].

### Three levels of working with emotions
Question addressed: serious meditation practice may label emotions negative, while therapy seems far from that view — who is right, and how to join the views into one map? [msg #1684]
1. **Psychology and therapy.** Every emotion has a function, a task and a logic of arising. Anger, anxiety, fear, desire are not considered bad or good; one speaks rather of pleasant and unpleasant emotions while avoiding moral evaluation, because a need lies behind each emotion. The author calls this an important shift in our collective growing up: it lets us leave the logic of suppression, shame and self-violence that saturated humanity for millennia. Many criticise this therapeutic shift for infantilising society, "forgetting history", but it returns to a person contact with their own experience, living needs and real subjectivity; in this sense modern psychology does "невероятно фундаментальную работу" ("incredibly fundamental work") — helping people feel again and be more alive and free than ever before [msg #1684].
2. **Modern (secular) mindfulness.** Emotions are usually not judged as negative or bad. Anger, anxiety, attachment or desire are seen as natural events arising and passing in the field of experience, to be observed without judgment and suppression. Here mindfulness converges with psychology: it returns contact with experience, reduces reactivity and widens freedom of choice in action. Yet for all its neutrality it keeps a functional distinction: attention goes not to which emotion arose but to what identification with it leads to. Emotions are not deemed mistaken, but it explores how involvement in anger or attachment narrows perception, strengthens clinging and reproduces suffering. Secular mindfulness usually does not formulate a goal of radical transformation of mind and does not name these states root causes of suffering — these ideas stay softened and implicit. As a result it mostly works at the level of regulation and non-involvement, a bridge between psychology and contemplative tradition, not reaching the depth where the very mechanism of the stable arising of anger, attachment and a sense of a separate subject is questioned [msg #1684].
3. **The contemplative approach (in its unsimplified form).** It sets a completely different horizon: not just handling emotions better, but transforming the very nature of human experience. Here emotions do differ — not morally but existentially. Some states are considered negative not because they are bad but because they lead to suffering, ours and others'; they strengthen clinging, identification and reactivity, and push a person to actions that reproduce pain. The task is not to express or live through destructive emotions in the usual therapeutic sense but to change the very way they unfold. With serious practice such emotions do not necessarily disappear completely, but the stance toward them changes — not because they are suppressed, but because the subject who would cling to and hold them becomes softer. The habitual construction "я злюсь", "я тревожусь", "я хочу" ("I am angry", "I am anxious", "I want") falls apart: there is an arising experience and an awareness in which it does not crystallise [msg #1684].

Synthesis: "Терапия учит быть с эмоциями и помогает сформировать здоровую субъектность. Контемплативные практики скорее учат не становиться ими" ("Therapy teaches us to be with emotions and helps form a healthy subjectivity. Contemplative practices rather teach not to become them") and question the very need for rigid subjectivity. When the levels are mixed without distinction, problems arise: either practices are used to suppress feelings, or therapy tries to solve tasks beyond its ontology [msg #1684].

### Complaint as a compass arrow (turning dissatisfaction into a growth engine)
**Purpose / when to use:** use this when you are dissatisfied with other people, especially a partner or a team. The signal is sharpest when your reaction is acute, the same complaints keep coming back, or you feel "literally overwhelmed" ("нас буквально накрывает" (*we are literally overwhelmed*)). The author calls Tift's idea radical, "совершенно неудобная" (*completely uncomfortable*), and one that "заставляет взрослеть" (*makes you grow up*). If you take it seriously, you can no longer just see "идиотов" (*idiots*) around you and have to work on yourself. The reward is "гораздо большего покоя и устойчивости" (*much greater peace and stability*). [msg #1786]

The underlying claim, quoted by the author from Bruce Tift on Andrew Holecek's podcast: «Наше недовольство по поводу других — особенно наших партнеров в отношениях — это возможность взять ответственность за собственные беспокоящие эмоции вместо того, чтобы винить других людей в том, что они расстроили нас» (*Our dissatisfaction with others — especially our relationship partners — is an opportunity to take responsibility for our own disturbing emotions instead of blaming other people for upsetting us*). [msg #1786]

Components, in the order the post develops them:
1. **Notice the signal.** An acute reaction, a repeating complaint or a feeling of being overwhelmed is "сигнал о том, что внутри нас что-то не хочет быть почувствованным" (*a signal that something inside us does not want to be felt*). Why: Tift's point is that "наша реакция на любые их проявления — всегда наша" (*our reaction to anything they do is always ours*). He is not saying others never do bad things or that we should simply accept the world as it is. [msg #1786]
2. **Recognise the automatic outward shift.** We meet feelings such as anxiety, shame or a sense of losing control. Instead of staying with them, we "мгновенно переносим причину наружу" (*instantly move the cause outside*). This is not a conscious choice. We usually don't stay with the emotion at all and instead live through an automatic cycle that ends in a conclusion about the other. The mechanism is explained in *Why we blame others* below. [msg #1786]
3. **Stay with what came up instead of sending a complaint.** When you are hurt, there are two strategies: "Либо оставаться с тем, что поднялось внутри. Либо отправить жалобой другому человеку" (*Either stay with what has risen inside. Or send it as a complaint to the other person*). The second seems honest, because you are telling the truth. But a "truth" framed as an accusation distorts the interaction and sends it the wrong way. [msg #1786]
4. **Name the feeling, to yourself first.** Behind "ты меня не слышишь" (*you don't hear me*) there is often "мне больно" (*it hurts me*). That is what needed to be said for the situation to have a chance of changing. Naming moves a person from reactive mode to a more conscious one (see Lieberman under *Evidence and numbers*). "Это и есть первый шаг. И его приходится делать не в сторону другого человека, а в свою собственную" (*This is the first step. And it has to be taken not toward the other person but toward oneself*). [msg #1786]
5. **Only then talk.** "Сначала нужно встретиться с тем, что внутри — и только потом разговаривать" (*First you need to meet what is inside, and only then talk*). [msg #1786]

The image that sums it up: "Жалоба — как стрелка компаса, у которое есть не только направление, но и начало" (*A complaint is like a compass arrow, which has not only a direction but also a starting point*). The complaint points at the other person, but it starts in you. [msg #1786]

### Team complaint self-inquiry
**When to use:** when the same complaint about a team keeps repeating. The author gives three typical complaints: «Они не берут ответственность» (*They don't take responsibility*), «Он снова тянет одеяло» (*He's pulling the blanket again*, i.e. grabbing credit or attention), «Команда не слышит» (*The team doesn't hear*). [msg #1786]

**Steps:**
1. Stop. [msg #1786]
2. Ask: "а что именно я сейчас чувствую?" (*what exactly am I feeling right now?*). Do not ask what is wrong with the team. Ask what is happening in your body right now, which emotions you feel, and what they are about. [msg #1786]
3. Check for the three things the author says are most often behind the complaint: [msg #1786]
   - **Anxiety about the result**, which is easier to express as a claim against others. [msg #1786]
   - **A sense of losing control**, which is easier to call someone else's irresponsibility. [msg #1786]
   - **Shame about your own decision that didn't work**, projected as "они подвели" (*they let us down*). [msg #1786]
4. Meet those feelings first, then have the conversation. [msg #1786]

**Caveat from the author:** real problems may exist in the team, and these feelings and ideas may be pointing to them. But in practice a conversation held from projection solves nothing. It only increases distance. [msg #1786]

### Three responses to one's own mistake: closedness, guilt, regret
**Purpose:** to tell apart three ways of relating to harm you have done in a relationship. Only the third keeps contact. [msg #1808]
1. **Closedness and punishment (закрытость).** The person strikes a pose and waits for the others to realise that they are the ones in the wrong. There is no contact at all, no empathy and no curiosity, and so no relationship. [msg #1808]
2. **Guilt (вина / Guilt).** There is little or no contact, because guilt "ставит в центр виновного" (*puts the guilty one at the centre*). The guilty person is busy with their own bad feelings and self-image, not with what the other person is going through. Instead of dealing with the real situation, the other person ends up having to talk the guilty one out of their badness. The author's classic example is the parent's line "С тобой что-то не так, потому что мы тебя плохо воспитали" (*Something is wrong with you because we raised you badly*). He calls this an ordinary case of self-enclosure, "которая в народе называется эгоизмом" (*which people call egoism*). [msg #1808]
3. **Regret (сожаление / Regret).** It works differently and faces outward: "Я вижу другого человека, вижу что произошло, вижу последствия — и беру за это ответственность. Не ради отпущения грехов, а потому что это правда" (*I see the other person, I see what happened, I see the consequences — and I take responsibility for it. Not for absolution, but because it is true*). Regret sees things systemically. It admits that something was lacking and that the relationship suffered because of it, "И это грустно, а иногда трагично" (*And that is sad, and sometimes tragic*). [msg #1808]

**Why options 1 and 2 are so common:** in a culture of "strength", admitting a mistake means showing weakness or becoming bad. So it is much easier either to defend yourself or to collapse into guilt, "лишь бы не столкнуться с реальным последствием своих действий" (*just so as not to face the real consequence of one's actions*). The author regrets how rarely he meets stories of people who take responsibility for their actions without sliding into closedness or guilt. [msg #1808]

**Conclusion:** "Guilt и Regret — разные вещи. Так что, стоит их отделить друг от друга" (*Guilt and Regret are different things. So it is worth separating them from each other*). Learning to act from sincere regret rather than guilt may bring "гораздо больше крепких связей и здоровых отношений" (*many more strong connections and healthy relationships*). Over the years it has become especially important to the author not to act from a sense of his own badness. He tries to reach the state where he truly turns toward others. That brings him into the network of relationships instead of excluding him from it and isolating him "в одиночество свой правоты или плохости" (*in the loneliness of his own rightness or badness*). [msg #1808]

### Morning regret practice (from guilt to regret)
**What it is:** a "social practice" the author did one morning and suggests to readers. You write to people whose relationship with you faded or suffered and tell them you regret how things went. [msg #1808]

**How the author did it (his case):**
1. He woke up earlier than usual, half-asleep, with stories and words forming in his mind. First he warmly congratulated his friend Dasha on her birthday. [msg #1808]
2. Lying in bed, he thought of a person with whom, a year earlier, he had lacked the attention to steer the relationship through a tense, difficult project. They went from a close partnership to hardly talking, and the contact faded away. He wrote to him and apologised for not being enough there, for not being able to "запроцессить" (*process*) it better. [msg #1808]
3. He thought of another person, then another, and wrote to three more people that he regretted how their relationships had turned out. [msg #1808]
4. **Inner stance:** he did this "абсолютно не из чувства вины, а из сожаления, из искреннего раскаяния" (*absolutely not out of guilt, but out of regret, out of sincere remorse*). It is genuinely sad that in life you sometimes lack the resources, skills or attention to handle human contacts in the best way. You can't always be ideal and support a relationship with your presence so that it opens up, grows and strengthens rather than going stale and suffocating. [msg #1808]

**Result he reports:** after the messages he felt wonderful. The morning started with connection to others, and "какие-то узелочки развязались внутри" (*some little knots came undone inside*). He suggests this may have been his meditation practice for the day and the best act to start the day with. The friend's birthday set off a feeling of connectedness, which turned into "цепочку вполне зрелых действий" (*a chain of quite mature actions*). [msg #1808]

**Instruction to readers:** do the same morning practice, but from the inner point where there is "не вина [про вас], а искреннее сожаление про сами обстоятельства, отношения и возможную или реальную боль другого" (*not guilt [about you], but sincere regret about the circumstances themselves, the relationship and the possible or real pain of the other*). ## Detailed insights

### Sensitivity versus resilience
In a forwarded post (from the channel soloveev: жизнь на марсе), the writer notes that therapy and meditation make us better at recognising emotions and feeling more — but there is a flip side: you become more sensitive, and joy as well as pain, anger and sadness are now felt more intensely, "and now you somehow have to live with that". The writer contrasts this with psychopaths, described as people who do not understand their own or others' feelings: by the figures given, 25% of people in prison are psychopaths and 10% of people at liberty, and the latter often sit in business class — leaders who compensate for not understanding feelings with high intelligence. Their advantage, per the writer: the trolley problem does not exist for them; when decisions must be made they do not come into conflict with feelings and morality — "the main thing for a leader is to do the right, not the comfortable, things"; deciding on layoffs, one must consider second- and third-order consequences, not the momentary disappointment of those laid off. Still, the writer lives in the paradigm that increased sensitivity is a greater good than insensitivity, because with sensitivity you get more tools for working with your states and thoughts — even when sad, you understand why and what to do about it. The key caveat: together with sensitivity you must also train resilience — "Чтобы стать по-настоящему живым, нужно полить израненное тело не только живой водой, но и мертвой" ("To become truly alive, you need to pour on the wounded body not only living water but also dead water" — a folk-tale image of healing water and life-giving water) [msg #408].

### Oversharing and emotional exhibitionism (DM Dictionary entries)
- **Non-ecological sharing (over-sharing):** sharing our state, feelings and thoughts without calibrating the other person's state and readiness to receive it. **Ecological sharing** means attention to the listener and checking the verbal and non-verbal response to our content, with readiness to wrap up if needed [msg #660].
- **Emotional exhibitionism:** systematically dumping our inner experiences into public space without caring how they affect others, what benefit they carry, and whether the message could be delivered in a way that demands less emotional response from other people. The author says it is sometimes a manifestation of narcissistic trauma, and sometimes simply emotional ignorance, "когда мы путаем аутентичность с аутичностью" ("when we confuse authenticity with autism" — a wordplay on self-absorbed disregard of others) [msg #681].
The accompanying images are only "DM DICTIONARY" title cards with no further content [img #660, img #681].

### Non-violence toward oneself as the root of emotional health
- Feeling is wonderful, vital and today counter-cultural. Loud music, overwork, caffeine and alcohol, social media — the whole culture tells us "не чувствуй, не замечай" ("don't feel, don't notice"), otherwise it could be too painful: painful from constant exploitation of the body by ourselves, culture and power. Examples given: sitting for hours to do more work and get more money; breast implants to gain sexual power [msg #798].
- **Mechanism (a loop).** Applying violence to ourselves, we feel less and less; or, not feeling, we apply more and more violence to ourselves — "who can tell where it started?" Treating ourselves aggressively, we become alienated from the parts that hurt and build inner walls between "the one who hurts and the one who must"; we believe ever more firmly that life is harsh and that we need tension and forcing to live [msg #798].
- **Spillover.** The more we get stuck in the head and in ideas cut off from the far more fundamental reality of feelings and body, the further behind the fence we keep the truth about pain — and the more suffering we are ready to cause others in the name of ourselves and our ideas. If we violate ourselves, multiplying pain and anger within, sooner or later we will apply this principle to partners, children and employees [msg #798].
- **Cynicism as a defence.** For someone too used to violence, talk of non-violence itself provokes disgust, usually shown as cynicism ("this is all chatter for the weak, the stupid, the idealists"). The author's challenge: have you tested your picture of the world persistently, or are you defending a story in which this picture has already led to years of violence? Is the world really like this by nature, or did we forget the moment it became so painful that we called the world cruel and closed off from it? [msg #798]
- **What non-violence is.** Not something good or bad as a moral social construct, but "наиболее глубинный способ самопознания, развития и исцеления себя и мира от раздробленности" ("the deepest way of self-knowledge, development and healing of oneself and the world from fragmentation") — the way to end the eternal war inside and inevitably reduce war outside: our aggression toward children and partners, colleagues and fellow citizens [msg #798].
- **What happens when practised.** Exploring how to stop building fences inside ourselves for the sake of ideas, and embodying non-violence, we let go of anger and resentment that arise from alienation of suppressed parts. Without anger the heart opens to reality, feeling both more joy and greater pain. Stopping the attempts to disconnect from pain, we begin to see the countless pain of all living beings, present now and tomorrow; in the compassion that arises automatically we can find connectedness that takes us beyond a subtler, still violence-creating fence — the one separating I from the other. Non-violence is necessary to heal the tornness, fragmentation and fragmentedness that lead to wars [msg #798].

### The science of emotions in ten minutes
The author's framing: because of emotional needs unmet in childhood (= traumas), adults find it hard to form a clear picture of emotions; we do not let ourselves feel "bad" emotions and then, overflowing with repressed feelings, explode impulsively with sad consequences. So he offers at least a cognitive update of the knowledge base. Claims, each attributed by him to a linked source [msg #809]:
1. All truly effective leaders find a way to reduce their own anxiety and use it regularly (Harvard Business Review article on how history's great leaders managed anxiety) [msg #809].
2. Showing empathy and attuning to others' emotions — part of leaders' required set — directly affects the brain chemistry of both leaders and their followers; called an outstanding finding of the neuroscience of leadership of recent years (HBR, "Social Intelligence and the Biology of Leadership") [msg #809].
3. People with high intelligence and developed rational thinking more often also show more developed emotional skills (Psychology Today) [msg #809].
4. People with high emotional intelligence tolerate stress better (Psychology Today) [msg #809].
5. Employees with high emotion-regulation ability show 2.5 times higher productivity than those with low ability; inability to manage emotions creates inner battles and undermines the ability to work with focus (BetterUp) [msg #809].
6. Ignoring emotions lowers decision quality because we reduce the amount of information (the University of Iowa findings described in *Emotions in decision-making*) [msg #809].
7. Suppression of emotions and emotional dysregulation lead to problems with mental and physical health (Psychology Today) [msg #809].
8. Effective emotion regulation rests on the skill of mindfulness — noticing your states and naming them (a PMC-indexed paper) [msg #809].
9. Even simply naming emotions — a skill well developed in the meditation practice of noting — helps reduce their intensity and handle them better (a SAGE journal paper) [msg #809].

### Taking rejections without taking them personally (personal case)
The author describes arriving in San Francisco to organise three local events with little time, many tasks and much uncertainty, and a weaker "social location" than in Moscow, so he must put in more effort and receives more refusals while looking for venues, asking for intros and inviting participants. One morning he saw one rejection after another in his messenger — about 15 refusals in a day (the attached screenshot shows refusals such as a group not allowing paid announcements and a person who is fundraising and not interested). His lessons [msg #1512, img #1512]:
- He calls the mode "Полезный! Бодрящий!" ("Useful! Invigorating!") and by that day had started laughing and joking back. He admits that somewhere he could have phrased requests better to raise conversion, and somewhere "lived another life" so connections would work more strongly — but overall, "as they say in Bali", he is "in acceptance" [msg #1512].
- Looking at the screenshots, one could start thinking all sorts of things about him, and he about himself — that he is doing nonsense, his network is weak, other sad thoughts. "All this is probably even true somewhere", but he sees no use in these thoughts and does not take refusals personally: he does not take offence at anyone and "does not keep accounts", though as a living person he can briefly feel upset, angry or lonely. This models acceptance of short-lived emotions without building a story or grudge on them [msg #1512].
- He reframes refusals by type: some are about inability to help, some about mismatched needs, some about interaction style. He likes being in an intense process with people because it helps understand and get to know them — and also brings the ego down "somewhere closer to modest size" [msg #1512].
- He cites Adam Grant's *Give and Take* (Брать или отдавать), which, based on serious sociological research per the author, demonstrated a substantial influence of interaction styles on success: most people are **takers** (personal gain first, they like receiving more than giving); others are **matchers** (relationships of mutual benefit, "you for me, I for you"); the last type are **givers** — ready to help others at the expense of their own interests, expecting no reward. Givers are a minority, but they reach the very top of success regardless of industry and type of business [msg #1512].

- He describes the US as a contrast — a social fabric that is harder to enter from outside, yet also "a ton of support, fast, serious and heartfelt"; life "with caresses and beatings" that enlivens and makes him look at emotions with the view of emptiness (воззрение пустотности) [msg #1512].

### Why we blame others: the predictive brain and attributed intent
The author cites Lisa Feldman Barrett, whom he calls one of the most popular emotion theorists. In her account the brain does not receive emotions from the world. It constantly predicts reality from past experience. When someone's behaviour violates that prediction, the brain generates an affective signal that we have to put somewhere, and "Легче всего — в другого" (*The easiest place is in the other person*). [msg #1786]

He adds a Duke University study that, in his words, found that when an interaction ends badly we automatically attribute *intent* to the other person. So when we have unpleasant feelings, we read the other's behaviour affectively, not rationally. For the author this explains why the cause is "instantly" moved outward. [msg #1786]

### Disturbing emotions in relationships
The author calls the romantic-love idea that a relationship is defined by the happiness it gives you "Откровенно вредная, но популярная" (*Frankly harmful, but popular*). Tift takes the opposite position: you can't be in a real relationship without disturbing emotions, without being hurt or triggered. That is a sign of a living relationship, not a bad one. The skill is in what you do when you get hurt (see *Complaint as a compass arrow*, step 3). [msg #1786]

### The artist as a role model: sensitivity and strength together
The author reads the book-interview with Nick Cave, "Faith, Hope and Carnage" (Вера, надежда, резня, with Sean O'Hagan, cover in the attached image), as a lesson in living with grief. Cave's beloved son died 11 years ago, and the book keeps returning to that grief. The pain is still there, but "в ней нет пафоса и подавленности" (*there is no pathos or depression in it*). Cave and his wife got through the grief. It didn't break them and didn't make them "stronger" in the banal sense. Instead it brought them closer to the essence of life as it is. The author sees in this "удивительной человечностью" (*astonishing humanity*): not a famous person playing to the public, but a completely open, vulnerable and at the same time strong person, "Выпуклого и настоящего" (*vivid and real*). [msg #1693, img #1693]

For the author, Cave is a role model as a musician, a person and a man. He describes a rare combination: the ability to feel and express feelings, sometimes completely dramatic ones, while staying strong and present. Cave is aggressive not in a destructive way but in a directed, collected way that doesn't look away. "Чувствительность здесь не отменяет силу, а сила не уничтожает чувствительность — они существуют одновременно" (*Here sensitivity does not cancel strength, and strength does not destroy sensitivity — they exist simultaneously*). [msg #1693]

The author turns to the artist archetype to remind himself that a person can be "не только эффективным или полезным, но и живым, чувствующим, созидающим и проявляющим правду" (*not only effective or useful, but also alive, feeling, creating and manifesting truth*). He would like artists to move into society's spotlight, perhaps eventually displacing entrepreneurs, politicians "и даже психологов" (*and even psychologists*), which he says with a laugh. His reason: real artists show us the potential of being human, something we seem to be increasingly losing in the modern world. Ai Weiwei calls the realisation of that potential *self-expression*. The author has always called it creativity. He says many theories could explain these words, but he feels their importance with his heart. Few things call him as strongly as meaningful creation and manifesting the truth of life, which he sees as the true realisation of an authentic artist. [msg #1693]

The author says this component is slowly, "и пока ещё сыро" (*and still rawly*), starting to grow in the group practices his team runs. Increasingly there is space where something is created not necessarily for use, results or transformation, but so that people can express life together, with all its complexity, vulnerability, beauty and love. His examples are The Village in Moscow, where 110 people silently played out ordinary city life in a separate micro-cosmos, and an online space called "Тишина" (*Silence*), where dozens of people simply stayed in silence for an hour. He also plays Cave's music in all group processes where music fits, because it helps people quickly switch perception. It is as if it changes "вязкость воздуха" (*the viscosity of the air*) around them, filling it with disarming naturalness and sensuality set on a masculine form. [msg #1693]

### Cave's artistic evolution as a lesson in letting go of identity
The author's main musical imprint of Cave is the 2004 album Abattoir Blues, classic rock-like music that he only likes when Cave performs it. Later Cave did what very few people can do, especially those who have long been big, recognised figures. He completely blew up his familiar self and, on the album Ghosteen, moved into an almost otherworldly sound. The author calls Ghosteen an outstanding work in music history that arises "не из эмоций и смыслов, а из чистого присутствия" (*not from emotions and meanings, but from pure presence*). He compares Cave to David Bowie, not in style but in the inner readiness to give up his own recognisability in order to move on toward what is right specifically for him. [msg #1693]

## Practical playbook

- **When you notice you want to skip over or dismiss something with disdain**, stop and look there carefully — per the author that is where your next level of effectiveness and resilience likely lies [msg #672].
- **When caught in fear or anger in a hard situation**, the minimum viable move is Letting be: be aware of the emotion without adding reactions that worsen the situation; then, when possible, let go (relax the body, complete restless thoughts) and let in self-compassion [msg #755].
- **Before sharing your feelings**, calibrate the listener's state and readiness, watch their verbal and non-verbal response, and be ready to wrap up; before posting inner experiences publicly, ask how it affects others, what use it brings and whether the message can be delivered with less emotional demand on others [msg #660, #681].
- **To audit your mindfulness or meditation system**, check that it includes all three steps — being with, letting go, and cultivating [msg #755].
- **To name an emotion faster**, practise noting; naming lowers intensity [msg #809].
- **When a difficult part (monster) activates**, do not fight or fix it and do not merge with it: see its form, motive and suffering, then ask it what it really needs and how it can get it without destruction [msg #1366].
- **When facing many rejections**, sort them (inability to help / mismatched needs / interaction style), allow brief upset without keeping accounts, and turn each refusal into help for someone else [msg #1512].
