# Motivation, talent and hiring

## What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel author (the founder of deep mind, a mindful-leadership consultancy) and guest authors taught about why people work well: intrinsic versus external motivation, self-determination theory, and Robert Hogan's motivators and derailers as used in executive assessment. It also covers hiring — what to look for (Slack's four qualities, cultural fit), what a hiring process signals to candidates, and why the author's own company became wary of candidates with only big-corporation experience. Finally it traces the author's reading of a shifting talent market: tech firms out-competing classic business for people, the rise of freelancing, younger generations' demands, and candidates who ask first about values. The source frames all this as practical: companies that keep relying on control and "the stick" lose their best people and, in the author's words, risk stagnation [msg #74, #202, #417].

## Core ideas

- **Employees are intrinsically motivated too.** Managers often think only they themselves have intrinsic motivation, while staff respond to money and fear; the author says research does not support this, and the GM–Toyota Fremont case shows the same people performing radically better when culture changes [msg #74].
- **Intrinsic motivation is the strongest kind.** Self-determination theory describes a spectrum from fully external to full self-determination and names autonomy, competence and belonging as the three forces behind intrinsic motivation [msg #854].
- **People have several drivers at once.** Hogan's 8 motivators describe why people work; usually one leads and two support. Seemingly contradictory drivers (helping others and earning money) can coexist and work best when both are realised [msg #553].
- **Dark sides of personality block careers.** Hogan's 11 derailers explain glass ceilings, but some of them can be career lifts when they match the company culture [msg #812].
- **Hire for qualities and culture contribution, not checklists.** Slack's four qualities and the idea of cultural fit; a hiring process is a "demo version" of how the company treats people [msg #413, #202].
- **The talent market is shifting toward human-centric companies.** Tech firms, freelancing and values-driven candidates put pressure on classic, controlling corporations; corporate culture becomes decisive for market success [msg #202, #417, #430, #1130].
- **Maturity is needed on both sides.** Younger employees' demands for non-toxic communication are a valid evolution, but must become adult partnership, not a one-sided list of demands or "a change of abuser" [msg #1103].
- **Corporate careers can slow growth.** Brand-name employers inflate egos, teach overwork and buy loyalty instead of development [msg #1266].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### GM Fremont / Toyota case: same people, different culture
The author uses this story to argue against the managerial belief that employees respond only to external motivation (money and fear), stating that this belief is not supported by research [msg #74].
1. **The situation (late 1970s).** GM's car plant in Fremont performed worse than the company's other plants: products had defects, absenteeism reached 20%, and workers deliberately damaged cars. Management considered the workers idiots ("дебилами"), and people behaved accordingly. Employees did not control their own duties — they were told only fragments of information about the tasks in their section and never given the whole picture [msg #74].
2. **Closure and restart.** In 1982 GM closed the plant. Just a few months later Toyota relaunched it, hiring 90% of the former employees [msg #74].
3. **Toyota's management idea.** "Если рабочему дать шанс, он захочет гордится своим делом, увидеть как его труд вписывается в более широкую картину. У него появится стимул работать лучше и количество брака уменьшится." (If you give a worker a chance, he will want to take pride in his work and see how his labour fits into the wider picture. He will have an incentive to work better, and the number of defects will fall.) [msg #74]
4. **Result.** Within a year the plant, with the same people, rose to first place by performance among GM's plants; absenteeism fell to 2% [msg #74].
5. **The only change was culture.** Management began to show more respect to people and to treat them as equals — as intrinsically motivated, conscientious team members with shared goals [msg #74].
6. **The author's application.** A trip to Kazan prompted the reflection: local authorities asked him not to be soft with school directors, i.e. they were deeply convinced that even their own managers need external motivation "with the stick". The author thinks the story scales to half of Russia's enterprises, where the attitude that the manager's role is control and supervision is deeply rooted, and that the force of such attitudes easily leads to economic stagnation and "the loss of the future of whole generations". He adds that intrinsic motivation matters for personal goals and even more for team goals [msg #74].

### Self-determination theory: four types of motivation and three basic needs
Presented under the headline that Maslow's pyramid is outdated and self-determination theory replaces it. Richard Ryan and Edward Deci formulated the theory in the mid-1980s and have kept developing it to the present; their research and experiments confirmed and extended its principles and applied them in education, healthcare, organisations and sport; the post says it gained wide recognition as one of the important concepts of modern psychology [msg #854].

Main premise: a person strives for intrinsic motivation of their own actions, and this intrinsic motivation creates the conditions for the greatest effectiveness, satisfaction and personal growth. The theory describes the full spectrum from external to internal motivation as four main types [msg #854]:
1. **Fully external motivation (Полностью внешняя мотивация).** You work hard because you want to feel, through external feedback and confirmation, that you are okay. External verification is the only thing that lets you feel it; without material symbols of success it is as if you do not exist. This type usually does not support the inner sense of satisfaction — eudaimonia — and does not promote personal growth [msg #854].
2. **Low self-determination (Низкая самодетерминация).** You work hard because you get a reward for the result. The reward forms an identity — "я человек, который хорош в X" (I am a person who is good at X) — and that keeps you going. With this motivation we feel our behaviour is controlled by others and we have no freedom of choice. It also does not promote inner well-being or personal development [msg #854].
3. **High self-determination (Высокая самодетерминация).** You work hard because you enjoy the action itself. The result gives pleasure and prompts you to keep doing further things you like. External factors are present ("everyone likes paying their bills"), but they are aligned with the pleasure of the activity [msg #854].
4. **Full self-determination (Полная самодетерминация).** You work hard because you love what you do. The joy of participation and process becomes the key motivator; external approval and rewards recede completely into the background — often when immersed in such tasks we even forget the external reward exists. This motivation arises when actions match our own values, interests and needs. Here occur the most intensive personal development and the highest levels of productivity and satisfaction; this is where masters and top performers are located [msg #854].

**The three forces behind intrinsic motivation.** After decades of research Ryan and Deci identified autonomy, competence and belonging (автономия, компетентность и принадлежность). When these needs are satisfied we are motivated, productive and happy, and vice versa. The theory therefore not only says intrinsic motivation is key but explains where it comes from and how to build flourishing organisations [msg #854].

**Organisational implication.** To sustain the strongest motivation, create conditions in which people feel freedom of choice and belonging and act in accordance with their own values rather than against them. Hence, the post concludes, genuine shared flourishing is possible only with respect for boundaries, individual values and differences [msg #854].

### Hogan motivators (8)
Guest author Leysan Shayakhmetova (managing partner of career/HR consultancy Career Booster, formerly an executive-search consultant at Ward Howell) introduces the motivators of American psychologist Robert Hogan, whose test is widely used by companies to analyse candidates, especially top managers. Purpose: to look at your team and yourself with fresh eyes. From analysing test results of managers at the Skolkovo business school she often sees seemingly contradictory drivers coexisting harmoniously in one person — e.g. the motivation to benefit others and to earn money for oneself; such people show themselves best in teamwork if they find a way to realise both drivers [msg #553].

Motivators are "8 ответов на вопрос, почему люди делают свою работу" (8 answers to the question of why people do their work) [msg #553]:
1. **Power (власть)** — the drive to take on a challenge and achieve a result, the wish to be first among competitors [msg #553].
2. **Affiliation (сопричастность)** — the need to feel part of a team [msg #553].
3. **Commerce (коммерция)** — the drive to link effort to financial result, the need to directly influence one's own income [msg #553].
4. **Hedonism (жажда наслаждений)** — orientation toward fun and pleasure in the process of work [msg #553].
5. **Recognition (признание)** — the drive for attention and praise, for delivering projects significant at company or industry level [msg #553].
6. **Altruism (альтруизм)** — the wish to help others and contribute to society, charity [msg #553].
7. **Security (безопасность)** — the drive for predictability and stability [msg #553].
8. **Tradition (традиционализм)** — love of rules and clear hierarchy, the wish to maintain traditions and standards [msg #553].

How they show up and how to identify them:
- As a rule, one motivator leads, two more are supplementary, and the rest are weakly expressed [msg #553].
- If you periodically analyse your behaviour and decisions, you may already have noticed what motivates you. For maximum accuracy, take the Hogan assessment or other verified tests; another option is to study yourself carefully in psychotherapy [msg #553].
- Use in teams: once you go deeper, you can readily identify the drivers of people on your team, therefore find out which reward will give each person the greatest satisfaction, and use this in building relationships [msg #553].

### Hogan derailers (11)
A follow-up by Leysan Shayakhmetova: after what motivates people, the dark sides of personality because of which one can hit a glass ceiling for years and wonder why one is not trusted with serious projects or a new role. Hogan called these dark sides derailers ("деструкторы"). He identified 11 characteristics, and anyone can try to diagnose them in themselves [msg #812]:
1. **Excitable (эмоциональный)** — mood swings confuse colleagues and destabilise team communication [msg #812].
2. **Diligent (прилежный)** — micromanages when much more significant things need attention [msg #812].
3. **Sceptical (скептичный)** — looks everywhere for a catch or hidden manipulation and does not trust people [msg #812].
4. **Cautious (осторожный)** — "measures" so many times that sometimes it is too late to "cut" (from the Russian proverb measure seven times, cut once) [msg #812].
5. **Reserved (сам в себе)** — under pressure becomes arrogant and distances themselves instead of solving the problem [msg #812].
6. **Leisurely (сам по себе)** — shows up as silent sabotage; dislikes spending time on explanations and compromises — will rather nod and then do it their own way [msg #812].
7. **Dutiful (исполненный сознания долга)** — always agrees with management's position and demonstrates loyalty without voicing their real view on work issues [msg #812].
8. **Bold (самоуверенный)** — does not weigh needed versus available resources, often starts with the thought "начнем, а потом разберемся" (let's start and figure it out later) [msg #812].
9. **Colorful (театральный)** — seeks to be the centre of attention even when they have nothing substantive to say [msg #812].
10. **Imaginative (с богатым воображением)** — problem-solving methods are excessively complex and costly where this is not needed [msg #812].
11. **Mischievous (увлекающийся)** — does the tasks that interest them at the expense of more important ones, always looking for new sensations and challenges [msg #812].

Caveat from the author: in some situations derailers are better not corrected. For example, dutiful and colorful can be career lifts if such behaviour matches the company's culture [msg #812].

### Slack hiring profile (4 qualities) and cultural fit
Stewart Butterfield, CEO of Slack, singled out four qualities for hiring; after he started looking for them in candidates, by his account the company culture worked much better than before. Each quality is defined against its misreading [msg #413]:
1. **Intelligence (УМ)** — not a high IQ (though it is a plus) but a predisposition to learning and constant growth [msg #413].
2. **Humility (СКРОМНОСТЬ)** — not submissiveness or lack of ambition, but an adequate self-assessment. Such humility increases self-awareness and the ability to learn and become smarter; it is the foundation for collaboration [msg #413].
3. **Diligence (ТРУДОЛЮБИЕ)** — not workaholism, but discipline, professionalism and focus at work. It can be complemented by competitiveness, decisiveness, persistence and firmness of character [msg #413].
4. **Collaboration (СОТРУДНИЧЕСТВО)** — not compliance or servility, but the skill of using the resources of the whole team; striving to organise the work process around oneself competently: building trust where it is missing, clarifying goals where they are unclear. When people can collaborate, the team gets the feeling that everyone shares responsibility for the common result [msg #413].

The author's verdict: he does not believe in universal recipes, but found this set workable enough to play with when creating your own cultural code or cultural fit — a model of selecting employees that takes into account what contribution they will make to the company culture. ### Five forecasts for the corporate world (2021)
Context: during a month in Moscow the author held about three in-person meetings a day with people who build the new economy and run their own or others' companies, trying to "attune to the field". His thesis for discussion: companies in classic sectors are starting to compete with IT companies, and we seem to be at the start of a cycle leading to a serious revision of business models and a major shift in mainstream corporate culture [msg #417].

Reasoning: wealth in society is now created by technology. Even if commodities boom on the exchange for 10 years, demand for them will be dictated by scarcity, not by the vector of social development, while technology will keep changing society. Tech companies work differently — most have already adopted a product approach and elements of agile management — and that is exactly where they solve at the level of strategy and business model the questions classic business leaves on the periphery [msg #417]:
- attracting the best specialists on the market;
- retaining them;
- their constant growth and development;
- creating a fundamentally new work culture: team-based, with fast and clear information exchange, non-toxic [msg #417].

IT companies attract every quality contractor in this new field (the author names his own firm as one), are gradually succeeding in creating a different work environment that also pays decently, and so become a staffing threat to any knowledge-work company. Example: this can break the familiar model of classic consulting, where years of climbing the ladder are part of the business model — a junior at a "conditional PwC" now has a real alternative of quickly retraining as a product manager and joining an ecosystem with far more comfortable conditions, no harassment and many learning and growth opportunities [msg #417].

Preliminary conclusions for the coming years [msg #417]:
1. Employees must feel great in the company — be genuinely loved and not harassed — and be given not so much perks ("плюшки") as strong tools for growth; otherwise they will leave for freelancing or for companies with a modern approach.
2. People in teams will learn to communicate very differently, and the average quality of communication will rise strongly.
3. The importance of corporate culture for a company's market success will grow many times over.
4. The role of HR will substantially strengthen.
5. A range of new professions will emerge and grow — in corporate-culture development, internal communication and employee satisfaction.

He closes that, as always in business, many companies will fail to grasp that the fundamental ways of doing business have changed, will not rebuild, and will die [msg #417].

### Three ways corporations slow career and growth
The author shares observations after a year in which his company hired and fired its historically largest number of people, including from leading large companies. He explicitly hedges: he is generalising and opening a dialogue, not writing from an ultimate expert position; it is not research, but "точно часть правды, которая может разниться от кейса к кейсу" (certainly part of the truth, which can differ from case to case) [msg #1266].
1. **Strong corporate brands on a CV often cloud people's objective view of themselves.** Working at well-known "cool" companies contributes substantially to ego-inflation and to inflated demands. Yet often (the author adds "not always!") behind that experience is mostly time spent in chaos, useless intrigues and a fairly inefficient environment where a separate team is created for every button and employees cannot work with an entrepreneurial mindset in the interests of the whole. In a startup or a genuinely excellent team such a person will perform poorly, but finds it hard to assess their skills objectively because the previous company constantly told them it employs the best people. Takeaway: do not be overly impressed by a brand for your career track; we become the best employees working with an excellent boss even in an average company, if that boss gives autonomy, supervises the process and helps develop mastery [msg #1266].
2. **Corporations and their culture do not teach people to take care of themselves — often the opposite.** Many famous companies' cultures rest on perfectionism that puts outdated, inefficient work patterns first. Employees work more than they are naturally capable of, experience a lot of stress and burn out. In many companies you are still looked at askance if you leave at 18:00, although this has no real relation to effectiveness. Such a culture knocks many people off their career track for years of recovery and prevents healthy work habits, reducing the capacity for continuous learning and creative solutions that matter today. Takeaway: in a BANI world it is critical for an employee to learn to regulate their state and build a culture that keeps them from dropping out of work; if a company does not genuinely help with this, it significantly affects the eventual peak of one's career [msg #1266].
3. **Corporations train loyalty and comfort instead of development.** A culture of performance and reviews seems to stimulate development, but on closer look it does not quite. Corporations invest heavily in employee loyalty and short-term results fixed in KPIs around which reviews are built: no matter the state of your team or how toxic a boss you are, there will be no dismissal if the KPI is met. They focus people on results while demanding loyalty to a toxic culture and ignoring wider consequences, and in exchange "bribe" employees with things unrelated to growth, personal mission and professional development — so loyalty is easily mistaken for what really ensures development. Loyalty benefits the company but never answers the truly important questions: who are you and what is your talent? how is a great result created? what consequences in life and career will this race bring? Takeaway: most corporations are not interested in the employee's real life, feelings and full potential, so employees must learn to remember themselves and understand what the business's real result is and how exactly they help achieve it [msg #1266].

Conclusion: corporations can certainly teach a lot and set up systems, but the author and his co-founder decided to no longer hire people with only corporate experience, and to look more carefully at famous brands on CVs [msg #1266].

## Detailed insights

### The hiring process as a "demo version" of the company (Gleb Kalinin's case)
The author quotes Gleb Kalinin, an ex-top manager of a corporation, under the headline that a company's human-centricity is the main competitive advantage of the future. Kalinin was approached by a large, well-known company (not for the first time) and agreed to talk although not actively searching, because the role combined a fairly unique mix of competencies — product work plus a certain public/media presence and interaction with many people — around a product at a "fuzzy" stage with nominally large freedom in choosing its direction [msg #202].

Despite that freedom, recruitment was standardised with no adaptation to the role. Over three interviews he was asked to solve combinatorics puzzles ("spiders, coins"), draw and describe web-service architectures and even write SQL queries ("I wrote them"); he expected to get to coding next and remarks it was odd they did not ask why manholes are round. He was never asked about people management, and there were no questions revealing him as a person, his particularities or strengths — "только галочки ставили в своих чеклистах" (they only ticked boxes in their checklists) [msg #202].

His lesson: hard times make people willing to go anywhere and adapt to any process, but "для меня процесс найма — это демо-версия подхода компании к человеку" (for me the hiring process is a demo version of the company's approach to a person). From this process, which he did not intend to complete, he became convinced the company was not for him: for it a person is a set of data points and functions. Such an approach probably allows scaling, but it clearly did not suit him; having left his previous job intending not to work for corporations again, this confirmed that decision [msg #202].

The channel author's commentary: even in hard times talented professionals refuse to work in traditional business that sees itself as a machine and people as functions; even in a crisis a company loses strong people if it does not match the spirit of the times, and this tendency will only strengthen because the crisis catalyses objective processes. The author says he fully agrees — he left the corporate world for the same reasons, and the archaic old management paradigm and employees' colossal unrealised potential motivated him to create deep mind [msg #202].

### Freelancing and the "internal product" (2021)
Russia entered the top ten countries by volume of the market of services performed by freelancers (a PwC analysis of global and Russian gig-economy and freelance trends is the cited source). The author expects the trend to continue and accelerate: a growing number of people want to leave the "matrix" of quasi-state/bureaucratic companies with toxic, health-damaging cultures, and few Russian companies can yet satisfy younger generations' value demands for flexibility, work-life balance, creativity and meaning at work [msg #430].

The freelance trend realises a "rich get richer, poor get poorer" principle: companies with the best conditions already attract the best people and are seriously concerned with employees' condition, continuous development and well-being. Many have recognised an internal product — "какой опыт покупают сами сотрудники" (what experience the employees themselves buy) when choosing to work at a company. From this come continuous-learning ecosystems, non-directive management approaches and nonviolent communication in teams. The author names the American recruiting service Toptal (mentioned in the analysis, with its remote-work playbook) as an advanced, "even somewhat cosmic for the Russian market", example to follow [msg #430].

The trend is double-edged: a freelancer is easier to "fire". But on the other side, global competition even in local markets and the further breakdown of barriers will favour freelancers — specifically those who can keep developing and raise the value of their hours. Such freelancers will take on a growing share of complex intellectual work and will become very demanding about the quality of management and communication directed at them [msg #430].

### Gen Z / Y demands and mature partnership
The author observes that people from generations Z and Y can frustrate their bosses with demands for ecological communication and adult treatment. His own example: a colleague under 25 (in his words, "весьма токсично" — quite toxically) accused him in a group chat of toxicity because he asked her boss to check private messages after having "ignored" her messages, which hurt her and she saw aggression in it. Many leaders complain that new-generation employees arrive with very clear requirements for how to interact with them but cannot reach visible results — quoted as: "Требуют много внимания и других ресурсов, но по сути не вносят полноценный вклад в то, чтобы машина ехала, а не просто была экологически безопасной." (They demand a lot of attention and other resources, but essentially do not fully contribute to making the car drive, not just be ecologically safe.) [msg #1103]

The author's position has two sides:
- **The trend is valid.** He considers ecological communication and rising psychological literacy a valid, natural evolutionary trend that helps companies and society move toward greater inclusivity and care, and so become healthier and richer together. He cites McKinsey: companies in the top quartile for gender and ethnic diversity are 25% more likely to outperform competitors financially [msg #1103].
- **Everything needs balancing.** Just as bosses in the developed world and sane business must free themselves from the "slave-owning echo of the past", many employees will have to grow up emotionally, freeing themselves from projections onto "bosses", "employer" or "company" as entities that by default owe them and by default oppress them — "otherwise the car won't go far" [msg #1103].

On boundaries: a boundary declared into the air that "the world owes me" can be a big achievement of growing up and an important stage of the therapeutic path if one previously struggled to stand up for one's rights or meet one's needs. But it does not happen in a vacuum: with other adults, unilaterally set boundaries and demands can become toxic if they ignore the other person and the context of the relationship — there is a living person on the other side. Adult relationships, which are loudly put on the flag, imply partnership, mutual respect and support [msg #1103].

The car metaphor: "В будущее возьмут всех, но не все почувствуют себя там уверенно и комфортно." (Everyone will be taken into the future, but not everyone will feel confident and comfortable there.) To be on tomorrow's renewed road one must genuinely become psychologically mature. Cars with too many toxic emissions will simply be banned from cities that value air quality, and much work remains against abuse of power, violence and immaturity in work communication — but if we deal only with the exhaust pipe while nobody can steer, refuel and service the car, it will never arrive [msg #1103].

What each side must learn:
- **Leaders** must learn to interact without paternalism with those they consider literally children (which the author notes is partly a discriminatory view), or whose behaviour looks infantile — either because it genuinely is maladaptive to life, or because of the leader's own immature interpretation of reality and misunderstanding of where life is heading [msg #1103].
- **New-generation employees** must see before them not bosses or a company that exist either to use them or to serve them, but living people with sometimes quite complex goals and circumstances, who hold their positions for a reason and also have needs and boundaries. Otherwise they will endlessly jump from job to job, thinking no company is good enough for them [msg #1103].

Bottom line: "Двигать надо к зрелому партнерству, а не к простой смене абьюзера." (We need to move toward mature partnership, not a simple change of abuser.) [msg #1103]

### Candidates now ask about values first (guest post, 2024)
Serial founder Alexander Likhtman (founder of the ITCOMMS agency, ex-founder of agency 2l, exited a month before the post) reports that until about a year earlier, candidates' main questions were about process — what we do, how processes are built, how KPIs are set. In the summer of 2024 almost all candidates first asked about the company's values, atmosphere and internal communication [msg #1130].

He rejects the easy "Gen Z has arrived" explanation: probably partly, but he sees a normal, natural transformation of the labour market. What matters more to people is what kind of company you are, how and by what it lives, rather than specific tasks and the motivation/punishment system. The trend existed for years and was linked not only to new generations (he cites a Forbes piece on the "great exodus" of people management did not plan to fire). It was hard but possible not to see it, and easy to ignore or attribute to special moods among the young — which, he says, many leaders did and keep doing en masse and even gloatingly. He is glad he moved, from 2017, into coaching and self-organisation when it was "strange"; by introducing that paradigm into management and leadership his team can now work the way the market is starting to want [msg #1130].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The GM/Toyota account and the claim that research contradicts managers' belief in external motivation are given without naming the studies [msg #74].
- The Slack qualities are offered with the author's own hedge that he does not believe in universal recipes [msg #413].
- The forecasts in #417 are explicitly "preliminary conclusions" from conversations, and #1266 is explicitly not research but a generalisation meant to start a dialogue [msg #417, #1266].
- Derailers are not always to be fixed: dutiful and colorful can help a career where the culture rewards them [msg #812].
- Possible tension: the Hogan motivators include security, tradition and commerce (external/structural drivers) as legitimate, while #854 ranks fully external motivation as not supporting well-being or growth. (!) UNCLEAR: the source does not reconcile the two models [msg #553, #854].
- The author's commercial interest: he notes that IT companies hire contractors in this new field "for example us" and that his dissatisfaction with corporate management motivated creating deep mind [msg #417, #202].
- The freelancing trend is described as double-edged (freelancers are easier to dismiss), with the benefit going only to freelancers who keep developing [msg #430].
