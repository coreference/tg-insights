# Relating to the future: images of the future, future self and mature progress

## What this covers

Three posts by the channel author on how we relate to the future. The first diagnoses why the future has started to feel darker, argues (via Fred Polak) that a positive image of the future drives a culture's flourishing, and offers Polak's four-attitude matrix plus Bill Sharpe's Three Horizons as ways to move from collapse to agency [msg #1905]. The second introduces the Consilience Project essay *Development in Progress* and four questions for distinguishing mature from immature progress in personal, team and product decisions [msg #1973]. The third describes the MIT Media Lab "Future You" experiment, in which talking to an AI-aged version of oneself lowered anxiety and raised motivation, and explains why it works (Hershfield's future-self research) [msg #1975]. According to the author, the stakes are high: losing the positive image of the future is "harmful for us", and the habitual responses (more work, more forecasting) do not fix it [msg #1905].

## Core ideas

- **The darkening of the future is real and widespread, not just a bubble feeling.** A paradigm in which the future was positive and improved step by step is "rapidly deflating" because of wars, failures of political leadership and the unpredictability of AI; a 2025 survey confirms broad pessimism [msg #1905].
- **The image of the future is an engine, not a by-product.** Following Polak, the author argues that we do not only dream because we live well — we live well as long as we are able to dream [msg #1905].
- **Anxiety about the future is a symptom to be read, not just suppressed.** Per the Three Horizons model it signals that the familiar world is losing fitness while the new one has not formed; the trap is technical fixes where adaptive ones are needed [msg #1905].
- **The goal is a shift of stance, from collapse to agency** — "I don't know how it ends, and I still do my part" — where anxiety becomes a support rather than a paralysis [msg #1905, img #1905].
- **Progress should be judged by its effect on the whole system**, not by isolated metrics of capability and efficiency; we need more wisdom in using science and technology, not less of them [msg #1973].
- **The brain treats the future self almost like a stranger**, which is why we sacrifice our future; making the future self concrete and warm (e.g. via an AI conversation) creates an "anchor" that reduces stress and improves future-oriented behaviour [msg #1975].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Polak's four attitudes toward the future (2x2 matrix)
Purpose: a diagnostic picture for noticing one's own stance toward the future and seeing where to move. The card is titled «Четыре отношения к будущему» (*Four attitudes toward the future*), credited to Fred Polak, *The Image of the Future*, 1955, and designed for the author's Resonance 5 "Emerging Future" programme [img #1905].

Axes [img #1905]:
- Vertical axis: top = «МИР СТАНЕТ ЛУЧШЕ» (*the world will get better*), bottom = «МИР СТАНЕТ ХУЖЕ» (*the world will get worse*).
- Horizontal axis: left = *it doesn't depend on me* (vertical label on the card), right = «я влияю» (*I influence it*).

The four quadrants [img #1905]:
1. **Ожидание (Waiting)** — top-left: world gets better, it doesn't depend on me. Signature belief: «Технологии все вырулят, рынок поправится.» (*Technology will sort everything out, the market will recover.*) and «Надо просто дождаться.» (*We just have to wait.*). Its tag: «НАДЕЖДА БЕЗ УЧАСТИЯ» (*hope without participation*) — optimism that does not involve one's own action.
2. **Прогресс (Progress)** — top-right: world gets better, I influence it. Signature belief: "Step by step we make the world better — and it works." Tagged «ЗДЕСЬ МЫ ЖИЛИ» (*this is where we lived*) and with the domains «КАРЬЕРЫ, ЭКОНОМИКА, ВАКЦИНЫ» (*careers, economy, vaccines*). This corresponds to the positive paradigm that the author says dominated for most (very privileged) people until recently [msg #1905].
3. **Схлопывание (Collapse)** — bottom-left: world gets worse, it doesn't depend on me. Signature belief: «Всё катится, и от меня всё равно ничего» … «не зависит.» (*Everything is going downhill, and nothing depends on me anyway.*). Tagged «КУДА СДУЛО» (*where we got blown to*) — i.e. where the deflating paradigm has pushed people — and «НИ ТРЕВОГИ, НИ ЖИЗНИ» (*neither anxiety nor life*): a numbed state.
4. **Субъектность (Agency / subjecthood)** — bottom-right: world may get worse, but I influence it. Signature belief: «Не знаю, чем всё кончится. И всё равно» … «делаю своё.» (*I don't know how it will all end. And I still do my part.*). Tagged «ХОД СЮДА» (*move here*) and «ТРЕВОГА, СТАВШАЯ ОПОРОЙ» (*anxiety that has become a support*).

How to use it: the author's invitation is to notice your own attitude and "stay a while with the picture" — which corner are you really in now, and how useful is it for you? [msg #1905]. The recommended movement is from destructive collapse to the point where anxiety becomes a support: "there I don't know how everything will end, and I still influence what happens in my life" [msg #1905]. Note that the target quadrant does not require believing the world will get better — it pairs an uncertain or darker outlook with personal influence [img #1905]. The card's footer repeats Polak's thesis: «Подъём и упадок образов будущего предшествует подъёму и упадку культур» (*The rise and fall of images of the future precedes the rise and fall of cultures*) [img #1905].

### Scharmer's three symptoms of destructive collapse
In his March text *Holding the Gaze Steady*, Otto Scharmer named three symptoms of the destructive collapse that follows losing the image of the future [msg #1905]:
1. **anomie** — «распад нормы, «все можно»» (*breakdown of norms, "anything goes"*).
2. **atomie** — «распад связи, одиночество» (*breakdown of connection, loneliness*).
3. **atrophy** — «распад способности воображать и создавать» (*breakdown of the capacity to imagine and create*).

Evidence for atrophy offered by the author: Elise Boulding, Polak's translator, spent her last 20 years running workshops in various countries in which she asked people to imagine a world where everything is good — and time after time found that people simply cannot do it. «Мышца атрофировалась.» (*The muscle has atrophied.*) [msg #1905].

### Three Horizons model (Bill Sharpe)
As presented by the author [msg #1905]:
- **What anxiety means:** anxiety here is a signal that the familiar world is losing its fitness/usability while the new one has not yet taken shape.
- **The main trap:** «суетливые технические решения, которые лечат симптом и укрепляют старое там, где нужны решения адаптивные» (*fussy technical solutions that treat the symptom and reinforce the old where adaptive solutions are needed*).
- **Implication:** we are unlikely to fix the situation through more obsessive habitual work or forecasting — these are repetitions of the old, sometimes more intense, and increasingly less useful. What may matter more is working on the adaptation itself — on the very perspective from which we see the world and our role in its development.

### Future You — a conversation with your AI future self (MIT Media Lab)
Purpose: use the technological environment itself to restore contact with oneself, reduce stress and strengthen connection to one's future [msg #1975].

Procedure as described [msg #1975]:
1. **Describe yourself.** Tell the system about your life, values and goals. This step already has value on its own: before the conversation you must articulate what matters to you and where you are heading — something "many of us don't formulate for years". The author calls preparing the "future version of yourself" a practice of developing awareness in the broad sense and of conative intelligence, which helps formulate intentions.
2. **Upload a selfie.** The AI ages the photo.
3. **The AI builds the future self.** It assembles "memories" and becomes you at age 60.
4. **Talk to your future self.** In the study, conversations lasted 10–30 minutes.

Results (344 participants): anxiety decreased; motivation increased; a stronger connection with the future was established [msg #1975].

Why it works [msg #1975]:
- It is hard for us to grasp that in the future we will still be ourselves. Researchers comparing brain activity found that when we think about ourselves in 10 years, the brain reacts almost as it does to thoughts about another person.
- Therefore today's version easily sacrifices sleep, health and money that will affect our own future: the brain literally perceives the future self as a stranger, and caring for a stranger is hard. The Future You conversation "makes the future one's own".
- A concrete and warm image of oneself 20–30 years ahead gives an **anchor** (Hershfield's own term). It lets a person bear present difficulties more steadily: looked at from the distant future, today's stress weakens and it becomes visible that "this will pass".

The tool is now available beyond study participants as a free project from the team, where the whole process can be completed; its start screen reads "Say Hello to Your Future Self — Engage with your virtual self to nurture personal growth and human flourishing" (co-branded with KBTG) [msg #1975, img #1975]. The author intends to try it and invites readers to report how the AI practice affects them, to check the study's findings [msg #1975].

### Four questions for mature progress (Development in Progress)
Context: we habitually equate progress with growth in capability, efficiency and productivity — in technology but also in organisational, social and even personal development. The essay asks: what if, improving individual indicators, we simultaneously worsen the state of the system as a whole? What distinguishes mature from immature progress, and why do some achievements, while solving some problems, generate others? [msg #1973]. Example given: social networks simplified communication, but optimising platforms for engagement created incentives for an endless fight for our attention — possibly because we define success too narrowly [msg #1973].

Use: a new way to evaluate one's own decisions — from organising daily life to managing a team or developing a product; helps notice non-obvious consequences, distinguish real improvements from pretty metrics, and critically assess whether a chosen solution actually brings us closer to the desired result [msg #1973]. The questions you can use right away [msg #1973]:
1. **What exactly are we trying to improve?** Have we substituted the real need with a convenient measurable indicator?
2. **Can we eliminate the cause of the problem** rather than create yet another tool for fighting its symptoms?
3. **What new problems might our solution generate?** Who will gain the advantages, who will bear the costs, and might a disproportionate concentration of power arise?
4. **How will our solution change human experience?** What will there be more or less of in people's lives? Where will it direct their attention, and what will it leave outside it?

## Detailed insights

### The darkening future: personal feeling mirrors the social trend
The author's team at Resonance discussed how the future had "suddenly" become darker than before. For most of them (acknowledged as "very privileged"), the dominant paradigm had been a positive future in which we made things better step by step; due to wars, failures of political leadership and AI's unpredictability this paradigm is rapidly deflating [msg #1905]. Checking whether this was only a bubble feeling, he found that "through the personal we sense the social well": a large 2025 Pew study across 36 countries found 57% believe their children will live worse than their parents, versus 34% expecting improvement; in Germany the share of pessimists rose over the last 5 years from 42% to 61% [msg #1905]. He then steps back to look at the anxiety itself as a symptom: what it says about where we already are, and how constructive the current emotional response is [msg #1905].

### Polak's thesis: losing a positive image ahead, we lose
In 1955 sociologist Fred Polak wrote *The Image of the Future*, with the thesis that the rise and fall of images of the future precedes the rise and fall of cultures. While hope lives in a family, company or society, culture flourishes; once it is lost, forward movement also deflates [msg #1905]. The author's conclusion: «Мы не только потому мечтаем, что живем хорошо. Мы и живем хорошо, пока способны мечтать.» (*We don't dream only because we live well. We also live well as long as we are able to dream.*) [msg #1905].

### The author's own shift: from anxiety to a sense of power
At a spring meeting with Otto Scharmer (whose methods Resonance is built on), the author experienced an inner shift: a sense of power arose out of anxiety — not thanks to a talented speaker's motivational speech but because of a change in the structure of attention. Scharmer seemed to embody a position in which the future not only *happens* to us but also *unfolds* through us: we ourselves, our choices and actions, largely determine where we will be in 10 or 20 years [msg #1905]. The author's response to growing pessimism was to make the programme thematic, devoting more time to the "Emerging Future" theme and practices for feeling one's position of power and authorship toward the future — in career, parenting, emigration and politics [msg #1905].

### Technology as a means of restoring contact
The author's broader question: in an era of accelerating cognitive load and overload, and general uncertainty that produces many ruptures — including internal ones, as constant stress and loss of bodily sensitivity — how can the technological environment itself be used to restore contact with oneself and with other people? He found Future You while preparing a talk on cyborg psychology, a new MIT direction studying the mutual influence of AI on people and vice versa; the experiment shows constructive rather than negative capacities of AI in overcoming inner ruptures and lowering stress [msg #1975].

### What the Development in Progress essay is and isn't
It is a long essay by The Consilience Project / Daniel Schmachtenberger (with the Civilization Research Institute), a deep reflection on the history of progress and the narratives and ideology around it, offering a fresh view through the lens of maturity [msg #1973]. The authors do not leave the reader feeling helpless: they give examples of mature progress and practical approaches [msg #1973]. It does not call for abandoning technology: the main take is that we need not less scientific and technological progress but more wisdom in how we manage it. It is a large-scale, debatable study at the intersection of philosophy, ecology, economics, history of technology and complex-systems theory, with 236 notes and references [msg #1973]. The author and translator consider its questions exceptionally important for our time, especially for those who manage large companies and big processes and drive change [msg #1973].

## Evidence and numbers

- Hal Hershfield's experiments (he co-authored Future You): people who saw their own aged face saved roughly twice as much for retirement [msg #1975].
- Brain-activity comparison cited by the author: thinking about oneself in 10 years produces a response almost like thinking about another person [msg #1975].
