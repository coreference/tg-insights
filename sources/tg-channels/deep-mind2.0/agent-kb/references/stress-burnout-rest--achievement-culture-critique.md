# Critique of achievement and productivity culture

## What this covers

This file collects the deep mind author's multi-year critique of "hustle" and productivity culture: the grind-for-success narrative, the belief that failure is always personal, Byung-Chul Han's analysis of the self-exploiting achievement society, techno-optimism and endless self-optimization as denials of human limitation, the "crisis of achievism" among Russia's urban efficient class, statistics on how many people cannot sustain "life as a project", the fragility hustle culture produces, the alternative of achieving from flourishing rather than trauma, the post-productivity transitions, and why slowing down is hard. The author, a founder who has himself worked in 18-hour-a-day environments, writes this not as an anti-business stance but because he regularly sees "successful" people suffering at the edges of the optimization myth, and because the dominant culture "во-многом противоречащей естественным нормам физиологии, биологии и психологии человека" (largely contradicts the natural norms of human physiology, biology and psychology) [msg #1764, #1768].

This part adds two personal essays to the topic. In the first [msg #1896], written in the mountains at the end of a vacation, the author retells a Gabor Maté reel about the ending of *Winnie-the-Pooh* and draws a lesson: we give up play and spontaneity to meet an unconscious need to prove we have a right to exist. In the second [msg #1967], "Я работаю 4 часа в день" (I work 4 hours a day), the author compares his old 16-hour days with his current 4-hour days. He argues that the number of hours says almost nothing about a person. What matters is whether you can stay in contact with the anxiety that drives optimization. He also argues that you have to choose without any guarantee [msg #1967].

How this relates to the existing file: these posts **extend** the section *Achieving from trauma vs achieving from flourishing and presence* with a first-person case and with Maté's testimony. They also **add a self-critique the existing file lacks**. The existing file mainly critiques hustle and achievism and recommends slowing down, rest, inner work and presence. Post #1967 warns that self-care culture and "working less" can turn into a new morality of the same productivity imperative, and that the hours a person works are not a moral indicator in either direction. That is a refinement, not a contradiction. The posts also add a new theme: optimization as a modern ritual that serves an ancient hope for safety, together with finitude as what gives choices their weight. This fits with the existing *Endless optimization as denial of finitude* section [msg #1896, #1967].

## Core ideas

- **The grind narrative is torn out of life's context and toxic.** Short bursts of effort can be legitimate, but scaling them to the whole process leads to people who feel sick at their task lists, years of inability to work, and projects that launch with hype and close within a year [msg #912].
- **Failure is not always a personal fault.** The "you didn't try hard enough" idea ignores power disparities and produces "narcissistic madness"; healthy self-assessment requires both agency and understanding of historical and social context [msg #1115].
- **Han: we exploit ourselves.** The disciplinary society has given way to the achievement society, where violence takes the soft form of positive coercion and burnout and depression are symptoms of the system, not personal failings [msg #1330].
- **Outer progress without inner development fails.** Technology and optimization cannot solve problems generated by an undeveloped mind; endless optimization is a denial of finitude [msg #1348, #1694].
- **Achievism is in crisis.** The urban efficient class's contract — invest in yourself, work efficiently, and life will improve — is breaking under economic stagnation, war, repression and AI [msg #1708].
- **The productivity model fits only a few.** By a conservative estimate 40–60% of ambitious educated people have traits making long-term high intensity unsustainable; burnout is allostatic load, not weak character [msg #1710].
- **Hustle culture breeds psychological fragility** by reducing reality to material things and equating happiness with intense pleasure [msg #1764].
- **One can achieve from trauma or from flourishing.** Trauma-driven, critic-driven achievement is not optimal; access to Self/presence requires inner work joined to the career path [msg #1765].
- **Slowing down is hard and needs support** because the nervous system works like compound interest and we are fleeing our own feelings [msg #1804].

- **We trade play for proof of the right to exist.** We give up play and spontaneity to meet an unconscious need for confirmation that we have a right to exist. That need comes from a deep feeling that something is wrong with us, which the author attributes to our traumas. Understanding this does not always help [msg #1896].
- **Hours say almost nothing.** Twelve "effective" hours and four gentle hours can each come either from fear or from freedom [msg #1967].
- **Both cultures can become traps.** Achievement culture burns people out. Self-care culture, its counterpart, easily becomes a new morality. Either one becomes a trap the moment it serves as proof that you are okay, or as a way to manage the basic anxiety of life's uncertainty [msg #1967].
- **Stay with the tension instead of numbing it.** The more mature skill is to stay in contact with the tension that pushes us to optimize, and to look inside it rather than numb it with work or with rest [msg #1967].
- **Optimization is a modern ritual.** Behind it lies an ancient hope that doing things right will protect us from disaster, but neither gods nor spreadsheets give guarantees [msg #1967].
- **Freedom means choosing without a guarantee.** Freedom is the ability to choose without any guarantee that you are living correctly. Finitude gives choices their weight [msg #1967].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Byung-Chul Han's achievement society (as presented by the author)
Purpose: to explain why fatigue and burnout have become epidemic in nominally free societies. The author summarizes Han's short book *The Burnout Society* (Общество усталости) as an analysis of the neurotic culture around the cult of success, which insists on endless productivity, self-development and efficiency. In such a "society of hyperactivity", fatigue is not a signal to stop but a weakness to overcome; the author notes the growing number of stress-reduction practices, antioxidant IV drips and cryochambers "лишь бы не почувствовать себя плохо в вечной гонке" (just so as not to feel bad in the eternal race) [msg #1330].

1. **The invisible key mechanism: internalization.** In democratic societies people are less and less forced to obey external authorities, but they internalize cultural demands for success. The person becomes their own overseer in constant forward motion. In authoritarian and totalitarian societies external coercion remains, but this trend penetrates there too [msg #1330].
2. **From disciplinary society to achievement society.** Drawing on Arendt, Foucault and others, Han describes a shift from a *society of discipline* — where control was external (repression, prohibitions, rigid hierarchies) — to an *achievement society*. The person is not so much suppressed by the system as suppressing themselves. Instead of submitting to a strict boss, we set goals and KPIs, build personal brands and constantly "level up" ourselves. "Больше не нужны тюремные стены – мы бежим по бесконечному кругу добровольно" (Prison walls are no longer needed — we run the endless circle voluntarily) [msg #1330].
3. **Fatigue as epidemic.** Behind this supposedly free choice lies deep exhaustion. The need to be productive, positive, creative and competitive leads to new forms of illness; self-exploitation shows up as the diseases of our century: burnout, depression, ADHD, chronic fatigue and anxiety [msg #1330].
4. **Violence through positivity.** Han's key thesis: modern violence is no longer explicit — no direct repression, prohibitions or forcible suppression. It has become soft and takes the form of positive coercion: "Нам больше не говорят, что делать – мы сами убеждены, что должны быть успешными, энергичными и проактивными" (We are no longer told what to do — we ourselves are convinced we must be successful, energetic and proactive). That is why depression and burnout are the main diseases of our time: not merely personal difficulties but a symptom of a system in which a person must endlessly work, improve and is *obliged* to be happy, yet ends up exhausted and dissatisfied [msg #1330].
5. **Why an excess of positivity destroys.** The achievement society puts personal happiness and the success that should bring it on a pedestal. Everything must work to increase the sum — do more, rest usefully, get everything done. This logic avoids everything negative — illness, weakness, fatigue, low mood — and that is precisely what makes us vulnerable. Negative experience is not a problem to be solved by acquiring more resources, consuming, or working through all one's traumas; it is a natural part of life. Meeting limits, accepting weaknesses and becoming aware of fatigue enable meaningful recovery. In an "intoxication of positivity" — a constant attempt to escape inefficiency — we lose depth and become unable to truly feel life [msg #1330].
6. **What to do.** Han does not propose fleeing to the woods or giving up ambition, but insists it is time to accept that not everything in the world must be productive: fatigue is not an enemy but a signal; rest is not a pause in work but a full part of life. The author lists three responses:
   - Notice, reflect on and refuse imposed positivism — it is not at all necessary to achieve happiness and success, constantly become better and be "in resource" 24/7.
   - Accept fatigue not as weakness but as a natural state that requires care.
   - Reconsider your relationship to work — work should not swallow all of life, and productivity, money and status should not be the measure of a person's worth [msg #1330].
7. **Nature of the book.** The author stresses it is not popular science but a conceptual philosophical reflection on freedom — here, the freedom to leave the endless race of achievement, dare to be imperfect and allow yourself simply to be; "an ode to contemplation and a simple way of life". The attached page from the book quotes Nietzsche: "Благодаря недостатку покоя наша цивилизация переходит в новое варварство. Никогда деятельные, т. е. беспокойные, не имели большего влияния, чем теперь." (Due to the lack of rest our civilization is passing into a new barbarism. Never have the active, i.e. the restless, had more influence than now.) The page states that even Nietzsche, who replaced being with will, knew that human life ends in deadly hyperactivity if the contemplative element is completely expelled, and that among the necessary corrections to humanity's character is a strengthening "в очень большой мере созерцательного элемента" (to a very great degree of the contemplative element) [msg #1330] [img #1330].

### The pushed vs walled-up door test (agency vs systemic constraints)
Purpose: to assess one's results soberly, distinguishing what feedback and effort can fix from what is structurally blocked [msg #1115].
- **Context — the idea being examined.** In entrepreneurship one often hears that if it didn't work out, you did something wrong: dug in the wrong place, made bad decisions, missed something, didn't try hard enough. The author grants this idea an important evolutionary role: it separates the conformist person who relies on gods, stars or bosses from the person who acts for themselves; this sense of one's own power and agency largely produced the successful modern civilizations that took responsibility for how their life looks [msg #1115].
- **The flaw.** Success and failure are multilayered phenomena influenced by internal and external factors; ideas fragment and simplify reality. This one ignores power disparities in modern society and creates "narcissistic madness" — the belief that everything is available to you, so if you don't get what you want, you are somehow wrong or did not do enough. When we hit systemic limits, instead of working with them we turn the blame on ourselves and feel weak [msg #1115].
- **Pushed door:** if I push a door and can't enter because it opens toward me, I really am doing something wrong — feedback will help change the situation [msg #1115].
- **Walled-up door:** however much I pull and push a bricked-up door, I won't get through. Women, LGBTQ+ people, national minorities and other discriminated communities did not do something "wrong" to get fewer opportunities; that is the power structure, and therefore that is where effort must be applied. If you are not embedded in a particular power pyramid, the door will be walled up; to open it, it is not enough to learn to push and pull skillfully — you must also work with the walled part and with those who walled it [msg #1115].
- **Deeper layer — whose success?** Citing social psychology (Lisa Barrett's work), the author says our perception of success and failure is deeply rooted in social and cultural expectations; the very notion of success is usually not an independent personal idea but distorted through the prism of dominant systems. So one can try hard "in the wrong direction" all one's life — a direction that won't bring satisfaction because it was imposed by those for whom it is easier or simply profitable [msg #1115].
- **Conclusion.** Freedom and a happy life depend not only on how skillfully and hard we work and develop, but on which large systems affect us and how. Awareness of this influence and working with large systems makes the path easier, because it clarifies which roles — with their privileges and limits — we live in this historical era and geographic location, and why it can be so hard. The author says attention to systems in his own therapy returns strength and inspires him: he sees how much of the sense of weakness and powerlessness is imposed, becomes more confident in his path, and the understanding that many barriers are systemic develops his empathy, compassion and activist stance, especially toward marginalized groups. He also acknowledges his own growth points: "не все зависит только от наших усилий" (not everything depends only on our efforts) [msg #1115].

### Three automatic reactions to overload and uncertainty
Purpose: to recognize the nervous system's automatic reactions to danger and pain in periods of decision overload, which the author says "make things only worse" — he checked them on himself and "результаты там так себе" (the results are so-so) [msg #1762].
Context: the author describes being badly overloaded by logistical uncertainty — trying to plan projects in two countries while in a third, months ahead, which takes up "all the RAM" and leaves no room for many other important decisions that don't go away. Many interlinked configurations get stuck in body and mind, and risks multiply "like midges"; sometimes it feels you'll go mad [msg #1762].
1. **Roll up your sleeves and try to out-grind everything** ("засучить рукава и попытаться перехерачить все на свете"). A typical "fight" stress response that fairly quickly leads to fatigue and lower decision quality. With simple activities and contexts it can be fine, but as soon as things get more complex, effectiveness drops sharply, and in the long run we burn out [msg #1762].
2. **Ignore reality's demands.** Also a typical reactive response, closer to freezing or fleeing. In the author's case it often turns one problem into two: shut your eyes for a couple of days and you have to not only renew documents but also find new plane tickets [msg #1762].
3. **Reduce existing complexity by rolling back to simpler versions of reality.** Make a radical decision that seems to let you jump out of the circumstances — e.g. go back to your home country after a year or two of being unable to choose among three new relocation countries. Neurobiologically this is a separate mechanism, the search for cognitive closure: under load the prefrontal cortex, which can hold several scenarios at once, switches off, and the limbic system starts perceiving uncertainty itself as a threat — so any definite outcome becomes preferable to an uncertain one, even if objectively worse. This is the only one of the three that "mathematically" can be right — sometimes complexity really is excessive and radical simplification is adequate prioritization. But it can also be simple flight from feelings leading to terrible results: if we act from inability to bear the tension of current complexity, we throw important meanings and tasks out of the deck. This has consequences, because life most likely became complex for a reason, and part of that complexity carries significant contexts that must be considered [msg #1762].
- **Why achievers' advice fails here.** The author falls into these reactions himself, because uncertainty and multiple contexts are "очень тонкого льда" (very thin ice): precisely when nervous-system regulation is most needed to make choices that don't worsen complexity, it is easiest to lose. "Achievers" advise prioritizing and holding hard boundaries — time for deciding and working, then evenings and weekends off. But they simplify and ignore the nervous system: with 100 decisions hanging inside you, it is literally impossible to switch off, yet often also impossible to make tangible progress — and we get stuck in anxiety, fear, anger or stress generally, unable to rest or hold boundaries [msg #1762].
- **Rule.** What becomes absolutely critical is not a *productivity system* but *counterintuitive self-regulation* that breaks the vicious circle. Only once regulated can one talk about systems, priorities and boundaries. ### Two processes of productivity-driven psychological fragility
Purpose: to explain how the striving to be unshakably strong and to optimize everything, including oneself, creates a modern kind of psychological fragility. The author announces two key processes [msg #1764].
1. **The belief in the god of productivity narrows reality to its material manifestations.** In this view there seems to be no choice but to believe happiness is achieved exclusively through material objects, since nothing else seems significant. Happiness is taken not as calm fullness and wholeness but as intense pleasure — always tasty food, only pleasant people around, no illness, an eternally good social position, energy always available. The holder of this view stakes everything on one aspect of reality, reinforcing a vicious circle of investing in what leads further away from reducing suffering, let alone sustainable subjective well-being. Example: "Купил новую квартиру, но тревога никуда не ушла" (Bought a new apartment, but the anxiety didn't go anywhere) — one would need to work with one's own mind, but there is no faith in that, since everything is determined by the material, so one must roll up the sleeves and earn a house on the hill [msg #1764].

### Achieving from trauma vs achieving from flourishing and presence
Purpose: a "constructive alternative" closing the series — a direction for the clearly significant social request of how to grow, develop and achieve not from pain, or how not to make one's business and career serve one's neurosis. The author says he sees such requests regularly in his Resonance process, where the need is strong for paths that move a person forward not by fear but by curiosity, generosity and even love. He calls the core fact consensual today both in therapeutic science and in new systemic approaches such as Theory U [msg #1765]. The attached image contrasts "Развитие из травмы" (development from trauma) and "Развитие из процветания" (development from flourishing) [img #1765].
1. **Path one — from trauma/deficit, driven by critical parts.** Working with critical parts in IFS (Internal Family Systems), the author notes that these parts are literally responsible for this first, habitual path. Everyone has critical parts, but in post-Soviet society they still occupy such an influential place that even some psychotherapists believe their idea that if nobody inside kicks us, we will sprawl on the couch and do nothing useful. This is a deep rut of worldview and action from trauma/deficit: it must be scary, painful or otherwise uncomfortable for us to act, and then we think that is an effective strategy. In corporate workshops on trauma, almost always someone asks: "Но ведь травма заставляет нас двигаться вперед, может быть она нужна?" (But trauma makes us move forward — maybe it's needed?) [msg #1765].
2. **The answer.** The neurobiological and therapeutic answer is fairly unambiguous: trauma is a normal way of coping with abnormal events. It has a helping function, but it reduces the amount of resource in the system and makes the person's path harder — "это не оптимальная стратегия" (it is not an optimal strategy). Example: you build a business in daily anxiety that nothing is working or that there still isn't enough money, stability or reputation; critics drive you daily, saying more is needed and everything is bad. Critics can produce actions, and actions can bear fruit — but that is a very narrow part of the truth: you don't know what you would have built, and how fast, if you felt there was enough of everything and you wanted to share it with the world rather than constantly closing a deficit [msg #1765].
3. **What science documents (per the author).** It is well documented that unprocessed trauma and the accompanying critical parts rather slow the unfolding of potential. Under the critics we sometimes move, but from pain, spending more resources along the way, and above all without access to what IFS calls **Self** — hard to explain quickly, but colloquially a state of maximal openness to the full diversity of data, perspectives and ways of acting; a healing state of natural wholeness and resource, in which "the impossible becomes available", as Steven Kotler shows in his research on the neurobiology of productivity [msg #1765].
4. **Path two — from flourishing and presence.** Psychotherapists clearly distinguish: one can achieve from *trauma*, as most people do, or from *flourishing and presence*, an existing, repeatedly described phenomenon: Self in IFS, Presencing in Theory U, Organismic self in Rogers's humanistic psychology [msg #1765].
5. **How to get onto the second path.** It means a simple but usually far from easy given: the material path — business, entrepreneurship, career — must be united with the path of inner work. That work is necessary to clear access to Self and to build very concrete, not magical, skills of presence. They are marginalized and pushed out by the mainstream, but that does not make them unreal or less significant. Taking this path lets one avoid most of the cultural diseases described in the series [msg #1765].

### Two post-achievism inner transitions
Purpose: orientation toward "seeds of the culture of the future" after the critique. The author cites that postconventional levels of development are observed, by the most conservative estimates, in 10% of the population (samples mostly from the US and Western Europe), with another 15–25% in developed countries approaching them — so in 25–35% of the population an exit from the productivity cult is happening or has happened. People begin to build themselves not around "how?" but around "for whom?" and "why does it matter?"; the center of gravity shifts from result to relationships and meaning [msg #1768]. ### The double trap of achievement culture and self-care culture

### The grind narrative from the inside (a founder's view)
The author responds to yet another critique of work-life balance built on the premise that you must grind if you want success, and that while you look for balance, competitors win faster. He sees in this trend an evolution of ways of ignoring one's state: where alcohol used to suppress pain, now "чтобы терпеть ад, нужна терапия и поддерживающий партнер" (to endure hell you need therapy and a supportive partner) [msg #912].
He describes his own split reaction: reading as a founder, feeling experienced and strong, he agrees — yes, I work long and hard and then see results. He identifies this as the response of an inner part "которая пытается обрести безопасность за счет успеха, и из страха запихивает другие голоса подальше, считая их слабыми" (which tries to gain safety through success and, out of fear, shoves other voices away, considering them weak). Then he steps onto the terrace, looks at the mountains, and feels as if he has walked away from a fortune-teller — as if drugged and caught in the dominant narrative "вижу цель — не вижу препятствий" (I see the goal, I don't see obstacles), through which we collectively get depressing results while behind the facade of successful people tears flow from pain or not knowing oneself [msg #912].
The bus metaphor: when we must jump on the last bus on the move, it is a chance of the present moment — make the effort or not; sometimes you have to strain. But why scale that action to the whole process? What bus is it, where does it go, will I really get where I need, or with traffic jams and crashes is it better to walk, thinking in the sun along the way? [msg #912].
The hidden costs: working with "the underside of the process", the author sees how toxic the narrative is. People grind for months or years, see many others doing the same and consider it normal, but don't see how many later feel physically sick looking at their task list, or can't work at all for years, letting down themselves and their families. Living in this narrative we prefer not to see scientific data which, per the author, say "тело требует 47% времени в сутках отдыхать" (the body requires 47% of the day's time for rest). Ignoring reality for years, we later collapse — with cancer, among other things — down to the foundation of all previous achievements and are left with zero; whether we had therapeutic support and a warm partner along the way won't matter to anyone [msg #912].
Results of grinding: "Хуячить может каждый" (Anyone can grind) — yet looking closely, we see projects that take off with pomp and hype using their historical chance, then close a year later. Making effort is habitual; building something truly sustainable that works for years in constantly changing circumstances is still something only a few can do [msg #912].
The wider-context questions: does all this create meaning for people around, or is our success only about ourselves? Do our efforts in the race benefit colleagues, families, the homeless, animals? Will they bring closer the fall of the regime? The author insists these questions must be addressed now, not once we become successful and can finally influence them — otherwise it is ordinary childish irresponsibility while considering ourselves the smartest [msg #912].
His reframe: he tries not to learn to support himself better in grinding, but to understand why grind at all — is his success needed by the world, or would everyone be better off if he became a more pleasant and compassionate person, able to get results without violence against himself and others? That alone sounds like the loudest success for him, even if the company never becomes worth a billion. And if success really is needed, can we achieve it not faster than competitors, but in partnership with them, or at worst through smarter actions than theirs? [msg #912].

### Techno-optimism and the neglected inner world
The author, fascinated by technology since childhood (choosing processors and graphics cards for his first computers, talking to AI almost daily by 2024), describes the breathtaking resources being invested in AI and a generation of techno-optimists betting that faster technology will solve fundamental crises causing suffering or threatening life [msg #1348].
He asks whether this view is balanced. We are in a technological experiment 150–170 years long — not that long — of the primacy of the scientific-materialist paradigm, which created a new world: many diseases defeated, unprecedented comfort, satellites around Earth, hot restaurant food in half an hour, any language translated on a smartphone. Yet this paradigm has not protected us from the metacrisis: rising depression, social polarization, ecological crisis and increasing cancer rates show that technological progress is not automatically accompanied by growth in ethical awareness, sustainability and happiness. The human mind created amazing tools but keeps producing wars, exploitation and destruction of the ecosystems it lives in. "Мы достигли невероятного прогресса, но стали ли мы счастливее? Technology alone does not lead to harmony; there is already enough to live a sustainable, harmonious life with the whole planet free of hunger, but we cannot implement it — the inability lies not in technology but in human minds. The fundamental problem of a time dominated by materialism and the techno-optimism it often breeds is an unbalanced understanding and development of the world: we develop the outer world through technology with all our might but pay no attention to the inner world — qualities and development of consciousness, the human mind [msg #1348].
The mind is the most powerful "technology" available to us, but a mind that has not developed far remains a source of suffering, anxiety and destruction. ### Endless optimization as denial of finitude (Bryan Johnson)
The author discusses a text by Bryan Johnson — a Silicon Valley millionaire who sold a company for about 800 million and became known for trying to optimize his health "literally to infinity": his body is a project, aging an engineering problem, life a system to be constantly measured, controlled and updated [msg #1694].
What Johnson gets right (per the author, "fairly accurately"): for most of history the future was at least roughly predictable — a 15th-century farmer knew who his grandson would be; even in the early 2000s one could enter university, choose a profession, build a career, imagine retirement. These scenarios worked not because life was simple but because the world changed slowly and large social and economic structures gave a sense of stability. In recent decades almost all classic institutions of predictability collapsed: stable employment, linear careers and stable professional identities, political foundations. Technology cycles shorten to a few years, experience and education stop being a guarantee; we no longer understand what to learn; five-year plans look not responsible but naive [msg #1694].
This creates background anxiety — not always acute fear but constant tension. We are creatures living in expectations; when the gap between what we assume and what happens becomes too large and too persistent, the body begins living in alert mode, which becomes chronic. Anxiety and depressive disorders increase, attention scatters, loneliness intensifies — even with outward social success [msg #1694].
Where the author disagrees: Johnson says the problem isn't bad maps of the future but that maps are no longer needed — we need flexible, adaptive, algorithmic systems that change as fast as technology, letting humans evolve with the world; the less attached we are to ideas, norms and expectations, the easier the glide. The author calls this a typical Silicon Valley perspective that senses acceleration accurately but asks almost no questions about the human: anxiety is reduced to a prediction error, vulnerability to an imperfect model, and the way out is still more optimization. The logic of endless bio- and psycho-optimization looks like self-care but at a deeper level is a form of denying finitude: aging becomes a bug, fatigue an error, slowing down a system failure. We try to eliminate not anxiety but the very fact of human limitation, as if vulnerability and finitude could be bracketed out by an engineering solution [msg #1694].
The alternative: people suffer not only from poor forecasting but from the loss of meaning anchors, the disintegration of collective containers, and the disappearance of rituals and languages that once helped bear uncertainty. Perhaps the problem is not that the world became too complex for our maps, nor (though there is some truth in it) that we lack better systems; perhaps we face the fundamental question: "что значит быть человеком в реальности, которая не обещает устойчивости, не гарантирует смысла и не поддаётся полному контролю?" (what does it mean to be human in a reality that does not promise stability, does not guarantee meaning and does not yield to full control?). Then the alternative to endless self-updating lies not in further optimization but in a change of perspective — not adapting even better to an accelerating world, but passing through a global initiation, an inner transition to another quality of adulthood, where uncertainty stops being a system error and becomes a condition of human existence that a more complex consciousness can handle [msg #1694].

### How we broke New Year: the lost container for the psyche
New Year historically arose as threshold time — when the old order has ended and the new has not yet formed. Anthropology called such periods *liminal*, marking the structure's openness to change. A person doesn't just live through the holidays but enters a special mode of perception — an altered state of consciousness in which habitual supports weaken and the psyche becomes more plastic [msg #1664].
Neuroscience framing (per the author): a temporary weakening of the dominance of the Default Mode Network, the network that sustains a stable "I" narrative and habitual interpretations of reality. In such states it is easier to complete, rethink and make decisions unavailable in the ordinary mode; Robin Carhart-Harris's research on states of consciousness shows that this is where the possibility of lasting change arises [msg #1664].
Collective threshold as a social mechanism: New Year was a collective agreement to stop, first described in detail by Arnold van Gennep. Society agreed that for this short time old roles and habitual functions loosen; this softening of social structure creates the altered state — safe, shared and permitted. When millions simultaneously leave their usual rhythm, slow down and synchronize emotionally, a broad collective regulation effect arises; modern research, per the author, shows that under such conditions, when not done alone, the psyche lets go of the old more easily [msg #1664].
Why it stopped working: the form of New Year persists but its function is largely lost. Modern people approach year-end in chronic overload, making entry into the liminal space difficult. Without resources to go deeper, the psyche chooses more accessible ways to change state: instead of reducing stimulation it intensifies it — noise, alcohol, shopping, external impressions. This is not a rejection of the altered state (which is healing and necessary) but its failed substitution with quick stimuli that require no vulnerability and lead to no real transition. The loss of the ritual container adds to this: historically every transition had a meaning frame — myth, community, a guide and subsequent integration. Today the person is alone with the experience; "Сакральное без контейнера психикой считывается как небезопасное" (The sacred without a container is read by the psyche as unsafe), and New Year turns into a simulation [msg #1664].
Sacred became consumption: Durkheim distinguished sacré and profane as different modes of social reality, each necessary with its own function. The sacred exists only with a clear boundary separating it from the everyday; when the boundary is erased, the sacred doesn't expand — it disappears. Modern culture shows this: the holiday is stretched, sold and endlessly reproduced, losing its ability to be a transition. Consumption creates the illusion of a change of state without touching the structure of the "I". The author calls this an understandable and adaptive choice "в условиях, где тишина стала дефицитом" (in conditions where silence has become scarce). (!) ### The crisis of achievism
Market shift: a few years ago the adult education market the author works in was almost entirely about usefulness and efficiency — courses, programs, retreats and trainings sold through "upgrade": become more productive and resilient, manage yourself and your career better; even inner practices and meditation were packaged as tools for state optimization and performance. This hasn't disappeared, but in the last year the author sees (not radically, not across the whole heterogeneous market) a clear trend: more formats distance themselves from the very idea of learning and usefulness — "Это не программа для апгрейда, а процесс про опыт, проживание, контакт, присутствие" (This is not an upgrade program but a process about experience, living through, contact, presence). Demand grows for safe spaces and value-sharing communities, places where one need not keep face and can explore and express authenticity; through this come recovery and new steps into an unprecedentedly uncertain future. The author says deep mind pioneered this in late 2024 with Resonance, a group process on new leadership rather than training — unusual for many then, but matching an emerging logic where linear frameworks stop answering important questions and what is needed is not another tool but a change in the point from which we look at the world [msg #1708].
The outdated contract of the urban efficient class: for about 20 years the cultural mainstream of Russia's urban class — a small % of the population that nonetheless sets lifestyle trends — rested on a simple contract: be rational, disciplined, educated, invest in yourself, work efficiently, and life will get better; effort converts into growth, competence and company position into a new standard of living. This logic was the life background of those building the new, globally integrated Russian economy: corporate careers, tech companies, the cult of education and productivity, the idea that a person is responsible for their own trajectory. In vertical-development terms this is called "orange" — essentially modernist rational individualism, faith in development through efficiency. It peaked around 2015–2020: large successful companies appeared, and a layer of people formed for whom professionalism, responsibility and productivity became not an idea but a way of life [msg #1708].
The system starts to glitch: per the author, the Russian economy has effectively not grown since 2014 and a crisis looms. ### Productivity as a flimsy foundation for happiness
The marketed figure: the entrepreneur or product manager — rational, collected, constantly testing hypotheses, learning fast, holding focus, working in uncertainty and mastering the latest tools faster than anyone. This figure sells well through the market, media, educational products and Telegram channels because it promises concrete steps: "Просто освой современный продуктовый фреймворк и будешь зарабатывать больше денег и жить лучшую жизнь!" (Just master the modern product framework and you'll earn more and live a better life!) — which the author presents ironically [msg #1710].
Bourdieu's point, as the author uses it: the dominant lifestyle disguises itself as the natural order of things, so we pass off the product of an era as human nature. For the efficiency model this is the style of late modernity, valuing autonomy, rationality, constant growth and self-control. The market presents the model as universal, but only a few can be happy in it. Structural limits remain invisible — power structure, starting positions, nervous-system features, economic conditions are bracketed out and responsibility is fully internalized: if it doesn't work, the problem is you; you didn't try hard enough and didn't master the right tools [msg #1710].
One formula for fundamentally different people: modern efficiency culture is like a clothing store with one garment, asking fundamentally differently configured people to fit into it and calling that happiness. Science is fairly clear, per the author, that the ability to live constantly under high cognitive load, uncertainty and engagement varies greatly: people differ in nervous-system type, recovery speed, stress resilience and capacity to hold attention. For some, life as an efficient project is nourishing; for others deeply destructive and joyless even if it looks successful. How many "successful" role models are long rusted under the hood? Burnout here is not weakness of character or lack of motivation but a simple consequence of chronic allostatic load — the organism working too long in adaptation mode without sufficient recovery. A person can keep achieving, earning and growing while increasingly losing contact with the body, joy and their own life. Non-universality is especially sharp with neurodivergence: people with very different nervous-system configurations are expected to have the same pace, productivity and resilience (figures: see *Prevalence estimate*) [msg #1710].

### Productivity culture as a collective myth
The author credits productivity culture and its extreme hustle form, widespread in business and entrepreneurship, with letting many people feel materially comfortable in a way unavailable to many previous generations of their families — a major achievement worth noting. Looked at honestly and with care, it has costs: cultural shadows, myths and misconceptions that create much suffering [msg #1764].
Because of its effectiveness, the culture often forms a collective illusion of chosenness or paradigmatic correctness — since it is effective, it must be the best. Results are measured only by numbers, through which we soon interpret and rank ourselves and others. Example: if you do music or teach, doing very difficult work but earning less than a tech lead, you may feel less significant in his company, because your contribution is not confirmed by business growth and you don't have a new phone. This is the reductionist mythical belief that if there is no metric, there is no objective significance — "Твоего вклада как бы и не существует, если тебе за него не платят" (Your contribution as if doesn't exist if you aren't paid for it). People in this environment live by these rules, often forgetting they are artificially created principles of a specific environment, not of life as a whole, which is nonlinear [msg #1764].
In productivity communities we exist as in a collective dream, daily and jointly sustaining the myth of the god of productivity and optimization as the main one, encouraging each other to lay ourselves on the altar of invulnerability, strength, speed and endless improvement. Myths are a very human way of coping with complex reality but always a reducing one; myths and beliefs (not to be confused with insight) describe either a nonexistent reality or grab too narrow a slice of it. They let people calm down together and unite, but we bump, sometimes very painfully, into things the myth does not describe or describes wrongly. The author says he would not write about this had he not seen with his own eyes how much "successful" representatives of hustle culture suffer when they meet the limits of the myth of optimization as the highest virtue (the resulting fragility: see *Two processes of productivity-driven psychological fragility*) [msg #1764].

### Why slowing down is hard
The invitation to slow down is one of the key invitations of most deep mind processes — from corporate well-being programs to team facilitation and Resonance. They always include a large block of work through attention and people's state, which requires slowing down: in habitual bustle and constant distraction we exist in a much more activated nervous-system state than the one that allows systemic, deep thinking, feeling and noticing fine details [msg #1804].
Most people, especially in the active urban class, are themselves tired of bustle and overload, intensified by the digital environment with always-available content and easy context switching. To cope, zoomers do the TikTok "Do Nothing Challenge", deliberately sitting without phone, music, podcasts, food and other stimuli — sometimes 10–15 minutes, sometimes an hour — to reset attention from constant stimulation [msg #1804].
Mechanism: "нервная система работает как инвестиции со сложным процентом" (the nervous system works like compound-interest investment): the more actively we practice certain states, the more we dwell in them. Unconsciously practicing stress, constant switching and permanent activation, we turn it into a habitual environment that is hard to leave. So when a person meets the invitation to slow down — whether a couple of minutes of mindfulness with nowhere to rush, or a retreat of several hours or a day of silence (as recently in Resonance) — many difficulties arise. Slowing down today is not easy and requires both a special setup and often support [msg #1804].
Two sources of the difficulty: we have not only deeply absorbed the external productivity imperative dictated by modern culture — as if every moment of life must be useful or it is wasted — but also unconsciously fuel it by growing an inner gap between what we actually feel and how we act. The body has long wanted rest, but the thought of finishing one more task drives us on; or fear of uncertainty terrifies us and, unable to cope, we direct attention away from the inner — outward, into content or tasks. So our actual experience — thoughts, feelings, emotions — is pushed out and stored "in a basement" where we dump everything unwanted by the god of usefulness or the god of fear of feelings. Fleeing what we don't want to feel and can't handle, we widen the gap and increasingly create the external culture of bustle and usefulness ourselves. Hence speed, busyness and distraction-with-entertainment are "две стороны одной травматичной медали" (two sides of one traumatic medal): the cultural imperative of eternal insufficiency and its reflection in our inner instability before feelings. ### Gabor Maté and the ending of Winnie-the-Pooh
The setting: the author and his wife Dasha are hiking alone deep in the mountains. Along the way they count about ten memorial plaques to climbers who died there. The vacation is ending, and he is already half out of it. Instead of really seeing the mountains and being with Dasha, he spends half the walk thinking about work. He does not fight this, but he feels some irritation and sadness. For so many years, thoughts about what needs to be done and how have taken up so much space in his mind that little is left for creativity and play, for childlike curiosity and the absence of a goal [msg #1896].

That evening, tired after four hours on the trail, he jumps off the sofa and announces that he has "won", meaning he managed to tear himself away from pointless content. Dasha laughs and asks whether he has watched a reel she sent him. He goes back to watch it with her [msg #1896].

The reel opens with Gabor Maté saying he would have lived his life differently if he had had the choice. He recalls the ending of the Winnie-the-Pooh book, which made him cry for many years. Christopher Robin tells his stuffed friends that he is going away to school and won't be able to play with them as often. Maté explains why the scene moved him so much. By the time he entered medical school, he already carried such a strong need to justify his own existence that he decided to work as intensely as humanly possible. Because of this motivation, he says, he missed what matters most [msg #1896].

What matters most, as the author retells it: taking a vacation, setting aside your important work and simply spending time with family, playing with the children, drinking coffee on a summer day. Simplicity, play and enjoying the time of one's life. Maté sacrificed all of this. He worked long hours and ran forward without stopping, as if he had chosen that as his form of existence, and now he regrets it [msg #1896].

The author's general lesson: "Мы жертвуем игрой и спонтанностью, чтобы закрыть неосознанную потребность в подтверждении того, что имеем право быть, а идет она из глубинного чувства, что с нами что-то не так, — из наших травм" (We sacrifice play and spontaneity to close an unconscious need for confirmation that we have a right to exist, and it comes from a deep feeling that something is wrong with us — from our traumas). He adds a caveat about insight: he thinks he understood this about himself long ago, "но понимание тут не всегда помогает" (but understanding doesn't always help here) [msg #1896].

The book's closing image stayed with Maté for years: wherever they go and whatever happens to them on the way, in an enchanted place at the top of the forest a boy and his bear will always be playing. Maté's conclusion: "В каком-то смысле мы всегда можем продолжать играть в этом волшебном лесу. И думаю, это самое главное" (In a sense we can always keep playing in that magic forest. And I think that is the most important thing). The author ends with the contrast that carries the lesson: all day they walked past plaques naming people who will never go anywhere again, and all day he thought about work. He does not know what to do about it right now, except to put the phone down and stay on the sofa next to his wife. The practical step he offers is to return to the present moment and the person beside you, not to adopt some technique [msg #1896].

### From 16 hours to 4 hours: the author's own trajectory
- **The ministry years.** In his ministry years, the workday stretched across all his waking hours. Rest meant the rare days when his boss flew off on a business trip and an almost graveyard silence settled in the corridors. Friends, travel and the very feeling of being alive were struck out of those years "так аккуратно, будто над ними поработал старательный цензор" (so neatly, as if a diligent censor had worked on them) [msg #1967].
- **Seneca's claim, tested on himself.** Seneca wrote that the busiest people live the least. The author says he tested this on himself both then and after he went into entrepreneurship, when for a long time he simply did not know weekends existed [msg #1967].
- **The forced shift.** Four years ago his sleep problems peaked and he "just couldn't anymore". Coping with the pain and recovering took up a large share of his waking time, so work mostly shrank to 3–5 hours a day [msg #1967].
- **The chosen shift.** Now that he has finally sorted out his sleep, he says he simply no longer wants to grind ("херачить", a crude Russian slang word for slogging). He lives next to a national park in Korea, with red maples and yellowing ginkgos, and can work a few hours in the morning, go into the mountains, and work a little in the evening [msg #1967].
- **Mixed feelings he names openly.** He feels anxiety that "так нельзя" (you can't live like this). He wants to justify the walks to himself and the team as "recovery". He also holds a long-ripened understanding that he does not want to define himself by hours of work at all [msg #1967].
