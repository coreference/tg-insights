# Retreats and the deeper contemplative path

## What this covers

This file collects what the deep mind channel teaches about going beyond casual meditation: what retreats really are and what they produce, the home "mini-vipassana", what vipassana is (and is not) and how the author evaluates Goenka centers, the prerequisites for deep practice, how to tell dissociation from the correct witness, the case for "healthy ranking" of practices and for "authentic"/"serious" practice, the requirements and risks of advanced methods such as tantra, dzogchen and mahamudra, and the motivations that make practice deep rather than neurotic. According to the author, misunderstanding these points leads people to stay at the level of "local insights", pleasant states, retraumatisation or dissociation instead of genuine transformation of personality [msg #138, #955, #982, #1280].

This part covers five posts by the author of deep mind on going deeper in practice. They include a set of "inverted" practices that turn obstacles into training, and the author's report from a two-week retreat in 2026, where exhaustion led him to a gentler, regulation-first way of practising and to scepticism about hard intensive retreats. There is also a critical cultural analysis of the jhana boom in Western secular mindfulness, the original meaning of the "dark night of the soul" set beside modern psychology, and the 2025 Harvard research on extended cessation (nirodha samapatti) with what it may mean for the science of consciousness and AI. The author's position is that practice is not a spa treatment or a pleasant escape from reality. Going deeper brings real difficulties, from discomfort and nervous-system overload to losing an old way of living, so it needs self-knowledge, honesty about your current capacity and a clear sense of the difference between path and state [msg #1328, #1688, #1698, #1701].

## Core ideas

- **A retreat is the hardest work of one's life, not a holiday.** Bali and Western commercial culture spread the wrong idea of retreats as pleasant vacations with a pool, good food, yoga and spa. In fact retreats are a valuable time and place for intensive meditative practice in traditions ranging from yoga and vipassana to dzogchen and secular practices; this is "the time of the hardest work in life", capable of extraordinary results and change [msg #138].
- **Real practice transforms personality, not just states.** Insights and experiences matter, but genuine inner work changes character traits, stably shifts one's angle of view and changes behaviour, making us happier inside while becoming more pleasant for others outside [msg #955, #1010].
- **Depth requires prerequisites**: disillusionment with the status quo, cultivated love and compassion, and understanding of the philosophy of contemplative systems (see *Three key prerequisites*) [msg #955].
- **Dissociation is a common counterfeit of meditation**; the correct witness opens to and integrates experience, while dissociation pushes it away (see *Dissociation vs correct witness*) [msg #982].
- **Healthy ranking of practices is necessary**: not all roads lead to the same goal, and some methods lack any mechanism for fundamental transformation (see *Two criteria of authentic practice*) [msg #1010].
- **Advanced methods are faster but demand more** — tantra, dzogchen and mahamudra are "high risk-high profit"; build a foundation of concentration and ethics first, but do not belittle your potential [msg #1252].
- **Vipassana proper is advanced insight practice**, not breath-watching or 10 days of silence; most beginners never reach it, yet intensive retreats still bring great benefit; preparation, integration and trauma-informed teachers are essential [msg #1280].
- **Motivation should move beyond utility and pleasure** toward practicing for life's unfolding and for all beings [msg #727].
- **Real practice is a way of being**, tested in daily life, not a hobby, an escape or another self-help tool [msg #1327].

- **Practice does not need ideal conditions. Obstacles can be the training.** Inverted meditations deliberately meet pain, mental overload, chaos and unpleasant emotion. The goal is to train the mind not to run away, which builds resilience and wisdom and offers a way out of the endless search for comfort (see *Four inverted meditations*) [msg #1328].
- **The results of deep practice overturn the usual idea of wellbeing.** The author holds that liberation does not come from constantly compensating, improving and optimising. It comes as freedom from reactivity and grasping, which he calls a felt sense of deep sufficiency [msg #1688].
- **For an exhausted nervous system, regulation comes before development.** Hard jumps into intensive retreats, especially for beginners, are, in the author's view, increasingly trauma-prone under today's load. Without good knowledge of yourself and the practice, "это не путь, а лотерея" (*it is not a path but a lottery*) [msg #1688].
- **Informal everyday practice is core, not an add-on.** In the Mahasi Sayadaw and Thich Nhat Hanh traditions this is where action becomes free of excess effort and self-coercion [msg #1688].
- **Jhanas are a tool, not a goal.** The current Western jhana boom is, in the author's view, more a cultural response to overload than a contemplative path. When the state replaces the path, it resembles a socially approved form of dissociation [msg #1698].
- **The dark night of the soul comes after the breakthrough, not before it.** It is the pain of losing a fuller way of being once you have seen it. It is a crisis of meaning, often mistaken for burnout or depression [msg #1701].
- **Cessation states have measurable brain signatures.** The author presents the Harvard research as early evidence that traditional reports of radically altered states are real. He reads it as suggesting that consciousness may exist where usual cognitive processing is minimal, and that thinking is only part of the mind, with implications for AI [msg #1740].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Three key prerequisites for depth in practice
Purpose: the author presents this as one of the main ideas from the first day of a teaching he attended in Asia at the end of 2023, framed as "if we want a transforming experience in meditation, it is important to do it the right way". Context: many people try different techniques, traditions and methods, exploring "branches and leaves" while completely missing "the trunk and the root" of the process, so they do not go beyond local insights, however inspiring. Insights are not bad — "как не плохо и доехать на феррари в магазин за продуктами. Но с феррари мы можем гораздо больше!" (just as it's not bad to drive a Ferrari to the grocery store. But with a Ferrari we can do far greater things!) — real methods of development can give incredible changes, not just reduced stress or anxiety [msg #955].

1. **Disillusionment with the status quo (Разочарование в статусе кво).** Before jumping headlong into serious practice, understand as clearly as possible what you are tired of in ordinary life. Honestly answer the question "oткуда возникает импульс к исследованию путей развития?" (where does the impulse to explore paths of development arise from?). Striving for something better is great, but one must also admit the reality in which we cannot find stable peace and satisfaction — if we were already fully realised, the request for practice would not arise. The task is to discover fatigue with the current order of things: the patterns and circumstances that bring suffering and prevent resting in fundamental peace. Why: this inspires the mind for the difficult and long work that practice requires and that "никому не получится делегировать" (cannot be delegated to anyone) [msg #955].
2. **Cultivating love and compassion (Культивация любви и сострадания).** Compassion and love are "не просто милые идеи" (not just nice ideas). With their help we can pass through and exit difficult and destructive states such as feelings of superiority, anger, envy, jealousy and fear. Love and compassion "moisten" practice ("увлажнить свою практику") and let us stay in it when it would be easy to quit. The practitioner should find love and compassion in themselves and develop them toward unconditionality toward all other beings [msg #955].
3. **Understanding the philosophy and logic of contemplative systems (Понимание философии и логики созерцательных систем).** Meditation never existed in a vacuum; it was developed by people who complemented each other and constantly systematised the process of development. Until recent decades practitioners reliably got results inside a specific cultural context and worldview. These philosophies can be seen as "deep common sense" one needs to understand in order to know why and how to practice. This may include studying contemplative traditions and their discoveries about the structure of reality, or modern physics — traditional and modern scientific studies advancing the ideas of:
   - the impermanence of objects of cognition;
   - the interdependence of all objects;
   - the emptiness of phenomena [msg #955].

How the three work together: we reflect on, remember and apply these three points in life. This creates the foundation for successful development, which is needed for the discipline, courage and creativity real practice requires; on this basis joy and pleasure in the process also appear. The author cites tantric texts: if we practice without understanding the basis of its logic and without love and compassion, "то это все лишь детские игры" (it is all just children's games). Without really committing to the path, walking it with love and compassion and with understanding, we do not move forward and most likely practice for show, for others or for ourselves [msg #955].

### Dissociation vs correct witness-observer
Purpose: distinguish genuine meditative observation from dissociation, a common problem; if we do not understand the difference and get stuck in dissociative perception thinking it is meditation, we may never get the substantial benefit of practice [msg #982].

Background mechanism: everyone dissociates periodically — "jumps out" of experience and perceives it as if from outside, especially when it is stressful or traumatic. With such a habit, or even a psychological defence significant for us, we can reproduce it in meditation, e.g. when observing the breath or scanning the body. Such "meditation" has created a misconception among body practitioners that meditators withdraw from bodily experience and lose sensitivity. Some teachers add fuel by giving incorrect instructions — having misunderstood the process themselves — that help us dissociate instead of creating an attentive observer: they claim the observed experience is not our experience, just an object to be viewed "from the side" [msg #982].

Why the error spreads: dissociative observation does give some liberation. But that relief is the result of a psychological defence that shields us from experience rather than transforming it. If we feel pain, we separate from it and things seem easier — fewer unpleasant sensations — but we lose involvement, vitality and the possibility to interact with the experience in a more mature way: to hold its intensity, live it through and let it go, as meditation teaches. Dissociation helps cope with many difficult emotions and situations but produces a form of loss of contact with one's sensations and with life around; dissociating, we perceive everything "словно через экран телевизора" (as if through a TV screen) [msg #982].

The test — how do I know I'm dissociating rather than developing the correct witness?
1. **Dissociation.** Inner formula: "Это не мой опыт, поэтому мне не так больно." (This is not my experience, so it hurts less.) We close off from experience, though we can examine it from outside. We try to push away, throw some part of experience aside, breaking the sense of connectedness and unity of what is happening [msg #982].
2. **Correct observer.** Inner formula: "Это мой опыт, но в нём нет 'Я', поэтому выдерживать интенсивность ощущений становится просто." (This is my experience, but there is no 'I' in it, so holding the intensity of sensations becomes simple.) We constantly try to open up and allow the actual experience, resting in attentive openness to it. Meditation with the correct observer creates conditions for contact with everything happening, overcomes the fragmentation of experience and integrates it into holistic perception — attention contacts all feelings and emotions, pleasant or unpleasant. We open to experience and connect with it, admitting it into consciousness through a kind and accepting attitude. This makes it possible to truly rest in the witness — to see and feel everything with equanimity, without the "visor of distance" dissociation seeks [msg #982].

Why it matters: the correct witness is a very integrating, health-giving and healing position — it adds sensitivity, returns us to the body and helps live through repressed emotions. It is "vitally important" to learn to distinguish it from watching experience as if on TV, which helps with none of this [msg #982].

### Mini-vipassana (home half-day retreat)
Origin: invented by a friend of the author after a classic retreat in Thailand; the friend did it for himself every Sunday for a long time. The author's family did it on a Friday (their family day off) [msg #991].

Protocol:
1. **Silence** — do not speak for the period [msg #991].
2. **Restrict information and stimulation** — no external input [msg #991].
3. **Meditate all available time from waking until 13–14h** [msg #991].
4. **Agree and arrange it** — with family/partners, set time aside and change the morning routine of the day off [msg #991].

Purpose and when to use: to support practice and the level of mindfulness. The author's case: after a January retreat he had a nourishing daily practice of 45–60 minutes twice a day. Then "the system started glitching": post-traumatic sleep difficulties plus high load as a founder meant he lives in a very delicate balancing of psychic resource — more limited than abundant. Such resource is necessary for regular practice, to maintain discipline and schedule. Although meditation itself is a resource-giving practice for him (gives more than it takes), allocating 2 hours a day to formal sits is hard — this is the maximum he has managed to sustain long-term outside a retreat while living an ordinary, busy, commitment-laden life. In the week in question several big tasks arrived, he slept badly, resource was depleted and the routine slipped: 50 minutes of practice one day, 10 the next [msg #991].

Attitude to lapses: he treats rollbacks and gaps in habits without much frustration, though he notices slight annoyance and clinging to the state of clarity, which he recognises also as kindness to himself and a wish to be in good shape ("Изменение состояния не является целью моей практики, но я радуюсь, когда хорошо себя чувствую" — changing my state is not the goal of my practice, but I'm glad when I feel good). Years of sleepless nights became an incredible training ground for deep acceptance that everything goes not according to plan, and a firm conviction that the main thing is to stay open to reality rather than to one's own expectations — to accept what actually happens, not what one would like. He considers this adaptability, which gives more freedom than a habit of controlling oneself and life; relying on it, they set the mini-retreat [msg #991].

Mechanism and result: focus on practice and limiting distractors make several extra hours of meditation possible in a day. Such immersion creates causes for restoring contact both with the practice and with one's emotions, sensations and needs. Practice is harder than usual, but by lunch the desire to continue seems rediscovered and motivation refreshed. Even after sleeping badly and spending 2 hours watching a frustrating jumble of unpleasant images of the week's events, half a day of this mini-retreat rebooted his nervous system and brought him somewhat back on track. He recommends it to everyone; for non-meditators, creating a deeper, calmer day off — e.g. spending the morning in silence without additional external stimulation — can be very inspiring [msg #991].

### Conditions for self-closure and bliss in meditation
Purpose: for people who tried meditating but noticed nothing special except relaxation. Meditation is a most powerful tool of self-knowledge and development, but its effects must be lived directly in experience; until then we can only believe in its benefit or consider it hype. "Чтобы не верить, а проверить" (so as not to believe but to check), create conditions and realise states that positively change meditative experience [msg #870].

The two states:
- **Self-closure (Самозамыкание).** Arises when we train mindfulness of the body with discipline — focusing, for example, on the sensations of breathing or scanning the body. We basically relax, and bodily sensations become so unusual or pleasant that they further support the calming of attention on the object and let practice become effortless. We become maximally concentrated on our body, perceiving it practically without distraction while calming down most deeply [msg #870].
- **Bliss (Блаженство).** Not the usual feeling of dopamine happiness, but a feeling of 100% fullness. It arises when we notice the space of silence and presence between two thoughts and rest in it for a while. An incredibly pleasant state: all tension goes, and we feel natural simplicity and openness, experiencing practically nothing unpleasant [msg #870].

How to create conditions (three steps):
1. **Learn a technique from a reliable source**, following clear instructions for the beginning, middle and end of practice [msg #870].
2. **Choose practices that teach deep relaxation and balancing of attention without loss of clarity**: the instruction must contain directions for relaxation on one hand and for awakening mindfulness on the other. The author's examples are a deep mind "body–speech–mind" audio practice and a recording of Alan Wallace's meditations on mind training [msg #870].
3. **Increase the time or frequency of regular practice** — e.g. start practicing daily, gradually increasing to 30–40 minutes, or practice 3–5 times a day for at least 5 minutes [msg #870].

Pitfalls: these states are hard to reach if you desire them strongly — desire creates tension that itself blocks them. They are not the final goal of meditation; do not attach strongly or try to reproduce them in every session. Self-closure and bliss can be so pleasant and inspiring that we cling to them, and such clinging will certainly lead to suffering when they pass — as in other analogous situations in life [msg #870].

### Ladder of motivation for practice
From the post "Медитация не ради пользы" (meditation not for the sake of benefit) [msg #727]:
1. **Functional benefit (the trap).** The boom in mindfulness is mostly due to scientific proof of its benefits for brain, health, development and relationships; hence the dominant pitch: look how meditation affects the brain and how much better you will become. The author argues this focuses entirely on a functional relation to oneself and to practice — on the benefit we are now "obliged" to get from each session — and simultaneously heats up the neurotic tension many live with nonstop: "мне срочно надо стать лучше [в конечном итоге — любимее]" (I urgently need to become better [ultimately — more loved]) and get more from life to feel safety and joy (ultimately — love). From such deficit we can force ourselves for years to grow without advancing a centimetre toward fullness [msg #727].
2. **Practice for pleasure (the quick exit).** One can quickly leave this "достиженческого ада" (achievement hell) by allowing oneself to practice for pleasure. As the saying goes, "нeт счастья равного покою" (there is no happiness equal to peace), which can be developed in systematic, consistent practice. Ultimately contact with one's feelings, calming of the mind and beyond-Ego states of wider consciousness give a fullness unattainable by any external results or hedonistic pleasures. It does not matter whether practice builds more grey matter or lets us do more tasks; it is "вполне легитимно" (quite legitimate) to enjoy 15 minutes of observing the breath and reconnecting with oneself [msg #727].
3. **Non-attachment to the pleasant (avoiding the next trap).** Not resisting but welcoming the simple joy of practice, it is important not to cling to pleasant sensations; clinging would make practice just another neurotic, hopeless attempt to take back love and safety [msg #727].
4. **Deeper motivation — life unfolding through us.** Traditional meditation teaching usually begins with motivation, not technique. With it we practice not for functional benefit, not for pleasure and not for ourselves at all, but for the natural unfolding of life itself through us, thanks to ever fuller, ever more authentic presence in it and understanding of the connections, causes and effects of the world. Practically anything can become practice this way [msg #727].
5. **Highest motivation — for all living beings.** Ultimately the fullest, highest "[и парадоксально — самой полезной]" (and paradoxically the most useful) motivation is to practice for all other living beings, who thanks to our mindfulness receive more compassion, attention, joy and inspiration for their own growth. This motivation allows developing boundless qualities of mind and going far beyond painful neurotic self-fixation. The author links further reading on the "insecure overachiever" and on bodhichitta as ultimate compassion [msg #727].

### Two criteria of authentic practice and healthy ranking
Thesis: many respected masters speak of *authentic* practice; this irritates people whose views rest on equality and pluralism, where ranking risks devaluing someone's process. The author's view: ranking is often necessary and absolutely healthy. As in surgery, where one must evaluate the effectiveness and risks of each method or drug because "одно часто хуже другого" (one is often worse than another), in the abstract but equally important field of human development we should use healthy ranking. Refusing clear evaluation of methods and practice can lead to serious misunderstanding of development and hinder growth [msg #1010].

Why masters use the word: teachers of serious methods who achieved clear results also see how often people engage in hopeless pursuits and think they practice and grow while having fundamental gaps and no actual progress. Calling an approach authentic is not always an attempt to build a sect through a unique offer and fear manipulation — that happens, perhaps too often — but honest, sufficiently aware practitioners want to point to a path leading to real results, i.e. stable changes in a person's life, rather than entertainment, pleasure, experiences, unusual states, insights, or nothing at all (a complete waste of time); and to identify what will *not* lead to stable results [msg #1010].

1. **From the method's side:** authentic practice is one that "предоставляет возможность фундаментально трансформировать человека к большей целостности" (provides the possibility to fundamentally transform a person toward greater wholeness), or scientifically, integration: fewer parts remain suppressed, they find recognition and a form of expression, and we gain more peace inside and outside. Some methods simply cannot make that promise because they lack a mechanism for fulfilling it [msg #1010].
2. **From the practitioner's side:** authenticity can arise when, after enough experimentation, a person is able to settle on long-term application of one method, sometimes even a single practice. If our goals are wholeness, stable happiness or radical reduction of suffering, the decision to pursue them weakens or stops self-distraction through pleasures and interesting experience, and instead means facing the full unfolding of everything that appears in daily life, routine and vulnerability [msg #1010].

Fruits, as described by Jack Kornfield (quoted in Russian by the author): when heart-felt attention begins to distinguish the reality of the present from the endless flow of thoughts, the world shines with brilliant beauty; we begin to see how many unnoticed inner states and unrecognised beliefs control our life; we wake up and see patterns of emotions and habits; we can feel the conflicts we carry from a wider perspective, from within the broad flow of the practice we have chosen; with each step we open up further [msg #1010].

The author adds that such transformation is very likely not to be expected from a massage or a training by media personalities on Bali. But healthy ranking does not close the door to various practices: it lets us match methods (or people offering them) with our goals and resources more clearly — to understand what we are doing and what it can or cannot lead to. "Не все дороги ведут к одной и той же цели. И не для всех целей нужна одна и та же дорога." (Not all roads lead to the same goal. And not every goal needs the same road.) [msg #1010]

### Advanced methods as a vehicle (tantra, dzogchen, mahamudra)
The author's thesis, explicitly hedged ("У меня есть основания полагать" — I have reasons to believe): skillful methods developed in later contemplative traditions, e.g. tantric practices or dzogchen, when correctly understood and performed, may be more effective and faster than basic mindfulness methods common in secular mindfulness and historically more typical of southern Buddhism (Thailand, Sri Lanka, Burma). His reasoning from the development of consciousness: the more complex human consciousness became, the more advanced psychopractice systems it could create or discover [msg #1252].

Components of the argument:
1. **Best teachers should invest in the most skilled students.** That is how overall progress is most effectively achieved: the method develops, outstanding results appear, and new great teachers emerge. Today we can complement this with a principle of inclusivity that adds stability and fairness, but students too should strive for the most effective teachers and methods available to them personally [msg #1252].
2. **Choose the best "vehicle" you can afford and drive.** For the fastest achievement of the goal we must seek, test and choose the best "транспортное средство" (vehicle), provided we can "acquire" it and steer it at its speed. As the technological revolution showed, advanced technology does not become more accessible but demands ever more preparation and skill; likewise advanced systems make ever-increasing demands on the student [msg #1252].
3. **Specific requirements of tantra:** great cultural flexibility and openness; development of subtle motivation in cultivating certain personal qualities rather than just a wish to change one's state in the moment; and the discipline of delayed gratification — readiness to struggle through many symbolic worlds incomprehensible to the Western mind before getting results. Even subtler abilities and higher requirements arise for progress in the most skillful systems of developing consciousness ever discovered, such as mahamudra or dzogchen [msg #1252].
4. **Ability as responsibility.** It is a call to take on harder tasks in inner work if we are capable or feel constructively drawn to them. The more whole, complex and developed we become, the more we influence the general "температуру по больнице" (average temperature in the hospital — the overall state of the field), transmitting values, attitudes and qualities in our circles [msg #1252].
5. **No false dichotomy — foundation first.** All methods are connected and may suit students of different inclinations at different times. Advanced methods would hardly have arisen without an important foundation of something simpler. Tantra or dzogchen practice is unlikely to be effective without sufficient development of concentration and ethical foundation, the main emphasis of early contemplative traditions. The author himself practices mainly in Tibetan Buddhism but deeply respects early southern Buddhism, does shamatha and vipassana retreats there once a year, has teachers there, and practiced exclusively in it for the first 6 years [msg #1252].
6. **Avoid the extreme of self-assured rushing.** Do not presumptuously run to the "best" practices, proclaiming oneself a tantrika or dzogchen-pa (or kriya, kundalini practitioner) without first building a foundation in something "simpler" that may suit us much better at the current stage. The most advanced methods are "high risk-high profit"; without stability and qualified teachers many "улетают совсем не туда" (fly off in completely the wrong direction) [msg #1252].
7. **But do not belittle your potential.** Do not dismiss the possibility of becoming a serious practitioner and mastering the most effective methods; ultimately we may glimpse and perhaps realise the possibility of becoming someone quite different — greater than we could imagine [msg #1252].

### Four points of real practice as a way of being (Alex Berzin, via the author)
Context: after short in-person teachings by Dzongsar Khyentse Rinpoche in Ho Chi Minh City, the author noticed his mind turned more toward practice, especially informal practice — using all arising discomforts to develop more compassion and involvement. He even has a "sporting interest" in being in Canggu, Bali, which he usually avoids: Canggu is "эпицентр стимулов желания и отвращения" (the epicentre of stimuli of desire and aversion), which he now uses to practice more. He then shares the view of real practice he heard from Alex Berzin (PhD, Harvard), considering it crucial now that "practice" has come to mean anything [msg #1327].

Core statement: practice is not a hobby, not a way to escape reality and not another self-help tool; it is not reducible to techniques, morning meditations or group dances. "Настоящая практика — не то, что мы иногда делаем, а то, как мы живём." (Real practice is not what we sometimes do but how we live.) It is a way of being in the world. Seeking on the path often turns into escape — into the comfortable space of rituals, theories or formal techniques; practice is not about sitting *correctly* on the cushion or mastering complex new theories, but about how we look at reality, interact with people and accept difficulties [msg #1327].

1. **Practice is about reality, not projections.** We rarely meet the world directly; we perceive it through layers of ideas, projections and cognitive distortions, build illusions about ourselves, others and the future, and attach to them. A key task is to see reality as it is, not as the mind constructs it. Progress is not learning more philosophical concepts but learning to notice: how emotions and beliefs distort perception; how expectations and fears shape reactions; how we cling to habitual ideas even when they harm us. Mindfulness is not just attention to the breath but seeing how we create our own world and being able to let go of the unnecessary [msg #1327].
2. **Critical thinking is the basis of practice.** Modern culture is overflowing with disinformation; we are constantly shaped by social ideas of what will bring happiness and rarely check where our beliefs come from. Real practice includes careful reality-checking and develops the ability to look critically at one's ideas, information and accepted social norms. It helps answer: What stands behind my actions? Where did my beliefs come from? Do they correspond to reality or are they just convenient and habitual? "Осознанность невозможна без интеллекта. Умение видеть ложь — важнейшая часть пути." (Mindfulness is impossible without intelligence. The ability to see lies is a crucial part of the path.) [msg #1327]
3. **Everyday life is the key field of practice.** Practice is thought to be formal exercise, but how we behave in daily life matters far more: How do we react to stress? How do we communicate with loved ones? How do we accept change and loss? Real practice does not happen in isolation from the world; it is performed and tested where there is irritation, conflict, expectation and disappointment. If we can keep mindfulness, openness and compassion in real life, that is real practice [msg #1327].
4. **Practice is not escape from, but ever fuller contact with life.** Some use practices to avoid difficulty: to create a "spiritual" identity, find consolation in groups, hide behind philosophical concepts. Authentic practice means direct interaction with reality: learning to see the world without embellishment; accepting complexity rather than avoiding it; not clinging to comfort and certainty but being able to be in the unknown. Real practice is expressed in how we accept life, what kind of people we become and what values we embody [msg #1327].

### Four inverted meditations (Andrew Holecek)
**What and why.** The author introduces these after a comment about practising in Canggu and the conversations it started. His point is that you do not have to go to vipassana or sit on a silk cushion "в окружении красивых книг и интерьеров" (*surrounded by beautiful books and interiors*) to practise mindfulness, and that many people may need something quite different. Inverted meditations "идут против распространенных представлений о практиках осознанности и саморегуляции" (*go against common ideas about mindfulness and self-regulation practices*). Most practice tries to create a place free of distraction and everyday difficulty. Inverted practice does the opposite and deliberately meets discomfort, "тренируя ум не убегать" (*training the mind not to run away*). The author says this kind of training can quickly build stability and strength. It can also bring wisdom: it can completely change how we see reality and save us from "тупикового пути непрестанного поиска комфорта и удовольствия" (*the dead-end path of the endless search for comfort and pleasure*). The four practices come from Andrew Holecek, a teacher of contemplative traditions from Colorado [msg #1328].

#### 1. Meditation on pain
- **What:** you deliberately make contact with physical pain instead of avoiding it. The goal is not to get rid of the pain but to change your relationship to it, and to see how it changes when you do not project fear and aversion onto it [msg #1328].
- **Step 1, choose a mild source of discomfort.** Examples: a gentle tingling in a finger, slight muscle fatigue, or even an ordinary headache [msg #1328].
- **Step 2, go into the sensation.** Do not distract yourself or complain. Break the sensation into its elements: intensity, temperature and pulsation [msg #1328].
- **Step 3, watch how the pain changes** when you do not try to suppress or avoid it [msg #1328].
- **Why it works:** "Когда мы сопротивляемся боли, мы страдаем вдвойне – от самой боли и от страха перед ней" (*When we resist pain, we suffer twice: from the pain itself and from the fear of it*). The practice teaches you to work with discomfort without panic [msg #1328].

#### 2. Deliberate stimulation of thinking ("намеренная стимуляция мыслетворчества")
- **What:** most meditation tries to calm the mind. This practice goes the opposite way. You deliberately speed up the flow of thought, load the brain and watch what happens [msg #1328].
- **Step 1, start with shamatha.** Stabilise attention by observing the breath or another object [msg #1328].
- **Step 2, consciously race the mind.** Think as fast as you can: recall cities, buildings, books and people's names, and build mental constructions [msg #1328].
- **Step 3, notice the moment** when the speed of thought hits its limit. Observe that state without being frightened by the chaos [msg #1328].
- **Why:** the practice shows that thoughts are not the enemy, and that you can stay present even when overloaded [msg #1328].

#### 3. Practice with chaos
- **What:** people often look for a quiet, secluded place to meditate. In this practice you put yourself in a noisy, chaotic environment and learn to stay present [msg #1328].
- **Step 1, create as chaotic a space as possible.** Put on loud music, or try meditating in the metro or on a street full of bars [msg #1328].
- **Step 2, relax into the chaos** instead of resisting the irritants [msg #1328].
- **Step 3, feel how you can be at peace** even when everything around you is unstable [msg #1328].
- **Why:** in life we cannot always control our surroundings. The practice teaches you to stay present anywhere and to turn every irritation into fuel for practice [msg #1328].

#### 4. Meeting unpleasant emotions
- **What:** instead of avoiding unpleasant emotions and fears, you deliberately make contact with their sources. One way is to watch horror films or other material that provokes strong reactions [msg #1328].
- **Step 1, choose a moderate ("модерируемый") trigger** that brings up disgust, fear or other unpleasant emotions. The wording implies a controlled dose, not an overwhelming one [msg #1328].
- **Step 2, observe your reactions** instead of looking away. Ask: what exactly causes the fear? How does the body react? [msg #1328]
- **Step 3, accept the feelings as part of yourself** without trying to get rid of them [msg #1328].
- **Why:** the practice teaches you to work with fear and unpleasant emotion directly, without suppressing them [msg #1328].

**Overall lesson.** Inverted meditations help correct a view of practice that modern wellness culture often treats like spa treatments. "Практики — не про приятное отключение от реальности" (*Practices are not about pleasantly switching off from reality*). They are more like a mountain trek: it can give you incredible experiences, but you will run into a lot along the way [msg #1328].

### Regulation-first retreat format for an exhausted nervous system
**Context.** The author had just finished a two-week retreat. His first retreat was 17 years earlier, so he is not a beginner. Even so, he says the last 3–4 years were among the hardest for his practice. Constant adaptive stress, frequent moves between countries, many risks and much unpredictability exhausted his nervous system, and that affected his ability to move forward in practice [msg #1688].

**The format, as he describes it:**
1. **Accept the unpleasant reality.** For him, the line between healthy effort and breakdown had become very thin. An extra half hour of concentration can easily wreck his sleep, and a setback in practice follows [msg #1688].
2. **Put regulation and stabilisation before development and insight.** He says plainly that "развитие и инсайт уступили место регуляции и стабилизации" (*development and insight gave way to regulation and stabilisation*) [msg #1688].
3. **Cap the volume.** His maximum is now about 3 hours of meditation a day, sometimes 5 [msg #1688].
4. **Simplify the content.** He dropped advanced methods and kept only the simplest forms of shamatha and metta, with special emphasis on relaxation [msg #1688].
5. **No forcing anywhere.** He favours simple presence and informal practice (see *Informal practice as the core*) [msg #1688].
6. **Be honest instead of idealistic.** This "санаторный режим" (*sanatorium mode*) is not what he wanted from a retreat, but he calls it "самая честная форма практики, которая сейчас возможна" (*the most honest form of practice possible right now*) [msg #1688].

**Generalisation and limits.** The author is increasingly convinced that this format suits most modern people. He adds that it is not a universal recommendation and not a romanticising of exhaustion. Development should not be abandoned. He is, however, increasingly sceptical of hard jumps into intensive retreats, especially for beginners, and especially those with no movement and fast schedules of 10–12, sometimes 14 hours of practice a day. Under today's load on the nervous system and on attention, he considers these increasingly trauma-prone ("травмаопасно"). His condition for such immersions: you need to know yourself and the practice well, or it is a lottery rather than a path [msg #1688].

### State versus path: the jhana framework
**Definition.** The author describes jhanas as states of deep concentration described in the Pali canon that arise from stable attention. Classically there are 8. They are marked by strong one-pointedness of attention, a sharp drop in mental noise and a narrowing of the perceptual field. Subjectively they may feel like peace, bliss, clarity or serenity, with a weakened sense of the body, of time and of the usual sense of self. From the standpoint of modern psychology, the author says, jhanas can be seen as a special class of altered states of consciousness. He links them to suppression of the default mode network, strengthening of voluntary attention networks and marked parasympathetic activation [msg #1698].

**In the traditions, a tool, not a goal.**
- In Theravada, where they belong, jhanas were never an end in themselves. They were treated as a possible tool: a temporary suppression of hindrances, training in stable concentration, a preparatory stage. In jhana the field of discernment is strongly narrowed. There is bliss, peace and one-pointedness, but little room to recognise the characteristics of reality, which means little room for insight to develop [msg #1698].
- For this reason the tradition itself argued for centuries over whether jhanas help insight develop or become "тонким тупиком, если в них застревать" (*a subtle dead end if you get stuck in them*) [msg #1698].
- In Tibetan Buddhism jhanas also never became a central practice. There, concentration is needed less for absorption in jhana than for further work in vipashyana, mahamudra or dzogchen [msg #1698].

**Three reasons for the current boom.** In recent years there has been a sharp rise in interest in jhanas in the English-speaking, especially American, secular mindfulness community. Courses and guides promise access to these states and ways to stabilise and repeat them. The author argues this looks like a return to the classics but is more a cultural phenomenon typical of the present moment. He gives three reasons [msg #1698]:
1. **Chronic nervous-system activation.** Modern people live with chronic activation of the nervous system, fragmented attention and constant information overload. Against that background, states of deep peace and bliss feel like a rare and valuable resource [msg #1698].
2. **Fatigue with "dry" mindfulness.** Recent decades of mindfulness culture stressed neutral observation, contact with sensations and discipline of attention. Jhanas look like an alternative, a meditation in which you can feel genuinely good in the usual sense of the word. The author's phrase: "Это как вещества без веществ" (*It's like substances without substances*) [msg #1698].
3. **The logic of the market and achievement.** Jhanas package neatly into levels, skills and measurable goals. They fit a culture of self-optimisation that values controllability, repeatability and reproducibility of experience [msg #1698].

**Where the boundary lies.**
- *Appropriate uses:* jhanas are not bad in themselves. They can fit as a stage in developing concentration on the path, or as temporary stabilisation of the nervous system and recovery of resources when exhausted. The author adds a bracketed doubt about the second use: "хотя вопросительно как в джану попасть при истощении" (*though it is questionable how to get into jhana when exhausted*). Either way, this holds only if you do not give jhanas what they are not, such as special meaning, or treat them as a path of development in their own right [msg #1698].
- *Problem uses:* "Проблема начинается там, где состояние подменяет собой путь" (*The problem starts where the state replaces the path*). That happens when jhanas become part of your identity, a way to avoid unresolved pain, or are declared to be the path. In psychological terms, the author says, these ways of relating to jhanas look like a subtle form of dissociation, "социально одобряемую и эстетически привлекательную" (*socially approved and aesthetically attractive*) [msg #1698].
- *Conclusion:* the author's verdict is that the modern interest in jhanas is less a meaningful contemplative path than the culture's response to overload, fragmentation and loss of depth: an attempt to find a controllable, pleasant state in a world that has lost its stability [msg #1698].

### Dark night of the soul as post-peak loss
**Popular versus original meaning.** Today the phrase "темная ночь души" (*dark night of the soul*) is often used in psychotherapy, trauma work and development practices. It usually means a hard stage of change: a person starts rebuilding something in themselves, old ways of living and understanding the world stop working, the new ones have not formed yet, and the gap between them is hard. The author grants that this makes sense and matches an experience many people know. He then says the concept originally meant something quite different [msg #1701].

**The original (John of the Cross).** The term first appears in John of the Cross, whom the author calls one of the most attentive explorers of human inner experience in his time. What John described comes not before an inner breakthrough but after it. It follows an experience of deeper inner connectedness, openness and wholeness, which are clearly special states in which the world for a while feels alive and full of meaning and presence. Then the state leaves and the person returns to ordinary everyday consciousness. The real nightmare comes from the gap between the fullness you lived and its absence. Things have not become worse than before. What is unbearable is going back to the old, flatter way of organising life. The person has seen another way of being, feeling and thinking, and now lives with that knowledge. In the author's words, "Это опыт утраты" (*It is an experience of loss*): the loss of the world's former obviousness, former supports and former psychic ecosystem [msg #1701].

**Parallels in modern psychology.** The author says modern psychology describes the same thing in other words:
1. **Donald Winnicott.** Contact with a more authentic state of Self makes it impossible to go on living only through adaptations. While a person lives in a mode of functioning and conforming to ordinary social norms, much can work. After touching a more alive and whole way of being, returning to the old forms of organisation feels inwardly false and painful. The suffering comes not from something breaking but from losing the illusions that used to hold the psyche together [msg #1701].
2. **David Yaden, post-peak experiences.** According to the author, Yaden's work shows that after peak experiences some people have a temporary drop in subjective wellbeing. At a deeper level, though, complexity of thinking, sensitivity and clarity of values often grow. "Рост не сопровождается ощущением комфорта" (*Growth is not accompanied by a feeling of comfort*) [msg #1701].
3. **Robert Kegan.** Kegan, a Harvard psychology professor, describes a similar structure, though for developmental levels rather than states. His theory has transitional periods where the old system of meaning can no longer hold up against reality and the new one has not yet formed. A person may then lose their usual integration and suffer greatly. This is not weakness but a natural crisis of growth between levels of organisation of consciousness [msg #1701].

**The common logic.** In all these accounts the person suffers not because something is missing but "потому что невозможно больше не знать" (*because it is impossible to not know any more*). You cannot return to the old simplicity, explanations and ordinariness. "Это кризис уровня смысла" (*It is a crisis at the level of meaning*) [msg #1701].

**Misdiagnosis.** Such states are often mistaken for burnout, depression or plain inefficiency. People try to treat them with rest, new goals, productivity strategies or yet more self-optimisation. The author says what is really happening is the breakdown of an old psychic ecosystem and the need to move to a new way of being in the world [msg #1701].

**The author's own case.** The author says he lived in this state, where ordinary life felt meaningless, and suffered in it for almost ten years. It was not an acute crisis but a background, drawn-out pain of mismatch. He kept working and reaching goals, but all of it felt fairly silly and flat compared with what he already knew about himself and life. The suffering almost completely passed through a total rebuilding of everything around him. That took years and is probably not finished. He stresses that this was not a search for a better version of himself but learning to live from a completely different foundation [msg #1701].

### Six discoveries of the Harvard extended-cessation study
**The study, as the author describes it.** At the end of 2025 there was a small stir and active discussion in science around Harvard research on extremely deep meditative states in which ordinary cognitive activity drops sharply or almost disappears. Traditionally these states are considered the end of suffering and one of the endpoints of the path. The research was done within the Harvard Meditation Research Project. It observed experienced meditators who can deliberately enter a deep state of cessation of conditioned experience, where there is no separate self but consciousness remains. The author names this "ниродха саматхи" [sic] (*nirodha samapatti*). High-precision MRI recorded brain-activity patterns that differed clearly from waking, from sleep and from loss of consciousness [msg #1740].

**The six findings**, from the attached infographic headed "Meditation Research Program, Massachusetts General Hospital / Harvard Medical School — 6 Discoveries on the Scientific Path to Enlightenment: EXTENDED CESSATION, MAPPED" [img #1740]:
1. **A Radical Reorganization.** Core brain systems, including visual, executive, default mode, subcortical and brainstem systems, restructured significantly. This reflects a distinct functional mode during cessation [img #1740].
2. **A Global Quieting.** Most brain regions communicated less, especially between the brainstem and cortex. Only select sensory and subcortical hubs stayed active [img #1740].
3. **Polarized, Not Powered Off.** Unlike in sleep or anaesthesia, the brain became more polarised. This suggests that certain sensory and cognitive functions may sharpen during cessation [img #1740].
4. **Sharp Energy Decline.** Overall energy use dropped steeply, especially in memory-linked regions. This indicates deep cortical quieting consistent with a mental "shutdown" [img #1740].
5. **The Clarity Signature.** Activity patterns aligned with areas rich in histamine H3 receptors, which are linked to alertness and clarity, and calmed in regions tied to emotion and cognition [img #1740].
6. **Absence of Suffering.** Cessation states correlated with brain modes linked to clarity and alertness, not with those associated with psychological suffering such as loss, pain, anxiety, fear or stress [img #1740].

**The author's interpretation.**
- Science is not yet making loud claims, but this is a fairly clear confirmation that subjective reports of radically altered states in traditional practice are true and not mere fantasy, and that they can come with observable neurophysiological changes. The author admits his own amusement: "Я в этом месте немного ржу и мотаю головой" (*At this point I laugh a bit and shake my head*). Still, he says the fact that such states can be recorded with instruments already changes the scientific discussion about meditation and about the nature of consciousness [msg #1740].
- He calls it the first report of its kind describing possibly measurable signs of a state that Buddhist literature traditionally describes as the highest state of peace. The research cautiously introduces the hypothesis that consciousness and subjectivity can exist in modes where usual cognitive processing is minimal. In his words, this "пока еще не опровергает материализм" (*does not yet refute materialism*) but significantly widens how we picture the mind working [msg #1740].
- Its main significance, he argues, is not religious or about meditation culture. It bears on a basic question of modern science and philosophy: is consciousness necessarily tied to continuous information processing and a stream of thought? The dominant model today describes the brain as a predictive system that constantly models reality. The author says these findings point beyond that model: they suggest states where the model stops operating while consciousness remains. Meditation then becomes a unique tool for studying the structure of subjective experience rather than a relaxation technique [msg #1740].
- **Convergence of maps.** For millennia, contemplative traditions built maps of inner reality, while modern science developed methods of objective external measurement. These lines long existed almost independently and are now starting to meet. They are forming a new field of consciousness research in which subjective experience is no longer seen as a purely personal or mystical phenomenon. The author says that subjectivity gradually becoming a legitimate object of scientific interest amounts to "целая революцию" (*a whole revolution*) [msg #1740].
- **AI implication.** Much of technology culture rests on a bold reductionist assumption: model thinking well enough and you get consciousness. The author argues that this and other research open a new scientific branch showing that thinking is only part of the mind, possibly a very narrow part. If that is so, he concludes, "предел AI — это очень мощный когнитивный интеллект, но никогда не сознающее существо" (*the limit of AI is a very powerful cognitive intelligence, but never a conscious being*) [msg #1740].
- **Closing claim.** The author says the end of suffering the traditions spoke of seems to be real and not invented, and that science is starting to suspect it [msg #1740].

### What a retreat changed: a Big 3 consultant's report
The author shared the conclusions of a participant of the deep mind mindfulness course, a consultant at a Big 3 firm, whom the team helped prepare for a retreat and integrate its results; the retreat was at a classic Thai meditation center ("не Гоенка, т.к. я настроен к ней скептически" — not Goenka, since I'm skeptical of it). ### Lessons from the first deep mind retreat (integrative format and safety)

### Vipassana: what it is, Goenka, and where to go
The author's introduction to meditation was a vipassana retreat 17 years before the post; it had such an impact that he has since tried to do at least one vipassana retreat a year [msg #1280].

- **Definition and tradition.** Vipassana is a traditional Buddhist practice present in versions in all major branches of Buddhism. Although it requires no religious affiliation and can be practiced with any worldview, the author finds it "совершенно неясно" (completely unclear) how non-Buddhists can lead retreats in it. It became widely known via southern Theravada Buddhism, which gives it much attention; a big refresh came from the systematisation and revival by the Burmese teacher Mahasi Sayadaw in the 20th century (the author met his direct student Ajahn Tong several times in northern Thailand). Most people know it through Goenka's centers, whose global peak was in the 1990s–2000s [msg #1280].
- **Critique of Goenka.** Goenka made an important contribution to spreading contemplative methods in the West, but what was spread and how raises questions. Goenka did not study with Mahasi Sayadaw and is not connected to his lineage; a businessman, he trained with U Ba Khin, who formally did not belong to any lineage and developed his own system based on mindful observation of physical sensations — "такой бодискан вместо традиционного осознания всех феноменов" (a kind of body scan instead of the traditional awareness of all phenomena). Goenka emphasised universalising and simplifying Buddhist practice, which made him popular. Though many benefited, the author judges the retreats controversial: sectarian in essence, transmitting a system no one verifies and simplifying the original approach and its philosophy; and, combined with strict rules and isolation, they often aggravate the common neurotic pattern of getting results through pain, creating a high risk of retraumatisation, "которая как факт часто происходит на ретритах Гоенки" (which as a fact often happens at Goenka retreats) [msg #1280, #138].
- **What vipassana is not.** "Випассана — это не наблюдение за дыханием, не 10 дней молчания, еды по два раза в день и аскетичных условий жизни." (Vipassana is not watching the breath, not 10 days of silence, eating twice a day and ascetic living conditions.) In Buddhism it is the deepest and incredibly advanced practice of insight, in which a concentrated, trained mind is directed at direct empirical investigation of the nature of reality [msg #1280].
- **Most beginners don't reach it — but still benefit.** Whether at Goenka or in more traditional places, most beginners essentially do not get to real vipassana, because access requires theoretical preparation and developed strength of attention. This does not cancel the great benefit of 7–10–14 days of intensive practice that forces us to direct attention to our experience, "inward", instead of the modern habit of living with attention somewhere out there — in the phone, thoughts, conversations — completely detached from immediate experience [msg #1280].
- **Preparation and integration.** For the modern psyche any vipassana retreat is an intense experience of an altered state of consciousness (though traditions see our ordinary state as the altered one — a trance of everyday life, far from the norm and causing suffering). Therefore the most important elements are preparation and subsequent integration, without which we usually cannot build the results into life; this needs qualified teachers who understand the tradition and are trauma-informed [msg #1280].

Where the author recommends doing traditional vipassana [msg #1280]:
1. Monasteries of Southeast Asia in the Mahasi Sayadaw tradition — he likes Wat Rampoeng and Wat Chomtong in Chiang Mai.
2. Maechee Brigitte — rather in the forest tradition, which pays more attention to loving kindness.
3. In Russia — the Paramita center, specifically Nadezhda Lyakhovich, whom he has known for many years "по тайским тропкам" (from Thai paths).
4. He himself awaits such a retreat from Alan Wallace or Glenn Svenson, who give these practices from the context of the Tibetan tradition and Dzogchen.

### "Life changing": what ten days of vipassana give
Visiting a young man in Brooklyn who had a vipassana center brochure, the author heard him describe his vipassana experience as "Life changing" — something that positively transforms our life, dividing it into before and after. The author reflected that he had stopped using the phrase, apparently because he had already forgotten what life was like before. One can say meditation gives calm, reduces stress, helps understand oneself — or one can look someone in the eyes and say these two words, which in some ways describe the experience much better. His claim: after 10 days of practice, as after other life-changing experiences, you understand where most of your suffering comes from, and how to change it — knowledge that truly turns things upside down [msg #1072].

### Classical tantra vs neo-tantra
The author enthusiastically recommends Lobsang Tenpa's lecture "Что такое тантра?" (What is tantra?, ~70 minutes), saying it is mandatory viewing for anyone interested in classical or neo-tantra and in inner work generally, because it gives a healthy, important reference point so we clearly understand what we are doing, what is happening and where it can or cannot lead. The lecture surveys: (1) **classical tantra**, which developed on the Indian subcontinent over the last 2,500 years; and (2) **neo-tantra** — a balanced, clear analysis of the scattered, usually unsystematic set of practices and initiatives that flowed out of New Age, promising to open all blocks, new peaks of pleasure and authentic self-knowledge, encountered in recent years at trainings near Moscow, on Phangan, Bali and in San Francisco. The author ironically mocks neo-tantric promises ("освободились от блоков, раскрыли чакры и взошли на Эверест наслаждения" — freed yourselves from blocks, opened chakras and climbed the Everest of pleasure). He values that the Russian-speaking space has such a researcher and access to authentic inner-work materials in the native language; he also recommends his own ~40-minute interview with Lobsang Tenpa, "Crisis of mindfulness" [msg #1166]. The accompanying photos are Colorado landscapes and a personal photo with no instructional content [msg #1166].

### Why the author goes on retreat: freedom from reactivity
The author wants to do a 50-day retreat next, though he does not know how to fit it with the rest of his life that year. He says he wants a long retreat not because he likes eating rice and sitting alone in a room, but because he sees the results of practice, and they overturn the usual ideas of how wellbeing works. We spend huge effort trying to be happy and not suffer, constantly compensating, improving and optimising, but practice shows again and again that liberation appears somewhere else entirely [msg #1688].

After the two weeks he felt, more strongly than in a long time, free from automatic grasping and constant craving. He stresses that this is not detachment from life. He describes it as "ощущение глубинной достаточности тем, что уже есть" (*a sense of deep sufficiency with what already is*), in which the need for a tiring, constant search to improve experience or fill an emptiness disappears. His everyday example: he can have coffee after a meal or not, and it makes no difference. When something goes differently from what he expected, he simply adapts to the new conditions [msg #1688].

### Informal practice as the core
The author discussed this at length with Manfred, a 70-year-old instructor who was a monk in his youth and now spends half the year at home in Germany and half at the centre helping practitioners. In the author's account, Westerners especially tend to miss the value of attentive presence in ordinary activities because we bring achievement-mindedness ("достигаторство") into meditation. We tend to think real practice only happens on the cushion: that is where I make progress, not from being mindful while I eat [msg #1688].

The author calls this not quite right. In both Mahasi Sayadaw's approach and Thich Nhat Hanh's tradition, everyday mindfulness is "не дополнение, а ядро практики" (*not an add-on but the core of practice*). It covers walking, eating, washing, writing and contact with others. This is where conscious action begins to free itself from excess effort and constant self-coercion. He sees this as critically important for people living in a modern, increasingly complex world where we are used to always demanding something of ourselves. Otherwise, he warns, "мы лишь воспроизводим и закрепляем те паттерны насилия, которые так нас утомляют и нередко ломают" (*we only reproduce and reinforce the patterns of violence that exhaust us so much and often break us*) [msg #1688].

### Letting go of how practice "should" be
The author ends his retreat report open-ended. Maybe later he will again be able to sit for many hours, and maybe he will manage a long retreat, or maybe not. He calls letting go of clinging to the idea of how things should be one of the hardest and most important steps in practice, and says he is still learning it [msg #1688].

## Practical playbook

- Treat a retreat as intensive work, not a spa vacation; get help with preparation before and integration after (the Big 3 consultant's case and the author's vipassana advice) [msg #138, #1280].
- Expect post-retreat habits to be worth protecting deliberately: early rising (by 6:30), no dinner, phone only after lunch and not after 6–7 pm, notifications off, no news — the consultant's maintained changes [msg #138].
- Outside retreat, about 2 hours a day of formal sitting (e.g. 45–60 minutes twice daily) is the maximum the author has sustained long-term in a busy life; when the routine collapses, use a mini-vipassana rather than self-criticism [msg #991].
- Pick retreat teachers who know the tradition and are trauma-informed; be wary of strict-rule, isolated formats that reinforce "results through pain" [msg #1280, #290].
- When observing pain in meditation, check your inner phrase: "not my experience" signals dissociation; "my experience, but no 'I' in it" signals the correct witness [msg #982].
- Use daily irritations as the practice ground — the author deliberately exposes himself to Canggu's stimuli of desire and aversion to practice compassion and involvement [msg #1327].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The author's preference for advanced (tantra/dzogchen) over basic southern-Buddhist methods is explicitly an opinion ("у меня есть основания полагать"), balanced by his insistence that a foundation in concentration and ethics comes first [msg #1252].

The posts do not reconcile these; the later post distinguishes "real vipassana" from what beginners experience at retreats.
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the Yale study cited as confirming the integrative-format hypothesis is only linked, not described [msg #290].
- The Big 3 consultant's report is a single self-report written 10 days after the retreat; he himself notes the method is a waste of time for many people [msg #138].
- The critique of Goenka (sectarian, retraumatising) is the author's assessment; he acknowledges many people benefited [msg #1280, #138].
- The author notes that states like bliss and self-closure are not the goal and clinging to them causes suffering; similarly, he says changing his state is not the goal of his practice [msg #870, #991].
- The author promised to write separately about how mini-vipassana supports mindfulness levels and to add his own commentary on the tantra lecture; neither is in this source [msg #991, #1166].

- (!) CONTRADICTION / UNCLEAR: the author suggests jhanas may serve as temporary nervous-system stabilisation when exhausted, then questions in the same breath how anyone could get into jhana while exhausted. The source leaves this unresolved [msg #1698].
- The author marks his regulation-first format as his conviction for "most modern people", not a universal recommendation, and explicitly not a romanticising of exhaustion. He still holds that development should not be abandoned [msg #1688].
