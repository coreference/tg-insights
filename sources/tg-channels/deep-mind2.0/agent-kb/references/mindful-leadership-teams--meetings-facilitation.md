# Meetings and group facilitation

## What this covers

This topic gathers the channel author's (deep mind founder, a team facilitator and consultant) teaching on meetings and group facilitation: the cost of bad meetings, rules and roles for good meetings, the leader's shift into a facilitator role with every member as co-facilitator, preparing participants' state for deep workshops, the move from "safe space" to "brave space", why top teams need an external facilitator, a structured year-end team session, and how to protect a group from attention capture through clear, early rules. It also includes deep mind's case reports (Qiwi, a Moscow private school, T-Bank's HR leadership team) as worked examples. According to the source, meetings are "almost the most important and expensive part of team work" because opinions are formed and decisions made there, and trust and psychological safety, which facilitation builds, are a key factor of team effectiveness [msg #345, #971].

## Core ideas

- **Bad meetings cost twice: during and after.** Unfacilitated meetings are a bottomless barrel of lost time, and frustrating ones also cost recovery time afterwards (meeting recovery syndrome). See *Meeting recovery syndrome* [msg #217].
- **Good meetings are rare, small, short, prepared and role-based.** See *Seven rules for good meetings* and *Basic meeting roles* [msg #219, #345, #346, #347].
- **The leader becomes a facilitator; everyone else is a co-facilitator.** Quality group process cannot be one-sided; each member owns their behaviour and contribution to the atmosphere [msg #284, #345].
- **Subtle group work requires switching the nervous system into reflective mode** via safety, body relaxation and calming attention [msg #1070].
- **Total safety is an illusion; aim for a brave space** that keeps safety requirements but adds courage and a focus on repair [msg #1234].
- **Systems reproduce themselves; strong teams need an outside facilitator** to see the culture's blind spots, especially at the top where power, status and responsibility dominate [msg #1382].
- **The end of a high-load period should be processed, not skipped**, via a facilitated session that harvests learning and closes emotional tails [msg #971, #1638].
- **Healthy, transparent, institutionalised power and early rules protect a group**; their absence produces shadow influence and a facilitator who suddenly looks like an autocrat [msg #1666].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Meeting recovery syndrome
Purpose: a lens for seeing the hidden cost of meetings that are badly facilitated or communicated in an "unecological" way [msg #217].

- The author opens with two jokes: a meeting is a gathering where people speak without saying anything of substance, after which everyone present disagrees with what was said; and meetings are vampires sucking life out of smart, creative people and money out of the company [msg #217].
- Seriously: meetings of all kinds now take the larger part of our time in organisations. If they are not skilfully facilitated, they act as a collective bottomless barrel of lost employee time. No meeting is free — every meeting costs money [msg #217].
- The added perspective: we lose not only *during* ineffective meetings but *after* them. If a meeting gives no clear result, or communication is conducted unecologically, people leave in negative emotions. They then "recover" from the meeting and spend other people's time complaining about colleagues [msg #217].
- The author cites US management research: the cost of unproductive time after frustrating meetings is $800,000 per year per 1,000 employees. This phenomenon is called meeting recovery syndrome and is linked to huge efficiency losses [msg #217].
- Lesson drawn by the author: these facts are "another bullet" into the traditional management paradigm that ignores people's inner world and considers it unmeasurable [msg #217].

### Seven rules for good meetings (collective wisdom summary)
Purpose: a summary the author compiled of "collective wisdom" from a discussion of meeting recovery syndrome, answering "how to run successful meetings?" [msg #219]
1. **Meet only out of real necessity.** All meetings are planned only when genuinely needed — and that need is more often about removing emotional tensions and miscommunications than about solving tasks [msg #219].
2. **Keep the participant list as small as possible.** Meetings are not required for information exchange; there are "a million other ways" to synchronise [msg #219].
3. **No meeting longer than one hour.** Build in mandatory breaks to drink water, do physical warm-ups and switch focus. Prefer physical stand-ups (meetings standing up) or meetings while walking [msg #219].
4. **A well-prepared agenda, in the spirit of Amazon meetings.** The main work is done outside the meeting [msg #219].
5. **Every participant clearly understands their role.** If a person's role is needed for 5 minutes, invite them for exactly those 5 minutes and release them once the question is resolved [msg #219].
6. **Guaranteed focus time without meetings or conversations** — no less than half of all working time [msg #219].
7. **Your own mechanism for raising collective awareness**, for example centering or a minute of silence before the meeting starts [msg #219].

Further reading recommended in the same post: Bridgewater's meeting organisation (Ray Dalio's meeting process checklist) and Ray Dalio's principle of idea meritocracy, which the author calls important for understanding the very essence of meetings in effective companies [msg #219].

### Two first steps to constructive meetings
Purpose: the most important first thing to do to move meetings from frustrating to constructive. Context: when consultants start improving communication in a team, they observe its meetings; even though participants adjust a little in front of an outsider, habitual communication patterns cannot be hidden. The team must understand why each meeting is needed and how to move toward goals through collective discussion [msg #345].

1. **Define and communicate the meeting's tasks to all participants (ideally in advance):**
   - *Goal of the meeting* — what we want to resolve, why we are meeting at all.
   - *Specific expected results* — how we see the outcome of the discussion that will achieve the goal [msg #345].
2. **Distribute the basic roles needed to move toward the result.** Moving a group to goals alone requires extraordinary facilitation skill; a facilitator focusing the group cannot be alone. There is always a **main facilitator** — the person who convenes the group and defines the goals — but every participant also takes the role of **co-facilitator**: watching their own and colleagues' behaviour and checking whether the group is progressing toward the goal. According to the author, only this creates the quality "field of attention" needed for focus and meaningful dialogue [msg #345].

### Basic meeting roles
Purpose: deep mind consulting's description of the basic roles used to set up meetings in flexible modern teams, based on Thomas Kayser's classic facilitation methodology (Xerox); the author says that relying on these roles can quickly improve the quality of group communication [msg #345]. Five roles, from two slides [img #346, #347]:

**1. Main facilitator (основной фасилитатор)** [img #346]
- Convenes the meeting; is the main coordinator.
- Prepares the goal and the results of the event.
- Supports successful achievement of the event's set goals.
- Watches group dynamics: observes the course of events and intervenes to keep the discussion aligned with the meeting's goals.
- Changes "hats" into the role of manager or expert if they switch to the subject matter of the discussion itself (i.e., makes explicit when they stop facilitating and start contributing content).

**2. Co-facilitators (ко-фасилитаторы)** — the role of *all* other participants; they share responsibility for the meeting's productivity [img #346, #347]. Their duties:
- Carefully watch their own behaviour and colleagues' behaviour.
- Try to minimise behaviour that is destructive relative to the meeting's goal.
- Intervene and stop any conversations or conflicts that negatively affect the meeting's productivity [img #346].
- Warn every time the group drifts off the path to the meeting goal — a code word is needed to make this easier [img #347].
- Stop destructive behaviour in the group: side-topic conversations, domination by one participant, use of phones [img #347].
- Say so when the discussion has descended into nitpicking and insignificant issues [img #347].
- Point out when they feel a decision on the question has already formed but discussion is still going on (the moderator checks this) [img #347].
- Do not allow participants, without weighty reasons, to return to already closed questions on which a decision was made [img #347].
- Try to obtain the maximum of needed information and get participants' opinions, especially from the quiet ones [img #347].
- Acknowledge participants' merits and encourage them; give short interim summaries during discussion [img #347].
- Check correct understanding of the question at the slightest doubt; perform any other actions that help the main facilitator [img #347].

**3. Timekeeper (хронометрист)** [img #346]
- Carefully tracks how much time the group spends on the meeting's tasks.
- Regularly tells the group how much time remains.
- The group (not the timekeeper) decides whether to stay on a question or move on.

**4. Minute-taker (протоколист)** [img #346]
- Records agreed decisions, specific assignments, decisions to stop discussing partially resolved questions, and the meeting's follow-up action plan.
- Reads out the summary at the end of the meeting, clarifying disagreements.
- Sends the document no later than 24 hours after the meeting, including to group members who were not present.

**5. Scribe (скрайбер)** [img #346]
- An optional role.
- Writes the main points of the meeting in front of the whole group.
- Creates a visible visual chronology of the discussion for easy access to the conversation's history.

### Distribution of responsibility for team atmosphere
Purpose: written as a response to feedback that a previous post on leaders' destructive behaviour demotivated some managers. Leaders who work hard at their role and their own development can feel guilt and exhaustion when something in the team is not working, and texts about leaders' flaws deepen that. The author's remedy is clarifying the zones of responsibility for behaviour and atmosphere in a team [msg #284].

- **Context shift.** As organisational forms and corporate culture develop, groups stop being hierarchical "manager and executors" units and become teams needing flexible interaction and the wisdom of every member. Growing company complexity and global competition require everyone to grow personally and develop new skills, but leaders get "a bit more": their role in creating team relationships is decisive and cannot be denied [msg #284].
- **Leader's zone.** The leader's role is transforming: leaders in modern teams increasingly become facilitators, so their key task — for which they are definitely responsible — is removing difficulties and obstacles on the way to group goals. The leader is not to blame for an employee's sexism or ageism, but is responsible for modelling trust and open communication and for regular co-creation and clarification of the group's expectations, goals and shared norms [msg #284].
- **Employees' zone.** Employees' role changes too: they can no longer be in child–parent relationships with the manager and organisation, as used to be the norm. From facilitation theory, a quality group process is impossible one-sidedly; each team member must understand their role as a co-facilitator ("КОфасилитатор") and be aware of their share of responsibility for the common result. Everyone in this environment is responsible for their own behaviour and contribution to the shared atmosphere [msg #284].
- The author's stated aim with critical posts: to draw attention to current corporate culture and behaviour that prevents teams from working engaged and with pleasure — the same destructive behaviour types can be traced in employees at any level, followed by thinking about how they affect team results and what to do [msg #284].

### Mimosa cocktail: switching a group into reflective mode
Purpose: physiological conditions for moving participants from a fussy, task-driven state into the reflective state that subtle group work (workshops on self-knowledge, strengths, values) needs [msg #1070].

Context and mechanism: the author ran deep mind's own internal workshop on superpowers and the "zone of genius" — subtle work in which people step away from usual roles and task talk, at first awkwardly, then more freely into "beautiful and quite vulnerable" themes of human nature. Due to time-zone difference and lack of sleep he was not in reflective mode himself and "literally saw" opportunities closing because of it. Teams often burst into such workshops straight from a flow of tasks that has nothing to do with reflection, attentive awareness and slowly savouring contact with oneself and the world. Without switching modes of the nervous system, subtle and useful things seem immaterial, not very applicable or unimportant — the inner voice says: "Да-да, ваша осознанность и таланты, конечно, хороши, но у меня там реальные задачи висят, а я тут время трачу." (Yes, yes, your mindfulness and talents are nice, but I have real tasks hanging there and I'm wasting time here.) Change the state, and the idea transforms: reflective mode helps understand oneself and other people, find creative solutions, directly affects well-being, and from access to slow states come systemic understanding and clear insights, from which order and strength arise. Not every trainer has a view on nervous-system state, nor must they, but everyone working with subtle questions in a group suffers from a fussy mode when depth is needed [msg #1070].

Ingredients:
1. **Safety.** The more risk and uncertainty in society and at work, the less people care about fine-tuning — and the more possibility of living at full potential we lose. To sink into a reflective state a person needs to feel safety and comfort: know what we are doing here, why, who these people are, how the space works. Neuro-regulation and regulation practices increase safety. In his workshops the author usually asks people to look around, feel their support, and runs short state check-ins to create safety at the level of the nervous system [msg #1070].
2. **Bodily relaxation.** Anxiety and stress keep a person toned up yet far from a useful receptive state. They always have a bodily correlate — physical tension that prevents sensing and simply being present in experience rather than only in thoughts. To go even slightly deeper, become aware of that tension and create a higher level of relaxation [msg #1070].
3. **Calming attention.** When attention jumps from task to task and object to object, we feel worse, experience stress, and lose the possibility of more satisfying contact with objects — a person nearby or one's own emotion. Mindfulness practices that go a bit beyond self-regulation and help notice the mind's fussiness have historically been used to change state; they help switch and tune to another wave [msg #1070].

### Safe space vs brave space
Purpose: deep mind's stance on the kind of space a facilitator creates; shared after a trauma lecture for facilitators of the Resonance programme. Safety remains extremely important, but deep mind now relies more on the concept of a space of courage and recovery [msg #1234].

History and rationale of safe space:
- In hard modern circumstances people crave a space to exhale, feel sheltered and live emotions calmly. Safety is one of the most important human needs and in a broad sense does not feel very available, so "safe space" is now invoked in almost any context — from the therapist's office to a circle of founders over a pint [msg #1234].
- In the 1970s feminists introduced "safe space" as a place where women could discuss sexism-related problems without risk of attack or judgment; the idea spread to other movements and marginalised groups as a tool for expressing vulnerability and building strength within a community [msg #1234].
- Its roots also lie in contemplative and spiritual traditions, which create a sacred space protected from outer chaos for deepening into inner processes. Because those systems formed when psychological trauma was poorly understood, modern practitioners borrowed the idea of an isolated space and enriched it with psychology [msg #1234].
- The key psychological foundation was "probably" laid by humanistic psychology: Carl Rogers' client-centred therapy stressed unconditional acceptance, empathy and authenticity to create an atmosphere where a person feels safe and can open up. According to the author, neuroscience later confirmed these empirical ideas: when a person feels safe, brain and nervous-system systems activate that make deep reflection, awareness and integration of experience possible [msg #1234].
- Further developments, especially breakthroughs in studying trauma, embodiment, sexuality and mindfulness, made safety not a nice feature but a necessary tool when work touches body, emotions and relationships. Skilfully striving for a safe space reduces the risk of retraumatisation — when intense experiences trigger painful memories and harm participants in the present. Thanks to this, today "only marginal" professionals doubt the need for ethics and a trauma-informed lens [msg #1234].

Three problems of the idea of total safety:
1. **Unrealistic illusions** — fully safe spaces do not exist; however hard the facilitator or group tries, someone can always get hurt in the process [msg #1234].
2. **Dogmatic prioritisation of safety** — it gets abused and effectively stalls the group process [msg #1234].
3. **Wrong expectations of no conflict, friction or tension** — which themselves end up causing retraumatisation [msg #1234].

Brave space: without in any way cancelling the focus on and requirement for safety, the idea shifts to courage and recovery. In a brave space we create not only safety but support courage to explore and express, and keep a focus on regenerating damage: something may happen, but it will not be left unattended, and both the participant and the group can be restored if needed, creating an inspiring experience of gaining strength [msg #1234].

### Changing the structure of attention: external facilitation of top teams
Purpose: deep mind's model for facilitating established (especially senior) teams, illustrated by work with the immediate team of T-Bank's HR director [msg #1382].

- **Power at the top.** Every team lives by laws of group dynamics and systemic constraints, but the higher the team in the structure and the larger the structure, the more of its internal process revolves around power, status and responsibility. The author's experience in federal government teams gave him the experience of a "purely power" process where everything else recedes and people deal almost exclusively with power; now he can clearly see power as a phenomenon that tends to be subtle, opaque, unspoken and therefore problem-generating [msg #1382].
- **Why external.** Teams live by the laws of systems, and systems reproduce themselves until someone brings in a new pattern. Strong teams often need an external facilitator not because competence is lacking inside, but because any internal culture, however mature, is limited by its own blind spots. Culture growing from founders and then shaped by current leaders creates its "invisible water" in which everyone swims — language, rhythm, ways of talking. Examples: Oleg Tinkov's spirit still invisibly present in T-Bank's culture, Arkady Volozh's or Ilya Segalovich's in Yandex — "not insider info, just logic" [msg #1382].
- **Method.** To remove obstacles for established teams, address the blind spots of their culture. As Otto Scharmer says, change the structure of attention, redirect leaders' and the group's attention, and show examples of other strategies and new role models. Facilitation works on many levels: in facilitation and educational sessions deep mind works with perception — smells, plants, changed seating. These send soft signals of novelty, and the group switches into exploration rather than defence mode; only then do words and a process arise in which something can shift out of the habitual [msg #1382].
- **Why the top leader cannot do this.** In many Russian teams the top leader sorts things out, but this is an archaic model: the leader is overloaded, often manipulated, increasingly busy with politics and losing touch with the ground, and responsibility and adult position sag in everyone else. Even the most advanced leader remains part of the system, with interests and a subtle but tangible role in the current arrangement, so cannot be neutral enough — and, per group dynamics theory, cannot be the one who carefully holds the field and changes the structure of interaction [msg #1382].
- **New model.** deep mind proposes a frame where not only the leader carries the team, but the team itself becomes a field that can see, feel and change itself; cultural limitations are transformed through practising a different way of being together. This requires the ability and skills to speak honestly and make the invisible visible. Such teams become more resilient than authoritarian-model teams that look strong but can unexpectedly fall apart at any moment [msg #1382].

### Year-end team facilitation process
Purpose: a group facilitation format deep mind designed three years earlier at an IT company's request to help a team pass from a high-load period to rest and the next stage; run regularly at year-end or after big projects [msg #1638].

Why: in the author's observation, September–December is when most teams work at maximum load, and almost always it ends the same way — the team simply vanishes into long holidays (someone leaves for a month, someone drops out for ten days, someone changes time zones). The dense cycle of joint work — stress, successes, mistakes, moments of mutual support — remains unprocessed "as if it never happened". An intense period is not only load and numbers but valuable lived experience; if it is not noticed and reflected on, and human processes not "backed up", people do not take their learning from it and often go on holiday with frustration and resentment [msg #1638].

What giving this moment space yields — the team:
- gathers the year's "harvest";
- completes unfinished emotional cycles;
- lets go of what is excess;
- consolidates successful patterns;
- enters vacation not exhausted but with inspiration and clarity [msg #1638].

The parts:
0. **Regulation and co-regulation.** Become aware of your own state and the state of other team members and bring it into a more stable, balanced form. The author calls this the most important foundation of any effective joint work, including summing up [msg #1638].
1. **Reflection and harvesting experience.** Calmly understand what really happened in the period: what worked, what was learned, which decisions brought results. Sometimes this is the first moment in a year when the team talks not about tasks but about its real dynamics [msg #1638].
2. **Cleaning part.** No drama, no blame. Naming tensions, apologising where needed, admitting mistakes. This is a light closing of emotional "tails" that otherwise sometimes drag on for years and affect the team's result [msg #1638].
3. **Strengthening strengths.** Becoming aware of what the team already does well, what became a resource this year, what it wants to keep and carry forward [msg #1638].
4. **Vector forward.** Defining what matters for the next stage — next year, quarter or new project; which values and vision to rely on; what intention to generate [msg #1638].

In essence this is a "meaningful corporate party" with the emphasis on connectedness, clarity and the ability to move on without old baggage, not on entertainment. With professional facilitators deep mind uses generative dialogue from Theory U, attention work and embodiment practices to run such processes ecologically and deeply, either as an established format or tailored to a team [msg #1638].

Why now more than classic celebrations: against the background of strong stress in recent years or general fatigue, this human component becomes "literally healing". Teams now more often need not alcohol or a cooking class but real attention. People do love to have fun and such formats need not be cancelled, but nothing replaces a sense of meaning — that we see each other, not just tasks; that important words were said aloud rather than swept under the rug. Then the new stage starts not from fatigue but from understanding who we are and what unites us [msg #1638].

### Three DIY year-end questions
If you have no facilitators, start with three simple questions; according to the author, sometimes this alone is enough for the team to feel a different level of contact [msg #1638]:
1. What was valuable in our shared experience in this period?
2. What should be completed, said, let go?
3. What from this experience do we want to carry into the next stage?

### Group rules against attention capture
Purpose: rules deep mind introduced in its fast-grown alumni community, learned from a failure (see *Case: how the author suddenly became an autocrat* below). The author's principle: rules must regulate the behaviour of specialists or those calling themselves such [msg #1666].

Explicitly prohibited in the group:
1. Attempts to lead participants without their request: "psychological", "constellation", "spiritual" or "field" interpretations [msg #1666].
2. Hidden leadership and hard top-down interpretations [msg #1666].
3. Promoting oneself as a helping specialist [msg #1666].
4. Ambiguous or discredited methods, which often appear in such a context [msg #1666].

Plus: a clear exit procedure set up from the start — short, private, without public trials or analyses [msg #1666].

Underlying principle: for a group to be alive and resilient, avoid the error of green thinking that *everything* is permissible in it, and overcome traumatic experience around power — power must be transparent and institutionalised, but must *be*, and serve the interests of the whole; otherwise someone will capture it for their own [msg #1666].

## Detailed insights

### Case: how the author suddenly became an autocrat
A cautionary story about the frequent abuse of shared attention in Russian-speaking groups [msg #1666].
- **Setup.** When the author was a much less experienced facilitator, in a group he led he was unexpectedly seen as authoritarian and pressuring — especially unpleasant because the starting position was the opposite: trust, horizontality, respect for adulthood. The group was meant as a container for joint exploration and inner work, without active sales, funnels or rigid hierarchy; it seemed that selecting people by values and common sense would be enough and the system would self-regulate [msg #1666].
- **The mechanics of influence (almost always unnoticed).** Over time a person appeared who positioned himself as a psychologist and expert. He spoke confidently, gave very clear interpretations and created a feeling of clarity. He took up ever more space, offering explanations of reality — of public events and of other participants' realities — as top-down interpretations, not as hypotheses but as knowledge of how everything really is. The author is immediately uneasy with this, but notes that for many it is very attractive: anxiety drops, a support appears, people want to join whoever speaks confidently. In parallel came invitations to private practice and training programmes — formally a natural continuation, since the interpretations resonated with some; in fact the shared attention of the container began working for one person, supplying him with clients and influence [msg #1666].
- **Why he did not stop it at once.** He felt the methods and communication forms were unsafe, but intervention was postponed — less from inexperience than from a green (pluralistic) worldview: people should sort it out themselves; I can't dictate methods; such boundaries smell of authoritarianism, which this community would reject. In the Russian-speaking environment, with a rich history of abuse of power, illegitimacy and violence, regulation is a hard question; power and its mechanisms often fall into participants' zone of allergy or anxiety. In addition, a strange culture of public spaces has emerged where they are seen not as places of joint work but as a resource for self-presentation and attracting clients [msg #1666].
- **The fundamental error.** The absence of transparent procedures and healthy power almost always leads to shadow, uncontrolled influence that harms the *whole* group. Not knowing how to quickly interrupt the capture of group attention was "fatal". The intervention came late and looked to some like an abrupt change of position — a shock from the gap between declared horizontality and manifested authoritarianism, when he clearly said such methods and behaviour were unacceptable here. Lesson: a facilitator turns into an autocrat not by becoming harsh, but because boundaries appeared too late, too abruptly or too loudly [msg #1666].

### Meaningful year-end gatherings and trust (Qiwi patterns workshop)
- In December of the previous year deep mind received several requests for "meaningful corporate parties". Dancing with wine and champagne is "not bad", but advanced teams now want something deeper: use the year-end meeting to restore contact in the group, get to know each other better, mark the shared path of the year, and ultimately raise the level of trust, with which the new year starts more easily and effectively [msg #971].
- The author states that the importance of trust as the key factor of team effectiveness was revealed "back in 2019" in Google's Project Aristotle. Trust goes hand in hand with psychological safety; in trust and safety employees feel they can rely on each other, and coherence, clarity of communication and creativity grow — and everyone simply wants to live and work in safe places with people they trust [msg #971].
- Ideal format per the author: a retreat — 2–3 days away with joint practices. Quick or online formats remain possible and are especially important for remote teams [msg #971].
- Example: for Qiwi deep mind designed and ran a 3-hour online workshop exploring personal patterns in the team in a game form. Participants learn each other's individual traits, strengths and weaknesses and rediscover themselves and one another. According to the author it was the first time an online format turned out so deep and warm it felt offline; the team both benefited and rested. Feedback from Dima Soloviev, head of the research team: "Мы сегодня на встречах 1-1 обсуждаем всех в терминах паттернов" (Today in our 1-1 meetings we discuss everyone in terms of patterns) [msg #971].
- Mechanism: in deep mind's processes, trust and safety form through conversations about what is important and human, not only professional — which teams still lack badly. In this workshop specifically, through understanding each person's traits via a quick, embodied exploration of personal patterns. Signature line: "Когда я тебя знаю и понимаю, то автоматом начинаю больше доверять. Ты перестаешь быть таким непредсказуемым и странным." (When I know and understand you, I automatically start trusting you more. You stop being so unpredictable and strange.) [msg #971]
- Tool: the method of analysing 4 movement patterns in a team, taken from Ginny Whitelaw's Focus Leadership approach; a holistic model connecting bodily expressions with personal qualities and management styles. (!) INCOMPLETE: the source does not name or describe the four patterns [msg #971].
- DIY advice: a meaningful corporate party can be run independently; first of all one must *want* to create more trust and safety in the group and use the important year-end meeting precisely for that, when everyone badly needs a pause and meaning [msg #971].

### Case: a full-team workshop at a Moscow private school
- deep mind built all its programmes, from corporate workshops five years earlier to the Resonance new-leadership space, on awareness-based systems change — a technology that helps achieve fundamental change in a system by raising its level of awareness, used for example at the UN. Working with awareness in IT teams is no longer new, but bringing it to Russian schools and directing the whole school team's attention to themselves and each other still is [msg #1193].
- deep mind trainers spent a day with leaders, teachers and other staff of the Moscow private school Olimp-Plus. What mindfulness and embodiment work looks like in a school: "roughly like everywhere", because these tools are about people, not professions [msg #1193].
- Session flow (as a worked example of a team workshop): (1) because at this time of year fatigue and tension accumulate while much active work lies ahead, part of the time went to stress and overall state — helping people become aware of it and teaching them in the moment to complete the stress cycle through special practices; (2) a joint exploration through movement aimed at more deeply recognising differences and personal leadership styles in the group, and thereby relieving tension and improving interaction; (3) exploring and embodying the values for which the team works; (4) a gratitude session in a group of 62 people, so touching it "could not end" [msg #1193].
- The author attributes the partnership to leadership from within the team and its openness to new methods, explained by the school's values: freedom, cooperation, respect, safety, development. His conclusion: mindfulness is needed and useful far beyond corporate and startup teams [msg #1193].

### Four effects of group work (carousel)
A four-card carousel on why group work matters [img #1162, #1163, #1164, #1165]:
1. **Group intelligence.** Research shows groups can form a collective mind, revealing and integrating complex interrelations unavailable individually; an example is the "wisdom of the crowd", when collective decisions are more accurate than expert opinion [img #1162].
2. **Systems thinking.** It helps integrate many variables and factors. Groups working by the Theory U model create "mosaic understanding", uncovering complex interrelations and scenarios impossible to discover alone, through the interaction of different viewpoints [img #1163].
3. **Emotional support.** Emotional support in group dynamics can activate brain networks responsible for creative thinking and problem solving; participants' emotional well-being promotes flexible thinking and innovative approaches, opening new paths for generating ideas and processing information [img #1164].
4. **Collective purpose.** Group work can lead to "group intention" — joining efforts toward a common goal. This phenomenon, known as "collective impact", lets a group develop clearer strategies and solutions through synergy and interaction [img #1165].

## Evidence and numbers
