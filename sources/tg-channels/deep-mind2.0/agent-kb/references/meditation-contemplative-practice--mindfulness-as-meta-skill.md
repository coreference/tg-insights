# Mindfulness as a core meta-skill

## What this covers

This file collects what the deep mind channel (author Maxim Rodin, plus guest posts and a forwarded conference digest) teaches about mindfulness as *the* base skill of development: what mindfulness actually is (noticing, not thinking about oneself), why it works as a real-time feedback loop on automatic patterns, how to gauge one's level of mindfulness in daily life, how well-being and happiness can be trained as skills, how mindfulness relates to meta-skills, leadership and business, and the limits of the practice (it cannot directly work with shadow, trauma and attachment; it can cultivate dissociation; mainstream hype and popular science reduce it to stress relief). According to the source this matters because nearly all of human behaviour is automatic and unexamined, and mindfulness is presented as the most powerful tool available for seeing and changing it [msg #574, #1198].

## Core ideas

- **Mindfulness is the foundational meta-skill.** Soft skills, emotional intelligence and leadership rest on subtler meta-skills, the main one being mindfulness; without it, training soft skills is "a suitcase without a handle" [msg #209, #1394, #1399]. Details in *Meta-skills, soft skills and the order of development*.
- **Mindfulness is noticing, not reflection.** To become aware is to see, as if from outside, how thoughts spin and shape the filter of perception; programmes that equate mindfulness with self-reflection (values, goals, career analysis) only push people deeper into thought [msg #273]. See *Three facts on why thinking alone is not enough*.
- **The real value is the feedback loop.** Its main gift is not stress relief but a real-time stream of first-hand information about oneself, which reveals automatic patterns and eventually allows catching and redirecting them [msg #574, #1198]. See *Mindfulness as a feedback loop*.
- **Mindfulness has levels in daily life**, from a reactive autopilot to high freedom of response even to traumatic triggers [msg #998].
- **Happiness/well-being is a trainable skill**, not an external state; the Davidson team names four skills: awareness, connection, insight, purpose [msg #977].
- **Meditation has limits and risks.** It cannot directly heal shadow processes, trauma, boundary and attachment problems, and practiced unsystematically it can cultivate dissociation [msg #323]; it is also a process of unpacking accumulated emotional material, not a trip into altered states [msg #449].
- **The mainstream framing undersells the practice.** Reducing meditation to quick state fixes (Huberman), stress relief, or a pleasant "spa" experience misses its essence as long-term development and investigation of reality [msg #1145, #1198, #1800].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Three facts on why thinking alone is not enough
Purpose: the first of a planned series clarifying what deep mind means by mindfulness ("Why thinking is not enough for happiness in the modern world", part 1), written because the concept's spread has brought distorted interpretations [msg #273].

The trigger case: deep mind was invited to build a leadership mindfulness programme for a university. The programme curator wanted many modules on self-understanding and reflection — working through values and goals, writing out the entire past career path to analyse it. Practical training of meta-skills was given 1 module out of 8 months. The author calls this "too broad" an understanding of mindfulness that knows nothing about the concrete skill — awareness (осознавание). Students of such a programme, instead of developing that skill, would sink ever deeper into their thoughts and learn little about themselves [msg #273].

Definition offered: "Осознать — значит заметить, увидеть как-бы со стороны как мысли крутятся в голове и формируют текущий фильтр восприятия." (To become aware means to notice, to see as if from the side how thoughts spin in the head and form the current filter of perception.) Mindfulness as deep mind develops it is "про принципиальную смену ракурса восприятия" (about a fundamental change of the angle of perception) — about how to stop endlessly interpreting oneself and the world only through one's "думалка" ("thinker", colloquial for the thinking mind). Something truly deep can be learned about oneself only by seeing the work of the perceptual system itself [msg #273].

Why this is a problem — the three facts:
1. **Most people perceive the world predominantly through thinking.** They are stuck "deeply" in their thoughts, with practically no gaps of direct perception through the senses [msg #273].
2. **Thinking is a good but very limited mode of perception.** Far more information is available to us if we develop our range and learn to feel it [msg #273].
3. **Thinking often strongly distorts the actual picture of the world.** Because of cognitive biases (the author notes whole books are written on this) we constantly run into problems, conflicts and misunderstandings, build castles in the air and conspiracy theories with our thoughts, and fail to see what is happening before our eyes [msg #273].

Illustration and cause: if you know the world only through thinking about it, even the simple question "what are you feeling now?" stumps you. The author has seen many faces of people trying, at that moment, to understand = think what they sense — "Они не знают как чувствовать" (They do not know how to feel). In simplified form, he explains, this develops because of pain that falls on us in life, especially in childhood: unable to bear betrayals, violence and hardship, we close ourselves off from the world in thinking and shut our senses so as not to feel pain — and with that lose the joy of living a full life [msg #273].

### Mindfulness as a feedback loop — from moments of awareness to changing patterns
Purpose: explains "why mindfulness is the main skill for development" (post title). The author's point: the beauty of mindfulness is *not* that noticing tension, emotions in the body or thoughts lets us release them and lower stress; it is that mindfulness can give us "самый совершенный эволюционный инструмент для развития — feedback loop" (the most perfect evolutionary tool for development — a feedback loop) [msg #574].

The progression, in order:
1. **Build systematic, regular training.** If we practice systematically, moments of mindfulness start happening increasingly often [msg #574].
2. **Moments of awareness become real-time insight.** These moments are a set of insights about ourselves that in real time tell us how we feel, behave and react in different situations [msg #574].
3. **Patterns become visible.** As attention develops and strengthens, from these moments we begin to notice more global automatic strategies or patterns, which, the author says, condition 95% of our behaviour; he links to a claim that "physiologists have proven that we act unconsciously most of the time" [msg #574].
4. **The information is first-hand.** Unlike tests, mental reflection or any external assessment, the skill of awareness gives the rawest and most real information about oneself, "из первоисточника" (from the primary source) — the most immediate and truthful [msg #574].
5. **Patterns can be changed.** Through the same skill, at some point it becomes possible not only to see these patterns but to change them by catching them at the very beginning and redirecting. Examples: stop constantly worrying; stop flaring into strong anger; stop turning away from what frightens us but must be faced to move forward in life [msg #574].
6. **Global level change.** A systematically developed skill of mindfulness helps move to a new level of development. The author says this is consistent with the "scientifically proven" concept of vertical development or complexification of consciousness, which makes us more effective and deeper people and leaders — and which regular mindfulness practice "really accelerates more than anything else" [msg #574].

The 2024 version of the same idea, from someone helping people set up a personal meditation practice: the deliberately cultivated awareness of meditation is "самый серьезный инструмент развития во всей истории человека" (the most serious development tool in all human history). Meditation and the skill of self-awareness are a unique feedback loop on the mind itself — the very foundation through which we perceive reality and operate in it: we see or ignore information, interpret it, take or skip actions in one way or another. For most people this foundation will remain practically completely unexplored their whole life, unknown in its essence, "как что-то буквально несуществующее" (as something literally non-existent) [msg #1198].

### Two consequences of an untrained mind ("fish on dry land")
Purpose: explains the situation of a person who completely ignores knowing and developing their own mind at a fundamental level (not at the level of knowledge). The author invokes contemplative traditions which said 2000 years ago that an untrained mind is like a fish on dry land [msg #1198].
1. **Ignorance of the nature of mind.** Such a person does not know how the mind works, what state it is in, how this affects their life, and how they in turn could influence it. Their main lens for interpreting the world, main tool for analysing it and making decisions, stays a total mystery, a blind spot — like drivers of sports cars whose life depends on the car knowing nothing about how the car is built and what its properties are [msg #1198].
2. **90-95% of actions are reflexive.** Because this lens is unknown and untrained, 90-95% of the person's actions are reflexive, as for most of us. The person lives by reproducing all the accumulated historical experience of ancestors, which may drag into the present for millennia and be very poorly suited to changed modern circumstances. Reflexivity, "эмоциональный бандитизм" (emotional banditry) and many other automatisms almost fully determine a person's life, creating huge obstacles to happiness and calm — while the person knows nothing about it and keeps trying to solve problems somewhere else [msg #1198].

Lesson drawn: positioning meditation this way, though "still a bit sadly functional", is a step toward explaining its essence in a much stronger way than as a stress-relief tool (nothing wrong with that, but the practice is much bigger than this task) [msg #1198]. See *How teachers should talk about meditation*.

### 4 levels of mindfulness in everyday life (deep mind descriptive model)
Purpose: unpacks the author's earlier statement that people live at different average levels of mindfulness. Mindfulness is again defined as "навык присутствия в собственном опыте и качество внимания" (the skill of presence in one's own experience and a quality of attention), developable through regular practice such as meditation [msg #998].

Precondition for noticing it at all: people usually begin to notice their level of mindfulness — or its very presence — after a 7-10-day retreat or daily mindfulness meditation of more than 30 minutes. Without that, the modern person's quality of attention is rather low and there is no anchor in experience to see this clearly, and no starting point showing what life with different levels of mindfulness could look like [msg #998].

The added lens: following the logic of the scientific tests (see *Mindfulness measurement*), the author proposes an additional criterion for more reliable self-analysis — the degree of automatism vs freedom in reactions [msg #998].

The four levels:
1. **🔴 Reactive level (Реактивный уровень).** Life tends to run on autopilot, with reflexive or impulsive reactions. Difficulty being aware of current experiences, especially in daily routine. Often stuck in gadgets or other "sticky" activities. Reacting to external requests more than clearly seeing one's own direction and priorities. Decisions and their implementation are routine rather than creative. One practically does not notice — or notices not daily but only in intense circumstances — what one feels in the body, which emotions one has, which thoughts often spin in the head [msg #998].
2. **🟡 Noticed reactivity (Замечаемая реактивность).** A daily process of becoming aware of one's feelings, thoughts and body sensations begins. One more often pulls oneself out of reactive states in which one would earlier have stayed longer — for example, out of scrolling [msg #998].
3. **🟢 Emerging freedom (Появляющаяся свобода).** One begins to be aware of one's state in difficult situations and learns to free oneself from the automatic stress reaction. What would earlier have upset us becomes visible together with the reaction, and gradually moderated and controllable. A choice of unaccustomed ways of behaving in stressful and anxious situations appears [msg #998].
4. **🟢 High freedom (Высокая свобода).** The ability to respond freely even to traumatic triggers — situations and circumstances that lead to intense stress and strong impulsive reactions. Thanks to this, self-trust, self-confidence and "literally all our abilities", from focusing to creativity and leadership, grow noticeably [msg #998].

The author's caveat: this logic allows assessing one's own level of mindfulness more objectively, "хотя делаем мы это субъективно — не через тестирование" (although we do it subjectively — not through testing) [msg #998].

### Mindfulness measurement — MAAS, FFMQ and the criteria
Scientifically grounded tests for measuring mindfulness that the author names [msg #998]:
1. **Mindful Attention Awareness Scale (MAAS)**, from Berkeley.
2. **Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire (FFMQ)**, from Ohio University (as the source states).

These tests are described as relatively simple and as measuring 2 main criteria [msg #998]:
- **Attention to the present moment** — assessment of the ability to notice current events, inner experiences and external circumstances.
- **Automatic functioning** — measuring how often everyday automatic actions happen without conscious attention.

The third lens deep mind adds is the degree of automatism/freedom in reactions, which underlies the 4-level model above [msg #998].

### 4 skills of sustainable well-being (Davidson team)
Source: guest post by Inna German, "Why happiness is always ahead" [msg #977].

Background argument: at a lecture by Mingyur Rinpoche (described as the "most famous" Tibetan monk), she heard the thesis that everything we do for happiness most often does not lead to the desired result: we get something, are soon dissatisfied again, and feel we must move on — happiness will surely come when we achieve something else. Rinpoche says that in this cycle we all make a cognitive error: we look for happiness where it is not, believing its sources are outside rather than inside us. Laurie Santos (Yale; author of the most popular psychology course on Coursera) calls such strivings "псевдожелания" (pseudo-desires); she cites a study by American sociologists in which 90% of the super-rich agreed that money can both increase and decrease happiness depending on how it is used [msg #977].

Mingyur Rinpoche's brain was studied for a long time by neuroscientist Richard Davidson (friend and student of the Dalai Lama, author of *The Emotional Life of Your Brain* and *Altered Traits*). His University of Wisconsin team concluded from this and other research that happiness is not a static state achievable from outside but the result of inner, trainable skills. Being stably satisfied regardless of external circumstances is as real as learning to play a musical instrument. For example, subtle skills can make the mind calmer and more focused — which is (linked as) proven to be a happier mind [msg #977].

Davidson's team combined numerous studies on happiness and derived its formula: to develop sustainable happiness or subjective well-being one needs to develop 4 subtle skills [msg #977]:
1. **Awareness (Осознанность).** Flexible attention both to the external environment and to internal signals: body sensations, thoughts, emotions — the attention management and contact with the actual experience of the moment that mindfulness practices develop. Its opposite is distraction. A study of 5000 people in 83 countries showed that almost half of people spend their life in a distracted state, and this lowers their well-being [msg #977].
2. **Connection (Сопричастность).** The sense of warm, trusting relationships with one's close social circle, positive experience of social contact. Many studies confirm (the source cites a book on aging well as support) how important this is [msg #977].
3. **Insight (Инсайт, "понимание").** Knowledge of how feelings, beliefs and thoughts shape subjective experience. If we understand where anxious thoughts or self-criticism come from and how they are tied to the past, attention does not get stuck on the negative aspects of that experience, and we can stay flexible and positive [msg #977].
4. **Purpose (Предназначение).** Clarity about what is personally significant to us, what we strive for and what steps we take. Understanding one's goal, values and important meanings leads to awareness of the significance of one's life and activities [msg #977].

How to train them: there are separate techniques for each dimension, including meditation, gratitude practices, therapy and working with a mentor or coach. Davidson's team built the free app Healthy Minds (English), in which each skill is worked through step by step [msg #977].

Conclusion of the guest author: if I am less distracted and more mindful, develop gratitude, maintain connections and live doing what matches my values, I feel better and happier — even when the future is uncertain or we face difficulties. "Счастье — не результат внешних достижений, а итог осознанной работы над собой." (Happiness is not a result of external achievements but the outcome of conscious work on oneself.) If the research does not convince you, the idea is easy to test in practice — you only need to actually start doing it [msg #977].

### Matrix of key meta-tasks and meta-skills for the next 10 years
Context: deep mind helps companies develop employees and leaders, adapting them to an era of AI, rapid change and high stress — the reality we already live in and will for the next decade. The more the team talks to companies and leaders, the more clearly they feel that serious environmental changes create tasks of a new kind — "мета-задачи" (meta-tasks), more abstract and subtle challenges/competencies of a volatile time. Overcoming new-order challenges is tied to development; for meta-tasks a person needs new, ever subtler skills, above all meta-skills [msg #1011].

Definition: meta-skills are the core competencies that allow people to learn, adapt and thrive amid change. Unlike traditional skills, which can become outdated, meta-skills are "eternal and universal"; they are the basis of adaptation, making them irreplaceable amid changing labour markets and technologies [msg #1011].

Built on the World Economic Forum's current leadership competencies combined with Theory U developed at MIT, the matrix pairs each meta-task (with its competency description, from the image) with a key meta-skill [msg #1011, img #1011]:
1. **Clarity (Ясность) → Mindfulness (Осознанность).** Understanding who you are and what is happening; the ability to withstand any emotions and information; and relying on many layers of reality in one's actions [img #1011].
2. **Direction (Направление) → Resilience (Резильентность).** The ability to focus, to recover and continue moving toward the goal, to know one's trigger points and to be able to regulate oneself in them [img #1011].
3. **Flexibility (Гибкость) → Adaptability (Адаптивность).** The ability to adapt to change, accept novelty, let go of old notions, change identity [img #1011].
4. **Contact (Контакт) → Empathy (Эмпатия).** The ability to share and receive objective information from others ecologically, feel others' emotions, express oneself and be present wholly in interaction [img #1011].

The author's comment: you probably feel an inner response to these meta-tasks, as if you want to add all of it to your life for more health, stability and joy — "for me it definitely is". All DM programmes and other interventions in companies have been tied to developing subtle skills for 5 years, and he is convinced the trend toward such employee development will only grow, as it matches the main tasks of the present. He notes that Yandex's HR director Dasha Zolotukhina wrote about meta-skills the day before and recommends her channel [msg #1011].

### Six-point picture of mindfulness, psychotherapy and shadow processes (2020)
Purpose: a picture that emerged while preparing a meta-skills programme, about where mindfulness practice and psychotherapy stand [msg #323].
1. **Mindfulness went mainstream.** In 2020 the trend running since 2010 accelerated: meditation, mindfulness or attentiveness practices crossed an important threshold and became mainstream in big cities — not only in the US, UK or the Netherlands, but also in Russia. Data points: more than 100% growth of interest in psychotherapy and meditation in 3 years since 2017; explosive jumps (50%) in interest in December 2019 and March 2020 (the author expected a similar jump a month later); most top Russian companies launched some practices and ways of working with employees' state in 2020; in some circles, especially IT companies and startups, culture approached that of Silicon Valley, illustrated by a line attributed to Dave Asprey (Bulletproof): 20 years ago you could count on your fingers the business founders who meditate and see a therapist; now you can count on your fingers those who do not [msg #323].
2. **The market was not ready.** In the author's expert view, the market was not ready for such demand growth: in Russia there are only a handful of mindfulness programmes with certified, trauma-informed and systemic teachers. In most programmes everything slides into "просто наблюдай, дыши, обрати внимание на ступни" (just observe, breathe, pay attention to your feet). Building a quality professional system takes time and resources; when demand jumps, quality supply cannot jump as fast [msg #323].
3. **Effective practice needs shadow work.** Truly effective mindfulness practice requires careful work with the shadow processes of the psyche. "Медитация, какой бы классной она не была, не может напрямую работать с теневыми процессами, травмами и проблемами границ и привязанности." (Meditation, however great, cannot work directly with shadow processes, traumas and problems of boundaries and attachment.) [msg #323]
4. **Meditation can postpone the real work.** Moreover, the dissolving of boundaries that meditation provides often denies and postpones real work on the fundamental therapeutic problem (boundaries, trauma and attachment) [msg #323].
5. **Mass unsafe practice cultivates dissociation.** As a result, huge numbers of people now practice unsystematically and unsafely, without working with shadow processes directly — in effect cultivating daily "on the cushion" dissociation, a process of alienation from one's emotions and problems instead of working them through [msg #323].
6. **Benefit and pathology together.** At this point of social development, along with huge benefit we also create considerable pathologies of development. Ken Wilber is quoted: "Если сначала не вернуть тень себе [через терапию и специальные практики работы с тенью], вместо здорового разотождествления, мы получим патологию." (If we do not first reclaim the shadow [through therapy and special shadow-work practices], instead of healthy disidentification we will get pathology.) The author's stated remedy is that special practices can complement and accelerate psychotherapy so as not to fall into this developmental trap [msg #323].

### Huberman critique — three systemic criticisms
Purpose: the author, walking around Stanford where Andrew Huberman teaches, decided to assess Huberman — in whom "many have found almost religious faith" — through his podcast episode on meditation, a subject the author knows well enough to judge expertise and approach. What attracts people to Huberman: an impressive combination of scientific terms and confident conclusions — "and he's a big strong guy"; it is sweet to lean on something solid and scientific, especially if we are prone to control. But digging deeper, something superficial or even controversial sometimes hides behind the scientific shell [msg #1145].
1. **Surface instead of depth.** Huberman presents meditation with an emphasis on concrete goals — better sleep, concentration, mood — and offers an arsenal of practices to reach them. The problem: this reduces a multifaceted practice to a simple tool for correcting mental state — like saying reading is merely a way to learn to pronounce words correctly. *Systemic criticism:* in traditional contemplative systems meditation is a process of knowing oneself and the world, which demonstrably promotes our maturation and can lead to the most important, essential steps of development available to a human; reducing it to a mechanical exercise for short-term results loses the very essence [msg #1145].
2. **Science as marketing.** The podcast is full of scientific terms and details — which brain areas activate during meditation and how they affect our state. It sounds convincing, but how much does it help us understand the essence of meditation? The impression is that he focuses more on impressing the listener than on conveying deep understanding — "or he simply does not have that understanding". *Systemic criticism:* the chase for scientific credibility sometimes leaves important contextual and philosophical aspects off-screen, creating the illusion that knowing the names of brain areas or how hormones work is enough to understand what happens in meditation [msg #1145].
3. **One-sided view.** Huberman claims meditation can bring quick state improvements ("state changes"). There is nothing wrong or bad in that, but real meditation practice is not just a way to perk up or relax for a short time — and this perspective is completely missing from Huberman. *Systemic criticism:* focusing on short-term results can create false expectations and devalue meditation's true meaning. In traditional systems the key role is played by long-term changes that alter personality and require study and deep commitment. That is why the excellent book by Davidson and Goleman — well-known scientists who are genuinely immersed in the subject — is called *Altered Traits* and not "Lower cortisol in 5 minutes" [msg #1145].

Overall verdict: Huberman is neither the first nor the last to use lots of scientific terms and simplify complex concepts for a wide audience. He is "definitely cool" at not talking bullshit and at popularising the scientific approach; in two hours the author did not find a single substantively wrong thesis, and that matters. On the other hand, the style can become "научным популизмом" (scientific populism), when a very one-sided opinion is shared from a very confident position, while throwing brain-area names at us and advertising supplements along the way. Ultimately the usefulness of his podcasts depends on how we take them — as a source of inspiration and general information, or as final truth [msg #1145].

### Question of the week — what part of reality you "may not see", plus a mini-practice
The observation: many of us live for years in a mode where outwardly everything works — tasks done, conversations held, decisions made, life moving — and this very functionality starts to look like proof that "в целом все ок" (on the whole everything is ok). But often it rests on an unspoken contract with oneself: some pieces of reality are better not touched by attention; if I start seeing them fully, I may fall apart, lose pace, stop coping. Then an inner censorship appears: "это не сейчас" (not now). Sometimes it saves us, but sometimes it increasingly cuts us off from wholeness — our own needs, authentic expression, the creativity of our life [msg #1719].

The question: "Какую часть реальности вы сейчас «не имеете права видеть», чтобы оставаться функциональн:ой?" (What part of reality do you currently "have no right to see" in order to stay functional?) It can be noticed in any domain of experience: sensations, emotions, thoughts, external circumstances [msg #1719].

Optional practice, 1-2 minutes, without forcing:
1. Name it to yourself in one word, and note what happens in the body when you acknowledge it: contracts / releases / goes numb / speeds up / other [msg #1719].
2. On the out-breath, try to release not "the whole fight with reality" but just one small gesture of resistance, and for a second acknowledge the fact: "сейчас во мне есть это" (right now this is in me). No need to go deeper; it is enough to stay a little near it — exactly as much as is comfortable now and "без героизма" (without heroics) [msg #1719].

Safety rule: if at some point it becomes too intense, this is not a failure or a problem but a signal to take care of yourself — open your eyes, look around, feel support in the area of the feet/pelvis/back, switch to the sounds around you [msg #1719].

### Meditation is not a spa-salon (Hougaard and Kabat-Zinn)
Purpose: addresses one of the main reasons people cannot meditate, according to Rasmus Hougaard (New York, founder of Potential Project, who brings mindful leadership into the world's largest companies and has taught thousands of leaders to meditate) [msg #1800].

Hougaard's points, as relayed:
- People want calm, clarity and spaciousness of mind from meditation and leave with a feeling of failure, because expectations do not match reality [msg #1800].
- Many think practice should feel pleasant and calm, that we should feel happiness there; these expectations are wrong and prepare the ground for disappointment [msg #1800].
- Calm, clarity and happiness may arise, but they are side effects, not the goal. If you wait for them and they do not come, you start thinking something is wrong with you or the practice. But everything is fine — only the expectations are wrong, and they are worth letting go [msg #1800].
- Avoid trying to define or guess what meditation should be like and how it should feel; instead focus on recognising ever better the space and nature of the mind [msg #1800].
- Meditation is not something we must strive hard to do correctly or be good at; it is more a journey than a destination [msg #1800].
- If we see practice as something that helps us explore reality as it is (rather than keep projecting our expectations onto it), it stops being strained, and enjoyment can arise from an ever deeper understanding of ourselves and the world [msg #1800].

The author adds that the experience of practice turning into a chase for the right state is familiar to most people who sit down to meditate, and complements Hougaard with Jon Kabat-Zinn's fresh description of the process of awareness (from a recent article), as a clearer orientation than the widespread idea that practice is something like a spa-salon [msg #1800]:
- Participants can be invited to recognise that in this moment we call now, whatever unfolds in experience, there is no better or more preferable place to go, nothing to do or achieve. We can rest in place even in the face of unpleasant experiences: pain, boredom, impatience, attachment to results, longing and all the rest [msg #1800].
- Meditation is an invitation simply to be aware of all sensations, emotions and thoughts — at least for a brief moment to look at them with full openness, without mixing them with stories about "I, my pain, my problems" [msg #1800].
- Such awareness becomes a base, a refuge, where one can settle or return again and again when swept away by a stream of thoughts or emotional reactivity [msg #1800].
- If we approach meditation with the mindset that we need to get into some other state, to be in some other place, we can sit for 15-20-30 years, suffering, wanting, idealising ourselves and the practice, and never get what we want [msg #1800].
- Awareness that is open and turned toward everything — not toward the pleasant, joy or calm, but toward everything that is — is what ultimately makes practice work. It becomes a truly stable ground for exploring reality, and makes us the same in life [msg #1800].

### What mindfulness practice is — and what it is not
Mindfulness practices (осознанность / майндфулнесс), which are part of all deep mind programmes, are defined as special exercises for training attentiveness at the individual and collective levels. Historically they developed within religious traditions, and because of confusion around the various forms of those traditions, many still cannot understand the topic correctly and systematically — despite the huge recent growth of popularity and abundant materials written from a scientific approach [msg #449].

They are *not* a way to alter consciousness and fly off into visions and trance states. On the contrary, by training attentiveness we pull ourselves out of the habitual trance of our own constant thoughts and worries and can feel reality less clouded. By directing attention to current experience during practice we start to be aware in more detail of the body, emotions and thoughts that exist together. "Мы не улетаем куда-то во вне, а приземляемся в более плотный контакт с собственной реальностью" (We do not fly off somewhere outside but land into denser contact with our own reality), which basically consists of what we can experience in body, emotions and mind [msg #449].

Unpacking phase: with each correctly done practice we learn to be more fully aware of our experience, and so increasingly touch bodily tensions and other somatic processes inseparably linked with emotions and traumas — which were present in our experience without our noticing. Therefore for most people in the early stages of practice (1-3 years, sometimes longer) mindfulness may look like, and be, mainly a gradual and at times unpleasant unpacking of accumulated emotional memories, stress, anxieties and fears. This is not identical to psychotherapy and is not psychotherapy, but it also has a healing effect, freeing us from excess tension and pain. In the process we develop a whole spectrum of skills and mental functions, come to understand ourselves and other people better, and our capacity to act in the world and manage teams increases (the team-management claim is backed in the source by a reference to a scientific article) [msg #449].

Hence the author's summary: "практика осознанности – это процесс развития в чистом виде, который включает в себя разные этапы, в том числе этап очищения от хвостов прошлого." (mindfulness practice is development in its purest form, which includes different stages, among them a stage of cleansing from the "tails" of the past) [msg #449].

What it takes: to actually be in this process and live through a cycle of liberation from accumulated emotions and traumas, one needs a daily practice of at least 15-20 minutes, kept up as long as needed. For this it is important to work, together with a community and a teacher, on the obstacles that prevent daily training, and to explore jointly the inner experience that unfolds in these sessions day by day [msg #449]. Related reading the post points to: how to choose a mindfulness instructor; why business needs mindfulness (deep mind's piece for Tsekh, "Mindfulness is not just breathing practices against stress"); and a large guide to scientific research on mindfulness titled along the lines of "why denying mindfulness means being a Luddite" [msg #449].

### Apps versus a teacher
Commenting on news of a corporate meditation initiative (Starbucks), the author calls it a good trend for corporate culture as a whole — it makes promoting his company's products easier, "since all modern technologies of effective leadership are based on mindfulness" (a disclosed interest: he sells such products) [msg #108].

The problem with all apps for working with the psyche: they do not see what is happening with the person and do not give personal feedback. In such processes this is very important — "I would say key" — both for progress in practice and for safety. Recommendation: reasonable apps are a good place to get a first experience of practice, but this is always only a test drive. If after it you did not understand what it is, or decided it does not work for you, find an experienced practitioner or teacher and talk to them before forming a final opinion about the tools [msg #108].

### How teachers should talk about meditation
Given the present popularity of meditation and mindfulness practices, the author argues it is more useful for teachers to switch to a quite traditional discourse of training and developing the mind: "познай себя, развивай свой ум, пробуждайся от рефлективности" (know yourself, develop your mind, awaken from reflexivity) — placing practice on the same level as, or even above, existing and understood categories of inner work such as psychotherapy or coaching. He acknowledges this is not a new but the most classical discourse [msg #1198].

He calls on the professional community, without discarding narrowly utilitarian goals, to stop belittling the aims of meditation and to speak of it first of all as the most fundamental tool of development and knowledge available to humanity. That, he says, is exactly why historically meditation was perceived in all cultures as the most precious and important thing available to a culture [msg #1198].

What genuine teachers of meditative traditions wanted to share, as the post describes it: a deep insight into what has been in us since beginningless time — the open, clear unboundedness of our mind. The mind is open like space, has no shape, colour or weight, is radiantly clear, full of various capacities and without any limits — that is, it knows everything and can do everything. Its true nature is great joy, active compassion and perfect unshakeability [msg #1198].

### One human day — a reflective exercise on presence
The author asks whether he can remember living a *whole* day without distractions or external stimulation — never going into abstractions: not solving tasks, not watching films, not reading books, not falling into chats. A day in which he simply felt, on his own skin, "что означает один человеческий день" (what one human day means) — what it is to truly live it from dawn to dusk, a single unbroken experience of human consciousness [msg #902].

The questions he poses as a kind of self-inquiry: when did I last really notice the day's speed — how fast and how endlessly long it lasted? What was it filled with — which feelings, tastes, smells and moments, and how was this unique, unrepeatable, only-mine day reflected in me? This experience of life through my eyes; this paradoxical attempt to embrace with a human mind the boundless flow of life. Perhaps then I even noticed how the world is created moment by moment right at the tip of my attention, without which it would not exist — this particular world I am living right now [msg #902].

Lesson: if I cannot remember such a day, perhaps something important has been lost in my life and keeps getting buried under new layers of oblivion each day. On the other hand, this important thing can always be found — one only needs to reach out a hand to one's genuine feelings [msg #902].

### The spread of mindfulness and openness to change
The author reflects on how the world has changed: 7 years before, on his last trip to the US before a long break, mindfulness books were starting to appear in fashionable hipster shops and he was still shy to write publicly that he meditates. Flying to San Francisco now, dozens of practices are built into the in-flight entertainment system — basic mindfulness, breathwork and even some neuro-practices of the kind deep mind teaches for regulating intense emotions. He calls this a big revolution that often happens unnoticed: behind small new pieces scattered through daily life the overall picture is not always visible, nor how much today differs from the same day 10 years ago. He jokes that in 5 years he might be offered CBD gummies on board instead of alcohol, which is poorly suited to flights [msg #1113].

Lesson drawn: life keeps changing — its natural property that no one has yet defeated. "В будущее возьмут всех, но не всем там будет комфортно." (Everyone will be taken into the future, but not everyone will be comfortable there.) We can practice openness to change to adapt more easily and not end up as fundamentalists, who, in his view, fight nature itself and try to turn the world backward by manipulating with a non-existent paradise and traditions, while in fact merely playing on human fear and taking away precious time for development and growth. He wishes for supports and sufficient resources to stay open and adaptive to the natural flow of life [msg #1113].

## Practical playbook

- **Starting out:** try a reasonable app as a test drive; if it seems not to work or remains unclear, talk to an experienced practitioner or teacher before concluding — apps cannot give personal feedback, which matters for progress and safety [msg #108].
- **Minimum dose for real process:** daily practice of at least 15-20 minutes, sustained as long as needed, with a community and teacher helping with obstacles and with exploring what arises [msg #449].
- **Threshold to even notice your level:** a 7-10-day retreat or more than 30 minutes of daily mindfulness meditation [msg #998].
- **Expect unpleasantness early:** for 1-3 years or more practice may mostly be unpacking stored emotions, stress and fears; that is normal, healing, but not therapy [msg #449].
- **Do not chase states:** treat calm, clarity and happiness as side effects; aim at being aware of everything that is [msg #1800].
- **Pair meditation with shadow work** (therapy, special practices) when trauma, boundaries or attachment are involved [msg #323].
- **Self-check by freedom of response:** ask how automatic versus free your reactions are, using the 4-level model [msg #998].
