# Digital detox and healthy screen habits

## What this covers

This file collects everything the deep mind channel taught about screens and the phone as a source of stress, a habitual way of regulating emotions and a deliberately engineered addiction — and about how to change one's relationship with them: information-detox checklists, a framework for weighing any digital activity, phone-free mornings and evenings, social-media breaks, the screen-apnea phenomenon, persuasive design, and a mindfulness-based way of working with craving instead of prohibitions. It also contains the author's critical counterpoints: that "cheap dopamine" is a myth, that bans tend to strengthen addiction, that the science on social media harm is not unanimous, and that imposed disconnection works opposite to chosen disconnection. [msg #400, #795, #1253, #1495, #1542, #1548, #1678, #1689, #1753, #1788]

According to the source, this matters because information arriving through the phone screen is "one of the main causes of stress today", because the smartphone has become a person's main source of stress triggers, and because a dysregulated attention and dopamine system stops us from going deep, reflecting and moving toward important but slow goals. [msg #400, #795, #1253]

This part covers one new post (#1579): the author's article for RBC-pro (republished on Telegraph) on JOMO — "joy of missing out" — as an emerging, more mature stage of digital culture. It explains the origin of the term, three reasons it is trending now, four everyday behaviours that make up JOMO, the cultural shift behind it and three small first steps. [msg #1579, article #1579]

**How it relates to the existing topic file.** The existing file already describes JOMO from post #1386 through the lens of attachment psychology (FOMO as a trace of anxious attachment, JOMO as an expression of secure attachment, the "avoidance mask" pitfall). This new post **extends** it with a different framing — cultural and historical: JOMO as a collective transition to "digital maturity", its attribution to Cal Newport and digital minimalism, a numeric claim about daily external signals, the three drivers of the trend, a concrete behaviour list and starter steps. It does not contradict #1386; both insist JOMO is not detachment or isolation. One minor tension is noted in *Caveats*. [msg #1579]

## Core ideas

- **The phone is a main source of stress triggers.** It delivers disturbing information, quick stimulation and scatters attention; many people have developed a stable body-mind habit of getting aroused on contact with the phone and tired afterwards, usually without noticing. Reducing the information flow is presented as a good way to reduce tension, stress and anxiety. [msg #400, #795]
- **Everything consumed stays in the "mindstream".** Content becomes experience that shapes state, body and personality, so information intake deserves care. [msg #470]
- **The screen has become a self-regulation tool — an anaesthetic.** When dysregulated, people now go to the screen instead of walks, books or friends; it creates a gap from direct experience. This is better seen as a pattern than a personal fault. [msg #1678, #1745]
- **Screens are deliberately sticky.** An attention industry using persuasive design turns human psychology into an architecture of addiction; a US court in 2026 found Meta and Google guilty of deliberately forming addiction in a child. We get stuck "not because we are weak". [msg #1548, #1753]
- **Bans and blockers do not work; attention does.** Prohibitions create internal conflict, breakdown, shame and a stronger addiction loop; timers and blockers feed an impulse–resistance–bypass pattern. What works is noticing the impulse, inserting a pause and investigating craving and the needs behind it. [msg #1548, #1678, #1689]
- **Screen apnea is a body-level marker of digital overload.** Breath-holding during screen use accumulates into chronic alarm; breath is both symptom and solution. [msg #1495]
- **"Cheap dopamine" is a reductionist myth.** Dopamine codes prediction error, not pleasure; behaviour comes from many interacting systems. [msg #1542]
- **Chosen limits help; imposed limits harm.** Voluntarily blocking mobile internet improved attention and wellbeing in a large study, but the same limit imposed from outside produces anxiety and helplessness. [msg #1788, #1745]
- **Balance, not Luddism.** The author owes a lot to screens and rejects nihilism, but sees that 50–70% of his screen time does not move him toward what matters. [msg #1689]

- **FOMO has not disappeared, but conscious people are moving to JOMO.** FOMO was long "one of the central emotions of the era of hyperconnectivity": switching off from the flow felt like leaving the game. Now a new word is heard increasingly often — JOMO, the joy of not being in the know, not participating, not being reachable. [article #1579]
- **JOMO is a sign of digital maturity.** The author compares the transition to the huge tables (feasts) of the 2000s that eventually gave way to more moderate consumption focused on quality rather than quantity: after a decade of hyper-involvement people have "eaten their fill" of digital abundance and start to distinguish what nourishes from what merely fills. [msg #1579, article #1579]
- **JOMO is about directing attention, not about not knowing.** It is a question of what exactly you want to point your attention at — and therefore about a greater degree of freedom, "the very freedom" for which people came into the world of smartphones and the absence of borders. [article #1579]

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Information detox task list (8 actions)
Purpose: a short task list for a holiday (posted on 8 March) when "everyone seems to have agreed on a breather", to put the relationship with gadgets in order and reduce psycho-emotional tension. It is based on the materials of the author's corporate course on reducing stress in teams, which looks at the question from the angle of neurobiology and specifically how memory and attention systems work. The author says from his experience this "really helps to substantially reduce psycho-emotional tension". [msg #400]

1. **Delete apps you have not used in the last 2–3 weeks.** A simple reduction of the digital surface. [msg #400]
2. **Set a completely black or dark screen.** The author's stated reason: it helps the eyes relax and reduces irritation of mitochondria located in the retina. [msg #400]
3. **Use airplane mode during meals, sleep, important conversations and strategic work.** Why: it helps you not be distracted and concentrate on what you are doing now. [msg #400]
4. **Schedule automatic "do not disturb" in the morning and before sleep.** Define those times and let the phone switch the mode on automatically. [msg #400]
5. **If possible, switch off all notifications and set specific time windows** — for yourself and for others — when you check information on the phone and answer messages. Why: it frees attention for more important tasks. [msg #400]
6. **Decide what the task and benefit of social networks and news feeds is for your career and personal life.** Then determine the minimum time needed to achieve those tasks, build these time slots into your daily routine, and strictly keep the limits you set for social networks and messengers. [msg #400]
7. **Calibrate your relationship with technology with the 4-parameter check** (see the next framework) and give up aimless, useless or destructive activity. [msg #400]
8. **Notice emotional discomfort or impulsiveness in the context of the new information boundaries.** If you notice it and tolerate it — i.e. do not break the boundaries you set — it soon starts to bother you less. [msg #400]

The author invited readers to share their own life hacks about relationships with information, "cheap dopamine" and gadgets. [msg #400]

### 4-parameter check for any digital activity
Purpose: to "calibrate" the relationship with technology by checking the importance of any activity in the digital environment against four parameters; anything aimless, useless or destructive is dropped. [msg #400]

1. **Намерение (Intention)** — what do you intend to get, and will it bring benefit? [msg #400]
2. **Ограничение по времени (Time limit)** — what is the minimum and maximum time you need to spend on this goal? [msg #400]
3. **Вдумчивость (Mindfulness / thoughtfulness)** — how does this specific digital activity affect you in the moment? Be aware of the impact that particular messengers, sites and social networks have on your state. [msg #400]
4. **Обогащение (Enrichment)** — does using the technology enrich you, or only distract and drain your attention? [msg #400]

### No phone one hour after waking and one hour before sleep
Purpose: the author's main recommendation for lowering tension, stress and anxiety. Reducing the information flow is "a good solution", and not taking the phone in hand for an hour after waking and an hour before sleep is "the best". It came out of a joint process of updating digital habits run in the author's community in January. He felt less stressed and clearer already on the third day of the habit. [msg #795]

Why the morning matters:
- On waking the nervous system is in a sensitive state and is tuned to react to information arising in the senses and mind, so phone information can easily trigger an emotional hyper-reaction or pull you away from feeling the body and meeting some important needs. [msg #795]
- After deep dreamless sleep, in which personality fully disappears, it is important to reintegrate back into the self — to understand who we are, where we are and what we want. The more attentively we do this, the better we meet real needs and the healthier we feel. Connecting to external information sources instead of this rebalancing will most likely raise stress for the rest of the day. [msg #795]
- Picking up the phone right after waking means losing control over one's life: we start reacting instead of acting and first plug into someone else's agenda. The author links this to a survey finding that only 1 person in 10 today feels enough control over how they spend their day. [msg #795]

Why the evening matters:
- Sleep quality depends on relaxation and calming down. Information and blue light (which is harmful for melatonin production) shortly before falling asleep make rest worse. [msg #795]
- This creates a vicious stress circle: the next day amygdala activity is higher, stress is higher, and during the day we reach more often for the phone and for sweet food that causes emotional swings. "More stress — less sleep — more stress". [msg #795]

The more radical next step the author took: he deleted Telegram from his phone and kept it only on the laptop (other social networks had long been absent from the phone). After 3 weeks of this experiment he was "unambiguously glad" of greater clarity of mind and concentration, which raise quality of life and help progress in truly important matters. Sometimes he has to reinstall social apps, but he deletes them again the same day. [msg #795]

### 4-step social-media break
Purpose: the author planned a social-media break of a month and a half (he traditionally rests all of January as "a basic hygiene measure for helping practitioners"), to reload habits and clear the mental clutter that accumulates, among other things, from constant social-media information. His diagnosis: even with high motivation, regular meditation and inward-directed practices had become harder in recent years. Technology is not the only culprit — he names the high stress of war, a marginalised position in emigration, uncertainty and collective trauma — but the digital space contributes substantially. [msg #1253]

The mechanism he describes: he does not often get stuck on memes (though it happens when tired and unable to steer himself to constructive rest), but even basic work "clearing out chats" demands large resources of the memory-attention system, loosening and depleting it. It also launches a harmful dopamine loop of quick pleasure: you answer in one chat and feel relief, jump into another; clearing them gives a feeling of calm from reduced chaos and feeds a sense of control and efficiency; then you cannot leave the scheme and seem to wait for new chats, hands reaching to clear more tasks. The result: loosened attention and an overloaded dopamine system that does not let you go deep, reflect and move toward important but not quick goals. His evidence: by the statistics he keeps in Remarkable (moving read books to a separate folder) he read about 30% less that year, and he noticed that on opening a difficult English-language study he reads only one page and drops off to somewhere with faster and easier reward. Without regular reading of books and scientific papers, he says, his professional fitness would crack quickly, he would start producing nonsense in texts and talks, which would reduce trust and his ability to help others. Hence regaining control over attention and dopamine requires "meaningful counter-intuitive actions". [msg #1253]

1. **Wide cleanup.** Start by easily limiting incoming stimuli and information, or simply unloading the digital space symbolically. Delete unused apps. Delete sticky social networks that are not needed constantly or for work from the smartphone — for him Instagram and Facebook. Also clean and structure main work spaces: notes in Remarkable, documents in Craft, the desktop, files. [msg #1253]
2. **Cleanup inside the main interface — Telegram**, where the main chats live and constant dopamine stimuli arise. Update the folders. Unsubscribe from all channels and chats accumulated over the year that are not a «hell yeah» — "there really aren't that many important spaces!". [msg #1253]
3. **Reflect and plan how to use Telegram and other social networks during the break.** Some can be left untouched the whole time, but a full detox is hard to organise; instead set clear boundaries — why and how you want to use Telegram in this period, and everything else "goes into the furnace". He hands some chats and processes over to his team and assistant and stops being a member of them for now. [msg #1253]
4. **Reflect on why you need the break and build motivation** to stay in the experiment until results — as in this very post. Also think about what important and valuable things you will add to your life in the freed space: for example, books by great writers or important professional literature, time alone with yourself, writing, and time in contact with loved ones. [msg #1253]

### Breathe → Blink → Break → Boundaries
Purpose: the working formula for *screen apnea* (see *Screen apnea* in Detailed insights), which the author says was born in the comments under a New York Times article and sounds like "an important mantra of the digital age": «Дыши. Моргай. Делай паузы. Определяй границы» ("Breathe. Blink. Take pauses. Set boundaries"). [msg #1495]

The five concrete practices that make up the solution:
1. **Long exhale, sometimes after a deep inhale** — resets the freeze loop. [msg #1495]
2. **Blink** — blinking is as important as breathing. [msg #1495]
3. **Change posture and visual focus.** (Commenters observed that breath follows the eyes — a fixed gaze holds the inhale, while closing the eyes for a minute brings back a deep exhale.) [msg #1495]
4. **Separate contexts** — work and personal space, different browsers or devices. [msg #1495]
5. **A screen-free pause** — a walk, music, bodily movement. [msg #1495]

### Mindful work with screen craving (attention instead of struggle, MBRP-inspired)
Purpose: a way of working with screen dependence that the author developed on a retreat and after it, after clearly seeing that the screen had built itself into his system of regulating states. [msg #1689]

Context and reasoning:
- **Why addictions stay invisible.** In ordinary life we often don't recognise such dependencies because we constantly indulge them and they look normal; each day's dose (coffee, sweets, socialising, work, screen) resets the cycle. Only when disconnected from the object does it suddenly become painful, anxious, empty — "a fairly precise marker" that the attachment was not neutral. Example: coffee seems unproblematic until the usual cup disappears and withdrawal hits hard. The author says he is not dependent on coffee (he switched to decaf years ago), but with the screen "there's a trap": it is hard to spend not just a week but even one full day without looking at it, even without objective need. [msg #1689]
- **See it objectively first.** He owes a lot to screens, phone and social networks — professional opportunities, connection with loved ones, development, learning — and it would be wrong to take a nihilist position or become a modern Luddite. At the same time, looking honestly, 50–70% of his screen time does not bring him closer to truly important tasks; if even half an hour or an hour a day of it went to practice or reading, in a few months quality of life and pace toward the important would be quite different. [msg #1689]
- **Why bans strengthen addiction.** Known in addiction psychology but repeatedly ignored: resolutions like «всё, завтра провожу без телефона» ("that's it, tomorrow without the phone"), «с утра не беру телефон» ("no phone in the morning") or «никакого экрана перед сном» ("no screen before sleep") mostly do not work sustainably and even strengthen dependence. A ban creates internal conflict → tension → an almost inevitable breakdown → shame and guilt; these states themselves become a trigger pushing us back to the object to relieve the experience, so the addiction loop strengthens. The author says he tested it himself. [msg #1689]

The method:
0. **Drop the bans.** The first thing that changed the situation: he gave up prohibitions and, despite the absurdity, allowed himself to watch videos and look into chats — on a retreat. This released a lot of tension from the need to control, and he agreed with himself to direct the energy to attentiveness in the process, relying on the contemplative mindfulness-based approach (MBRP) to make the whole cycle of addiction conscious. [msg #1689]
1. **Notice and investigate the craving (тяга).** The very desire to pick up the phone, look at something, get stimulation — what contemplative approaches call craving ("thirst, pull, passionate desire"). Don't run to the phone immediately; stay at least a minute with the impulse itself. Feel where it is in the body, how it feels, how it moves. Don't evaluate it or try to remove it — just be with it with a quality of kindness and interest. [msg #1689]
2. **Notice the needs behind the craving.** Behind or next to the pull there are almost always needs. Instead of prohibition and control, the agreement is: before dissolving into the screen, first try to notice why I go there and what I'm looking for. Often it is loneliness, the wish to be included in a community, fear of missing something, fatigue, emptiness. The task is not to fix this but to notice it and treat it with acceptance. [msg #1689]

### The pause between impulse and action
Purpose: reduce the load on the "attention muscle" and the constant stress caused by scattered attention from getting stuck in the screen. The author borrowed it from a Silicon Valley entrepreneur, Mike Yan. [msg #1548]

- **Why usual digital hygiene fails.** Blockers, timers and time limits create an illusion of control but actually maintain the same pattern: impulse → resistance → bypass. We learn to trick the protection system but never train the skill of noticing the impulse itself — and that moment is the key one. [msg #1548]
- **What worked better for him: the app one sec.** It neither forbids nor limits; it inserts a pause — "that very second between impulse and action" in which it again becomes possible to remember yourself and check your needs. Every time you try to open a habitual app where you get stuck, it invites you to wait, take a breath and ask yourself about the purpose of the action. This short gap of awareness, the author explains, helps return activity to the prefrontal cortex — simplified, the area responsible for conscious rather than impulsive decisions; from a neuropsychological view, this is where agency (субъектность) is born: choosing actions yourself rather than flowing automatically along choices prepared for us by someone else. [msg #1548]
- **Limits of the tool.** Such solutions do not help fundamentally; most important is to systematically train attentiveness through formal and informal mindfulness practices. But it is "nice to share something that works even on a relative level". In an era when technology is embedded ever deeper in cognitive processes, mindfulness stops being a spiritual metaphor and becomes a matter of personal ecology. [msg #1548]

The same pause appears as the closing practice of the screen-as-regulator post: notice why we go to the screen; create a small pause between impulse and action; and — at least sometimes — stay with the feelings we are used to escaping from into content and chats. The author calls it "simple but not always easy". [msg #1678]

### Three questions about the screen as a regulator
Purpose: once the screen-as-anaesthetic pattern (see Detailed insights) is seen as a scheme rather than a mistake, it opens room for questions and choice. [msg #1678]
1. **Do we want to live in constant anaesthesia?** It lowers overall sensitivity and makes it harder to digest experiences — to live through them and naturally move into another state. [msg #1678]
2. **What are we training?** Through reading we develop reflection; through meeting friends, presence and contact; constant regulation through the screen rather trains switching off. We become less aware and understand worse what is really happening with us — not because something is wrong with us, but because there is simply no room left for it. [msg #1678]
3. **Is it effective?** The screen can really help short-term, but regulation that supports more deeply and reliably usually lies not in distraction but in the body, breath and contact with what is. That, not information noise, really helps the nervous system return to balance. [msg #1678]

### Three reasons JOMO is trending now
Context: the author states that every day we receive on average 6,000 to 10,000 external signals — from notifications to digital conversations — so skipping things has become "an act of survival", and recently also an act of culture. He gives three drivers: [article #1579]

1. **Overfatigue from constant presence.** Research by the American Psychological Association, as cited by the author, shows that constant involvement in messengers, Zoom calls and news streams lowers cognitive functions, worsens sleep and contributes to anxiety. People began to feel that life slips away in the attempt to be everywhere — and almost nowhere for real. Conclusion: JOMO is an attempt to adapt to overload. [article #1579]
2. **Sharper attention to mental health.** The pandemic and post-pandemic overload led people to place greater value on a healthy body, boundaries and personal ecology; words like «цифровая гигиена» ("digital hygiene"), «информационный детокс» ("information detox") and «психоэмоциональный ресурс» ("psycho-emotional resource") are heard ever more often. Conclusion: JOMO is a new form of caring for health. [article #1579]
3. **A changed idea of success and engagement.** FOMO comes from old career thinking: be everywhere, take part, do, don't fall behind. JOMO comes from a new value — attention, depth and inner engagement. If "missing everything" used to look like failure, today it can be a choice of maturity and of tuning in to what is your own. Conclusion: JOMO is a new form of identity in which success looks different. [article #1579]

### JOMO in action (4 everyday behaviours)
What JOMO looks like in practice, per the author — each item pairs a behaviour with the absence of the usual negative feeling: [article #1579]

1. **Switch the phone off in the evening — and feel no anxiety.** [article #1579]
2. **Don't open a chat with 238 messages — and feel no guilt.** [article #1579]
3. **Don't answer instantly** — because you are in a meeting or simply want to be alone with yourself; and **not be in the know — without treating that as a threat to your competence**. [article #1579]
4. **Don't go to a meeting even if you were invited** — simply because your "no" has become possible. [article #1579]

### Start small with JOMO (3 first steps)
Purpose: for readers who do not yet manage to enjoy missing out. (If you already notice yourself rejoicing at what you missed, the author says "congratulations": your attention is coming back to you; it is not a sign of lost opportunities but a sign that you can choose again.) [article #1579]

1. **Unsubscribe from notifications of channels that bring neither joy nor meaning.** The filter is joy or meaning — anything that gives neither goes. [article #1579]
2. **Allow yourself not to answer right away.** [article #1579]
3. **Try to spend at least one evening a week without internet access** — and treat it "not as a feat but as rest". [article #1579]

## Detailed insights

### The screen as a self-regulation tool (anaesthetic)
When we feel bad — overloaded by a stress or trauma reaction, stuck in anxiety, aggression or depression — this almost always means we are dysregulated, out of the nervous-system balance from which it is easier to cope with life's difficulties. Feeling pain, knowing this scheme or not, we start doing something to regulate our state; the important question is how we do it and what our habitual strategies lead to. [msg #1678]

The author's self-observation: when dysregulated recently, he tried to remember how he used to cope — a solitary walk, music, a book, meeting friends, a conversation, silence. Just listing these aloud already felt pleasant, and he noticed that all of them seem healthy, mature and constructive ways of regulation, yet feel much less accessible than before. Today, when he really feels bad, he often finds himself not on a walk or with a book but on the phone or computer — watching, writing, consuming information, talking with GPT, scrolling, reading, switching. "So the function of regulating my nervous system was largely taken over by the screen" — and he supposes not only for him. [msg #1678]

Beyond the known addictive effect of smartphones, screens and social networks (they are built to capture and hold attention), there is another layer: modern people have developed the habit of regulating themselves through the screen. The screen is always in the pocket, something is always happening there, it is extremely accessible and socially approved, requires no effort and almost instantly offers a substitute for the experience. [msg #1678]

Mechanism: it lets you dissolve in information and fill attention with content — creating a gap from immediate experience. In this sense the screen acts as an anaesthetic: we turn on YouTube, a podcast, a feed, a dialogue, not only to learn something but to not feel for a while, so as not to stay alone with what is inside. Often all this noise exists only to keep distance from bodily and emotional experiences that may be unpleasant, anxious or frightening right now. It is important to see this as a scheme, not as an error or a bad habit. [msg #1678]

### Mindstream: content as lived experience
Starting from a post by Pavel Durov with three points — (1) the mind is an incredibly powerful tool that works even while we sleep and rest; (2) the state of mind depends on the quality of the information we consume; (3) we need to free the mind from the "sticky" information fed to us by social-network and search algorithms — the author sets aside Durov's agenda against the big tech companies and focuses on the idea of the mindstream. [msg #470]

The idea: everything we perceive affects wellbeing and is stored in memory forever without exception, and content influences perception, bodily state and hormonal background. The author affirms "that is so": these are properties of the mindstream, or "stream of mind" (he notes it is not quite a scientific term), which is continuous and holds memories of all lived experience. For the mindstream, content also becomes experience that creates positive or negative causes for our state. For example, he says, we know sticky content increases the likelihood of chronic stress and depressive states, or reduces our ability to think deeply and see the big picture. [msg #470]

Practical consequence: knowing that everything that enters the mindstream stays and affects personality, one can treat consumed information far more attentively — reduce consumption of clip-like content that lowers concentration and focus, and even avoid scenes of violence in TV series, which are experienced by the body as real. [msg #470]

### How the smartphone drives stress
The smartphone gives us disturbing information, "cheap dopamine" and scatters attention: we jump between activities on average every 20 seconds (a figure the author cites from a study). As a result many have a stable body-mind habit of getting aroused on contact with the phone and tired afterwards, though we usually do not notice it. The author is sure that the exhale and calm that come with reduced informational stimulation are simply "prescribed" for most of us. [msg #795]

### Digital fatigue
Screens are used for literally everything and mainstream work is now closely associated with screen time, so average screen time keeps growing. **Digital fatigue** is defined as a state of physical and mental exhaustion caused by prolonged time in front of screens; many who suffer from it feel there is no boundary between work and free time. The author admits that he sometimes feels huge irritation and even powerlessness when he cannot tear himself away from the screen because of tasks or exhaustion of the dopamine system: "any information already feels bad, but just stopping doesn't work". [msg #1294]

Two main factors:
1. **Work.** Work has become more distributed — more remote and hybrid employees and companies — and companies have not yet learned to fight the culture of toxic productivity. Employees get more tired, which affects their results. Effects of digital fatigue on employees: it lowers productivity (cognitive overload as a consequence of digital fatigue dramatically affects performance); it lowers wellbeing scores; it lowers the quality of communication and collaboration with colleagues. [msg #1294]
2. **Social networks.** Even outside work people spend more time on social media; we tire more when the information causes stress or unpleasant emotions, so social networks can contribute substantially to digital fatigue and to anxious and depressive states. Constant, even unconscious, comparison of oneself with others is painful for the ego and can produce unpleasant states. [msg #1294]

The image attached to the post (from a Deloitte digital-fatigue report) reads: "Digital life often delivers daily benefits but can also fuel tech fatigue and well-being worries. People rely on technology devices and appreciate their benefits, but some say "too much digital" has its drawbacks." [img #1294]

### Screen apnea
James Nestor in his book *Breath* writes: «У тебя открыто десять экранов. Кто-то пишет тебе сообщение, кто-то звонит, кто-то шлёт письмо… Мы не эволюционировали для того, чтобы быть постоянно стимулируемыми» ("You have ten screens open. Someone is texting, someone calling, someone emailing… We did not evolve to be constantly stimulated"). [msg #1495]

Physiology, as the author explains it: an unexpected stimulus can be perceived by the nervous system as a possible threat; we freeze, breathing becomes shallow, attention narrows. Linda Stone, a Microsoft top manager, noticed these breath stops during screen interaction back in 2007 and called it *email apnea* — most people held or almost stopped breathing when opening their email. A New York Times article later extended this to *screen apnea* in general: breath-holding, and what happens in the body at that moment, during any interaction with a screen. [msg #1495]

The phenomenon is not deadly but insidious: short "freeze" episodes dozens of times a day accumulate and move the organism into a chronic alarm mode; we then experience constant stress, tire faster and burn out. [msg #1495]

What the author found in the article's comments (lived experience of contemporaries):
- People say gadgets and laptops have turned into a «цифровую плётку» ("digital whip") — not just about breath but about the culture of constant availability and its effect on bodies. [msg #1495]
- Detailed bodily observation: breath follows the eyes. Fixing the gaze holds the inhale; conversely, simply closing the eyes for a minute brings back a deep exhale. [msg #1495]
- Many recall Reich, for whom shallow breathing is interpreted as a way of avoiding emotions. [msg #1495]

Conclusion: breath is simultaneously a symptom of digital overload, a physiological problem if we regularly hold it, and the solution (see *Breathe → Blink → Break → Boundaries*). For the author, screen apnea is not a breakdown or weakness but a marker of the gap between mind and body, a consequence of the world's changes; and a phenomenon that looks like "some trifle about messengers" may point our collective attention to the main challenge of our time: how to stay alive in a culture where everything calls us to freeze. [msg #1495]

### Persuasive design and the attention industry
The author finds it almost dramatic that the main resource of the 21st century is not gold, oil or even data, but human attention. Every glance, finger movement on the screen and even microsecond of interest is fought for by billions of dollars and thousands of cognitive and behavioural scientists. It is a finely tuned attention industry with labs studying how the human nervous system reacts to notifications, scrolling, pop-ups and like counters. All of these are elements of *persuasive design*, which essentially turns human psychology and biology into an architecture of addiction. We get stuck not because we are weak but because modern media are very sticky for the brain and try ever harder to succeed at it — the more of our attention they hold, the more they earn. [msg #1548]

### The 2026 US verdict on platform addiction
Case: a six-year-old girl began watching YouTube and at 9 got into Instagram. By the end of primary school she had 284 videos on YouTube, plus depression, body dysmorphia and thoughts of self-harm. Her family sued and won, setting a significant precedent. During the trial jurors studied internal Meta documents containing the phrase «Если мы хотим выиграть по-крупному с подростками, мы должны цеплять их ещё детьми» ("If we want to win big with teenagers, we have to hook them as children"); Zuckerberg and other top managers had seen the documents — "the company knew what it was doing". On 25 March a court in LA found Meta and Google guilty of deliberately forming addiction in children; compensation was $6 million, small by American standards. [msg #1753]

Lesson the author draws: what matters is not the money but that it was legally and publicly recognised that platforms consciously exploit attention, and that decisions of specific people stand behind this — "we didn't imagine it", in line with his earlier posts on the screen as a regulator and on screen addiction. [msg #1753]

Legal mechanism: platform content is protected by a US law that effectively gives companies immunity for everything users publish. The lawyers found a loophole: instead of content they attacked the design itself — infinite scroll, autoplay, algorithmic recommendations and notifications. They argued these are not just features but a system with a *variable reward ratio* — exactly the principle Skinner described in 1950: irregular, unpredictable reward forms the most persistent addiction possible; a slot machine works on it. [msg #1753]

Political consensus vs scientific dissensus: the fight against platforms unites polarities in American society. Conservatives say Big Tech destroys family, childhood and traditional values and demand removal of platform immunity; progressive activists see a classic case of capitalist exploitation (a rich company knew of the harm, hid it and profited from children) — "meat-eaters and vegans suddenly at the same rally with roughly the same banner". Science, however, is not united (see *Evidence and numbers*). With tobacco, the order was different: first the 1964 US Surgeon General's report with strong data, then, years later, a wave of lawsuits. Here lawsuits came without scientific consensus; the author wonders whether it is normal that the legal system does what science cannot yet prove — names what millions feel in their lives but is hard to catch in lab data — and honestly says he does not know. [msg #1753]

His broader point: our culture is good at naming manifest, material things but struggles with inner dimensions and behaviour; when there is no molecule causing chemical dependence, it is easy to get lost between "I choose to get stuck" and "I am held by a system designed specifically for that". He is glad we collectively took another step toward stopping the systemic gaslighting of our real experience. [msg #1753, img #1753]

### The myth of "cheap dopamine"
(Written, the author notes, under the supervision of a clinical psychologist.) "Cheap dopamine addiction" is today used to explain anything: scrolling, craving for quick pleasures, likes and sugar. It seems to give a simple biological explanation — the brain gets used to easy stimulation and we lose the ability to enjoy deep things. But such ideas are not scientific and radically simplify reality, as happened with the widely replicated triune-brain concept. [msg #1542]

- **Dopamine is not the happiness molecule.** Modern neuroscience has shown its main function is transmitting phasic signals about surprise and the result of an action. When an event turns out better or worse than expected, dopamine neurons change their firing rate, coding the so-called *reward prediction error* — not "joy itself" but a mechanism of learning and correcting the model of the world. Example: when we try a new dish, dopamine neuron activity really rises; if we eat it every day, the reaction disappears — the taste pleasure remains, but the novelty-and-learning signal no longer appears. Dopamine reacts not to pleasure as such but to how unexpected or informative an event is. [msg #1542]
- **More dopamine ≠ more happiness.** If dopamine were the "happiness hormone", high levels would always make us joyful; research shows dopamine neurons can activate on reward, on threat and on an unexpected sound. In one context this strengthens drive to act, in another attention to danger. [msg #1542]
- **Why reduction is a mistake.** The same dopamine signal is perceived differently depending on genetics, experience and mental health — for one person a stimulus becomes motivation, for another a source of anxiety. Biology sets the frame but does not dictate the script. Neurotransmitters act in parallel systems: dopamine interacts with serotonin, noradrenaline, GABA and endorphins; serotonin regulates mood and impulses, dopamine supports motivation and learning, GABA brakes excess excitation — "more like an orchestra than a solo instrument". Behaviour results from the interaction of neurobiology, cognitive processes, emotions, memory and social context; explaining complex behaviour through one molecule is harsh reduction. [msg #1542]
- **Danger of simple explanations.** Reduction devalues psychological and social factors: if everything is brain chemistry, why work with trauma, attention, relationships, cultural context, large systems, politics — just switch off cheap dopamine. Such simplifications push people toward pseudo-practices, which the author lists: dopamine detox; dopamine-management practices; "superfood for dopamine"; working with addictions through dopamine; working with depression through dopamine. [msg #1542]
- **The alternative.** The myth is popular because it is simple and offers a clear story that non-experts love, but the simplicity distorts the complexity of behaviour and creates false hopes. The honest alternative is a systemic and integral view: biochemistry matters, but so do cognitive habits, self-regulation skills, social connections and culture. Science confirms, the author says, that sustainable change happens when we work with the whole context, not one molecule. A person is a whole system "in a constant dance with other systems", and the key to understanding human nature lies in that complexity. [msg #1542]

(Note the tension with the author's own earlier use of "cheap dopamine" — see *Caveats*.)

### JOMO — the joy of missing out
FOMO (fear of missing out) is described not just as anxiety about a missed meeting or trend but as touching attachment style and important needs. FOMO demands being connected, in the information flow and actively involved in the outside world; this part believes that if you drop out of a common process you seem to stop existing. It forms attachment not only to people and events but to the information field: it is scary not to answer, not to be in the know, not to know who cancelled whom, what is trending, what happened in the last two hours. The author sees in FOMO a trace of anxious attachment — a feeling from the past that threatens total annihilation if we are not included — and deep human needs to be accepted and to participate in a meaningful group, without which one might once not have survived at all. [msg #1386]

The opposite of this anxiety (which partly helps and partly hinders living calmly and freely) lies in more contact not with the information field but with one's own rhythms — and it need not mean isolation. The "new trend" offers the mental model **JOMO — Joy of Missing Out**: joy that you are not everything, not everywhere, not immediately, and that in this you have not lost yourself but rather kept yourself. JOMO grows not from avoidance but from attunement and inner support — from knowing that you exist, that your place is not determined by how many events you attended, and your value does not change with chat reaction speed, events visited or photos of you on social media. Joy in not-doing and absence is not necessarily detachment; it can be about having an inner place where it is possible and important to be, and about fuller presence in life. [msg #1386]

Example: a friends' chat discusses a new event; everyone is going. You look and feel no response, yet inside rises the familiar «если не поеду — буду чужим» ("if I don't go, I'll be an outsider"). You stand at a fork. With clear contact with yourself and your reactions and needs — not conceptual but bodily, living contact — you can feel: no, right now it's more important for me to be alone; not out of fear of others, fear of being expelled or of missing opportunities, but out of wholeness, your own rhythm and clarity of needs. Then you stay — and that will be the most right action, from which calm joy arises. [msg #1386]

Psychologically JOMO can be seen as an expression of secure attachment, in which you do not need constant confirmation of your significance and are not afraid of being forgotten, because you already know you exist and matter; or as an established part with a multidimensional set of needs, not only those that respond to the reflexive fear of missing something. [msg #1386]

Pitfall: sometimes behind the mask of JOMO hides not joy but an avoidant style of interaction. So ask yourself: «Я не иду туда потому что не хочу? Или потому что боюсь быть собой среди других?» ("Am I not going because I don't want to? Or because I'm afraid to be myself around other people?"). Summary line: JOMO is not "I don't want to be with you" but "I want to be with myself". [msg #1386]

### A minimalist gadget: reading without distraction
The author's favourite gadget is the reMarkable 2, a Norwegian e-ink notebook-tablet ("in places quite limited and rather expensive"), which he liked so much he bought a second one as a gift within a couple of weeks. It lets you take handwritten notes, draw and make visual comments in documents or books digitally but with the full feel of paper and no blue screen. Compared with an iPad it has very limited functionality — no apps, few functions beyond reading, drawing and writing. He chose it over an iPad because the screen doesn't shine into the eyes, it feels like paper, and, most importantly, it is free of "cheap dopamine": when reading an article there is no easy way to switch to messengers, YouTube or mail. [msg #1042]

Why this matters, per the author: reading printed texts improves comprehension 6–8 times more than reading digitally (a figure he cites from a Guardian report on research), partly because reading on gadgets involves frequent distractions. Reading on a laptop or smartphone, there is a high chance of going to Telegram a couple of times or running off to other social networks even with notifications off; these attention switches catastrophically reduce the efficiency of working with information and are very tiring. [msg #1042]

Result of his self-experiment: he felt physically drawn to the device "like a ship to a quiet harbour", wanting to slowly study the latest Harvard Business Review or slowly design a programme by drawing its modules on paper and sending it to the team chat. Over half a year he read 3 times more books than in a comparable period before (and started quoting Nabokov in business podcasts). Main downside: no automatic transcription of handwritten Russian (English works) and no external Russian keyboard; otherwise he would write all channel texts there. [msg #1042]

Lesson: it is an example of a new generation of gadgets aimed at minimalism and offloading attention; real productivity comes when focus on narrow functionality and concrete tasks radically changes the digital experience. The Light Phone belongs to the same trend — a phone that helps radically filter unnecessary information and distractions; the author was considering it. [msg #1042]

### When the internet is taken away: what is really lost
Writing about internet shutdowns in the capital (Moscow) in 2026, the author says he almost physically felt a collective breath-holding, anxiety and apathy in the Russian-speaking environment. Such shutdowns do not just make travel inconvenient or cause losses to small and medium business; they activate a stress response touching deep aspects of psychic reality and our sense of safety. [msg #1745]

Three points:
1. **The internet is not a pleasant appendix to life but its basic environment** — a commodity on which modernity runs; over the last 20–30 years we moved not a piece but a huge part of life into the digital environment. [msg #1745]
2. **The internet and messengers are very close to our nervous system and ability to stay adequate.** Today we regulate ourselves through the screen: through it a person calms down, gets distracted, switches, fills emptiness and regains the sense that life goes on. Research, he says, shows the internet and smartphone have become for many a habitual way of coping with tension, not just a channel of information. [msg #1745]
3. **The internet is a key space of witnessing and co-regulation.** The psyche needs not only to experience but to see that its experience is noticed, confirmed and shared. Amid expanding propaganda and political gaslighting, people find islands of recognition and shared experience online; research describes such forms of support as significant for coping and recovery. [msg #1745]

So the loss is not a small inconvenience but deep stress, when several layers of support are lost or under threat at once: the basic, fundamental infrastructure of life; habitual ways of self-regulation; islands of co-regulation and of checking the normality of our picture of the world. If on top of that there is no clarity whether it is temporary or long-term, accidental or a new norm, body-psyche can get stuck in uncertainty, a long-known amplifier of distress. [msg #1745]

Why say it aloud, "without unnecessary drama and panic": first, to reduce possible traumatisation, which is more likely the longer and more abruptly this happens, or if a substantial part of one's life (e.g. all income or professional identity) is tied to the internet/Telegram; second, so as not to lie to oneself about the scale and reduce it to everyday comfort ("oh well, I'll go to Max" — Max being a messenger; the author jokes that he himself is Max, not the messenger that disgraces the name). It is not just a change of platform but a threat to the environment in which modern life — our ways of regulating ourselves, communities, connections and sense of reality — is built; seeing it this way may make it harder to simply give up and strengthen the fight not to lose each other. [msg #1745]

### Chosen versus imposed disconnection
The author says a scientific consensus is gradually forming about the negative effect of mobile internet on the nervous system and mental health. A February 2025 study showed for the first time at such scale what happens if this load is removed (numbers in *Evidence and numbers*). Co-author Adrian Ward: in 15 years smartphones radically changed our lives and behaviour, but the basic human psyche stayed the same; are we adapted to constant connection to everything always? The data say no. [msg #1788]

Why such studies cannot justify forced disconnection: there is no direct data on forced mobile-internet shutdowns, but a stable line of psychological research shows that the same restriction affects the nervous system differently depending on whether it is chosen or imposed. The study participants installed the blocking app themselves — they owned the frame, from which their nervous systems got the positive effects. So the effect is not from the absence of internet as such but from the combined benefit of free choice, which returns safety to the nervous system and creates more room to regulate. When the same pause is imposed from outside, at an arbitrary moment and without explanation, the nervous system tends to react in the opposite way: anxiety, waiting, helplessness, background irritation. Externally similar circumstances, internally two different phenomena with mirror consequences. [msg #1788]

What the numbers mean for practice: mobile internet is clearly not a neutral technology; it is an environment that keeps the nervous system all day in micro-switching and anticipation of reward. From such hyper-involvement and activation it is impossible to listen attentively to another person, notice tension rising in a room, or stop and ask oneself what is guiding me right now. Constructive contact and presence — the basis of leadership mastery and any quality interaction — need an attention resource that constant network access spends. However, the decision about restrictions must be made by the person themselves; otherwise the effect not only disappears but becomes its opposite, and forced shutdowns breed growing insecurity, irritation and anger. The problems of technology's impact still have to be dealt with, but that is secondary if a person is deprived of freedom of choice and their agency is under attack. [msg #1788]

### Origin of the term
According to the author, JOMO first appeared as the opposite of FOMO in the context of digital minimalism and was popularized by technology writer Cal Newport, author of *Digital Minimalism: Choosing a Focused Life in a Noisy World* and *Deep Work: Rules for Focused Success in a Distracted World*. In these books Newport writes about how multitasking and constant digital presence fragment attention and make experience meaningless. JOMO offers a radically different vector: conscious disconnection from the superfluous in favour of the present. [article #1579]

Contrast drawn by the author: FOMO is about the anxiety "what if I'm missing something", while JOMO is about the silence in which there is finally everything you need and nothing is missed. [article #1579]

### The cultural shift: from feasting to eating when hungry
The author insists JOMO is "neither a whim nor an escape" but part of a new stage of digital maturity. We lived through a decade of hyper-involvement; the digital abundance became not nourishing but exhausting, and now we start to distinguish what saturates from what merely fills. His image: it is like learning to eat when you are hungry, not when everyone around is eating. [article #1579]

Closing thesis: JOMO is not loneliness and not detachment; it is the joy of being in life — your own life, not everyone's at once. [article #1579]

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- (!) CONTRADICTION: early posts rely on the notion of «дешевый дофамин» ("cheap dopamine") as an explanation for phone stickiness (#400, #795, #1042, #1253 — e.g. "overloaded dopamine system", "harmful dopamine loop of quick pleasure"), while #1542 (written under a clinical psychologist's supervision) calls "cheap dopamine" a non-scientific, reductionist myth and lists "dopamine detox" among pseudo-practices. [msg #400, #795, #1042, #1253, #1542]
- (!) CONTRADICTION: #795 recommends not taking the phone for an hour after waking and before sleep and presents it as "the best" solution; #1689 says resolutions such as "no phone in the morning" or "no screen before sleep" mostly fail and strengthen addiction because they are bans. One possible reading is the difference between a chosen practice and a self-imposed prohibition (cf. #1788 on chosen vs imposed limits), but the source does not reconcile them explicitly. [msg #795, #1689, #1788]
- (!) CONTRADICTION: #1253 recommends deleting apps and cleanup steps, and #400 recommends "strictly" keeping self-set limits, while #1548 says blockers, timers and limits only create an illusion of control. [msg #400, #1253, #1548]
- (!) UNCLEAR: the claim in #400 that a dark screen "reduces irritation of mitochondria located in the retina" is given without a source or explanation. [msg #400]
- The author's own hedges: the mindstream is "not quite a scientific term" (#470); one sec does not help fundamentally (#1548); the 57% figure is the app authors' claim (#1548); a full Telegram detox is "hard to organise" (#1253); technology is not the only cause of his attention difficulties — war, emigration and collective trauma also contribute (#1253). [msg #470, #1548, #1253]
- Pitfall: JOMO can mask an avoidant style; check whether you skip events from lack of desire or from fear of being yourself around other people. [msg #1386]
- Science vs law: there is no scientific consensus on social-media harm (Oxford vs Haidt), yet lawsuits are being won; the author says he does not know whether this is normal. [msg #1753]
- #1788 claims a forming consensus on the negative effect of mobile internet, while #1753 says there is no scientific consensus on social media and teen mental health — these concern somewhat different questions, but the framing differs. [msg #1753, #1788]
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the MBRP method in #1689 continues in a comment not included in the source; only the first two steps (craving, needs) are available. [msg #1689]
- The author is not anti-technology: he stresses how much he owes to screens and rejects nihilism or Luddism (#1689), and argues the internet is a basic environment and space of co-regulation whose forced loss is a serious stressor (#1745, #1788). [msg #1689, #1745, #1788]

- The 6,000–10,000 daily signals figure and the APA findings are stated without specific study references in the article. [article #1579]
- (!) UNCLEAR: the post frames JOMO as something "conscious people have already moved to" and as a broad cultural trend; the article gives no data on how widespread it is. [article #1579]
- Tension with #1386 (existing file): #1386 calls JOMO a "new trend" grounded in secure attachment and warns that JOMO can mask an avoidant style; #1579 presents declining an invitation "simply because your 'no' has become possible" as JOMO without repeating that avoidance check. The two framings are compatible but #1579 omits the pitfall. [msg #1579]
- Tension with #1689 (existing file): #1689 warns that self-imposed bans like "no screen before sleep" tend to backfire, while #1579's starter step of one internet-free evening a week is framed explicitly as rest rather than a feat — which may be the author's way of avoiding a ban framing, but the source does not connect the two. [article #1579]
