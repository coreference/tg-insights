# Biorhythms and energy peaks

## What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's posts on how human energy actually moves through a day and through the seasons: the circadian curve with two daily peaks and an afternoon dip, the 90-minute ultradian cycles of focus and recovery, how many deep-work blocks are realistic, how to rest and how to perk up after lunch without coffee or sugar, why mornings can be energyless and how to diagnose it, how seasonal change raises sleep need, and one famous person's (Rick Owens') routine built around conserving limited energy [msg #12, #49, #71, #176, #179, #198, #199].

According to the author, forcing yourself to be active outside your natural active periods costs energy and productivity (and, she notes in passing, health), while planning the most important tasks for natural peaks makes you "much more effective and productive". She frames this against a cultural myth of non-stop performance that leaves people feeling guilty for being tired [msg #12, #49].

## Core ideas

1. **You cannot be at peak energy all day.** The author's short answer to "how to be at peak energy all day" is "Никак" (no way). Energy follows natural cycles; the goal is to place the most important work on natural peaks, not to sustain a peak [msg #12]. Detail: *Daily energy curve*.
2. **Energy is a wave, not a flat line.** The "work non-stop from waking to sleeping" picture is a myth; real energy rises and falls repeatedly through the day [msg #49, img #49].
3. **Ultradian 90-minute cycles cap deep work.** Every ~90 minutes the body needs at least 20 minutes of recovery; on average only about two 90-minute concentrated-work cycles are possible per day [msg #49]. Detail: *Ultradian cycle*.
4. **Afternoon sleepiness is normal and is better treated with rest than stimulants.** A nap, NSDR or activating breathwork recharges more than coffee or sweets [msg #179].
5. **Morning energy has identifiable causes.** Low morning energy is traced to seven possible sources, from sleep debt to illness, and can be investigated with a 4-step coaching exercise [msg #198, #199].
6. **Seasonal change is a stress that requires more sleep.** Sleeping an extra hour in autumn is an adaptation to allow, not a failure [msg #176].
7. **Energy is limited and must be conserved; each person finds their own way.** Rick Owens' unconventional routine shows that a sustainable, energy-protecting lifestyle need not match idealised wellness prescriptions [msg #71].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Daily energy curve (five phases of the circadian day)
**Purpose.** To plan the day so that the most important tasks fall on natural energy peaks, and routine tasks and rest fill the rest of the day [msg #12].

**Underlying claim.** Circadian rhythms (the author calls them biorhythms) are the natural energy cycles over the 24-hour day. "Если грубо: мы активны, когда мы активны" (*Roughly: we are active when we are active*) — if we force ourselves to be active at other times, we lose energy and productivity (and health, which the author defers to another time). According to circadian rhythms, the average person has two activity peaks: roughly **2–3 hours after waking** and **9–10 hours after waking**. The author says practice shows that planning the most important tasks for exactly these windows makes you much more effective and productive; the remaining time is for routine tasks and rest [msg #12].

The card "Пики энергии в течение дня" (*Energy peaks during the day*) draws a dashed energy curve with two humps and maps five phases, each with an icon [img #12]:
1. **Пробуждение (Waking)** — 0–1 hour after getting up. Energy is rising (upward arrow, sun icon). This is the ramp-up phase before the first peak [img #12].
2. **Первый пик (First peak)** — 1–3 hours after getting up. The top of the first hump; icons of a running figure and a light bulb indicate activity and ideas/thinking — the slot for the most important tasks [img #12, msg #12].
3. **Дневной спад (Afternoon dip)** — 1–2 hours after lunch. The trough of the curve, marked with a STOP sign: a time not to push, suited to routine and rest [img #12].
4. **Второй пик (Second peak)** — 0–2 hours before dinner. The second hump; icons of a running figure and a crossed-out coffee cup (activity again, without coffee) [img #12].
5. **Засыпание (Falling asleep)** — 1 hour to 30 minutes before sleep. Energy is falling (downward arrow, moon icon) [img #12].

The author invites readers to share their own experience with this pattern [msg #12].

### Ultradian 90-minute activity–recovery cycle
**Purpose.** To explain why "active work 8 hours a day is a myth" and to set realistic expectations for focused work [msg #49].

**Context — the myth.** The author addresses readers from families where it was customary to rest "примерно никогда" (*approximately never*): they live with the idea that they must "бесконечно фонтанировать энергией" (*gush energy endlessly*) from waking until going to (very short — "в гробу отоспимся", *we'll sleep in the grave*) sleep, and perform at "146%" for all 8 working hours. Meanwhile they feel guilty about tiredness and "comfort" themselves with Instagram scrolling or chewing chocolate. The alternative she proposes: admit the need for rest, actually schedule it, and do what gives rather than takes energy [msg #49]. The accompanying image contrasts "Что нам рассказывали про энергию в течение дня" (*What we were told about energy during the day*) — a flat plateau from "Проснулась" (*woke up*) to "Уснула" (*fell asleep*) labelled "Фигачишь без остановки" (*you grind non-stop*) — with "Как РЕАЛЬНО выглядит энергия в течение дня" (*What energy REALLY looks like during the day*) — a repeating wave of five peaks and troughs between waking and sleeping [img #49].

**How it works.**
1. **Cycle length.** Ultradian rhythms govern cycles of activity and recovery; one cycle lasts **90 minutes** [msg #49].
2. **Mandatory break.** Every 90 minutes the body needs a break to recover — **at least 20 minutes** [msg #49].
3. **Three phases inside each cycle: rise, peak, decline (подъем, пик и спад).** You first have to *arrive* at the peak, and you cannot stay there to the end of the cycle. At best you are at peak for **50–70 minutes out of 90**. The author discloses that she personally cannot concentrate for more than 40 minutes but is working on it [msg #49].
4. **Daily capacity.** It is considered that on average a person can do **2** concentrated 90-minute work cycles per day; if you don't monitor your state and don't work on attention, then only one or even zero [msg #49].

### Rules for resting between cycles (switch modes)
How to rest during the recovery break [msg #49]:
1. **Switch the mode of activity.** If you sat for a long time — move; if you were moving — sit; if you were concentrated — relax [msg #49].
2. **Small chores are allowed.** Tidying the desk, sorting papers, taking out the trash are acceptable during a break [msg #49].
3. **Avoid strain and gadgets.** Avoid cognitive or physical strain and avoid using gadgets during the break [msg #49].

### Four conclusions for planning around ultradian rhythms
The author's takeaways ("Какой вывод?") [msg #49]:
1. **Plan no more than 2 high-concentration tasks per day** — or, alternatively, don't expect super-results from yourself. This follows directly from the average of two 90-minute deep cycles a day [msg #49].
2. **Build in warm-up time ("раскачку", *warming up*)** — come up with your own ways of "switching on", because each cycle starts with a rise before the peak [msg #49].
3. **Find phone-free rituals of rest and switching** — consistent with the rule to avoid gadgets during breaks [msg #49].
4. **Invest time in developing attention and concentration** — since without working on attention the number of deep cycles drops to one or zero [msg #49].

### Three practices to perk up after lunch (Power Nap, NSDR, breathwork)
**Purpose.** To recover focus during the afternoon slump instead of reaching for coffee or something sweet, which the author says "не то, что поможет зарядиться до конца дня" (*is not what will charge you up until the end of the day*). She uses these practices herself and recommends them to clients [msg #179].

**Why the slump happens.** Given circadian and ultradian rhythms, daytime fatigue and sleepiness are unsurprising; a bad night's sleep plus eating too much at lunch (or lunching on sweets) makes the search for focus inevitable [msg #179].

1. **Power Nap — short controlled sleep.** Rationale: sleep is the main source of energy, so if you need to perk up, the optimal option is to sleep (the author cites a PubMed study as support). How: the key is to set an alarm for **20–30 minutes**; in that time the brain manages to rest and you won't feel groggy ("разбитыми") [msg #179].
2. **NSDR — Non Sleep Deep Rest** ("по-русски очень странно «естественное восстановление без сна»", *in Russian, very oddly, "natural recovery without sleep"*). For whom: people who, like the author, can't sleep during the day, or who work in an office where a lunchtime nap would attract attention and questions (she notes that the number of offices considering it normal is growing). How: sit or lie down with eyes closed and listen to a guided practice — focus returns too (she cites a PubMed study). She personally listens to one 10-minute practice when she has 10 minutes and a 20-minute one when she has 20, but tells readers to find whatever suits them. One of her clients described it: "Это просто какое-то волшебство!" (*This is just some kind of magic!*) [msg #179].
3. **Breathing practices.** The author, speaking as a certified functional-breathing instructor, calls breathwork the closest thing to a "magic pill" that can literally change your state. Mechanism: breathing lets you switch between two nervous systems — the **sympathetic** (responsible for activity and the stress response) and the **parasympathetic** (responsible for relaxation). If you need more energy, it makes sense to activate the sympathetic one, which is done by **shortening and quickening inhales and exhales and by breath holds**. She recommends a favourite guided activating breathwork on Insight Timer ("Dynamic Immune Boosting Breathwork 2.0") plus a beginner version of the same practice. Caveat: she does not recommend doing this in the evening, as it can disrupt sleep [msg #179].

**The hardest part.** Allowing yourself those 10–20–30 minutes of sitting or lying down in the middle of the day. The author says she is still learning this; what helps is remembering how much energy you'll gain afterwards [msg #179].

### Seven places to look for lost morning energy
**Purpose.** For people who go to bed dreaming of a fresh morning but instead keep snoozing the alarm, struggle to open their eyes, then spend another ~30 minutes on the phone "чтобы проснуться" (*to wake up*). The author (with her team: "Мы пропылесосили интернет", *we vacuumed the internet*) lists where to look for missing morning energy. She notes that, according to an American sleep study, women wake up tired 1.5 times more often than men [msg #198].

1. **Not enough sleep (Недостаток сна).** Data vary somewhat, but overall research (the author cites the US CDC) says an adult needs **7+ hours of sleep every night**, and some research (the author cites a paper in Scientific Reports) even suggests average sleep need is **8 hours 40 minutes ± 10 minutes**. There is also "долг сна" (*sleep debt*) — the amount of sleep you owe your body; when you sleep too little, a deficit accumulates and tiredness follows you — even if you slept the full eight hours last night [msg #198].
2. **No regular schedule (Отсутствие режима).** If your bedtime and wake time differ from day to day, or you live on different schedules on weekdays and weekends, you are not living in accordance with natural biorhythms and keep knocking them off. The author adds that lack of morning energy is not the worst thing that can happen if you neglect circadian rhythms [msg #198].
3. **Poor sleep hygiene (Плохая гигиена сна).** It makes sleep lower-quality, so expecting morning energy is odd. It includes: lack of light in the morning or too much of it in the evening; looking at gadgets before bed; stuffiness; high temperature or noise in the bedroom; someone who constantly wakes you at night [msg #198].
4. **Food and "bad" habits (Пищевые и дурные привычки).** Everything we eat and drink during the day and right before bed strongly affects sleep quality and thus morning energy. Habits that affect sleep quality: lots of sugar in the diet; food or water close to bedtime; too much caffeine; alcohol [msg #198].
5. **Too little movement (Мало движения).** Exercise during the day generally helps you fall asleep better, and a 2022 study the author cites (published in Nature Communications) showed that morning energy is linked to how much physical activity there was the day before. Important: this refers to daytime activity; it is better not to overexert right before bed [msg #198].
6. **No motivation to get up (Нет мотивации вставать).** Even if you follow all recommendations and enjoy uninterrupted night rest, if you don't want to get up, energy won't appear. Think about what should (or should not) be scheduled for the morning and the day so that you want to jump out of bed [msg #198].
7. **Physical or mental illness (Физическое или психическое заболевание).** This happens too, but here "вам скорее к врачу, а не в телеграм" (*you should go to a doctor rather than to Telegram*). Lack of morning energy can be a symptom of dozens of diagnoses, from anemia to depression — but a diagnosis can only be made in the presence of other symptoms (and when you understand that everything in the items above is fine) [msg #198].

### Four-step coaching practice "where is your morning energy"
**Purpose.** A weekend assignment accompanying the seven-causes post, to help answer where *your* morning energy is [msg #199].
1. **Recall contrasting mornings.** Remember when it was easy and pleasant to get up, and the opposite. Briefly describe these situations, starting from the previous day (since the causes — sleep, food, movement, plans — often lie in the day before) [msg #199].
2. **Analyse the differences.** Analyse what distinguished those days; make a list and write it down [msg #199].
3. **Separate desirable from feasible changes.** Think about which changes are worth introducing, and which are realistically possible to introduce right now [msg #199].
4. **Pick one step and act.** Choose one step, plan it and act on it [msg #199].

The author offers coaching sessions with herself or a colleague for help with this; contact details are logistics and omitted [msg #199].

## Detailed insights

### Seasonal change and sleep need
The author describes sensing autumn approaching by the end of August — not because it got colder (in Miami Beach it was still +29 °C even at night) but because she **started sleeping longer: from her usual 7.5–8 hours to a full 9** [msg #176].

Her reasoning: "изменение сезона — стресс, и нужно дать организму адаптироваться" (*a change of season is stress, and you need to let the body adapt*). The phenomenon is temporary. She doesn't worry that she no longer gets everything done in the morning that she used to — she simply sleeps an extra hour and still feels full of energy [msg #176].

Lesson she draws from her own change over recent years: earlier she would have suffered rather than give up an extra hour "для себя" (*for herself*, i.e. the late-evening free time). Now she considers that the best and most luxurious thing you can do for yourself is not a $5,000 retreat, not a Prada coat, not another hour on the phone, but to let yourself sleep as much as you need. Post title: "Настоящая роскошь — разрешить себе спать на час дольше" (*True luxury is allowing yourself to sleep an hour longer*), framed as how to take care of yourself in autumn [msg #176].

Tool: a **smart alarm clock** is called indispensable for anyone who won't be waking with the dawn until next summer [msg #176].

### Case study: Rick Owens' energy-conserving routine
The author retells, in the first person as if Owens had chatted with her on Zoom, a WSJ interview in which designer Rick Owens describes the daily routines that keep his energy stable through the day. Headline: "Моя энергия ограничена, и я обязан её беречь" (*My energy is limited, and I'm obliged to conserve it*) [msg #71].

**Author's framing.** His habits are "far from the refined picture" painted by Andrew Huberman ("дядя Андрей Хуберман", *uncle Andrew Huberman*), yet they let him create and be independent, "and that is worth a lot". She concludes this shows that each of us can and should find our own unique way of life that fills and delights us. She says she was deeply taken with his slow, authentic lifestyle [msg #71].

His routine, as retold [msg #71]:
1. **Morning:** first thing after waking, a double espresso (there's a mini-bar in his walk-in wardrobe); then back to bed to look through emails that came overnight.
2. **Inspiration, not news:** his bedroom has an open shower, so he watches TV with the sound off from there and from the wardrobe — not news but films that inspire him at the moment (currently "The Black Cat", 1934). In parallel he plays music — either Montserrat Caballé singing Wagner or Julie London.
3. **No life/work separation:** his office is a couple of floors below the bedroom; he has never separated life and work.
4. **Breakfast for lunch:** most often avocado, eggs and toast, or avocado, salmon and tortillas; he likes eating outdoors — on the terrace or in the garden under a tree.
5. **Water (admitted weakness):** "unfortunately, I don't drink enough water" — and he hasn't done anything about it yet.
6. **After lunch:** smokes a couple of cigarettes, goes indoors, closes the curtains, scrolls Instagram a little.
7. **Daily one-hour afternoon nap:** every day after lunch he sleeps for an hour, right in the wardrobe. Sleep is his way to be alone with himself and preserve energy.
8. **Evening gym around 8 p.m.:** ab exercises — crunches and folds (складки), about 90 reps of each (30-30-30), plus 120 mountain climbers. "Кому-то кажется, что это тяжело, но это всего лишь одна песня в наушниках" (*Some think it's hard, but it's just one song in your headphones*) — it can be done in the length of Donna Summer's "I Feel Love".
9. **Weekends:** the best activities are a long walk around the city before lunch and a "quiet hour" (nap) afterwards.
10. **Little travel:** he travels little because it rarely justifies the energy spent.

Note that his afternoon nap falls in the post-lunch slot that the energy curve marks as the dip [msg #71, img #12].
