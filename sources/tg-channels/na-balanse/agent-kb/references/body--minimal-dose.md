# Minimal effective dose of movement

## What this covers

This topic gathers the author's series on "movement on the minimum settings" (`движение на минималках` — "movement at minimal settings") [msg #393]: how many steps per day bring most of the health benefit (about 7,000), why short workouts of 10 minutes count, and why ordinary household and walking activity ("zero zone", NEAT) is what actually makes a person "active". It also covers two books the author recommends for falling in love with movement — Kelly McGonigal's *The Joy of Movement* (with its explanation of why nature walks make us happier) and Haruki Murakami's *What I Talk About When I Talk About Running*. According to the source, this matters because people chronically underestimate the movement they already do, punish themselves with an "an hour in the gym or nothing" mindset, and miss that most of the benefit comes from modest, regular, enjoyable movement [msg #371, #373, #393].

## Core ideas

- **About 7,000 steps a day captures most of the benefit.** A meta-analysis the author cites shows benefit rising steeply up to 5–7 thousand steps and only modestly after 7–8 thousand; more is not dramatically better. See *Dose–response of daily steps* [msg #371].
- **Total weekly active minutes matter, not the length of a workout.** Any bout of moderate-or-vigorous activity counts, even very short ones; 10 minutes is a legitimate workout. See *Weekly active minutes rule* and *Start-small workout ladder* [msg #373].
- **Everyday "zero-zone" activity is what makes a person active.** The author's view is that constant, low-level base movement (cooking, cleaning, walking instead of taking a taxi) — not gym sessions — defines an active lifestyle, builds the habit and identity of a moving person, and supports recovery and health. See *Zero-zone activity* [msg #393].
- **Movement should be joyful, and nature amplifies it.** Following McGonigal, physical activity "rewires" the brain, treats anxiety and depression, and trains the capacity for joy; outdoors, the brain's default network switches from self-centred rumination to open "soft fascination". See *Two scenarios of the Default Mode Network* [msg #360].
- **Books can be a low-effort entry point to movement.** The author presents McGonigal's and Murakami's books as an "easy way to start moving" — reading them produces the desire to put on sneakers [msg #360, #454].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Dose–response of daily steps
Purpose: to answer "how many steps are enough" and to relieve people of the pressure of 10,000+ steps. The author titles the post "**7 000 шагов тоже норм**" (*7,000 steps is fine too*) and opens with "Ребята, выдыхаем и расходимся" (*Guys, exhale and go home*) [msg #371].

1. **The study.** A meta-analysis published "the other day" (August 2025) in *The Lancet Public Health* — 57 studies, more than 160,000 participants — compared how different daily step counts affect life expectancy [msg #371].
2. **Steep zone: up to 5–7 thousand steps.** Benefit grows sharply as steps increase up to 5–7 thousand a day [msg #371]. In the follow-up post the author restates the gradient in plain terms: 3 thousand is better than 2 thousand, 5 thousand is better than 3 thousand, 7 thousand is better than 5 thousand — "дальше все уже не так драматично" (*after that it's all not so dramatic*) [msg #373].
3. **Plateau zone: after 7–8 thousand.** Gains still exist above 7–8 thousand steps but are "no longer critical" [msg #371].
4. **What ~7,000 steps buys** (as reported by the author from the meta-analysis): risk of premature death reduced by 47%, dementia by 38%, depression by 22%, diabetes by 14% [msg #371].
5. **Lesson against "gigantomania".** The author admits she always "suffered from gigantomania" and thought more is always better; the data changed that view [msg #371].
6. **Practical translation.** 7,000 steps is roughly an hour of moderately brisk walking. In the city she easily logs 15–20 thousand; in her subtropical village at +33°C she is happy if a morning walk yields 7 thousand, and with walking around the house the day reaches about 10 thousand. Her step-tracker screenshots show a day of 10,305 steps and a peak day (Sun, Jun 8, 2025) of 24,080 steps [msg #371, img #371, img #372].
7. **Seasonal permission.** She loves walking and goes all out when she can, but in certain seasons one can focus on other directions of fitness — strength, balance, mobility — "без зазрений совести" (*without pangs of conscience*) [msg #371].

### Weekly active minutes rule
Purpose: to judge whether one's training "counts". Thesis (bold in the source): "**Для здоровья важна не длительность тренировки, а суммарные активные минуты за неделю.**" (*For health, what matters is not the length of a workout but the total active minutes per week.*) And "«малыши» тоже считаются" (*the "little ones" count too*) [msg #373].

1. **US guidelines dropped the 10-minute minimum.** The new US recommendations removed the statement about "at least 10-minute blocks": now any activity of any duration counts, provided it is of moderate or high intensity [msg #373].
2. **WHO target.** Accumulate 150–300 minutes of moderate activity per week, or 75–150 minutes of vigorous activity — and not necessarily in one piece [msg #373].
3. **Micro-bursts.** According to data the author cites (a Health.com article), even very short "bursts" of a minute or two, as long as they noticeably raise the heart rate, give benefits for longevity and risk reduction [msg #373].
4. **Intensity condition.** The counting rule applies to moderate or vigorous effort; for the micro-bursts the criterion is a perceptible increase in pulse [msg #373].

### Start-small workout ladder
Purpose: for someone who has no hour for training — or, like the author, finds it hard to make herself train for an hour [msg #373].

1. **Choose 10, 15 or 20 minutes** — "наш вариант" (*our option*) when an hour is not available or not realistic [msg #373].
2. **Build gradually.** This is how the author herself gradually reached a comfortable 30 minutes [msg #373].
3. **Accept the short day.** "Но если сегодня получается только десять — отлично, десять и делаем. Завтра добавим еще." (*But if today only ten works out — great, we do ten. Tomorrow we'll add more.*) The point is that a short session is a success, not a failure [msg #373].

### Formal vs informal movement
Purpose: to see the movement you are already doing. Movement is either **formal** (workouts) or **informal** (household activity), and it is the second that we chronically underestimate and count as "under-moving" [msg #393].

- **Case — the client and the Oura ring.** A client told the author she "doesn't move enough". It turned out she simply wasn't recording all her movement: she took off her Oura ring while cooking and working (she works with her hands), scolded herself for "too little movement", yet was exhausted by evening. As an experiment she kept the ring on — over the day she accumulated "a whole kitchen" of steps and bending, and the rings on the tracker closed without effort. Lesson: self-assessment of activity based on incomplete tracking can be wrong; unrecognized informal movement is real movement [msg #393].
- **Longevity observation.** "При этом среди долгожителей мало атлетов, зато много тех, кто заботится о семье, ходит пешком, копается на огороде." (*Meanwhile among long-livers there are few athletes, but many who take care of family, walk, dig in the garden.*) [msg #393]

### Zero-zone activity
Purpose: to define the base layer of movement beneath formal training and to replace the all-or-nothing mindset [msg #393].

1. **Definition.** Fitness usually talks about Zone 2 and Zone 3; zero zone is ultra-light activity *below* Zone 1 — a pace at which you breathe calmly and can talk [msg #393].
2. **Examples (complete list from the source):** walking back and forth ("ходить туда-сюда"); cooking; cleaning; carrying bags; watering plants; taking a walk after dinner; walking somewhere instead of taking a taxi. "Не спорт — но движение." (*Not sport — but movement.*) [msg #393]
3. **What makes someone "active".** The author's opinion: "**По мне именно это базовое постоянное движение делает человека «активным».**" (*To me it is exactly this basic constant movement that makes a person "active".*) [msg #393]
4. **Who it is for.** Not only beginners for whom "grown-up" sport is still scary or uncomfortable; it is also an excellent mode between hard training days [msg #393].
5. **Benefits claimed.** Helps recovery, supports mental state, sleep, blood-sugar regulation and lowers heart risks; the author points to reviews of the topic in The Conversation and The Guardian [msg #393].
6. **The main benefit — habit and identity.** Zero zone helps create the habit of moving and forms the identity of an active person. It is the background, the base — the NEAT activity (non-exercise activity thermogenesis) that erases the belief "или час в зале, или ничего" (*either an hour in the gym or nothing*) [msg #393].
7. **Limit — not a replacement for training.** The author is not talking anyone out of training: zero zone does not replace strength and interval work if the goals are strength, speed or volume. It is about health, recovery and the habit of "being in motion" [msg #393].

### Author's everyday NEAT habits
The author's personal practices for informal movement [msg #393]:
1. **Wash dishes and/or unload the dishwasher after a meal** — she says she knows that standing and bending after eating stabilises blood sugar.
2. **Never ask someone to bring her something** — she gets up and takes it herself.
3. **Always prefer a walk to a ride on transport.**
4. **Train less on days with an evening of cooking ahead** — she deliberately lowers training load when she knows she will be on her feet cooking.

### Two scenarios of the Default Mode Network
Purpose: explains, per McGonigal's book as retold by the author, why walking in nature makes us feel happier ("high") [msg #360].

- **Background.** Only about two decades ago scientists realised that when a person lies in an MRI scanner "doing nothing", the brain does not switch off: the so-called Default Mode Network (`сеть пассивной работы мозга` — network of passive brain work) activates. It is the inner "background" where we replay past scenes, rehearse future dialogues and argue with ourselves [msg #360].
1. **Urban, self-centred scenario** (in Russian: Городской, я-центричный). Within four walls the passive network easily couples with the reward system: each obsessive thought delivers a pinch of dopamine, the brain whispers «да-да, крути ещё» (*yes-yes, spin it more*). The result is a loop of self-criticism, anxiety and chewing over grievances [msg #360].
2. **Natural, open scenario** (in Russian: Природный, открытый). Outdoors the network switches into *soft fascination* — gentle, non-strained absorption in the current moment. Self-digging fades; zones of spatial imagination and social navigation activate; we feel sheltered and at the same time look at life «с птичьего полёта» (*from a bird's-eye view*) [msg #360].
- **Evidence cited.** Psychologists analysing people's written notes after park walks found the most frequent words were "love, life, time, world, God" [msg #360].
- **Evolutionary explanation.** In the "stone jungle" the brain must quickly assess social status and potential threats — hence self-fixation. On a trail (even in a city park) it is more useful to open up to the surroundings and notice birds, berries, wind direction. Nature switches the second branch of the passive mode back on — and instead of rumination we get a sense of perspective and a surge of calm joy [msg #360].

### McGonigal's movement advice
The closing advice of *The Joy of Movement*, which the author quotes in full (Russian translation) and says she wants to read aloud "like a poem". Its framing: "Общей формулы не существует; нет единого пути или метода. Просто делайте то, что приносит радость." (*There is no general formula; there is no single path or method. Just do what brings joy.*) Then the advice, item by item [msg #360]:
1. **Move.** "Любое движение в любом количестве сделает вас счастливыми." (*Any movement in any amount will make you happy.*)
2. **Move the parts of your body that can move**, and be grateful that you can.
3. **Move alone and in company.**
4. **Move at home and in nature.**
5. **Move to music and in silence.**
6. **Set goals that matter to you personally.**
7. **Go toward the goal in small steps, then take on serious challenges.**
8. **Seek new experiences, expand the boundaries of your personality.**
9. **Pay attention to sensations** — how do particular exercises affect you, how do they change you?
10. **Listen to your body.**
11. **Allow yourself to do what brings pleasure.**
12. **Look for metaphors and meanings.**
13. **Seek people, places and communities that inspire you and make you feel at home.**
14. **Follow the guiding star of joy** and do not leave that path for as long as you can.

## Detailed insights

### Movement as a brain "rewiring", and biophilia
The author describes *The Joy of Movement* as an attempt to answer why physical activity makes us happier, but "in fact a real manifesto of movement": movement doesn't just "pump" endorphins, it rewires the brain — treats anxiety and depression, trains the capacity to feel joy, helps feel the taste of life, "especially if you leave the room" (she crosses out "make the mistake of" leaving the room as a joke) [msg #360].

The most interesting discovery for her: her pull towards forest walks and "talking to squirrels and birds" has a scientific name — **biophilia** (`Биофилия — любовь ко всему живому` — love for all living things). McGonigal collected dozens of studies showing that people who are regularly outdoors rate themselves as more energetic and satisfied, with more meaning in life and less despair. The author says she really feels "high" during a walk [msg #360].

### The author's own result from reading
The book produced the kind of resolve the author last felt ten years ago reading Allen Carr. Concretely: after eight months she finally reactivated ClassPass and went to her first group yoga class, and plans to continue. She challenges readers: if after this book you don't want to put on sneakers, come back and tell her — she finds it hard to believe [msg #360]. The book's cover gives its full subtitle: "how physical activity helps find happiness, meaning, self-confidence and overcome difficulties" [img #361].

### Murakami: running, goals and conscious ageing
The author places Murakami's *What I Talk About When I Talk About Running* in the same category as McGonigal's book — an easy way to start moving: a book "of incredible simplicity, sincerity and depth" after which you want to love movement [msg #454].
- **Biographical lesson.** Murakami became a writer only at 33 and before that owned a bar; he believes this happened exclusively thanks to running [msg #454].
- **What the book is.** An incomplete autobiography which Murakami himself calls "зарисовками о беге, но никак не секретами здорового образа жизни" (*sketches about running, but by no means secrets of a healthy lifestyle*). The author's reading: whoever knows how to read will find the latter there too, and also a tribute to conscious ageing [msg #454].
- **Goal over metrics.** Murakami quote chosen by the author: time, other metrics and appearance are inessential and secondary; the most important thing is reaching the goal he set himself, running to it on his own legs, and then realising that what he had to do he did, and what he had to endure he endured [msg #454].
- **Radical routine change.** After selling the bar and becoming a writer, the first thing he and his wife did was radically change their daily routine: go to bed as soon as it gets dark and get up with the sun — it felt natural, how "self-respecting people" should live. Second, they decided to meet only people they enjoy meeting and, where possible, avoid unpleasant ones [img #454].
- **Need for a routine.** Life must have a routine by which you spend time and energy; if by a certain age you have not developed one, your life will soon lose clarity and contrast [img #455].
- **Unfairness.** Life is unfair — an indisputable fact; but in almost every "unfair" situation one can find some element of fairness. It will probably take time and effort — and it is equally likely that the time and effort will be wasted [img #456].
- **Ageing as a first experience.** For him, as for everyone, life is the first and only experience of ageing, so everything he feels about it, he feels for the first time [img #457].
- **Independence.** Emotional pain is the price we pay for our independence [img #458].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The health-risk percentages for 7,000 steps (47%, 38%, 22%, 14%) are reported by the author from the meta-analysis; the source gives no confidence intervals or population details beyond 57 studies and 160,000+ participants [msg #371].
- The zero-zone benefits (recovery, mental state, sleep, sugar regulation, heart risk) are stated with reference to press reviews (The Conversation, The Guardian), not specific studies [msg #393].
- The claim that standing and bending after meals stabilises blood sugar is presented as the author's own knowledge ("знаю"), without a cited source [msg #393].
- Zero zone is explicitly not a substitute for strength and interval training when the goals are strength, speed or volume [msg #393].
- The DMN mechanism and park-diary word analysis come from McGonigal's book as retold by the author; the original studies are not named [msg #360].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the author's "hacks" for making herself train (post on "fitness independence") are referenced but not contained in this topic's source [msg #373].
