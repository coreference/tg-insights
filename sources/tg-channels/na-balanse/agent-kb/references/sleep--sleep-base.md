# Sleep as the base

## What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's posts arguing that sleep is the foundation ("база") of energy and an active life, and explaining what happens when it is neglected: hormonal and metabolic disruption, prediabetic glucose levels, weakened immunity, cardiovascular risk, gene-expression changes, mood problems and visible ageing of the face. It also covers how daytime behaviour (morning exercise, heavy dinners, smoking, evening screens), alcohol and light at night shape sleep, simple self-tests for whether you sleep enough, the practical difference between sleep time and time in bed, and an honest note that much of sleep science is still uncertain. The source frames this as decisive: without sleep, nothing else that people try for energy works [msg #64, #87, #265].

## Core ideas

- **Sleep is the base of energy; nothing substitutes for it.** Katya Strelnikova states that without sleep no coffee, energy drink, "magic vitamin", breathing practice or planning will save you; without full sleep it is impossible to live actively, get everything done, reach goals and be happy [msg #64].
- **Quantity and quality both matter.** The recommended 7–9 hours is the target, but what happens during those hours (passing through complete REM/non-REM cycles) determines whether sleep is restorative [msg #23].
- **Chronic deprivation, not one bad night, is the problem.** Even a single short night shifts hormones, but the real danger is chronic loss, which within about a week can push glucose regulation into the prediabetic range and disrupt hundreds of genes [msg #87, #265].
- **Feeling fine is not evidence.** Alcohol improves perceived but not actual sleep, and chronically sleep-deprived people stop noticing their own cognitive decline — subjective impressions are unreliable [msg #23, #265].
- **Daytime behaviour and night-time environment shape sleep.** Morning activity and regular exercise help; heavy dinners, smoking, evening screens, alcohol close to bedtime and even small amounts of light at night hurt [msg #13, #23, #75, #81].
- **Time in bed must be longer than the sleep you need.** To get 7–9 hours of actual sleep, plan at least 30 minutes extra in bed [msg #143].
- **Sleep science is still uncertain.** How much sleep a given person needs is not known exactly, and some data from Walker's *Why We Sleep* reportedly did not hold up [msg #265].

### Daytime behaviour and sleep quality (4 findings with mechanisms)
Purpose: Marina Sidorich's summary of a recent large study on how behaviour during the day relates to sleep. Study design as reported: 2,050 participants, of whom 1,500 were women, mean age 34, mostly with higher education ("Нам актуально!" — *relevant to us!*); results are based on observation and self-report, i.e. associations rather than proven causes [msg #13].
1. **Morning physical activity** — specifically morning (not just any) activity was associated with longer sleep and fewer awakenings. Why (Marina's explanation): it improves synchronisation of the internal biological rhythms with the natural day–night cycle; bright light during morning exercise may help suppress melatonin (the hormone regulating sleep and wakefulness) in the morning and strengthen its effect at night [msg #13].
2. **Dinner as the largest meal** — those who named dinner their biggest meal took longer to fall asleep and slept less. Why: energy intake, nutrient processing and wakefulness belong to the daytime, when melatonin is low; sleep and recovery processes belong to the night, when melatonin is high. A large meal before sleep shifts the circadian rhythm [msg #13].
3. **Smoking** — associated with longer time to fall asleep and shorter sleep. Why: nicotine stimulates release of neurotransmitters such as dopamine that disrupt sleep, so smokers may stay awake longer, wake more often at night or even develop insomnia compared with non-smokers [msg #13].
4. **Screens before bed** — reports showed a linear relationship between screen time before bed, time to fall asleep and sleep duration; using screens for ≥2 hours before bed increased the likelihood of poor sleep quality. Why: internal circadian rhythms depend on regular alternation of light and dark; evening artificial light affects melatonin secretion and therefore sleep pattern and quality; in addition, incoming information can excite the nervous system and raise alertness exactly when the body should be preparing for sleep [msg #13].
Marina's addition: in practice "чужой опыт ничему не учит" (*other people's experience teaches nothing*), so if you want to fix your sleep, carefully observe your own routine and how it connects to your sleep [msg #13].

### How alcohol changes the night's sleep architecture
Purpose: answers "Хорошо ли спится под алкоголем?" (*Do you sleep well after alcohol?*) — it always seems that you fall asleep faster and sleep more soundly [msg #23].
- **Baseline:** adults need 7–9 hours for full recovery, but quality matters as much as quantity. During a normal night sleep alternates between REM and non-REM phases roughly every 90 minutes; ideally a person passes through 5–6 full cycles per night, which is equivalent to 7.5–9 hours [msg #23].
- **Alcohol's action:** it is a nervous-system depressant with a sedative effect that causes drowsiness [msg #23].
- **Perception vs reality:** in the same daytime-behaviour study (see above), participants tracked how alcohol affected their sleep; most reported falling asleep faster and sleeping more soundly. The study's conclusion was that alcohol improves *perceived* sleep quality, not sleep itself — a cognitive error. Under alcohol people did not notice their night awakenings but remembered falling asleep faster than usual [msg #23].
- **Effect on REM:** some studies cited in the post show alcohol disrupts sleep cycles and substantially shortens REM sleep, which the post describes as responsible for nervous-system recovery and critical for cognitive function and emotional state; lack of REM is said to weaken immunity and slow the growth of new healthy cells and tissues [msg #23].
- **Hypnogram on the card** (normal sleep vs under alcohol, over an 8-hour night, stages: wake, stage 1–4, REM) annotates four effects [img #23]:
  1. Alcohol shortens the time needed to fall asleep.
  2. Alcohol increases deep sleep (stage 4) in the first half of the night.
  3. Alcohol suppresses REM sleep in the first half of the night.
  4. Alcohol increases the number of awakenings and lengthens the REM phase in the second half of the night.
- **Conclusion:** with alcohol sleep may be long but not restorative ("может быть и долгий, но не восстановительный"). The minimum recommended step is not to drink 2–3 hours before sleep [msg #23].

### Matthew Walker's 5 domains of sleep-deprivation damage
Purpose: Marina Sidorich's summary of insights from Dr Matthew Walker, professor of neuroscience and psychology whose lab studies the effects of insufficient sleep on health, answering "what happens if you neglect sleep?" [msg #87].
1. **Hormones.** Even one night of insufficient sleep can affect testosterone, oestrogen and insulin levels. After a week of chronic sleep shortage, blood-sugar regulation can be disrupted to the extent that people could be classified as prediabetic. Short sleep can upset the balance of leptin and ghrelin (the hormones regulating hunger and satiety), leading to increased appetite and cravings for unhealthy food [msg #87].
2. **Immune system.** Sleep restriction leads to a significant reduction in cells important for fighting cancer. Insufficient sleep before a flu shot lowers the antibody response and the vaccine's effectiveness. Chronic sleep deprivation raises the likelihood of colds and flu [msg #87].
3. **Cardiovascular system.** In the US it was calculated that when clocks move one hour forward to daylight saving time, heart-attack risk rises by 24%; conversely in autumn, when people get an hour more sleep, heart-attack risk falls by 21% [msg #87].
4. **Mental and emotional wellbeing.** Sleep shortage leads to increased irritability, moodiness and negative emotional reactions [msg #87].
5. **Genes.** One week of poor sleep can throw the activity of more than 700 genes into disarray: some genes important for immunity are pushed into the background, while others linked to chronic inflammation and cardiovascular problems start working overtime [msg #87].
Key caveat: "Одна ночь плохого сна — не конец света, хроническое лишение сна — вот где таится проблема." (*One night of bad sleep is not the end of the world; chronic sleep deprivation is where the problem lies.*) [msg #87]

### Sleep time vs time in bed (3-step calculation)
Purpose: Katya Strelnikova's "kind reminder", prompted by comments under an earlier post on light alarm clocks: the 7–9 hours recommended for adults refers to actual sleep, not total time in bed [msg #143].
- Definitions: **sleep time** ("Время сна") is the actual time spent asleep; **time in bed** ("Время в кровати") includes falling asleep, night awakenings, lying awake, and the morning minutes before you "burst into the day" [msg #143].
- Principle: to reach the desired recovery you need more time in bed than the sleep target [msg #143].
Steps:
1. **Measure how long you take to fall asleep.** On average adults need 10–20 minutes [msg #143].
2. **Track how long you lie in bed after waking** — and check whether your gadget counts that as sleep [msg #143].
3. **If you wake at night, count how long you can spend awake** [msg #143].
Rule of thumb: on top of 7–9 hours of sleep, add at least 30 minutes for all the above; e.g. if you plan to sleep 8 hours, time in bed may be 8.5 or even 9 hours [msg #143].
Pitfall/warning: taking too long to fall asleep, being unable to wake up, or constantly waking at night may be a sign of a sleep disorder; Katya points such readers to a sleep guide and to a consultation [msg #143].

### Two-question sleep sufficiency test
Purpose: a simple practical method, attributed to Matthew Walker's *Why We Sleep* and shared in a forwarded post from "dreamers become doers", to tell whether you usually sleep enough [msg #331].
1. **After waking in the morning, could you fall asleep again at 10 or 11 a.m.?** If "yes", you are probably short of sleep and/or your sleep quality is insufficient [msg #331].
2. **Can you work at full capacity without spurring yourself on with caffeine at least until noon?** If "no", you are most likely self-medicating chronic sleep deprivation [msg #331].
The forwarded author credits the book for her data of the last week, adding jokingly that if it keeps going like this she is ready to read it for a year. Her Sleep Score chart (scores over September–May in the range 48–92, monthly averages around 60–80) and a later chart with a value of 92 accompany the post; the nightly screenshots are listed under *Sleep time vs time in bed* [msg #331, #332].

### Sleep vacation method for finding your personal sleep need
Purpose: from a New Scientist piece retold in a forwarded post (channel BitchyK, rubric "#переменныйЗОЖ"): since how much sleep each person needs is unclear, determine it yourself [msg #265].
- How: arrange "сонные каникулы" (*sleep vacation*) — go to bed at the same time every day but set no alarm; whatever time you wake up naturally is your answer. Condition: during the day you must be in excellent shape and not irritable [msg #265].
- Author's objection: she does not fully agree — she wakes without an alarm even when she has not had enough sleep, so spontaneous waking is not proof of sufficiency for everyone [msg #265].

## Detailed insights

### Light at night and blood glucose
Marina Sidorich: blackout curtains and diabetes are not directly linked, but according to a study mentioned by Andrew Huberman, even a small amount of light at night can raise morning blood-glucose levels. Her recommendation: sleep in complete darkness ("Спите в полной темноте"), because stable glucose is necessary to keep health and longevity in balance [msg #75].

### Regular exercise and insomnia
A forwarded study summary (forwarded from Misha Petrov's channel): people who did at least 2 hours of physical activity per week were 42% less likely to have trouble falling asleep and 22% less likely to suffer from insomnia. It was a 10-year study of about 4,300 people; the forwarded author notes as a curiosity that the most physically active participants were from Norway and the least active from Spain and Estonia [msg #81].

### Sleep deprivation and appearance
A forwarded post (from BIOHACKER): the mattress company Simba AI-modelled the faces of people with insomnia. Sleeping under 7 hours is said to give more wrinkles, dryness and "panda-like" dark circles under the eyes; according to the company, this happens because cells do not have enough time to recover. The comparison image ("ABOVE 7 HOURS SLEEP" vs "UNDER 7 HOURS SLEEP") labels the under-7-hours face with: weary facial expression, dehydrated skin, under-eye bags, dark circles and light wrinkles [msg #88, #89].

### What sleep science does and does not know (New Scientist retelling)
The forwarded BitchyK author calls sleep one of the hardest topics: apart from "sleep is wildly important", almost nothing can be said for certain, and some data on which the "great" *Why We Sleep* relies apparently have not been confirmed. Points she took from New Scientist [msg #265]:
- How much sleep is needed is unclear [msg #265].
- Genes that allow sleeping little without harm are extremely rare [msg #265].
- The sleep-vacation method (see *Sleep vacation method*) [msg #265].
- "The world is built for larks" — she, a lark herself, rejects this crudely; if you are an owl, it is important to get bright sunlight straight away in the morning so that falling asleep in the evening is easier. She uses Lumiette 3 light glasses for this [msg #265].
- The worst part: people who do not get enough sleep quite quickly stop noticing that they are getting duller. They think "at first I wasn't used to it, three days of adaptation, now I'm back to normal", while all tests show they keep getting duller. This is her answer to the claim "я приучил себя спать мало" (*I trained myself to sleep little*) [msg #265].
- Microbiome: people who are "active sleepwalkers" have little butyrate (which vegans have a lot of) and many inflammation-causing bacteria; *Bifidobacterium longum* 1714 improved sleep versus placebo over 8 weeks. Cause vs effect remains an open question [msg #265].
- Hormones/neurotransmitters: short sleep can disturb growth-hormone production (athletes build muscle not in the gym but during sleep); when people in an experiment were made to sleep 5 hours, their insulin sensitivity in some cases went from "healthy" to "prediabetes" in 3 days, and the way back to healthy took 6 to 10 days [msg #265].
- Sleep and learning: she calls sleep the cheapest "medicine" for learning and memory, though it takes a lot of effort — for example, switch off bright lights everywhere at 21:00, put the phone in airplane mode and skip two episodes of something, so that you wake without an alarm [msg #265].
- Personal note with a lesson: she dreams of averaging at least 7.5 hours instead of her current 6.5, imagining what she could achieve with an extra hour [msg #265].

## Practical playbook

- Move your physical activity to the morning where possible, ideally in bright light [msg #13].
- Do not make dinner the largest meal of the day [msg #13].
- Limit screens before bed; ≥2 hours of evening screen time was linked to poor sleep [msg #13].
- Do not drink alcohol within 2–3 hours of bedtime [msg #23].
- Sleep in complete darkness [msg #75].
- Aim for at least 2 hours of physical activity a week [msg #81].
- Owls: get bright light immediately after waking; evening routine: bright lights off at 21:00, phone in airplane mode [msg #265].
- Observe your own routine rather than relying on others' results [msg #13].
