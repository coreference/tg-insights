# Wellness independence and industry critique

## What this covers

This topic gathers Katya Strelnikova's opinion pieces (tagged #pov_набалансе, #тренды_набалансе, #гаджеты_набалансе) on the commercial side of health and wellness: a list of eleven widespread beliefs paired with the industries that profit from them, her concept of "wellness independence" (skills and basics over tools), a hands-on review of nine wellness gadgets she bought, and her reading of 2026 wellness trends as self-care turned into a "shop window". According to the author it matters because gadget- and trend-driven content creates misconceptions — that health is only for those who can afford devices, that popular means effective, and that a device can supply discipline — while the things that actually work are accessible to everyone [msg #338, #419, #434, #655].

## Core ideas

- **Ask who profits.** Whenever she hears "that's how it's done" or "there's no other way", the author asks whether someone is putting money in their pocket while she believes it; many everyday beliefs about rest, food, beauty and success turn out to serve a specific industry (full list in *Who profits from our beliefs*) [msg #338].
- **Skills over tools.** The healthiest, most energetic people the author knows "just sleep, eat normally, move"; the real "main gadget" is doing the basic, free things. Gadgets are at most pleasant add-ons and a poor investment [msg #419].
- **Gadgets are toys, not solutions.** Most gadgets have a disputed evidence base, and they do not solve habit problems; without a system, understanding of motivation and inner support, no $500 mask will change anything (see *Three misconceptions*) [msg #419].
- **"If there is no problem, you won't get insights."** Her own testing repeatedly found that devices aimed at a problem she does not have (nose, glucose) gave no meaningful benefit (see *Gadget review*) [msg #434].
- **Wellness as a shop window.** Overpriced devices and trend products are not isolated cases but a pervasive pattern, confirmed in her view by the VML Future 100 2026 trend report [msg #655].

### Who profits from our beliefs (11 beliefs and their beneficiaries)
Purpose: a critical-thinking habit. The author keeps a separate list in her phone where she records such cases; the attached screenshot shows a phone reminders-style list whose title begins «Нае» with the middle pixelated (a censored, likely crude word) and a counter of 21 entries [msg #338, img #338]. She was prompted while writing about why people today cannot rest and even fear rest, and she links it to having been called a «борцом за справедливость» ("fighter for justice") at school — always wanting to dig to the truth. The method: when you hear «так принято» ("that's how it's done") or «иначе никак» ("there's no other way"), ask «а есть ли кто-то, кто кладёт деньги в карман, пока я верю в эту лабуду?» ("is there someone putting money in their pocket while I believe this nonsense?") [msg #338].

Her selection of popular beliefs, each with who actually profits:
1. **"Rest is for the lazy" / "Your value = hours spent working"** — profits: corporations [msg #338].
2. **"Wrinkles are a sign of ill health / lead to disease"** — profits: the cosmetics industry [msg #338].
3. **"Beautiful / happy / successful means thin" / "Healthy means thin"** — profits: the weight-loss industry [msg #338].
4. **"Eating properly is impossible without added vitamins"** — profits: supplement (БАД) makers and pharma giants [msg #338].
5. **"There are safe doses of alcohol, and a celebration without a glass is no celebration"** — profits: alcohol giants [msg #338].
6. **"Fat-free = healthy"** — profits: sugar producers and processors who sweetened everything they stripped of fat [msg #338].
7. **"Sweets are the only natural, accessible way to treat yourself"** — profits: makers of «сахарного мусора» ("sugary junk") [msg #338].
8. **"Breakfast is the most important meal of the day"** — profits: cereal makers and the dairy industry, which, according to the author, launched the slogan in the 1940s [msg #338].
9. **"Without coffee in the morning I'm not human"** — profits: coffee chains and grain conglomerates [msg #338].
10. **"Screen time = rest"** — profits: Big Tech and advertisers [msg #338].
11. **"A big house and a new car are the mandatory markers of success"** — profits: banks (mortgages, car loans) and developers [msg #338].

### Three misconceptions created by gadget-heavy wellness content
Context: the trigger was a Reels video by a well-known wellness blogger showing her habits — infrared mask, heated thermos, massage gun, meditation gadget, subscription to a "mega-trainer's" workouts. The author finds it pretty but says it forms a whole set of misconceptions; she admits she sometimes shows gadgets herself but tries to be increasingly ethical and honest [msg #419].
1. **"If I don't have these gadgets, there's no point starting."** This content suggests health is only for those who can afford a trainer with a unique method, biohacker lab tests, a trendy gadget and a jar of lion's mane (ежовик). In reality, she says, the most energetic and healthy people she knows simply sleep, eat normally and move — «Все.» ("That's it.") [msg #419].
2. **"If everyone talks about it, it works."** Buzz creates a feeling of effectiveness, but most gadgets have a disputed base — at best FDA-approved, and she adds the ironic aside that the FDA once approved heroin. So they are toys, not solutions. She sometimes buys them herself not for health but to see "how it works"; her answer, regularly: it doesn't work, or it is no better than "grandfather's" (old-fashioned) analogues [msg #419].
3. **"With a gadget I'll become disciplined."** «Спойлер: нет.» ("Spoiler: no.") A gadget doesn't solve the habit problem. Without a system, an understanding of motivation and inner support, no $500 mask will turn us into a "person of the future". She knows many people whose massage guns and infrared masks just gather dust [msg #419].

### Wellness independence — four qualities to develop
Definition: wellness independence is readiness to take responsibility for one's own state and health and to do what is needed («делать, что дОлжно»). What is needed is usually available to everyone and requires no mask, thermos, chip or supplement: going to bed an hour earlier, walking instead of taking a taxi, putting the phone down, drinking a glass of water, eating normal food instead of sweets on an empty stomach — «Вот это — главный гаджет.» ("This is the main gadget.") Everything else is at most a pleasant add-on and a very bad investment; she notes she is currently trying, unsuccessfully, to sell her mask and massage gun. She frames it as freedom — from unnecessary marketing, from misconceptions, and from resistance to being healthy and happy [msg #419].

What to develop if you want wellness independence:
1. **Interest in yourself and your life, and curiosity.** Want to improve life out of curiosity, not fear. Ask: «Что я хочу сейчас? Как я могу сделать чуть лучше?» ("What do I want now? How can I make it a little better?") — instead of racing for "everything at once and forever" [msg #419].
2. **Wisdom and critical thinking.** Understand what works and what is marketing. Don't believe in miracle jars; know the basics: sleep, movement, nutrition, relationships [msg #419].
3. **Mindfulness (осознанность).** Notice your true needs, and also the inner voices that get in the way, such as perfectionism and self-criticism [msg #419].
4. **Flexibility and resilience.** On one hand, the ability to allow yourself different seasons; on the other, not to break down after a lapse but to gently return to yourself and the process [msg #419].

All of this, she concludes, is far more valuable than the chance to do another unboxing [msg #419].

### Gadget review — nine wellness gadgets tested
Purpose: a follow-up to the wellness-independence post, for readers who are eyeing gadgets. Her general verdict: gadgets don't suit everyone, don't always work and often turn out to be a poor investment. Over the last couple of years she also broke her sleep with a trendy supplement and caught a nocebo effect from trackers (described in other posts) [msg #434].
1. **Facial steamer / steam sauna for the face.** Looks great in bloggers' videos, but with her skin (fine apart from dryness) it was pointless; used at most three times. She bought it under an "Instagram fantasy" of a spa day (towel, steaming, mask, filing nails). Her real spa day is 2 hours of walking in the forest and an hour in a magnesium bath, or a sauna/floating session, without steam or masks. Verdict: not needed [msg #434].
2. **Nasal dilator ("nose strips", Intake Breathing linked).** If you have no nose problems you don't need it — no difference at all. Her husband, who has chronic sinusitis, also noticed no effect. To be fair, it is comfortable to sleep in. If you do have breathing/nose problems, it may be worth a try [msg #434].
3. **Massage gun (Theragun).** Pleasant and a nice ritual, but far from even myofascial release, let alone a good massage. She self-releases (MFR) at least once a week and allows herself a massage once a month. She later sold the gun [msg #434].
4. **TheraFace Depuffing Wand (Therabody).** She loved the effect, but after her first cosmetologist visit it turned out it was harming her skin: with her skin type such mechanical and thermal tools are not allowed. She switched to microcurrents — less luxurious and less noticeable at removing puffiness, but still a great effect. She gave the wand to a friend. Lesson: check device suitability for your skin type with a specialist [msg #434].
5. **Glucose tracker (Lingo).** Again: without a problem you are unlikely to get amazing insights. It confirmed her nutrition and glucose are fine. Interesting finding: oversensitivity to fruit after lunch and dinner — even eaten after a balanced plate with vegetables and protein, glucose easily jumps to 150+ (curiously, lower for sweets), but returns to normal fairly quickly without a rebound into hypoglycaemia. Her hypothesis that night awakenings were linked to sugar drops was not confirmed [msg #434].
6. **Massage glasses (Renpho Eyeris 3).** She uses them daily, but had she tried someone else's first she would hardly have bought them. A sleep mask plus noise-cancelling headphones is her choice. Hard to call it "massage" — a kind of squeezing of the skull with heat, and very noisy [msg #434].
7. **Weighted vest.** A cool and useful thing, but she saved money by not buying the ergonomic "specifically for women" version; after a month or two her chest started to hurt and she had to sell it. Lesson: don't economise on ergonomic fit. She plans to buy a new one later [msg #434].
8. **Infrared (red-light) mask (HigherDose).** She likes it and her cosmetologist approved it. The unexpected problem: the goggles leave severe marks; she tried without them and is now treating her eyes. Not a common problem, but if you also get "elastic marks", this may not be your option. She is looking at other mask form factors later [msg #434].
9. **Cortisol-measuring "gadget" (Eli Health).** The advertising sounded promising, but it is a test like a COVID test where the promised "instant" takes 20 minutes. She bought it to quickly measure cortisol at night. It simply doesn't work so far — the app gives no results; 2 out of 2 attempts failed [msg #434].

## Detailed insights

### Wellness trends 2026: self-care as another shop window
The author reads current wellness novelties as a pattern rather than exceptions or one-off promotions. Examples she cites: the CryoTherm Palm device from Therabody for cooling palms between sets in the gym, at $400 — she says she had never even heard of this problem — and a commenter's line that it is a virtuoso repackaging of an ordinary bottle of ice-cold liquid; and a body scanner news item attributed to Midjourney that "everyone" republished, which she reads as simply an advertisement for their salons, adding that she can picture the marketers inventing it because she has done such work herself [msg #655].

She says the pattern is confirmed by VML's annual trend report Future 100 (2026 edition). She analysed its wellness section in detail in a long text published on her T-Zh (Т—Ж) blog; per the post, the trends include water sommeliers, "enhancement during sleep" (улучшайзинг во сне), and resilience retreats costing €11,000. ## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- All entries are the author's opinions and single-person self-experiments (n=1), explicitly tagged as point-of-view posts; the beneficiary attributions in the beliefs list (including the 1940s origin of the breakfast slogan) are her claims, not sourced in the post [msg #338, #419, #434].
- Conflict of interest the author discloses: she sometimes showcases gadgets herself, and she was offering her infrared mask for sale at half price in the gadget post; she also states she has worked in marketing [msg #419, #434, #655].
- Her gadget verdicts are explicitly conditional: nasal strips "may be worth a try" if you have nose/breathing problems; she still uses massage glasses daily and still likes the infrared mask; the weighted vest is "useful" in the right (women's ergonomic) version [msg #434].
