# Impermanence, uncertainty and resilience

## What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel taught about living with things outside your control. It covers the author's own case — a refused Spanish residence permit after five years of moves — as a demonstration of the practice of impermanence and the "two arrows"; a six-step method she uses with clients for chronic fatigue and anxiety in wartime; and a guest text by Arsen Nizhnikov on thinking in scenarios rather than trying to predict the future when making big life decisions. According to the source, the point is that unpleasant events will keep happening, but the extra layer of suffering on top of them is optional and can be reduced through practice; and that in a world that keeps changing, the skill of thinking well about decisions matters more than finding "one right decision" [msg #665, #678, #687].

## Core ideas

- **Suffering is a second layer, not the event itself.** The author distinguishes the unpleasant event (and the body's natural stress reaction to it) from emotional suffering, which comes from the mismatch between reality and expectations and from resisting change. The first is unavoidable; the second can be removed through practice. Full detail in *Principle of impermanence* and *Concept of the two arrows* [msg #665, #687].
- **Impermanence is practised by living, not on a meditation cushion.** Life is "soaked" in impermanence; even if you change nothing, change waits around the corner, so the head has to be prepared in advance [msg #665].
- **Calm is a result of practice, not character.** The author's reaction to two nearly identical crises one year apart differed "by 100%"; she attributes this to a year of study, reflection, meditation and self-observation, not to innate calm [msg #687].
- **Under real threat, anxiety and fatigue are normal; work on what you control.** The six-step method moves from naming reality, through normalising and self-compassion, to finding the main energy drains, separating controllable from uncontrollable, and taking small actions to restore agency [msg #667].
- **Think in scenarios, not forecasts.** Arsen Nizhnikov argues that the "guaranteed development" view of the future is outdated and that each competing view of the future holds only part of the truth; decisions should be tested across several plausible scenarios and should keep options open [msg #678].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Principle of impermanence (принцип непостоянства)
**Purpose.** A lens for meeting change without suffering. The author encountered it at the "Laboratory for developing philosophical intelligence" and named it one of the two concepts that "hooked" her most [msg #687].

**Content.** Everything changes; nothing stands still for even a second. The author's formulation: "мы страдаем не из-за самих перемен, а из-за того, что сопротивляемся им" (we suffer not because of the changes themselves, but because we resist them). We cling to a stability that "в природе вещей просто не существует" (simply does not exist in the nature of things) [msg #687].

**What practising it means, per the author.** The practice is about recognising that everything in life is outside our control and is constantly changing, and about not adding to the troubles that will inevitably happen "еще и слой страданий от несоответствия реальности ожиданиям" (an extra layer of suffering from the mismatch between reality and expectations). Bodies change, circumstances change, people change, plans fall apart. Even when we change nothing and hold on to the habitual and familiar, impermanence still "waits around the corner". If you do not prepare your head for this, then every time, on top of the natural anxiety of approaching uncertainty, there will be "тяжелый довесок страдания" (a heavy add-on of suffering) [msg #665].

**Signature line:** "непостоянство не практикуется в позе лотоса" (impermanence is not practised in the lotus position) — it is practised by life itself. The author describes how she had expected her year of impermanence practice to be "a beautiful practice — you meditate, read, reflect, spiritual growth", and instead life delivered the real test [msg #665].

**What it is not.** The author stresses that the absence of suffering does not mean she has become "some enlightened being"; she still feels hurt and unpleasant ("мне обидно, неприятно" — I feel hurt, it is unpleasant), but "эмоционального страдания — нет" (there is no emotional suffering) [msg #665].

### Concept of the two arrows (концепция двух стрел)
**Purpose.** To separate unavoidable pain from self-inflicted suffering; the second concept from the Laboratory that most struck the author [msg #687].

1. **The first arrow** — the pain itself: the fact, the event, the loss. In the author's case, the refusal of the residence permit and the urgent need to leave Spain [msg #687].
2. **The second arrow** — the way we finish ourselves off with that pain: endless analysis, replaying, "а что если" (what if), "почему именно я" (why me), "а вот если бы" (if only) [msg #687].

**The insight it gave the author.** Applying it to her earlier crisis, she realised: "моя тревога не из-за эмиграции и не из-за денег. А из-за сопротивления переменам, неумения быть в неизвестности без попытки все контролировать и постоянного проваливания в фантазии" (my anxiety was not because of emigration and not because of money, but because of resisting change, being unable to stay in the unknown without trying to control everything, and constantly falling into fantasies) [msg #687].

### Six steps for chronic fatigue and anxiety in wartime
**Purpose / context.** A text the author wrote as a guest post for another channel (Zhuravleva's channel) and reposted, on what she and her clients talk about and do in sessions when living under real threat (night-time drone attacks, alarm sirens, searching for petrol) while having to go to work and function in the morning. Her own entry point: she happened to be in Dubai on 28 February when war reached it; waking up to the sound of sirens, she understood "я не умру под забором" (I won't die in a ditch), her habitual anxieties seemed to drain of blood and fade away, and it became clear that you simply have to live while you have the chance. She is now far from that reality, but many of her clients are in it [msg #667].

1. **Name things by their names (Называем все своими именами).** The first and hardest step is to acknowledge reality as it is. Not "everything will be fine" and not "don't think about it", but honestly: yes, it is hard now, I'm scared, I'm tired. Why: the brain spends a huge amount of energy on *not* thinking. The paradox, per the author, is that accepting reality is "не капитуляция, а освобождение ресурса" (not capitulation but a release of resources). When we name things, anxiety does not grow — it becomes manageable [msg #667].
2. **Normalise anxiety and fatigue (Нормализуем тревогу и усталость).** Anxiety under real threat is a sign that the nervous system is working normally; the body reacts to danger as it should. Fatigue in these conditions is also normal: constant readiness for threat drains you no less than physical exertion. The author jokes that if you have neither anxiety nor fatigue now, it is worth asking whether you are by chance a sociopath. Instruction: allow yourself to be tired [msg #667].
3. **Treat yourself with compassion (Относимся к себе с состраданием).** Living where drones may come at night and you must be a functioning person at work in the morning is a huge load, and most people carry it silently while also reproaching themselves for "coping badly". Feel sorry for yourself: anyone in your place would be tired and anxious — "Это не слабость. Это человечность." (This is not weakness. This is humanity.) The inner critic is especially destructive in such conditions because it adds an internal threat to the external one. How: speak to yourself as you would to a close friend — what would you say to them, how would you advise them to take care of themselves? [msg #667]
4. **See what drains you most (Смотрим, что истощает больше всего).** Answer honestly: what exactly takes the most energy right now — the news? conversations? uncertainty? the feeling of helplessness? Why: once you can see where the drain comes from, it becomes possible to work with it [msg #667].
5. **Sort out what is in your control and what is not (Разбираемся, что в нашем контроле, а что нет).** The author calls this one of the most liberating questions she knows. We spend colossal energy resisting what we cannot change and barely notice how much is still in our power. How: see *Control / no-control two-column sheet* below. Result: "Теперь вы можете вернуть контроль" (Now you can take back control) [msg #667].
6. **Take back your agency (Возвращаем себе агентность).** The feeling of helplessness is one of the main sources of exhaustion; when we feel we can do nothing, energy leaks away into nothing. So the next step is to start doing what is in your power — not globally, but little by little ("тихой сапой", quietly and steadily), one issue at a time. Examples: cook a proper lunch; go for a walk. Why: this will not solve the situation in the moment, but it returns the sense that you choose, you act, "что вы живете, а не только выживаете" (that you are living, not only surviving) [msg #667].

### Control / no-control two-column sheet
Used in step 5 above. Take a sheet of paper and divide it into two columns: "что я контролирую / что я не контролирую" (what I control / what I don't control). The author's examples [msg #667]:
- News running 24/7 — not in my control; how many hours I spend watching the news — in my control.
- The situation around me — not in my control.
- My body, sleep, food, who I talk to — in my control.

Once done, the controllable column is where you redirect energy (see step 6) [msg #667].

### Scenario thinking instead of predicting the future
**Author and context.** Guest text by Arsen Nizhnikov, invited by the channel author because clients constantly face uncertainty when deciding whether and where to move, what profession to learn, whether to start a business, buy an apartment or have children. The author's framing: in such fundamental questions what matters is not to "find one right decision" but to develop the skill of thinking about it properly [msg #678].

**Diagnosis (Arsen's view).** Since 2020 "we regularly wait for 'normal life' to return — only the date in the memes changes". The view of the future people are used to relying on is outdated: the "guaranteed development" scenario — tomorrow roughly like yesterday, only with better technology, a growing economy and a more comfortable and safer life — is, in his words, probably no longer worth waiting for; nor is the belief that studying, working and trying hard is enough to secure a decent future. The competing positions — wait for the old trajectory to return; expect only deterioration and give up on planning; hope that AI, a quantum computer or another technology will solve all problems — each contain only part of the truth, and it is short-sighted to make any one of them the single main one. "Дело в том, что мы не знаем" (The thing is, we don't know) [msg #678].

**Main idea.** Drawn from his study of systems thinkers (Nate Hagens, Daniel Schmachtenberger, Donella Meadows): "нам стоит меньше пытаться угадать будущее и больше учиться мыслить сценариями" (we should try less to guess the future and learn more to think in scenarios) [msg #678].

**Definition.** A scenario is not a forecast of the "it will be exactly like this" kind. It is one of several coherent and plausible versions of how events may unfold. The method:
1. Consider several different variants (scenarios) of how things may develop.
2. Test your decisions in each of them.
3. Look for actions that keep their meaning across different outcomes [msg #678].

**Worked examples by decision type** [msg #678]:
- **Choosing a profession:** think about which skills will stay useful under different economic and technological conditions, and how to make your path less dependent on a single market or technology.
- **Planning a move:** assess not only the current standard of living in the country but also political stability, climate risks, access to medicine, social ties, and how easy it would be to leave in the future if the need arises.
- **Starting a business or buying a home:** think about what happens if income falls, prices rise, demand changes, or you need to leave quickly. Which obligations leave you freedom, and which make you too dependent on one favourable scenario?

**Closing theses (Arsen):** finding your place now probably means learning to navigate by a map that keeps changing. Nobody knows what the future will be, but those who have learned to think about it more rationally, with higher quality and with greater calm live more resiliently. The aim is to see what is happening "без самоуспокоения или катастрофизации" (without self-reassurance or catastrophising), to account for different scenarios and to keep the ability to act [msg #678].

### Four general questions for decisions under uncertainty
Arsen's general questions to apply to any big decision, alongside scenario thinking [msg #678]:
1. Which decisions keep more paths open for me?
2. What am I becoming too dependent on?
3. What would be hard to restore if I lost it: health, relationships, reputation, skills, trust?
4. Which actions will be useful even if my main forecast turns out to be wrong?

## Detailed insights

### The author's case: the residence-permit refusal as an "exam"
In 2021 the author and her husband stated the intention to find the ideal place to live; she jokes that the Universe took this literally and arranged a large-scale study: in five years, 8 moves and three emigrations — Indonesia during covid, 2.5 years in the USA (which she found really hard), and now the failed Spain (where she felt very good and thought everything had worked out and the card was a formality). She has lived in Moscow, Bali, Java, Istanbul, Miami, Raleigh and now Barcelona. Her summary: the good news is they now understand a great deal about the ideal place; the bad news is that the Universe apparently has not finished [msg #665].

A year before, after the Philosophical Intelligence course, she realised that her weak spot was control and accepting impermanence, so she chose to devote a whole year to practising and studying these themes, asking her teacher to compile a programme of books, articles, videos and retreats. Then came, in sequence: having to return from the US to Russia, wintering in Moscow, being caught in the war in Dubai, nervously collecting documents for a Spanish visa and residence permit, waiting — and finally a refusal, meaning an urgent exit from Spain, a new Schengen visa and a new application, with six to twelve more months of hassle ahead. Her lesson: "Как говорится, бойтесь своих желаний" (As they say, be careful what you wish for) [msg #665].

Her reaction is the evidence she offers that the practice works: hurt and unpleasantness, but no emotional suffering [msg #665].

### The body still reacts — observe it without judgement
Even with the practice working, the body reacts. The first night after the refusal the author barely slept because her hands itched terribly; under stress she has a strong histamine release. She frames this as normal, no longer surprising and no longer a source of suffering: "Смотрю на это без осуждения, просто наблюдаю, как организм переваривает новость" (I look at it without judgement, just observe how the body digests the news). In the follow-up post she again calls the body's reaction "a natural reaction to change — stress", while "страданий больше нет" (there is no more suffering) [msg #665, #687].

### Before and after: same crisis, different reaction
The author contrasts two nearly identical events. In late July 2025 her husband was laid off and they had to leave the US. She felt as if the stool had been kicked out from under her: she had only just got used to the States, started making plans and identifying with the place. She was badly shaken — stress "pouring out of her", crying, unable to sleep, crazy dreams, waking at night with a lump in her throat and mush in her head. In July of the following year the same story repeated (residence-permit refusal, fog, uncertainty, urgent departure from Spain), yet her reaction "отличается на 100%" (differs by 100%). She received many compliments on her calm and stability and jokes that she sometimes feels like "a walking advertisement for mindfulness" [msg #687].

### What changed: a year of practice
In the earlier stress, "with literally shaking hands", she enrolled in the Laboratory for developing philosophical intelligence, seeking basic stability and wanting to stop falling apart at every turn of fate — while doubting what "philosophical intelligence" had to do with it. The Laboratory examined a wide range of concepts around which participants built their own reflection and practice; this turned out to be necessary for finally making sense of important things that otherwise live in the head on their own, on autopilot. Concepts covered included: the preciousness of human life, awareness of one's mortality, errors in perceiving reality, the difference between pain and suffering, and contact with one's deep essence. The two that hooked her were impermanence and the two arrows (see *Frameworks*) [msg #687].

Afterwards she asked her teacher Lobsang to put together a development programme (books, articles, videos, retreats), studied it diligently from the autumn, and — "the main thing" — meditated almost every day. Over the year she learned to observe the mind, let go of control, and stop fighting for everything to be "понятно и навсегда" (clear and forever). Life then set her an "exam" [msg #687].

Her emphasis: "это не я такая от природы… Это не характер и не врожденное спокойствие. Это сила практики" (it is not that I am like this by nature… It is not character and not innate calm. It is the power of practice) — of study, reflection, meditation and self-reflection. A year earlier she was shaking just like anyone. She also calls it work in progress [msg #687].

### Why study philosophy, per the author
Studying philosophy is a way to look soberly at your inner settings and understand where to adjust them so as to suffer less — like updating the "drivers" you run on, the ones inherited from parents, culture and childhood experience that are long overdue for review. She recommends the Laboratory to those looking for stability and flourishing [msg #687].
