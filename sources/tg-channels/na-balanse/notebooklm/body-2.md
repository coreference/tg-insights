## Body image, face and beauty

### What this covers

This topic gathers everything the channel На балансе taught about how we relate to our bodies and faces: the author's shift from body positivity to body neutrality, why the number on the scale is not a health marker, how video calls and auto-retouching distort self-image (Zoom dysmorphia), beauty burnout and the instrumental trap of beauty culture, a critique of Ozempic/GLP-1 marketing, and — on the practical side — the author's long, self-documented journey with facial puffiness, skincare, SPF, microcurrents, Botox and supplements, including what it all cost her in a year [#24, #133, #183, #301, #576, #613, #659].

The source frames this as important because the author is a veteran of a 20-year struggle with anorexia and eating disorders: obsession with appearance costs time, money and energy, damages health (eating disorders, hormonal failures, hair loss) and keeps people from investing in what actually matters, while calm, system-based care of the body is still worth doing [#24, #576, #659].

### Core ideas

- Neutrality, not love. Acceptance does not have to be positive; it is enough to treat the body neutrally, care for it and respect its function (see *Body neutrality instead of body positivity*) [#24].
- The scale is not health. Weight says nothing about energy, strength, hormones, digestion, sleep or how you feel; strength training, nourishing food and non-scale victories matter more (see *Scale-free markers of health*) [#183].
- The camera is not a mirror. Video calls and auto-retouch create a distorted image of oneself that people do not recognise as distorted, fuelling Zoom dysmorphia and an unattainable ideal version of oneself [#133, #286].
- Beauty culture exhausts and traps. Beauty burnout is a recognisable state with six symptoms; at a deeper level beauty has turned from a tool into a goal (the instrumental trap), which Naomi Wolf describes as a mechanism of control [#301, #576].
- Weight cycling is the danger; drugs and diets work the same way. Bodies have a natural weight range; holding weight below it triggers defence mechanisms; a BMJ meta-analysis the author cites shows Ozempic works like a restrictive diet and weight returns after stopping [#577, #613].
- The face reflects how you live — and it is a system, not a magic jar. Sleep, food, movement and stress form the base; on top come a skincare system matched to skin type with working concentrations of actives, daily SPF, de-puffing, Botox and supplements — and it is expensive, which is worth remembering before comparing yourself to others [#380, #444, #461, #659].

- Cosmetology as prevention, not chasing youth. Because she plans to live to 100–120 in good health, the author treats cosmetology as conscious management of what happens to the body: "do less, but earlier", the same principle she applies to the rest of her health (see *Cosmetology as prevention*) [#669].
- Target the ageing type, not "beauty". Her request was limited: less sagging and swelling for a puffy-deformational ageing type, with no aim to "make a princess" of herself. The cosmetologist combined an RF device with injections for a synergistic effect (see *Oligio + biorevitalisation + Mesosculpt*) [#669].
- Compliments and insults do the same thing. People who insist she is beautiful and people who insist she is ugly both try to place her on a scale she is trying to leave (see *Leaving the beauty caste system*) [#695].
- No single image is the truth. She looks different from moment to moment, so the honest label is "a spectrum". Freedom means no longer reducing yourself to one point on someone else's scale [#695].
- We never see our own face, and we judge photos through the inner critic. We dislike photos partly because we are used to our mirror image. The bigger cause is that we look for flaws, the way the beauty industry taught us. A kind photographer's gaze can be borrowed, and accepting yourself is built from many small moments of noticing something beautiful (see *Photo self-acceptance practice*) [#728].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Body neutrality instead of body positivity
Purpose: a stance toward the body for people for whom loving every fold feels false or demanding. The author titled her end-of-year post «Я отказываюсь от бодипозитива» (I am giving up body positivity) and arrived at this after 20 years of struggling with anorexia, 5 years of active work on her eating disorder and a couple of years of body-positive content. She did not give up — she realised that what she preaches is called something else, and that «позитив» and «любовь» (positivity and love) are too demanding and too far from her true goals [#24].

1. Acceptance need not be positive. Her realisation of the year: «“принятие” не обязано быть “позитивным”» (acceptance does not have to be positive), and «бодинейтральности мне вполне достаточно» (body neutrality is quite enough for me). You do not need to admire every wrinkle; it is enough to treat it as something that exists, and it is okay simply because it is on your body [#24].
2. Why not positivity: it is the same game. Heightened reverence for body features looks to her like another flirtation with the beauty industry — «Игра в ту же игру: “Кто красивее?” И выстраивание очередных стандартов.» (Playing the same game: who is prettier? And building yet more standards.) [#24].
3. The body is a dynamic process. At 37 she knows for sure that the body keeps changing, so loving the body would actually mean loving change — a much harder goal from a different opera, a fight not with the beauty industry but with evolution (we are not born to love change) [#24].
4. Stop fixating. She does not want to think about the body constantly and loop on it: «Ну складка и складка....» (Well, a fold is a fold....). Her happiest moments of life happen far from such thoughts [#24].
5. Shift focus to neutrality and function. In the end «тело нужно не для того, чтобы оно быть красивым, а чтобы выполнять свои функции и доносить меня лет до 100, а лучше 120» [sic] (the body is not there to be beautiful but to perform its functions and carry me to 100, or better 120) [#24].
6. Respect and care regardless of liking. Neither love nor hate: she wants to respect the body and care for it whether or not she likes the folds, wrinkles and unevenness. This correlates better with her wider goals — becoming attentive, mindful, compassionate, non-critical and non-reactive — which is what she wants to practise first of all [#24].
7. Non-judgement as the first step. She hedges that neutrality may be an intermediate stage on the way to positivity, or may not; either way, learning to relate to the body without evaluation is the necessary first step [#24].

Illustration of progress: after years invested in loving her folds she admits it is still not absolute love, but she has progressed fundamentally. Five years earlier, while doing self-acceptance meditations on Insight Timer, she was screaming in her head about what gratitude for one's body could possibly mean; now she regularly catches herself thinking «вообще-то я понимаю, о чем она» (actually, I understand what she means) [#24].

#### Scale-free markers of health
Purpose: for anyone who still thinks they need to lose a couple of kilos for health. Marina Sidorich's thesis: the number on the scale is not a marker of a healthy body; real progress is how we feel, how much stronger we become and how harmoniously the body functions [#183].

What the scale will not tell you (5 items):
1. How much more energy you have [#183].
2. How strength and endurance change [#183].
3. What is happening with hormones and digestion [#183].
4. Whether sleep quality has improved [#183].
5. How you feel in your body [#183].

What really matters (3 components):
1. Building muscle through strength training. This is what creates the toned, healthy look; it speeds up metabolism, improves hormonal balance and psycho-emotional state, and gives confidence in your own strength — a healthy body in the long term. Caveat: the number on the scale may go up, because muscle mass is heavier than fat [#183].
2. Food that nourishes and supports the body. Cutting or counting calories is described as a direct path to breakdowns (срывы) and short-term results. The healthy goal is to choose valuable foods and amounts that give maximum healthy energy and support; often this means eating larger amounts and a wider variety, which in practice raises only energy, not weight [#183].
3. Victories unrelated to the scale. More energy, better digestion, balanced hormones, sleep quality — this is what to pay attention to; the scale reflects neither mood nor emotional stability [#183].

Rule of thumb: we are all living people — there are days you want a break from training or to eat something comforting, and that is normal. What counts is not single moments but the overall system and the actions chosen regularly: «Здоровье — это не цифра на весах, а результат наших привычек и выбора, который мы делаем чаще.» (Health is not a number on the scale but the result of our habits and the choices we make more often.) [#183].

#### Zoom dysmorphia — definition and what to do
Definition (also printed on the post's card): Zoom dysmorphia is «повышенная зацикленность или недовольство собственной внешностью из-за участия в видеозвонках» (heightened fixation on or dissatisfaction with one's own appearance due to taking part in video calls); it can cause anxiety, lower self-esteem and even depression [#133, img #133].

Mechanism and context: the author had earlier written about Zoom fatigue (lower productivity and creativity from calls), then checked what else calls do to one's state and diagnosed herself anew. A source she links (an NCBI/PMC article) says constant video calling can cause body-acceptance problems even in healthy people, or worsen an existing problem — she adds a greeting from an ED veteran with twenty years' experience. She has heard of Snapchat or selfie dysmorphia since 2015 — people literally get plastic surgery to look more polished, as under a filter — and the COVID boom of video calls only strengthened this trend. She reports, hedged with «Говорят» (they say), that people with various dysmorphias or a tendency to them outnumber those with OCD, which is itself one of the most common mental disorders in the world [#133].

The scariest feature of this variety: «отсутствие понимания, что внешность искажена» (no awareness that the appearance is distorted). With filters people understand a distortion is taking place; with calls we simply consider ourselves ugly [#133].

Key fact: cameras — especially low-quality ones — certain angles, and poor or wrong lighting distort facial features. «Камера — не зеркало.» (The camera is not a mirror.) It shows not a reflection of your appearance but a distortion, a variation on the theme [#133].

What to do (5 actions):
1. Turn off self-view in the app settings [#133].
2. Turn the camera off more often if there is no way to hide self-view [#133].
3. Improve image quality: buy a good lamp, a stand for the computer, possibly a dedicated camera — because low-quality cameras, angles and light are what distort the face [#133].
4. Consciously focus on the conversation: listen attentively, take notes — attention goes to the talk rather than your own image [#133].
5. Record a mantra of positive affirmations that stresses what you like about your appearance, and say it before calls [#133].

Escalation: if you understand that your dysmorphia (ugly nose, wrinkles, awful face oval and the like) is already progressing and interferes with life and work, see a psychotherapist [#133].

#### Beauty burnout — 6 symptoms and 3 coping steps
Source: a post forwarded from the channel Ko-Lab, prompted by the success of the film The Substance. Beauty burnout («Выгорание красотой») is a state in which constant pressure to meet beauty standards causes fatigue, anxiety and a sense of overload; women spend time, money and energy maintaining their looks but feel stress instead of pleasure. The term arrived a few years ago together with social-media trends that are often inapplicable to real life [#301].

Symptoms:
1. Content fatigue — the constant stream of beauty information causes overload [#301].
2. Financial burden — spending on procedures and products becomes onerous [#301].
3. Trend fatigue — new trends feel endless and exhausting [#301].
4. Social isolation — getting ready for events becomes so complicated that it is easier to decline [#301].
5. Appearance anxiety — panic before going to work or a meeting if your look is not perfect [#301].
6. Hyper-awareness — constant attention to your own appearance causes discomfort [#301].

How to cope:
1. Revisit priorities — define what truly matters to you and what is imposed by society [#301].
2. Simplify the routine — drop superfluous procedures and products [#301].
3. Focus on health — instead of chasing an ideal, take care of your physical and mental state [#301].

#### The instrumental trap of beauty
Context: the author's opinion piece titled «Как задолбала красота» (How sick I am of beauty), written after watching Ryan Murphy's series The Beauty (Красота). She recommends it to those who liked The Substance (more shocking scenes); not Murphy's best work, but it moved her because she feels so sorry for us humans, simply obsessed with beauty, and she has no doubt that if such an injection existed today things would play out exactly as in the series [#576].

1. Observation. Around us are plenty of people injecting anything to become beautiful: Ozempic and other GLP-1 drugs without indications; those with less means chew metformin; fillers until unrecognisable; buccal fat removal; facelifts at 25; rhinoplasty from Instagram photos; fasting, detoxes, cutting out whole food groups — and as a consequence chronic illness, hormonal failures, eating disorders. The saddest part: people invest far less in having something real in life [#576].
2. The trap. She calls it a classic case of what philosophers call an «инструментальной ловушкой» (instrumental trap): beauty was originally a tool — to attract, to be liked, to feel good — but at some point the tool became the goal, and everything else (health, relationships, meaning, one's own life) turned into a means to reach it [#576].
3. What it sounds like — the conditional life-on-hold scripts:
   - «Не похудею — не начну жить, как хочу.» (If I don't lose weight, I won't start living the way I want.) [#576]
   - «Не исправлю нос — не пойду на свидание.» (If I don't fix my nose, I won't go on a date.) [#576]
   - «Не уберу морщины — не начну искать новую работу.» (If I don't remove my wrinkles, I won't start looking for a new job.) [#576]
4. The mechanism (Naomi Wolf, The Beauty Myth). Using women as the example, Wolf explains that striving for beauty is not a natural need but an instrument of control: «Человек, который ненавидит свое тело, покупает больше, требует меньше и думает о себе в последнюю очередь.» (A person who hates their body buys more, demands less and thinks of themselves last.) Very convenient for everyone except that person. Wolf wrote about women; the author notes the series reminds us the beauty myth has long stopped being only a women's story [#576].
5. Object vs subject (Simone de Beauvoir). Woman historically existed as an object, not a subject — not the one who looks but the one who is looked at. As with Wolf, the author thinks men too increasingly find themselves in the position of the evaluated rather than the evaluator. As long as all of us assess ourselves through the eyes of an imaginary observer, the mirror matters more than our own sensations and the external more than the internal; we remain objects even when nobody is looking. She very much wants to leave this wheel of Samsara [#576].
6. The way out (the end of Wolf's book). The author believes there is one way to do it: «Нельзя победить, карабкаясь на вершину кастовой системы. Но можно отказаться играть по этим правилам» (You cannot win by climbing to the top of the caste system. But you can refuse to play by these rules). She wants to put all the energy she once spent chasing beauty into health, self-development, relationships and professional growth — what is worth waking up for. Nobody on their deathbed, she suspects, regrets not watching the oval of their face enough [#576].

Her position, explicitly marked for copying: she is not against beauty or conventionally beautiful people, but raising beauty to the main goal is at the very least strange and illogical. The Dove study she cites found only 2% of women consider themselves beautiful; she doubts this improved in 20 years (except for the worse) — so we chase a standard impossible to meet. In the bathhouse, the gym and the street, 99% of people around her are not supermodels, and she finds them very likable for that [#576].

Her personal evidence: she has met many incredibly beautiful and incredibly unhappy women; none of her own 20-kg weight losses made her happy, but they gave her 20 years of eating disorder and hair loss, and she considers she got off lightly. For the past few years she invests in the inner: emotional intelligence, philosophical reasonableness, wisdom, self-understanding. She is for caring for the body — we have only one — but without fanaticism and without chasing perfect looks; she simply got bored: the race brings neither joy nor meaning and takes a lot of time and energy [#576].

#### Three skincare revelations at 39
Context: lately the author keeps getting compliments on her fresh face and porcelain skin; an aunt at a document-photo studio spent five minutes asking for her products, and she refused to share them (reason below). She cites Murakami's What I Talk About When I Talk About Running (which she calls a wonderful book): people with ailments are in some way lucky — they learn to do the right things and accept themselves earlier; it works the other way too. Her skin had always been perfect — no acne, no oily shine, no tightness — so for the first 38 years she thought nothing needed to be applied, that it was all marketing [#380].

The problem: after moving to the US — stress, age, air or water (probably all together) — her skin simply dried out; looking in the mirror she thought of early perimenopause, which is philosophically okay, but it became clear the skin needed care. As a decent marketing victim in remission she bought everything: cleansers, serums, eye and face creams, masks, patches, gadgets; she heard buzzwords — retinol, exfoliant, vitamin C, niacinamide — and thought she needed them; she assumed pricier is better («ну не может же херня стоить $120?» — surely junk can't cost $120?); she consulted ChatGPT — and the money might as well have been thrown to the wind [#380].

The fix: she stopped suffering and booked an online cosmetologist consultation for choosing her care — a skin-type test, many photos and videos, a description of her problems, and her entire existing cosmetic arsenal laid out. After 2–3 months (4 months and a second consultation by the time of writing) she saw results: the main pain, chronic dryness, was gone; tone evened out; dark circles almost disappeared (especially on the upper eyelid — the panda look that annoyed her). The routine is still ramping up [#380].

Before/after cards attached to the post: autumn — «крем не ложится, кожа очень сухая, яркие круги вокруг глаз, морщины» (cream doesn't absorb, skin very dry, bright circles around the eyes, wrinkles); now, without makeup in the morning — skin moisturised and as if whitened, circles minimal; now with makeup — porcelain on the face; the cards are titled КАК Я НАУЧИЛАСЬ ПОЛЬЗОВАТЬСЯ КОСМЕТИКОЙ В 39 (how I learned to use cosmetics at 39) [#381, img #382, img #383, img #384, img #385].

The three revelations (perhaps banal to others, she says, but she learned them at 39):
1. She was washing her face wrong. With wrong cleansing, whatever you apply afterwards will not work. Now: in the evening a two-step cleansing — first remove the SPF, then cleanse gently. Once a week a powder exfoliant to gently remove the excess [#380].
2. She has reactive hyperaemia. The vessels lie close to the surface and the face reddens quickly, which means minimum mechanical impact. Her beloved hard massagers were out. On the cosmetologist's advice she replaced TheraFace with NuFACE microcurrents (a delight, she says, with a promise to tell more separately); she kept the infrared mask — it's okay [#380].
3. Don't fall for brands and buzzwords — look at the composition and concentrations. Her vitamin C and retinol products turned out to contain the actives in negligible percentages, so they did nothing. «Активы работают в рабочих концентрациях, а не у известного бренда.» (Actives work at working concentrations, not because of a famous brand.) Most of her new products cost up to $30 a jar (some $7); she remembers asking with slight snobbery whether these were really okay to put on — they are, and they work [#380].

She also finally understood what toner is for, where vitamin C fits, whether masks and patches are needed, how acids differ and at what dosages they work, and what a mineral sunscreen is (and why she specifically needs one) — the post does not spell out these answers. She could have read it online but had neither the time nor the desire: «Заплатила и сэкономила деньги и ресурс.» (I paid and saved money and resources.) [#380].

Main lesson: the result is written all over her face. She still has many jars, but now it is a system, not random: «Сработала не магическая банка, а система» (It wasn't a magic jar that worked but a system) — your skin type, correct cleansing, the actives you need in the needed concentration, regularity. Your set will be different — which is exactly why she will not photograph her jars: «Не хочу плодить жертв маркетинга.» (I don't want to breed marketing victims.) Choosing cosmetics is not the only step to a fresh face (the other steps became *7 not-secrets*). P.S.: for removing wrinkles, Botox is still the fastest way [#380].

#### 7 not-secrets of a fresh face at 39
Purpose: what the author did and does to look fresher, written in two parts — the base (what affects us every day and is backed by research) and the add-ons (care, procedures and supplements). Premise: «Лицо — зеркало жизни» (the face is a mirror of life), so whatever masks and procedures we use, the final condition of the skin depends on how we live. In Part 2 she adds that without this base a lively, fresh face seems impossible if you are over 35, without some ideal genes, a simple mortal like her. She also remarks that Instagram has more telling videos showing the traces of an unhealthy lifestyle [#444, #461].

Part 1 — the base

1. Sleep. «Плохой сон старит — и визуально, и буквально.» (Bad sleep ages you — visually and literally.) She sees very literally how a lack of sleep or poor-quality sleep hits the face. She recalls the AI-generated images showing a person ageing after a sleepless night and says this is not a scare story: research she links shows sleep deprivation worsens skin-barrier recovery, lowers elasticity and increases inflammation. After poor sleep the skin looks dull, less firm, holds moisture worse and heals micro-damage worse. Since sleep is also the main source of energy and a regulator of hormones, without it there will be no productivity, no radiance and no inspiration. Her practice: she does not deny herself sleep — sleeps as much as she needs (often 8+ hours), without an alarm, going to bed and getting up at roughly the same time [#444].
2. Food. «То, что ты ешь на 100% влияет на то, как ты выглядишь.» (What you eat affects how you look 100%.) The causal links she gives, each with a linked study: protein shortage reduces collagen and elastin synthesis and makes skin flabby; a lack of iron, zinc and vitamin B12 leads to pallor and flaking; a deficit of fatty acids (omega-3) leads to dryness and inflammation; too little water and the skin loses turgor while toxins are removed worse. Snacking on sweets and snacks on an empty stomach is a guarantee of morning puffiness, inflammation and irritation, because sharp sugar spikes increase protein glycation, which damages collagen fibres and speeds up skin ageing. Her practice: she eats balanced and does not indulge in sweets or coffee on an empty stomach — she knows how that ends [#444].
3. Movement — to remove puffiness and keep an even skin tone. Movement drives lymph, improves blood flow and delivers oxygen and nutrients to skin cells. Moderate physical activity also lowers cortisol and improves insulin sensitivity, which means it slows inflammatory processes and supports an even skin tone. Her practice: six mornings a week start with a 30-minute workout; she walks at least an hour a day and runs 1–2 times a week; sometimes it is just stretching and a walk. The main thing is for the body to live rather than sit [#444].
4. Stress. «От стресса чувствуешь себя старее, да и стареешь быстрее» (Stress makes you feel older, and you also age faster). When she says rest she means more than just lying down. Chronic stress destroys collagen and increases inflammation; research she links confirms skin ages faster in periods of stress and subjective age also rises — and if a person feels in control of the situation, this effect is smoothed out. People often tell her she glows; when she answers that she has not changed her skincare, they add that then it comes from within. She knows it is the result of psychotherapy, reflection and meditation, and she notices that in periods of anxiety and stress her skin literally dulls and the face sags. «Сияние — это не хайлайтер, это нервная система в балансе.» (Radiance isn't a highlighter, it's a nervous system in balance.) [#444]

Part 2 — care, procedures, supplements

5. Skincare and sunscreen. A year earlier she would not have thought she would say it, but correct care and moisturising work wonders. After finally building a base with active ingredients — cleansing, toning, serum, cream, SPF — her skin really changed: moisturised, smooth, alive (how she got there: *Three skincare revelations at 39*). Her one strong regret: treating SPF carelessly until 37; she thinks she could have saved a couple of wrinkles, creases and pigment spots (for example, the panda circles around her eyes). Research she cites says 80% of the signs of ageing are linked to UV radiation. Her rule: sun protection is mandatory — even in winter, even in Moscow. She now also always wears a cap visor, to be sure [#461].
6. Microcurrents and Botox. As a person of the puffy type she rescues her mornings with microcurrents or a short lymphatic-drainage massage (see *Microcurrents vs hand massage*). Even with balanced food and movement, puffiness happens more often in people with this tendency than in those without it. Microcurrents are said to help not only de-puff but also improve microcirculation and stimulate ATP production in cells, which theoretically gives a cumulative effect — though research data so far are moderate. Botox: she has injected since about 27, because from 25 she had a very pronounced forehead crease. Neither facial gymnastics (four months in 2023), nor massagers, nor creams work the way it does. She has not tried tapes yet — they are next in line. She now treats both the upper and the lower third of the face, with an excellent effect. The main downside for her is not always finding a specialist she trusts (living out of suitcases, 7 moves in 5 years), so lately she does it once a year, though being in Moscow she will probably go twice this year [#461].
7. Supplements. Since July she takes Biosil and collagen (one brand in the US; in Russia she ordered TRAD to try and invites recommendations). Although the evidence base is considered weak, a collagen article on her favourite Reminder convinced her it is still worth taking — not against wrinkles, but for hydration. It is too early for conclusions; subjectively her skin is denser and her hair and nails grow faster [#461].

She closes Part 2 saying she is now happy with how her face looks but, as usual, remains open to experiments [#461].

#### Microcurrents vs hand massage
Question answered: «нужен ли гаджет за $300, чтобы убрать утренний отек?» (do you need a $300 gadget to remove morning puffiness?) — or is a habit of 5 minutes of massage enough. She uses the gadget to remove swelling in the morning, chasing instant results, as she puts it [#420].

Background: the author swells strongly, sometimes catastrophically. A video (not available in the export) shows her puffiness in Moscow, with her observation that flying west-to-east makes you swell more than flying east-to-west [#159, #420]. Her de-puffing toolkit evolved:
- 2024: two massagers used several mornings a week, even when there is no swelling — a dry-massage brush for the face (softer than a body brush) and an ice massager (a LATME device in the photo), which also helps with headaches. Both stimulate blood circulation and lymph outflow. Although they say such things work only with regular use, she noticed a difference from the start. She jokes that she is a homely lifestyle blogger, not someone with innovative, pretty gadgets. Contrast: dry *body* brushing, done almost daily for three months, showed her no difference at all [#160, img #160].
- 2025: visiting Moscow again a year later she swelled less than the year before, she does not know why, but still more than in ordinary life. A cosmetologist reviewing her everyday care told her: «Екатерина, ну вам вообще любых механических и температурных воздействий на кожу лучше избегать с вашими сосудами» (Ekaterina, with your blood vessels you'd better avoid any mechanical and temperature impact on the skin at all). So she said goodbye to the brush, the ice massager and her beloved TheraFace de-puffing wand (whose effect had been super). Interim solution until buying a microcurrent gadget: cosmetologist Masha Lesnova's short lymphatic-drainage massage (4 minutes), done daily; Lesnova's longer 27-minute massage is also top-notch but for once a week, not every day [#341].

#### Oligio + biorevitalisation + Mesosculpt combination protocol
Purpose: the cosmetologist's plan for the author's request (less downward sliding and swelling, smaller malar bags). The explanation below is cosmetologist Irina Yakunina's, in her own words as the author asked her to give it. It is her professional claim, not independently verified in the source [#669].

1. Oligio — the main procedure. A monopolar radiofrequency device. Ira's claims:
   - Unlike many other RF devices, it works without traumatising tissue. It heats the deep layers of the skin in a controlled way and so triggers the synthesis of new collagen and elastin [#669].
   - It is «Очень редкий случай, когда не больно, но при этом есть эффект» (a very rare case when it doesn't hurt but still has an effect) [#669].
   - The skin becomes denser [#669].
   - The fat tissue is not burned. Instead there is «компактизация жировых пакетов» (compaction of the fat pads), so the face "gathers" and its contour becomes sharper [#669].
   - The thermal and radiofrequency action on its own already densifies and lifts tissue [#669].
2. Biorevitalisation before the procedure — "filling up the car". Ira's analogy: «это как заправить машину перед дальней дорогой» (it is like filling up the car before a long road). Mechanism: moisture-saturated skin conducts heat better and is less likely to become irritated or dry. That allows a higher temperature and gives a better tissue response [#669].

Combined with Oligio, Ira says, there is a "wow effect" after the first course [#669].

#### Photo self-acceptance practice ("giving yourself to the photographer")
Purpose: for anyone who says «Я ужасно получаюсь на фото» (I look terrible in photos). The author calls it «Практика принятия себя, о которой вам никто не говорил» (a self-acceptance practice nobody told you about) [#728].

Why we dislike our photos (the diagnosis):
1. We have never seen our own face. We only know the mirror reflection (flipped and familiar) and photographs. So "how I look" is really put together from the mirror, photos and comments we once heard, adjusted for how we relate to ourselves [#728].
2. The brain prefers the familiar. In a 1977 experiment the author cites, psychologists showed women two pictures of their faces, the normal one and a mirror-flipped one. The participants more often chose the mirror version; their friends more often chose the real one. Her conclusion: the brain simply loves what it is used to, so in a photo we see someone not quite ourselves, and we often record that as "I look terrible in photos" [#728].
3. The main problem: the inner critic's gaze. «часто мы смотрим глазами внутреннего критика. Не с любопытством, а с желанием найти недостатки» (we often look through the eyes of the inner critic: not with curiosity, but wanting to find flaws). We were trained in this gaze for a long time by magazines, advertising and the beauty industry, with endless "fix, tighten, remove". For years we looked at ourselves through the eyes of beauty standards. So it is no surprise that in a photo we first find the double chin, not the lively look [#728].

The practice, step by step:
1. Book a photographer, not a styled shoot. One of her non-obvious self-love practices is «“отдаваться” фотографу» ("giving yourself" to a photographer). That means no staged shoot with makeup, hairstyling and special outfits. It means just being yourself, as you are, and allowing yourself to be seen [#728].
2. Borrow the photographer's gaze. «Хороший фотограф как раз смотрит с любопытством и с интересом. Его задача не найти изъян, а найти жизнь.» (A good photographer looks with curiosity and interest. Their task is not to find a flaw but to find life.) They see what we have long stopped noticing in ourselves, or never noticed [#728].
3. The hard part is not posing. «Самое сложное — позволить себе быть собой и себя увидеть.» (The hardest thing is to allow yourself to be yourself and to see yourself.) Do not hide and do not "hold your face" [#728].
4. Do not dismiss the shots at once. When you look at the pictures afterwards, do not wave them away immediately. Try to look through the photographer's eyes, as if trying on someone else's kinder gaze. Sometimes it turns out to be more accurate than our own [#728].
5. Give it time and look again. Her repeated experience: the photographer sends the selection, and at first she does not like the photos. The critic immediately produces the usual: cheeks, eyelids, wrinkles, folds, «бе-бе-бе» (blah-blah-blah). But she gives herself time and looks again. Then she sees herself: not ideal, but alive, funny, real. Maybe not the whole of herself, but piece by piece: here the look, there the smile, there the hands [#728].
6. Repeat regularly. It is not easy, but «это, как обычно, дело практики и количества повторений» (as usual, it is a matter of practice and the number of repetitions). For several years, as part of her recovery from an eating disorder (РПП), she has tried to arrange a photoshoot with a photographer once a year. Self-portrait studios are also a great way to see yourself from the outside, and she goes to them too [#728].

What acceptance looks like in practice: «не внезапное озарение “о боже, какая же я шикарная и как же я себя люблю”, а много маленьких “а вот это во мне, кстати, очень даже красиво”» (not a sudden revelation "oh God, how gorgeous I am and how I love myself", but many small "and this in me, by the way, is actually quite beautiful") [#728].

Closing call: «И разрешите уже себя увидеть. И разрешите уже себе увидеть себя.» (So let yourself be seen. And let yourself see yourself.) [#728]

Side story with a lesson about "the skill of seeing": she found the photographer, Masha, through a reel in which Masha said she needed to get out of debt. Masha was not asking for money but offering her work, which the author found noble. The author did not expect much and paid a 1,000-ruble deposit thinking "if it's a scam, it's no big deal", and got a great experience and many excellent portraits. #### Weight, the natural range and weight cycling
From the author's T—Zh (Т—Ж) article Fitness vs thinness: why you should get healthier rather than thinner, as she summarises it in the post:

#### Ozempic / GLP-1 critique
The author's first public statement on Ozempic, under the headline «Оземпик — не волшебная таблетка» (Ozempic is not a magic pill), prompted by the WSJ article Here's What Happens When You Stop Taking Ozempic and Wegovy, which opens by describing an Ozempic ad featuring Serena Williams. She says explicitly it is purely her opinion and she does not want to scare anyone [#613].

1. The OxyContin analogy. Every time an ad with celebrities selling yet another revolutionary remedy (for example for weight loss) catches up with her, she recalls OxyContin and does not want to test the remedy on herself. The history as she tells it: in the late 1990s the Sackler family (Purdue Pharma) launched a powerful opioid painkiller, promoted through doctors and celebrities as a safe drug (TV ads, billboards). The ads claimed the addiction risk was less than 1%; according to a study the FDA eventually required, the real figure was 22% — addiction developed in roughly one in five patients within the first year of use. Millions became addicted without understanding what was happening to them; by conservative estimates more than 500,000 Americans died of opioid overdoses from 1999 to 2021 [#613].
2. Not against indications. Marked for copying: she is not against GLP-1 drugs when there are indications; many of her clients consciously arrive at this decision and take them. What pleased her in the article is that it plainly calls these drugs «препаратами для пожизненного применения при хроническом заболевании» (drugs for lifelong use in a chronic disease), not a magic pill to slim down for summer as in the ads with Serena and a thousand other celebrities [#613].
3. The meta-analysis. A fresh BMJ meta-analysis (37 studies) she cites: by its mechanism of action Ozempic works exactly like a restrictive diet, and the consequences of stopping are the same. After stopping, weight returns in about 1.5 years; blood pressure, blood sugar and cholesterol return too. GLP-1 suppresses appetite and regulates metabolism only while it is in the body; as soon as it is stopped, the system goes back to its original settings [#613].
4. Weight-cycling link. Recalling her Fitness vs thinness piece: the greatest health risk is carried not by fat people or thin people but by those riding the weight swings [#613].
5. Best outcomes. The greatest success was in those who combined the drug with lifestyle change: they regained weight more slowly and kept more of the result. But full retention of the effect without the drug was recorded in practically nobody in the studies — which is exactly why the drug is called lifelong [#613].
6. Conclusion. With or without Ozempic it is all as boring as always: the question is not cheat codes and restrictions but choosing yourself, forming a healthy mindset that leads to a healthy lifestyle, and finding the optimal balance between the amount of daily effort you are willing to make and your weight. Motto: «Продолжаем не худеть, а здороветь.» (We keep not slimming down but getting healthier.) [#613].

The WSJ reader comment she highlights on a card (Chris Lee): like many things in life, it takes a mindset shift, active hard work and drag-yourself-out-of-bed commitment and desire to exercise and change how you live — it has not yet been bottled into a drug [#613].

On the OxyContin story she recommends the documentary series The Crime of the Century (in her view a hundred times better than the dramatised version), the Netflix drama Painkiller, and Dopesick, which she plans to watch [#613].
