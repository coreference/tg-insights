## Sugar, sweets and glucose

### What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel taught about sugar: why the calorie count alone does not describe the effect of food on health and energy (Robert Lustig via the Huberman podcast, and an excerpt from Mark Hyman's book "Сахарная ловушка"), how excess glucose overloads the mitochondria and drains energy, why fructose is singled out as especially harmful, and how to fit sweets into life without strict bans, binge-restrict cycles or self-blame. It matters, according to the source, because sugar spikes measurably affect energy through the day, because excess glucose "легко приводит к упадку сил" (easily leads to a loss of strength), and because strict prohibitions raise the risk of breakdowns ("срывов") [#28, #30, #170].

### Core ideas

- Calories are not equal. The number of calories that go into the mouth is not what the body counts, absorbs at the same speed, or uses in the same way; some calories heal, others harm. Katya Strelnikova (after Lustig) and Mark Hyman both reject "a calorie is a calorie" as the basis of health and weight loss [#28, #29].
- Fast sugar spikes glucose and insulin; insulin is the villain of metabolic health. Lustig considers insulin one of the main drivers of worsening metabolic health, obesity and reduced lifespan; Hyman lists the downstream harms of high insulin [#28, #29].
- Glucose is needed, fructose is not. Glucose is vital for energy and hormones; fructose (in soda and shop sweets) is described as addictive, functionless and damaging. Fruit and berries remain recommended because their fibre limits fructose absorption [#28].
- Too much glucose drains energy. Mitochondria make energy from glucose, but overload produces free radicals, oxidative stress and mitochondrial death — the stoker buried in coal [#30].
- Sweets belong in life, without bans. Eat sweets after a real meal with protein and fibre, keep sugar to about 10% (5–10% added sugar), and build toward roughly 85% whole foods by adding foods and non-food joys rather than restricting [#30, #170].
- Cravings have causes beyond willpower. Lack of sleep, under-eating and a bland diet drive sweet overeating; comfort-eating in hard periods signals unmet basic needs and calls for kindness, not self-scolding [#170, #257].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Four reasons calories are not equal (Lustig, via Huberman)
Purpose: to dismantle the slogan «Калория — это просто калория!» ("A calorie is just a calorie!"). Katya Strelnikova listened to the December Huberman episode with Professor Robert Lustig on how sugar and processed food affect health — her summary: they "делают нездоровье из здоровья" (turn health into un-health) — and picked the parts relevant to the channel. She strongly recommends the whole episode to anyone interested in metabolic health and weight loss [#28].

Who uses the slogan and why: according to the author, it is used by marketers of food giants — to downplay the influence of food on health — and by specialists who promise to make you "lose 30 kg in a month" — to sweeten the pill for those who still believe a quality result is possible without changing lifestyle and views on food. In both cases the claim concerns only a crude count of calories at the entry to the mouth, and in that very narrow sense, the author concedes, "они, прости Господи, правы" (they are, God forgive me, right). But for health and energy level, calories are not the same [#28].

1. Calories eaten are not equal to calories credited. The calories that enter the mouth are not always the calories the body "credits". Almonds "officially" contain 160 calories, but because of their fibre only about 130 are absorbed. Protein is an even stronger case: the body spends more energy processing amino acids, so a fatty steak of 1,600 calories may look very high-calorie, but only about 750 are actually "stored" [#28].
2. Calories are absorbed at different speeds. Calories from fast-carbohydrate food (sweets, chips, soda and similar) are absorbed almost instantly, raising blood glucose and, after it, insulin. Dr. Lustig considers insulin one of the main factors of worsening metabolic health, rising obesity and shortened lifespan; the author adds that glucose spikes have been proven to affect energy levels through the day. Whole food — fibre, proteins, fats and complex carbohydrates — behaves differently and, as a rule, does not start this "инсулиновое колесо Сансары" (insulin wheel of Samsara) [#28].
3. Calories can be beneficial or destructive. Fibre forms a gel-like substance in the gut; this is the part of calories that is not absorbed and instead feeds beneficial gut microbiota. This lets the microbiome produce short-chain fatty acids such as acetate and butyrate, which have anti-inflammatory and protective effects against chronic metabolic diseases and conditions such as Alzheimer's disease. Proteins, fats and complex carbohydrates are necessary for the body to function; simple sugars are "пустые калории" (empty calories) that bring no benefit and only create energy imbalance and metabolic disorders [#28].
4. We need glucose, not fructose. Glucose is so important for producing energy and hormones that in extreme cases the body produces it even without carbohydrate intake. Fructose, often found in sodas and shop-bought sweets, is described as addictive and as having no function in the human body. Moreover, the post says, our bodies cannot digest fructose, so it destroys them — causing "дырявый кишечник" (leaky gut), fatty liver and interfering with normal mitochondrial function; mitochondria, as the channel explained earlier, are decisive for metabolic health and energy production [#28].

Fruit caveat (P.S. to the post): fruit also contains fructose, but fruit — and especially berries — can and should still be eaten, because they contain a lot of fibre, which hinders fructose absorption and feeds the microbiome [#28].

#### 750 calories of soda vs 750 calories of broccoli (Mark Hyman)
Katya Strelnikova recalled where she had seen the same argument — Mark Hyman's book «Сахарная ловушка» (*"The Sugar Trap"*; full Russian subtitle: reclaim your health from sweets manufacturers and overcome unhealthy craving for junk food in just 10 days) — recommended reading it and shared an excerpt (book pp. 43–46). It is part of Hyman's "10-day detox diet program" introduction, which argues that you must first get rid of the thoughts and beliefs that doom weight-loss attempts: "Если вы будете продолжать мыслить и действовать по-прежнему, то и результат получите прежний" (If you keep thinking and acting the same way, you will get the same result). Hyman claims the vast majority of modern dietitians and doctors approach weight loss fundamentally wrongly — if their advice worked and were doable, we would all be slim and healthy — and the media add confusion. He then dismantles myths, of which the excerpt covers two [#29].

Myth one: all calories are equal.
- *The sixth-grader test.* Show children 1,000 calories of broccoli and 1,000 calories of sweet soda and ask whether they affect the body the same way; they answer "No!" unanimously, because everyone intuitively understands the nutritional difference. Hyman quotes Mark Twain: "Проблема со здравым смыслом в том, что он есть далеко не у всех" (The problem with common sense is that not everyone has it). He supposes this is why doctors, nutrition specialists, government, food producers and media keep promoting the outdated, scientifically refuted idea, and calls the claim that you must burn more calories than you eat to lose weight fundamentally wrong [#29].
- *Conservation of energy does not apply to a living body.* In a lab ("closed system"), burning broccoli and cola of equal energy value releases the same energy — Hyman does not dispute that. But a living, breathing, digesting organism is not an isolated system: food interacts with the body physiologically and each bite is transformed in its own way [#29].

Step 1 — trace 750 calories of soda. A 1.5-litre bottle of regular Coca-Cola has 750 calories, all from sugar: 186 g, or 46 teaspoons — and many people really drink that much.
- The gut absorbs its glucose and fructose almost instantly because there is no fibre to slow it.
- Glucose spikes blood sugar → insulin jumps → a chain of hormonal reactions. High insulin increases belly fat stores, increases inflammation, raises triglycerides and lowers HDL, raises blood pressure, lowers testosterone in men and contributes to infertility in women.
- Insulin acts on the brain and increases appetite: it blocks leptin, the main appetite-controlling hormone; leptin resistance rises, so the "you are full" signal does not reach the brain, which keeps thinking you need food. The pleasure centre is activated and an internal reinforcement loop starts, making you want ever larger amounts of sugar — "Так формируется и закрепляется ваша зависимость" (This is how your addiction forms and becomes fixed).
- Fructose is worse: it goes straight to the liver and is turned into fat, which increases cellular insulin resistance and leads to chronically high insulin, so all the food you eat turns into dangerous belly fat. The liver becomes fat-infiltrated, inflammation rises; chronic systemic inflammation leads to more weight gain and "diabesity" (Russian диажирение, hyphenated across a line break in the excerpt: a combined term for diabetes plus obesity), and anything that promotes inflammation worsens insulin resistance.
- Fructose also sends no report to the brain about the calories just received and does not lower ghrelin (the hunger hormone), which normally falls after eating natural food.
- Soda lacks vitamins, fibre, minerals and phytochemicals that help process calories — "empty" calories that cause full-scale problems. The body does not perceive soda as food, so you eat far more calories during the day than needed.
- Sugar takes over the taste buds, so any food that is not distinctly sweet seems not tasty enough. Hyman's self-test: give up sugar entirely for a week, then taste a glass of blueberries — sweet; then taste the same blueberries after a big glass of sweet cola — they seem bland and uninteresting [#29].

Step 2 — trace 750 calories of broccoli. These calories are also mostly (not all) carbohydrates, which is why Hyman explains carbohydrate types: carbohydrates are plant compounds of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, formally split into sugars and starch (which the body turns into sugar). The key difference is the effect on blood sugar: high-fibre, low-sugar carbs like broccoli's are absorbed gradually and cause no glucose or insulin spikes, whereas table sugar and bread are fast-absorbed and make glucose shoot up. Broccoli's slow carbs bring more good than harm.
- 750 calories = 21 cups of broccoli, containing 67 g of fibre. Broccoli is 23% protein, 9% fat and 68% carbohydrate, so 510 calories come from carbs; sugar in all 21 cups amounts to a mere one and a half teaspoons, and the remaining carbs have the low glycemic index typical of all non-starchy vegetables because they are digested very slowly [#29].

Step 3 — compare the outcomes.
- You physically could not eat 21 cups at once — it would not fit in the stomach.
- If you did, fibre means the body absorbs only a small part of those calories, and very slowly: no glucose or insulin spikes, no fatty liver, no hormonal mess.
- The stomach stretches (unlike with soda — carbonation bloating does not count), signalling the brain that you are full.
- The pleasure centre is not engaged: no internal reinforcement, no addiction.
- The broccoli optimises metabolism, lowers cholesterol and inflammation and speeds up detoxification: its glucosinolates stimulate the liver's toxin removal; the flavonoid kaempferol is strongly anti-inflammatory; vitamin C and folates protect against cancer and cardiovascular disease; glucosinolates and sulforaphane change gene behaviour to help restore and maintain sex-hormone balance, lowering the risk of breast and other cancers [#29].

Conclusion. The same number of calories in different foods can have radically different physiological effects: "Одни калории вызывают зависимость, другие – лечат" (Some calories cause addiction, others heal); some promote obesity, others speed up metabolism. Food is not only calories but information — with each bite you pass the body a set of encoded instructions that lead either to healing or to disease. Hyman's closing question: a large glass of Coca-Cola or a plate of boiled broccoli? [#29].

#### Stoker-and-coal metaphor of mitochondria overload (Jessie Inchauspé)
Purpose: to explain how sugar "takes away" energy. Marina Sidorich builds on the channel's earlier post on where energy comes from: energy is created by mitochondria, and one of the main ingredients is glucose [#30].

1. Everything becomes glucose. All carbohydrates are broken down to glucose in the small intestine. The most glucose is in ordinary white sugar, but there is also a lot in bread and flour products, white rice, pasta, potatoes, honey, dried fruit, fruit juice, sausages, shop-bought sauce, chips and other processed foods. The modern diet is built on large amounts of glucose, so even without eating many sweets ("но мы-то едим" — *but we do*), we still consume quite a lot of sugar [#30].
2. The stoker. In "Glucose Revolution" Jessie Inchauspé compares a mitochondrion to a locomotive stoker who is brought coal non-stop: «Кочегар не может сжечь уголь быстрее, поэтому вокруг него накапливаются кучи.» (The stoker cannot burn coal any faster, so piles accumulate around him.) Coal piles up to the ceiling, he can barely move or feed the fire, "Поезд останавливается, люди злятся, кочегар увольняется" (The train stops, people get angry, the stoker quits) [#30].
3. What it means in the cell. While raw material is moderate and sufficient, work runs smoothly. With excess glucose, free radicals accumulate in cells, leading to oxidative stress and mitochondrial death [#30].
4. Conclusion. "Глюкоза — жизненно-необходимая молекула, но ее избыток легко приводит к упадку сил" (Glucose is a vital molecule, but its excess easily leads to a loss of strength) [#30].

#### Three rules for sweets that keep energy stable (channel)
Marina Sidorich's "what to do" list [#30]:
1. Do not deny yourself the pleasure of a favourite dessert — but not instead of a meal and not on an empty stomach.
2. Make sure the daily diet has enough vegetables, whole grains and legumes, quality protein sources and healthy fats, and give sugar only 10%.
3. Move, to burn the energy received from food.

#### 85/15 balance by addition, not restriction (Katya Galasheva)
In an interview by Marina Sidorich, Katya Galasheva — nutritionist with a medical degree and coach — answers whether sweets can be part of a healthy lifestyle: "Именно этому я и учу!" (That is exactly what I teach!). The interview opens from the channel's premise that, from personal experience, "чем строже себе что-то запрещаешь, тем выше риск срывов" (the stricter you forbid yourself something, the higher the risk of breakdowns) [#170].
- The proportion. The balance between sweets and the rest of the diet is not 50/50 but roughly 85/15. The more whole foods (about 85%), the easier it is to reduce sweet cravings [#170].
- How to get there: add, don't restrict. Move toward this proportion gently and gradually. Add: protein foods, whole grains, fats, vegetables, fruits, greens and berries — and also comfortable physical activity, new and forgotten hobbies, and emotions outside food [#170].
- "Safe doses". It is considered that added sugar may make up to 5–10% of the diet. But Galasheva relies first on how the person feels inside rather than on a set portion or exact measurement, because any restrictions cause an even greater emotional need for sweets [#170].
- Her main goal, as a specialist with a "нетревожным подходом" (non-anxious approach): "научить осознанно подходить к выбору сладостей и понимать свои эмоции вокруг них" (to teach a mindful approach to choosing sweets and understanding one's emotions around them) [#170].

#### Mindful check before eating a sweet (Katya Galasheva)
1. Ask whether you are actually hungry right now and need a proper main meal instead.
2. If you still want the chocolate bar — ask which emotion it is covering.
3. Ask why you are eating it at this moment.
4. General recommendation: eat the sweet after a main meal that contained protein and fibre; absorption is then slower and you will not be hungry again in 30 minutes [#170].

#### Three hidden causes of overeating sweets (Katya Galasheva)
Causes that, in her words, few people think about [#170]:
1. Lack of sleep. Hormonal balance is disrupted and ghrelin (the hunger hormone) rises, which strengthens sweet cravings. Sweets give energy quickly but for a short time.
2. Under-eating. This can be too little food over the week or in the first half of the day — for example, skipping breakfast or a meagre lunch leads to overeating in the evening.
3. A meagre and tasteless diet. When food brings no pleasure, a person may compensate with sweets.

She adds there are many other causes and the problem should be looked at as a whole; many of her clients work in parallel with her (the nutrition specialist) and a psychologist, which she says makes the work many times more effective [#170].

#### Self-kindness instead of self-blame when comfort-eating (Marina Sidorich)
Context: Marina Sidorich's personal story in the first months after her second daughter's birth — heavy physical wear from chronic sleep deprivation, no usual rhythm, no time for herself; a period when you want to find a comfort zone and stay in it. Her comfort turned out to be food: emotional snacks, fast carbs, everything tasty and sweet, plus increased appetite while breastfeeding. She caught herself thinking «Что ж я за коуч по питанию с очередной печенькой в зубах?» (What kind of nutrition coach am I, with yet another cookie in my teeth?) [#257].
1. Recognise the mechanism. It is hard to put eating in order and stop seeking comfort in food while other basic needs are unmet — "И это нормально" (And that is normal). With chronic sleep deprivation, constant stress and lack of movement, sweets and other comfort food become an easy way to restore joy and energy, if only for a moment [#257].
2. Replace scolding with a question. Instead of self-criticism ask: «Что я могу сделать для себя прямо сейчас, чтобы поддержать своё состояние?» (What can I do for myself right now to support my state?) [#257].
3. Accept what you are not ready to change, care for yourself another way. She is not ready to give up the cookie now, and that too is normal; instead she chose a beautiful yoga mat and downloaded an app for morning practice. For her this is not about perfection but about finding balance; eating will normalise once she starts recovering strength and filling up with energy [#257].
4. Small doable steps. In difficult periods small, manageable steps prove not only easier but much more effective than strict rules. "Главное — быть к себе чуть добрее" (The main thing is to be a little kinder to yourself) [#257].

### Detailed insights

#### Should sweets be eaten at once or spread out? (Katya Galasheva)
She calls it a question of balance. For people used to strict restrictions, it can help to allow a small sweet after every meal; gradually they learn to choose when and how much they really need. Some clients prefer sweets in the evening, but with awareness comes the understanding that it is not always necessary. The key is finding an approach that works for each individual [#170].

#### Myth two: bad heredity cannot be helped (Mark Hyman)
- The myth. It is easy to believe your physiology is a lottery — you inherited an obesity or diabetes gene, your parents and grandparents were overweight, so nothing can be done [#29].
- Genome evidence. The human genome was decoded partly in hope of finding the secret of obesity and diabetes; nothing was found that helps people struggling with weight. There are 32 genes linked to obesity in the general population, but they account for only 9% of obesity cases; even having all 32 would add at most 10 kg of excess weight. Genes change only about 2% in 20,000 years, while obesity rose from 9% to 30% since 1960 and is projected to reach 50% by 2050 if trends continue — so something other than heredity is to blame [#29].
- The real causes (many factors). Over the last 10,000 years the food supply changed radically and sugar consumption rose from 20 teaspoons a year to 21 a day. Toxins (which, Hyman says, are now reliably known to promote obesity) surround us. A diet high in fat and sugar and low in fibre made gut flora toxic, sharply increasing "микроожирения" (micro-obesity) — weight gain linked to inflammation-causing gut bacteria. Lack of sleep and obesity-promoting viruses also contribute. Social pressure matters: we imitate the people we socialise and work with; studies he cites show that having overweight friends predicts weight gain and obesity more reliably than having overweight parents — social ties may matter more than genetic ones. "У эпидемии ожирения сотни причин" (The obesity epidemic has hundreds of causes), and bad heredity comes last on the list [#29].
- What genes do explain. We are programmed to love sweet and fatty food and to store belly fat in response to sugar so as to survive winters with food shortages. Genes play some role but contribute only a little to the global pandemic of obesity and diabetes [#29].
- Examples. China: on a trip 30 years earlier Hyman met only one overweight woman, and she was riding a bicycle; type 2 diabetes was practically unknown. Now China has more diabetics than any other country and one in five Chinese people has type 2 diabetes — an example of the destructive effect of the Western diet. The Pima Indians a hundred years ago had no obesity, diabetes or other modern chronic diseases; now they are the second-highest obesity group (the excerpt breaks off mid-sentence here) [#29].

### Evidence and numbers

- A study Hyman cites, covering diets in 154 countries and the links between calories, sugar and diabetes: adding 150 calories a day barely raised diabetes risk in the population, but if those 150 calories came from sweet soda, diabetes risk rose by 700% [#29].

## Nutrition myths and evidence-based guidelines

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's evidence-based corrections to popular nutrition beliefs: a two-part interview with gastroenterologist Natasha Suvorova debunking six myths about diets, gluten, milk, fruit, supplements and liver "detox"; a point-by-point digest of Norway's updated dietary guidelines (August 2024); and Andrew Huberman's argument that oral health is a core pillar of health, with his practical tooth-care rules. The author frames the myth series as a response to the fact that "в последнее время появилось множество противоречивой информации о питании и здоровье ЖКТ, которая часто не имеет научного обоснования" (lately a lot of contradictory information about nutrition and gut health has appeared, often without scientific grounding) — the goal is "не попадаться на крючок" (not to get hooked) by such claims [#151].

### Core ideas

- Prefer research-confirmed eating patterns over fashionable diets. DASH and Mediterranean diets have proven benefits; keto gives only short-term weight loss and may raise cardiovascular risk (see *Myth 1*) [#151].
- Most "food is harmful" claims apply only to diagnosed subgroups. Gluten is a problem for people with celiac disease or gluten sensitivity, lactose for people with lactase deficiency, fruit for people with a sensitive gut — and even then the response is individual (see *Myths 2–4*) [#151, #154].
- Supplements and detoxes are not harmless or necessary by default. Supplements are not clinically tested and can be contaminated or toxic to the liver; a healthy liver detoxifies itself (see *Myths 5–6*) [#154].
- Current official guidance trends toward "less meat, more plants". Norway's 2024 guidelines cap red meat at 350 g/week and build the diet on plant foods (see *Norway's guidelines*) [#204].
- Oral health is a whole-body issue, and caries is about acidity, not sugar per se. Oral bacteria can affect the nervous and cardiovascular systems; teeth remineralise when oral pH is kept favourable (see *Huberman's 7 oral-health rules*) [#56].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Six nutrition myths debunked (gastroenterologist Natasha Suvorova)
A two-part series (parts 1 and 2) in which the channel interviewed Natasha Suvorova, a gastroenterologist at an evidence-based medicine clinic. Each myth is stated, then corrected by her [#151, #154].

Myth 1: "The keto diet is good for your health" ("Кето-диета полезна для здоровья"). According to Suvorova, the DASH diet and the Mediterranean diet are the ones confirmed by scientific research: they have been shown to reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease, obesity, diabetes and cancer. Other diets such as keto give short-term weight-loss results, but can increase the risk of cardiovascular events because of their high content of harmful fats. In the long run, keto is not more effective than balanced eating or a simple calorie deficit. Her recommendation: always discuss your diet with a dietitian physician or nutritionist to choose the optimal type of eating for you and to avoid a reduction in dietary diversity ("избежать снижения пищевого разнообразия") [#151].

Myth 2: "Gluten is harmful to everyone" ("Глютен вреден всем"). Many people believe gluten harms the body and causes bloating, diarrhoea and abdominal pain. Suvorova says this applies only to people with celiac disease — about 1% of the world population — and celiac disease must be confirmed by blood tests and a biopsy. There is also non-celiac gluten sensitivity, in which a person can have symptoms without true celiac disease. But even a genetic predisposition does not mean 100% gluten intolerance. What to do: discuss all symptoms with a doctor, keep a food diary and track your reactions [#151].

Myth 3: "Milk forms mucus in the gut" ("Молоко образует слизь в кишечнике"). The widespread belief is that milk causes mucus in the intestine and in the ENT organs (ear, nose, throat). Scientific studies since 1964 have not confirmed this hypothesis. Some people do feel thick, viscous saliva after dairy, but this is due to the organoleptic properties of milk (its texture and taste), and has nothing to do with the mucous membranes. Milk is a valuable source of calcium. People with proven lactase deficiency can consume up to 24 g of lactose per day without developing symptoms — though "здесь все индивидуально" (here everything is individual) [#151].

Myth 4: "Eating fruit is harmful" ("Фрукты есть вредно"). The myth holds that fruit is harmful because of its fructose. In fact, Suvorova says, fruits are among the most beneficial sources of fibre, which people often under-eat. If bloating does not bother you, there is no need to limit fruit. If you have a sensitive gut, you can follow the principles of a low-FODMAP diet — excluding or reducing foods high in fermentable oligosaccharides, disaccharides and polyols. Practical swap: instead of apples and grapes, eat blueberries, strawberries and citrus fruits to avoid unpleasant symptoms [#154].

Myth 5: "All supplements are absolutely safe" ("все БАДы абсолютно безопасны"). Unlike medicines, supplements (БАДы) do not go through strict clinical trials, so their safety and efficacy are often unknown. Studies show that the actual content of some supplements differed from the declared composition and could contain admixtures of drugs, hormones and even mouse droppings. Case from Suvorova's own practice: a patient took a supplement to improve memory and developed elevated liver enzymes, which led to serious consequences. Cases of drug-induced hepatitis after supplement use are known. Although some supplements may be safe, it is always better to consult a doctor before using them and to check information on specialised resources such as the LiverTox website (Ливертокс) [#154].

Myth 6: "The liver needs a detox" ("Печени нужен детокс"). The liver itself performs detoxification, plus a great many other useful functions: it stores (deposits) vitamins, metabolises hormones and produces bile. Suvorova calls it "своеобразной фабрикой для организма" (a kind of factory for the body), and when it works normally it needs no additional cleanses. Detoxifying therapy is carried out only in proven liver failure — for example in viral or alcoholic hepatitis; in that case toxins accumulate in the body, which can lead to neurological consequences. The main task remains a healthy lifestyle and avoiding factors that can harm the liver: limit alcohol, follow the principles of rational eating, get vaccinated against viral hepatitis. If you have problems with the liver, gallbladder or pancreas, consult a doctor to determine the exact cause and treatment approach [#154].

#### Norway's updated dietary guidelines (August 2024)
The author describes these as Norway's updated dietary recommendations published in August 2024, "самые свежие на сегодняшний день" (the most recent to date), headlined "меньше мяса, больше растений" (less meat, more plants). The points she highlights, all twelve [#204]:

1. Red meat: the maximum amount of red meat has been lowered to 350 g per week [#204].
2. Plant base: the foundation of the diet should be plant foods — from vegetables to legumes and whole grains [#204].
3. Nuts and seeds: eat 20–30 g of unsalted nuts per day (roughly a small handful) and add various kinds of seeds to the diet [#204].
4. Vegetables, fruit and berries: 500–800 g per day, of which vegetables should make up at least half [#204].
5. Potatoes: part of a healthy diet, but not counted in the vegetable category [#204].
6. Juice: 100 ml of freshly squeezed juice a day is acceptable, but only together with food; for children, juice intake should be limited [#204].
7. Whole grains: include whole-grain foods in at least 2 meals every day [#204].
8. Fish: 2–3 times per week (300–450 g in total), at least once oily fish (for example salmon) and once lean fish (for example cod) [#204].
9. Legumes instead of meat: replace the meat component of dishes — for example in soups and stews — with beans and lentils [#204].
10. Dairy: part of a healthy diet if it is lower-fat. Recommended 3 servings daily, for example yogurt + a glass of milk + 2 slices of cheese. People with lactose intolerance can choose suitable alternatives [#204].
11. Sweets: a healthy, varied diet has room for sweets too, but they are recommended in limited amounts and for occasions [#204].
12. Food culture and shared meals: food is part of culture and tradition; eating together is an important part of healthy habits, especially significant for children [#204].

#### Huberman's 7 oral-health rules
Source and rationale: in a podcast episode on oral health, Dr. Andrew Huberman placed oral health among the main pillars of psycho-emotional and physical state, alongside sleep, nutrition, exercise and stress management. The reason: the mouth hosts a rich microbiome, and the bacteria that cause gum recession can penetrate further into the body, with unpleasant consequences for the nervous and cardiovascular systems [#56].

The mechanism behind the rules: "Сахар особо не виноват" (sugar isn't really to blame). Sugar itself does not cause caries; the bacteria that feed on it and release acid do. An acidic oral environment promotes demineralisation of teeth, while an alkaline one promotes restoration — remineralisation. So the main goal of dental health is to maintain the pH balance at which saliva can restore enamel, reversing the formation of caries [#56].

What "uncle Andrew" (дядя Андрей) advises [#56]:
1. Never skip brushing in the evening — at night we have less saliva, so the teeth are more vulnerable [#56].
2. Floss before bed [#56].
3. Rinse your mouth after eating [#56].
4. Breathe through your nose, especially during sleep [#56].
5. Keep "clean" gaps between meals so that the right pH is maintained in the mouth (this ties to the pH mechanism above) [#56].
6. Do not use mouthwashes that contain alcohol [#56].
7. Visit the dentist twice a year [#56].

The author closes by urging readers to get their teeth checked and to bring their children along [#56].

## Marina's nutrition column

### What this covers

A food column for energy and mood that the channel launched in January 2026, written by Marina Sidorich, a certified nutrition coach and guest author [#521]. It contains three self-contained lessons: calcium (why it matters especially for women and how to reach the daily amount from food), fiber (the real daily target and what actually gets you there), and calorie-tracking apps (what Marina found when she tested them on herself and how to use them without harm). The column's stance is consistently non-restrictive: attention to food culture, diet quality, hunger and satiety signals and energy level is treated as more useful than counting [#651].

### Core ideas

- Calcium is a common, overlooked deficiency, and women are the main risk group. Marina did not have "hit my calcium norm every day" among her goals either, until she consulted a nutritionist herself and received what she calls a very fair remark. Her situation illustrates two risk factors: she had just finished breastfeeding, so for the previous 1.5 years her body had been giving calcium from her diet to the child's growth and development; and at 34 the peak of bone density is already behind her. In the future, with perimenopause and menopause, loss of density only accelerates because estrogen falls, which is why it is specifically women who are in the elevated-risk group for osteoporosis [#521].
- Calcium is not only about bones. It is the most abundant mineral in the body; most of it is in bones and teeth, but it is also needed for blood clotting, muscle contraction and relaxation, heart function and transmission of nerve impulses. Marina translates this into everyday terms: calcium matters for sleep quality, muscle tone and resilience to stress [#521].
- Calcium is easy to get from food. The reference point is 1000 mg per day, and the column gives a food table to add it up (see Frameworks) [#521].
- Fiber has proven long-term and short-term benefits. According to the post it is already proven that fiber intake is associated with lower risk of cardiovascular disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes and bowel cancer. The short-term bonuses are regular stool, stable blood sugar, lower "bad" cholesterol, help with weight control and support of the gut microbiome, and gut bacteria in turn influence energy and mood. The average recommendation for an adult is 30 g of fiber a day [#533].
- "Eat more fiber" is usually misread as "add cucumbers and tomatoes". In reality you would have to eat about 3 kg of cucumbers and tomatoes in a day to reach 30 g: one tomato plus one cucumber gives only about 3 g of fiber [#533]. Other foods are far denser sources (see the cheat sheet below).
- Calorie-tracker "norms" are just calculations, and they disagree with each other. When Marina installed several popular AI calorie apps, every app gave a different daily calorie norm after the same questionnaire; the spread was up to 200-300 kcal a day. The apps use different formulas and nowhere explain which ones or why [#651].
- The apps tended to under-feed. Several services calculated less than 1700 kcal a day for a goal of losing 2 kg in 3 months. Marina knows that for her parameters she needs roughly 1900-2100 kcal a day to feel well, do sport and cope with two children. Her verdict on the app's number: the weight loss is guaranteed, together with the absence of energy and recovery [#651].
- Counting has a psychological effect that expertise does not protect against. This surprised her most: within a few days she noticed guilt after some meals, then the urge to "save" calories now to leave them for later, and at some point it ended with her eating a whole pack of cookies. Her dry comment: so much for controlling nutrition [#651].
- Tracking does not guarantee sustainable weight loss. Attempts to stay inside rigid limits very often end in the familiar swing: restriction, breakdown, guilt, new restriction. Behind that sits a separate question worth asking: why did I decide to lose weight at all [#651].
- Trackers are not bad in themselves. Marina does not consider calorie trackers bad; in some situations they are a useful tool, for example when you are simply curious how much your food roughly "weighs" in calories. She explicitly leaves professional athletes out of the discussion [#651].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Calcium food table (reaching 1000 mg a day)

Purpose: check whether your ordinary daily food adds up to the 1000 mg reference. Marina's invitation is to look at your own diet and count whether you manage to reach 1000 [#521]. Approximate calcium content per portion:

1. Yogurt, 200 g: about 250 mg [#521]
2. Hard cheese, 30 g: about 250 mg [#521]
3. A glass of milk or kefir: about 240 mg [#521]
4. Cottage cheese (tvorog), 150 g: about 150 mg [#521]
5. Sardines with bones, 100 g: about 350 mg [#521]
6. Tahini, 2 teaspoons: about 150 mg [#521]
7. Broccoli, 200 g: about 90 mg [#521]
8. A handful of arugula or spinach: about 50 mg [#521]

Worked example using the table's own numbers: yogurt (250) plus hard cheese (250) plus a glass of kefir (240) plus sardines (350) already exceeds 1000 mg; a day without dairy or fish with bones makes the target hard to reach from greens alone, since broccoli and leafy greens contribute only 50-90 mg per portion [#521].

#### Five steps to really increase fiber toward 30 g a day

Purpose: move from the ineffective "add a cucumber" approach to changes that actually raise fiber intake. The steps are the ones that, in Marina's words, really help to increase fiber in the diet and approach the norm [#533]:

1. Learn to cook and eat lentils, beans and other legumes. The emphasis is on learning: legumes have to become something you know how to prepare and are used to eating [#533].
2. Accept and come to love whole carbohydrates: quinoa, brown rice, whole-grain pasta [#533].
3. Add seeds and nuts to porridges, yogurts and bowls [#533].
4. Eat berries and crunchy fruit [#533].
5. Try the whole variety of vegetables, rather than repeating the same two [#533].

Pacing rule: not everything at once. If you are not used to eating fiber, start little by little; otherwise you can get bloated and immediately fall out of love with fiber [#533]. Suggested self-check: try calculating with ChatGPT how much fiber your current diet contains [#533].

#### Fiber content cheat sheet (image cards)

The post's image cards compare how much fiber single portions give, which makes clear why steps 1-4 matter more than salad vegetables:

- 1 tomato + 1 cucumber: 3 g of fiber [#533]
- 1 slice of whole-grain bread: 4 g of fiber [#534]
- 1 tablespoon of chia seeds: 5 g of fiber [#535]
- 1 pear: 5 g of fiber [#536]

So a single pear or a spoon of chia gives more fiber than a tomato and a cucumber together, and ten "tomato plus cucumber" portions (about 3 kg) would be needed for the daily 30 g [#533].

#### Four rules for using calorie trackers

Marina's own summary after her experiment [#651]:

1. Do not take the app's calorie norm as truth. It is only a calculation, and different apps calculate differently (200-300 kcal apart, often too low) [#651].
2. Remember your goal first. Ask whether you really need to lose weight or whether the problem is in something else [#651].
3. Watch whether counting food makes you more anxious and fixated on eating. Guilt after meals and "saving" calories for later are the warning signs she noticed in herself within days [#651].
4. Look not only at the quantity of food but at its quality and variety, because the calorie number says very little about health [#651].

### Detailed insights

#### Why Marina tested trackers at all

AI-based fitness products are on a hype wave: new AI counters, trackers and "personal" health assistants keep appearing. In ordinary life Marina is not a supporter of calorie counting. But against a background of regular training and normal habits she wanted to try a classic fitness protocol, and decided to briefly be "an ordinary woman who wants to lose a little weight", downloading several popular apps [#651]. The experiment is therefore a first-person test by a nutrition professional, not a study.

#### What research says, as cited in the column

Marina notes that this is not only her experience: studies show that regular use of calorie-counting apps is associated with a higher risk of obsessive control of food, body dissatisfaction and symptoms of eating disorders. She qualifies it: not in all people and not always, but the risk definitely exists [#651].

### Practical playbook

- Add up a typical day's calcium against the table and see whether it reaches 1000 mg; dairy portions and sardines with bones are the dense sources, greens the small ones [#521].
- If you recently breastfed, or are approaching perimenopause, treat calcium as a priority nutrient, not an afterthought [#521].
- For fiber, plan around legumes, whole grains, seeds, nuts, berries and crunchy fruit; count salad vegetables as a small contribution (about 3 g per tomato-plus-cucumber) [#533].
- Raise fiber gradually to avoid bloating [#533].
- If you use a calorie app: treat its norm as an estimate, compare it with what you know you need to feel well and train, and stop or step back if you notice guilt, "saving" calories or fixation [#651].
- Before starting any weight-loss protocol, answer the question "do I actually need to lose weight, or is the problem something else?" [#651].
