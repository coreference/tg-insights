## Digital hygiene and attention

### What this covers

This topic gathers everything the channel teaches about the relationship with the smartphone and the information flow: the concept of an "information diet" and concrete personal rules for phone use; the author's step-by-step method for stopping reading chats in the morning and configuring Telegram so it does not provoke anxiety; a reader's one-week replication of that method; five focus habits with the attention research behind them (from a Jay Shetty podcast); and later experiments — the Brick NFC blocker, Telegram removed from the laptop, unsubscribing, and screen-time numbers. [#45, #220, #227, #245, #328, #370]

Why it matters according to the source: excessive content consumption is linked to worse sleep, anxiety, depression and lower physical activity [#45]; large numbers of unread messages and junk subscriptions can substantially raise stress [#184]; checking chats first thing in the morning produced, in the author's own observation, a sense of rush, anxiety and lost focus from the very start of the day [#220]; and the author frames the work with social media as one of the "fronts" of her year-long project to research and build her energy [#328, #370].

### Core ideas

- Information is "digested" like food — so diet it, don't fast. An information diet is a set of personal rules and habits for balanced consumption, not a total digital detox: take your "main calories" from sources that match your interests and goals, minimize "ultra-processed" content, and manage screen time. See *Information diet — 7 practical recommendations*. [#45]
- The morning is the most vulnerable window. Grabbing the phone right after waking and seeing a pile of unread messages triggers panic, the urge to answer "urgently", and mood drops; removing the phone from the morning reduces anxiety for the whole day. See *6-step method* and the *Mila case*. [#220, #227]
- The real obstacle to change is not wanting to admit "something is wrong with me". The author notes that this reluctance is often the very block to change — for her and for her clients. See *Admitting the problem*. [#220]
- Environment design beats willpower. Phone in another room, phone outside the workspace, Brick cube on the fridge where it is too lazy to walk, Telegram closed by default in a browser, unread folder hidden — each change adds friction or removes the trigger rather than relying on self-control. [#220, #245, #328, #370]
- Multitasking is mostly an illusion and attention spans have collapsed. Only about 2.5% of people can truly multitask; average attention on a task fell from about 2.5 minutes (2004) to 75 seconds (2012) to 47 seconds on screens. Jumping between tasks feels productive but is a cognitive error. See *5 simple habits* and *Evidence and numbers*. [#245]
- The phone affects you even when it is just lying nearby. Hence the recommendation to leave it outside the space where you work, and the author's preference for physically putting it away for deep focus. [#245, #328, #370]
- Technology is here forever, so build a conscious relationship with it early. "The sooner we start building conscious and effective relationships with them, the better we will feel." [#245]

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Information diet — 7 practical recommendations (Marina Sidorich, after Clay Johnson)
Purpose / when to use: to control what you consume for the sake of mental health without fully disconnecting. The underlying idea comes from Clay Johnson, author of the book "The Information Diet" ("Информационная диета"), who proposes a parallel with mindful eating: just as we choose food to be physically healthy, an information diet is a way to control what we consume for psychological health. Marina notes that Russian already has the expression «переварить информацию» ("to digest information"). It differs from a digital detox, where you must disconnect completely: it is about «формировании личных правил и привычек, которые помогут получать информацию сбалансированно» ("forming personal rules and habits that help you receive information in a balanced way") — getting the main "calories" from sources that correlate with your interests and goals, minimizing "ultra-processed" content, and managing time spent in front of the screen. [#45]

The seven recommendations, in the source's order:
1. Do not scroll during working hours. Marina's food analogy: «не закидываться чипсами во время обеда» ("don't snack on chips during lunch") — mindless feed scrolling during work is the equivalent of junk food in the middle of a proper meal. [#45]
2. Read the news at most once a day. A fixed maximum frequency instead of continuous news checking. [#45]
3. Set fixed times for checking email and work messages. Rather than responding to every incoming message as it arrives, batch checking into scheduled slots. [#45]
4. Make a list of sources you like — those that give inspiration and development rather than leaving you drained («а не опустошение»). This list is your "main calories". [#45]
5. Choose relevant podcasts that can be listened to while walking or doing household chores — information consumption attached to activities that do not need your eyes. [#45]
6. Make a list of books and scientific articles you want to read. Rationale: «Важные и развивающие темы требуют лонг-ридов» ("Important and developmental topics require long reads") — short-form feeds cannot carry them. [#45]
7. Leave room for a "cheat meal", but define an appropriate amount of time for it. Junk content is not banned completely; it is allowed within a set time budget, as in a food diet with a planned cheat meal. [#45]

#### Husband's 8 phone rules (personal example, Marina Sidorich)
Context: Marina's husband is described as «ролевая модель современного человека, который много работает и проводит большую часть времени в телефоне» ("a role model of the modern person who works a lot and spends most of the time on the phone"). The post opens by describing the typical overload scenario: you go into Telegram to check incoming messages, a batch of posts from various channels arrives and you read them too, a news tab and email are open on the laptop, your hands scroll the Instagram feed by themselves — and at that very moment you are on a Zoom call listening to colleagues. Marina admits she exaggerates, but says we have all done some of these simultaneously many times and for long periods. The rules below are those her husband defined for himself and has «уже интегрировал» ("already integrated"); she offers them in case something resonates. [#45]

1. Do not pick up the phone after 8 p.m. [#45]
2. Focus on one task at a time. [#45]
3. Do not read the phone while walking or driving. [#45]
4. Read the news 2 times a day. [#45]
5. Scroll Instagram 2 times a day. [#45]
6. Do not scroll Telegram aimlessly. [#45]
7. Check work chats and email 4 times a day. [#45]
8. Do not take the phone to the toilet. [#45]

Note that his news rule (2 times a day) is looser than the general recommendation of at most once a day — the rules are personal and adapted, not a fixed norm. [#45]

#### Admitting the problem and the observation diary (pre-step to the 6-step method)
Purpose: to get from denial to action before changing a digital habit. Katya Strelnikova describes this sequence from her own case. [#220]

1. Notice the symptom. In a buddy-coaching session with Sasha Smolokurova, the author started complaining about life — about «ощущение спешки и тревоги с самого утра» ("a sense of rush and anxiety from the very morning"). [#220]
2. Trace it to the behaviour. Looking closer, it became obvious that the first thing she did, literally while lowering the toilet lid, was grab the phone and start CHECKING — then replying, then getting upset about something. She saw 150 unread messages and fell into panic, because all of it had to be read and answered "URGENTLY". The habit had returned during a vulnerable period: after moving to the US, when waking at 6 a.m. she was "already late everywhere" (because of the time difference). She had previously broken the morning-phone habit before COVID, so this was a relapse. [#220]
3. Notice resistance — and name it. When Sasha pointed it out and started discussing how good it would be to get rid of it, the author was angry, because she very much did not want to admit the fact: «со мной что-то не в порядке» ("something is not right with me"). Her insight: this reluctance to admit «зачастую является тем самым ступором в изменениях — и у меня, и у клиентов» ("is often exactly the block to change — both for me and for clients"). It was easier to close one's eyes and deceive oneself. [#220]
4. Accept the facts. «Нет, это не совпадение. Да, привычка вернулась.» ("No, it is not a coincidence. Yes, the habit has returned.") As soon as "the spell broke", constructive thinking appeared: what to do? [#220]
5. Observe before changing — keep a diary. She decided first to observe and wrote down her physical and emotional reactions. Her diary entries: she physically felt loss of focus and anxiety afterwards ("it seems this has to end"); when she decided to go into social media, her stomach twisted with some kind of excitement and anxiety; she opened Instagram, saw 400 views and 20 likes on a post and everything immediately sank, with the mood "nobody loves me" and irritation; picking up the phone to check social media she felt agitation, "as if something in the throat", and arousal. Conclusion: «Вообще. Не то. Что хочу. Испытывать. С утра.» ("Not at all. What I want. To feel. In the morning.") [#220]

#### 6-step method to stop reading chats in the morning and set up Telegram hygiene (Katya Strelnikova)
Purpose / when to use: when the morning starts with the phone and unread messages cause rush, anxiety and triggers throughout the day. Result reported: by the time of writing she had been not reading chats in the morning for the second week and was triggered by them less during the day. [#220]

1. Put the phone in another room. Instead of the phone, when she feels like "zoning out" on something («потупить»), she again zones out with a Kindle / e-reader. Why: this is exactly how she weaned herself off the first time, in 2018 or 2019 — the urge to "stare at something" is redirected to a device without chats and feeds. [#220]
2. Use a second phone/SIM for a separate Telegram account that contains only what you need in the morning. She remembered she had a second phone and SIM, registered a new Telegram account and moved all her Kundalini (yoga) chats there, so there would be no excuse "I need to go into Telegram" — the classes are there, and she does them first thing in the morning after the toilet. The screenshot shows how this second account looks: it contains only a chat «КУНДАЛИНИ 10-19 Ноября» (Kundalini November 10–19), a chat with a friend, and Saved Messages, with her own caption «просто знайте, что в этом мире есть человек, чья телега выглядит так» ("just know that there is a person in this world whose Telegram looks like this"). Another screenshot shows a friend from a chat asking «Новый аккаунт твой?» ("Is the new account yours?") and her answer «сделала, чтобы только в кундалини-чат по утрам заходить» ("made it so I only go into the Kundalini chat in the mornings"); the friend replies «такая ты у меня осознанная» ("you are so mindful"). [#220, img #220, img #221]
3. Allow yourself not to hurry after breakfast and formulate an explicit rule for the phone. Her rule: «проверяю чаты только после того, как выпью кофе с книжкой» ("I check chats only after I have had coffee with a book") — applied on Mondays, Fridays, Saturdays and Sundays, when she has no early meetings. Bonus: within a week she almost finished the book «Накопительный эффект» ("The Compound Effect"), which had been gathering dust for about three months. [#220]
4. Pin your own chats, or chats where little happens, at the top of the main list. Now when she opens Telegram, she does not see a heap of unread messages in front of her eyes. The screenshot of her main account shows the top of the list occupied by pinned low-activity items: «Отложила» (her "saved for later"-type chat, with a saved Reels article), her own health-coach channel, «велнес планы» (wellness plans), her own channel «На балансе», then work-related chats (Happy Women, Happy Women Chat, a "Робо"/work item, «Бадди-коучинг»). [#220, img #222]
5. Swap the folders so that the "Unread" folder is at the very end of the list. On the computer it no longer catches the eye; on the phone it is not visible at all. The screenshots show her folder tabs on the phone: All, Личное (Personal), Друзья (Friends), Клиенты (Clients), Мои проекты (My projects) — with no Unread tab in view; on the computer the folder column runs Личное, Друзья, Клиенты, Мои проекты, на компе, Wellness, Personal, and only then Unread near the bottom. [#220, img #222, img #223]
6. Remove the archived-chats folder from the main screen. She had never even tried and it turned out to be possible. The screenshot shows the Telegram "Archived Chats" preview with a context menu containing «Mark All as Read» and «Hide from Chat List», the latter circled — i.e. the archive is hidden from the chat list via that menu. [#220, img #224]

Outcome: «Реально чувствую меньше суеты и тревоги.» ("I really feel less fuss and anxiety.") And: «никто не умер от того, что я не была онлайн с самого утра» ("nobody died because I was not online from the very morning"). [#220]

#### 5 simple habits to improve focus and stop getting distracted (from Jay Shetty's podcast, relayed by Katya Strelnikova)
Purpose: to regain focus, especially in relation to smartphones. The author relays research and advice from a Jay Shetty podcast episode that, in her words, inspires her personally to keep setting up her smartphone hygiene; she came to it right after writing an article for Bolshoi Gorod (BG) on why doing one thing at a time is a good habit and after her post on not looking at the phone in the morning. [#245]

1. Pomodoro technique. Work without distractions for 25 minutes, then a 5–10 minute break; after 4 cycles, a longer rest. If 25 minutes is hard, start with shorter intervals and gradually increase. During the 25 minutes you turn off the phone, disable notifications and remove all distracting items from the workspace. Why it works: if you know a break is coming after a set time, it is easier to concentrate on the task — «Социальные сети могут подождать 25 минут, верно?» ("Social media can wait 25 minutes, right?"). [#245]
2. Phone outside the space. If you do not want to be distracted by the phone, leave it outside the space where you plan to be. Why: if it is somewhere nearby, you increase the probability that you will unconsciously be distracted by it, even if it is in your pocket or out of sight. [#245]
3. Tech-free zones. Create boundaries — spaces and times with no room for gadgets. Examples: rules not to use the phone at the dining table and in the bedroom, or giving up gadgets before sleep and after waking up. A further example in the podcast's first person (masculine, i.e. Jay Shetty speaking): he disabled social media apps on his laptop, so that at the computer he is fully focused on work — email, documents, spreadsheets — and simply has no access to social media. [#245]
4. Walks without the phone. Device-free walks improve mood and connection with the surrounding world. Related: the author mentions that some studies say walking with a smartphone negatively affects health and mood, and adds about herself: «Мне точно есть над чем работать.» ("I definitely have something to work on.") [#245]
5. Finishing what you started. A very simple rule: do not start anything new until you have finished what you started. Do not jump between tasks; do one at a time. This increases the sense of accomplishment and lowers stress. Why: when we fuss and jump between tasks it seems we do more, but this is a cognitive error — we do less, and productivity drops because the brain spends a lot of energy on the switching itself. [#245]

#### Brick NFC blocker (Katya Strelnikova's later experiment)
What it is: Brick is a small cube with an NFC tag that blocks whatever you choose on the phone (she chose all social networks). How it works: tap the iPhone to the cube → "Work" mode switches on → the chosen apps are deactivated until you physically go back to the cube and tap again to remove the block. [#328, #370]

How she uses it: the cube hangs on her fridge. Walking over to switch it off is too much effort, so she does not twitch toward the phone unnecessarily and stays off social media. Brick works on two phones at once (she bought it as a gift for her husband, but both use it — "the ideal gift option", she jokes). [#328, #370]

When to use Brick vs. putting the phone away: for deep focus she still prefers physically putting the phone away («старую добрую банку», "the good old jar"), because, as she recalls, research says the phone has an effect even when it is just lying nearby. But when the navigator, calendar or calculator must be at hand, Brick is the ideal option: «Функции остаются, дофаминовые ловушки – нет.» ("The functions remain, the dopamine traps do not.") [#328, #370]

#### Telegram only as a browser link on the laptop
A friend she lent her computer to asked «А где телега?» ("Where's Telegram?") — apps have grown so deeply into phones and computers that their absence surprises people. She had caught herself clicking the Telegram icon automatically and did not like it. Her solution: Telegram on the laptop is now only a link in the browser, closed by default. Result in her words: «Минус один источник невроза и рассеянного внимания.» ("Minus one source of neurosis and scattered attention.") [#328, #370]

### Detailed insights

#### The information overload context
Marina Sidorich claims that over the last ten years humanity has created more information than in all of its previous history, and that this whole flow passes through us from the moment we open our eyes in the morning until we fall asleep, through every available channel of communication. She cites a study (linked to WebMD's article on digital detox) that about 25% of smartphone owners aged 18–44 do not remember the last time their phone was not next to them, and states that excessive content consumption can lead to worse sleep, anxiety and depression, and reduces physical activity. [#45]

#### Unread messages and junk subscriptions
A forwarded teaser from Bolshoi Gorod, shared by the author, states that a large number of unread messages in social networks and junk subscriptions can significantly increase stress levels, and frames the response as controlling the information flow and unsubscribing without regret from neighbours, former classmates, distant relatives, successful bloggers and «сыновей маминых подруг» ("sons of mum's friends" — the proverbial comparison figure). #### The Mila case — a week without the phone in the morning
After the author's post on morning chats, Mila Plyushch (channel Невыгоревшая СМО) promised not to pick up her phone in the morning, and reported back after a week. The author shares it because she is "endlessly inspired" when posts and advice become a call to action. [#228]

Mila's log: Day 1 — forgot. Day 2 — for the first time in a month she wrote her morning pages. Days 3–7 — wrote morning pages diligently every day and felt good. She plans to continue. Her key realization: she had thought it was more important not to sit on the phone at night, but in fact she had not even noticed how anxiously her day started when the first thing she did was check work chats. Without the phone she immediately starts doing something for herself in the morning and postpones the "little panic" by about half an hour: «Без телефона сразу начинаю делать что-то для себя утром и паничку отодвигаю где-то на пол часика.» The lesson: the morning phone habit can be invisible, and removing it frees the morning for self-directed practices (here, morning pages). [#227, #228]

#### Unsubscribing and the remaining "Everest"
By the time of the Brick post the author no longer watches Reels and has unsubscribed from everyone on Telegram. What remains, she says, is «мой Эверест» ("my Everest") — the habit she calls the hardest: constantly going into social media to check who viewed and liked. This links back to her diary entry where 400 views and 20 likes on an Instagram post instantly crashed her mood. [#328, #370, #220]

### Evidence and numbers

- About 25% of smartphone owners aged 18–44 do not remember when their phone was last not near them — a study cited by Marina Sidorich. [#45]
- People grab their phones more than 200 times a day — figure cited by the author in connection with Jay Shetty's podcast. [#245]
- A study found only 2.5% of people can truly multitask; when the rest hear this, they all think they belong to that small group. [#245]
- University of California, Irvine, 2004: researchers studied how quickly IT analysts, programmers and managers switched between email, desk work and phones; on average people concentrated on a task for about two and a half minutes before attention switched — and this was before smartphones, social networks and 24/7 availability. [#245]
- The same researchers' repeat study in 2012: average time on one task fell to 75 seconds. More recently, average attention on computers and smartphones was found to be only 47 seconds. [#245]
- Self-reported: author's morning-chat experiment — second week without morning chats, fewer triggers during the day; almost finished a book in one week. [#220]

## Impermanence, uncertainty and resilience

### What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel taught about living with things outside your control. It covers the author's own case — a refused Spanish residence permit after five years of moves — as a demonstration of the practice of impermanence and the "two arrows"; a six-step method she uses with clients for chronic fatigue and anxiety in wartime; and a guest text by Arsen Nizhnikov on thinking in scenarios rather than trying to predict the future when making big life decisions. According to the source, the point is that unpleasant events will keep happening, but the extra layer of suffering on top of them is optional and can be reduced through practice; and that in a world that keeps changing, the skill of thinking well about decisions matters more than finding "one right decision" [#665, #678, #687].

### Core ideas

- Suffering is a second layer, not the event itself. The author distinguishes the unpleasant event (and the body's natural stress reaction to it) from emotional suffering, which comes from the mismatch between reality and expectations and from resisting change. The first is unavoidable; the second can be removed through practice. Full detail in *Principle of impermanence* and *Concept of the two arrows* [#665, #687].
- Impermanence is practised by living, not on a meditation cushion. Life is "soaked" in impermanence; even if you change nothing, change waits around the corner, so the head has to be prepared in advance [#665].
- Calm is a result of practice, not character. The author's reaction to two nearly identical crises one year apart differed "by 100%"; she attributes this to a year of study, reflection, meditation and self-observation, not to innate calm [#687].
- Under real threat, anxiety and fatigue are normal; work on what you control. The six-step method moves from naming reality, through normalising and self-compassion, to finding the main energy drains, separating controllable from uncontrollable, and taking small actions to restore agency [#667].
- Think in scenarios, not forecasts. Arsen Nizhnikov argues that the "guaranteed development" view of the future is outdated and that each competing view of the future holds only part of the truth; decisions should be tested across several plausible scenarios and should keep options open [#678].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Principle of impermanence (принцип непостоянства)
Purpose. A lens for meeting change without suffering. The author encountered it at the "Laboratory for developing philosophical intelligence" and named it one of the two concepts that "hooked" her most [#687].

Content. Everything changes; nothing stands still for even a second. The author's formulation: "мы страдаем не из-за самих перемен, а из-за того, что сопротивляемся им" (we suffer not because of the changes themselves, but because we resist them). We cling to a stability that "в природе вещей просто не существует" (simply does not exist in the nature of things) [#687].

What practising it means, per the author. The practice is about recognising that everything in life is outside our control and is constantly changing, and about not adding to the troubles that will inevitably happen "еще и слой страданий от несоответствия реальности ожиданиям" (an extra layer of suffering from the mismatch between reality and expectations). Bodies change, circumstances change, people change, plans fall apart. Even when we change nothing and hold on to the habitual and familiar, impermanence still "waits around the corner". If you do not prepare your head for this, then every time, on top of the natural anxiety of approaching uncertainty, there will be "тяжелый довесок страдания" (a heavy add-on of suffering) [#665].

Signature line: "непостоянство не практикуется в позе лотоса" (impermanence is not practised in the lotus position) — it is practised by life itself. The author describes how she had expected her year of impermanence practice to be "a beautiful practice — you meditate, read, reflect, spiritual growth", and instead life delivered the real test [#665].

What it is not. The author stresses that the absence of suffering does not mean she has become "some enlightened being"; she still feels hurt and unpleasant ("мне обидно, неприятно" — I feel hurt, it is unpleasant), but "эмоционального страдания — нет" (there is no emotional suffering) [#665].

#### Concept of the two arrows (концепция двух стрел)
Purpose. To separate unavoidable pain from self-inflicted suffering; the second concept from the Laboratory that most struck the author [#687].

1. The first arrow — the pain itself: the fact, the event, the loss. In the author's case, the refusal of the residence permit and the urgent need to leave Spain [#687].
2. The second arrow — the way we finish ourselves off with that pain: endless analysis, replaying, "а что если" (what if), "почему именно я" (why me), "а вот если бы" (if only) [#687].

The insight it gave the author. Applying it to her earlier crisis, she realised: "моя тревога не из-за эмиграции и не из-за денег. А из-за сопротивления переменам, неумения быть в неизвестности без попытки все контролировать и постоянного проваливания в фантазии" (my anxiety was not because of emigration and not because of money, but because of resisting change, being unable to stay in the unknown without trying to control everything, and constantly falling into fantasies) [#687].

#### Six steps for chronic fatigue and anxiety in wartime
Purpose / context. A text the author wrote as a guest post for another channel (Zhuravleva's channel) and reposted, on what she and her clients talk about and do in sessions when living under real threat (night-time drone attacks, alarm sirens, searching for petrol) while having to go to work and function in the morning. Her own entry point: she happened to be in Dubai on 28 February when war reached it; waking up to the sound of sirens, she understood "я не умру под забором" (I won't die in a ditch), her habitual anxieties seemed to drain of blood and fade away, and it became clear that you simply have to live while you have the chance. She is now far from that reality, but many of her clients are in it [#667].

1. Name things by their names (Называем все своими именами). The first and hardest step is to acknowledge reality as it is. Not "everything will be fine" and not "don't think about it", but honestly: yes, it is hard now, I'm scared, I'm tired. Why: the brain spends a huge amount of energy on *not* thinking. The paradox, per the author, is that accepting reality is "не капитуляция, а освобождение ресурса" (not capitulation but a release of resources). When we name things, anxiety does not grow — it becomes manageable [#667].
2. Normalise anxiety and fatigue (Нормализуем тревогу и усталость). Anxiety under real threat is a sign that the nervous system is working normally; the body reacts to danger as it should. Fatigue in these conditions is also normal: constant readiness for threat drains you no less than physical exertion. The author jokes that if you have neither anxiety nor fatigue now, it is worth asking whether you are by chance a sociopath. Instruction: allow yourself to be tired [#667].
3. Treat yourself with compassion (Относимся к себе с состраданием). Living where drones may come at night and you must be a functioning person at work in the morning is a huge load, and most people carry it silently while also reproaching themselves for "coping badly". Feel sorry for yourself: anyone in your place would be tired and anxious — "Это не слабость. Это человечность." (This is not weakness. This is humanity.) The inner critic is especially destructive in such conditions because it adds an internal threat to the external one. How: speak to yourself as you would to a close friend — what would you say to them, how would you advise them to take care of themselves? [#667]
4. See what drains you most (Смотрим, что истощает больше всего). Answer honestly: what exactly takes the most energy right now — the news? conversations? uncertainty? the feeling of helplessness? Why: once you can see where the drain comes from, it becomes possible to work with it [#667].
5. Sort out what is in your control and what is not (Разбираемся, что в нашем контроле, а что нет). The author calls this one of the most liberating questions she knows. We spend colossal energy resisting what we cannot change and barely notice how much is still in our power. How: see *Control / no-control two-column sheet* below. Result: "Теперь вы можете вернуть контроль" (Now you can take back control) [#667].
6. Take back your agency (Возвращаем себе агентность). The feeling of helplessness is one of the main sources of exhaustion; when we feel we can do nothing, energy leaks away into nothing. So the next step is to start doing what is in your power — not globally, but little by little ("тихой сапой", quietly and steadily), one issue at a time. Examples: cook a proper lunch; go for a walk. Why: this will not solve the situation in the moment, but it returns the sense that you choose, you act, "что вы живете, а не только выживаете" (that you are living, not only surviving) [#667].

#### Control / no-control two-column sheet
Used in step 5 above. Take a sheet of paper and divide it into two columns: "что я контролирую / что я не контролирую" (what I control / what I don't control). The author's examples [#667]:
- News running 24/7 — not in my control; how many hours I spend watching the news — in my control.
- The situation around me — not in my control.
- My body, sleep, food, who I talk to — in my control.

Once done, the controllable column is where you redirect energy (see step 6) [#667].

#### Scenario thinking instead of predicting the future
Author and context. Guest text by Arsen Nizhnikov, invited by the channel author because clients constantly face uncertainty when deciding whether and where to move, what profession to learn, whether to start a business, buy an apartment or have children. The author's framing: in such fundamental questions what matters is not to "find one right decision" but to develop the skill of thinking about it properly [#678].

Diagnosis (Arsen's view). Since 2020 "we regularly wait for 'normal life' to return — only the date in the memes changes". The view of the future people are used to relying on is outdated: the "guaranteed development" scenario — tomorrow roughly like yesterday, only with better technology, a growing economy and a more comfortable and safer life — is, in his words, probably no longer worth waiting for; nor is the belief that studying, working and trying hard is enough to secure a decent future. The competing positions — wait for the old trajectory to return; expect only deterioration and give up on planning; hope that AI, a quantum computer or another technology will solve all problems — each contain only part of the truth, and it is short-sighted to make any one of them the single main one. "Дело в том, что мы не знаем" (The thing is, we don't know) [#678].

Main idea. Drawn from his study of systems thinkers (Nate Hagens, Daniel Schmachtenberger, Donella Meadows): "нам стоит меньше пытаться угадать будущее и больше учиться мыслить сценариями" (we should try less to guess the future and learn more to think in scenarios) [#678].

Definition. A scenario is not a forecast of the "it will be exactly like this" kind. It is one of several coherent and plausible versions of how events may unfold. The method:
1. Consider several different variants (scenarios) of how things may develop.
2. Test your decisions in each of them.
3. Look for actions that keep their meaning across different outcomes [#678].

Worked examples by decision type [#678]:
- Choosing a profession: think about which skills will stay useful under different economic and technological conditions, and how to make your path less dependent on a single market or technology.
- Planning a move: assess not only the current standard of living in the country but also political stability, climate risks, access to medicine, social ties, and how easy it would be to leave in the future if the need arises.
- Starting a business or buying a home: think about what happens if income falls, prices rise, demand changes, or you need to leave quickly. Which obligations leave you freedom, and which make you too dependent on one favourable scenario?

Closing theses (Arsen): finding your place now probably means learning to navigate by a map that keeps changing. Nobody knows what the future will be, but those who have learned to think about it more rationally, with higher quality and with greater calm live more resiliently. The aim is to see what is happening "без самоуспокоения или катастрофизации" (without self-reassurance or catastrophising), to account for different scenarios and to keep the ability to act [#678].

#### Four general questions for decisions under uncertainty
Arsen's general questions to apply to any big decision, alongside scenario thinking [#678]:
1. Which decisions keep more paths open for me?
2. What am I becoming too dependent on?
3. What would be hard to restore if I lost it: health, relationships, reputation, skills, trust?
4. Which actions will be useful even if my main forecast turns out to be wrong?

### Detailed insights

#### The author's case: the residence-permit refusal as an "exam"
In 2021 the author and her husband stated the intention to find the ideal place to live; she jokes that the Universe took this literally and arranged a large-scale study: in five years, 8 moves and three emigrations — Indonesia during covid, 2.5 years in the USA (which she found really hard), and now the failed Spain (where she felt very good and thought everything had worked out and the card was a formality). She has lived in Moscow, Bali, Java, Istanbul, Miami, Raleigh and now Barcelona. Her summary: the good news is they now understand a great deal about the ideal place; the bad news is that the Universe apparently has not finished [#665].

A year before, after the Philosophical Intelligence course, she realised that her weak spot was control and accepting impermanence, so she chose to devote a whole year to practising and studying these themes, asking her teacher to compile a programme of books, articles, videos and retreats. Then came, in sequence: having to return from the US to Russia, wintering in Moscow, being caught in the war in Dubai, nervously collecting documents for a Spanish visa and residence permit, waiting — and finally a refusal, meaning an urgent exit from Spain, a new Schengen visa and a new application, with six to twelve more months of hassle ahead. Her lesson: "Как говорится, бойтесь своих желаний" (As they say, be careful what you wish for) [#665].

Her reaction is the evidence she offers that the practice works: hurt and unpleasantness, but no emotional suffering [#665].

#### The body still reacts — observe it without judgement
Even with the practice working, the body reacts. The first night after the refusal the author barely slept because her hands itched terribly; under stress she has a strong histamine release. She frames this as normal, no longer surprising and no longer a source of suffering: "Смотрю на это без осуждения, просто наблюдаю, как организм переваривает новость" (I look at it without judgement, just observe how the body digests the news). In the follow-up post she again calls the body's reaction "a natural reaction to change — stress", while "страданий больше нет" (there is no more suffering) [#665, #687].

#### Before and after: same crisis, different reaction
The author contrasts two nearly identical events. In late July 2025 her husband was laid off and they had to leave the US. She felt as if the stool had been kicked out from under her: she had only just got used to the States, started making plans and identifying with the place. She was badly shaken — stress "pouring out of her", crying, unable to sleep, crazy dreams, waking at night with a lump in her throat and mush in her head. In July of the following year the same story repeated (residence-permit refusal, fog, uncertainty, urgent departure from Spain), yet her reaction "отличается на 100%" (differs by 100%). She received many compliments on her calm and stability and jokes that she sometimes feels like "a walking advertisement for mindfulness" [#687].

#### What changed: a year of practice
In the earlier stress, "with literally shaking hands", she enrolled in the Laboratory for developing philosophical intelligence, seeking basic stability and wanting to stop falling apart at every turn of fate — while doubting what "philosophical intelligence" had to do with it. The Laboratory examined a wide range of concepts around which participants built their own reflection and practice; this turned out to be necessary for finally making sense of important things that otherwise live in the head on their own, on autopilot. Concepts covered included: the preciousness of human life, awareness of one's mortality, errors in perceiving reality, the difference between pain and suffering, and contact with one's deep essence. The two that hooked her were impermanence and the two arrows (see *Frameworks*) [#687].

Afterwards she asked her teacher Lobsang to put together a development programme (books, articles, videos, retreats), studied it diligently from the autumn, and — "the main thing" — meditated almost every day. Over the year she learned to observe the mind, let go of control, and stop fighting for everything to be "понятно и навсегда" (clear and forever). Life then set her an "exam" [#687].

Her emphasis: "это не я такая от природы… Это не характер и не врожденное спокойствие. Это сила практики" (it is not that I am like this by nature… It is not character and not innate calm. It is the power of practice) — of study, reflection, meditation and self-reflection. A year earlier she was shaking just like anyone. She also calls it work in progress [#687].

#### Why study philosophy, per the author
Studying philosophy is a way to look soberly at your inner settings and understand where to adjust them so as to suffer less — like updating the "drivers" you run on, the ones inherited from parents, culture and childhood experience that are long overdue for review. She recommends the Laboratory to those looking for stability and flourishing [#687].
