## Energy drains, leaks and myths

### What this covers

This topic collects what, according to health coach Katya Strelnikova, steals energy: popular beliefs that "pretend to be helpers" (coffee, sugar, alcohol, energy drinks and bars, weekend sleep catch-up, supplements, lying down to wait for energy, being energetic all day), video calls (Zoom fatigue), a checklist of everyday leaks, and — most strongly in her later posts — emotions, thoughts and expectations. It also covers protective counter-moves: boundaries, the 80/20 day, being unreachable, a test for telling procrastination from low charge, and meditation as training in noticing. The author frames the myths as dangerous precisely because they look like help: «притворяются помощниками, но, на самом деле, нагло воруют силы» (they pretend to be helpers but in fact brazenly steal your strength) [#78, #95].

### Core ideas

- Quick fixes cost more than they give. Coffee overuse, sugar, energy drinks, bars on the run and supplements give a short boost followed by a crash or side effects, and distract from the real causes of low energy; stability and regularity (meals, sleep times) give the biggest energy gain. See *Dangerous energy myths* [#78, #95].
- Movement beats lying down — except in exhaustion. Rest-to-wait and skipping exercise only make sense in severe burnout, exhaustion or depression; otherwise energy has to be "started" like a stalled car [#78, #95].
- Resource is finite per day. Productive work for 8 hours is a myth; you can count on no more than a couple of resource-heavy tasks a day, and calls and creative work both belong to that category [#78, #82].
- Video calls are an especially expensive drain (Zoom fatigue), visible both in the author's creative output and in her sleep [#82, #262].
- Emotions and thoughts often drain more than tiredness. Guilt, anticipation, fear and pessimism exhaust "no less than a hard working day"; much of this suffering is self-created "second arrows" that can be trained with meditation and worked on in therapy [#311, #649].
- Protect energy by subtraction. Boundaries are not restrictions but removing what drains; energy is a currency to be spent on what is worth it; being unreachable is necessary because people physically lack that much energy and attention [#187, #188].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Dangerous energy myths (10 myths, in two parts)
Purpose: a running list of beliefs that seem to help energy but drain it. The author wrote it as a special post for her friend Kris Vazovski's channel about productivity in the creative field, said there turned out to be a lot of misconceptions, and invited readers to add their own finds in comments to build an ultimate list («составим ультимативных список»). Both parts end "To be continued…" — no third part is in this topic [#78, #95].

1. Myth 1: "I must be fountaining with energy all day." Energy during the day depends on biorhythms, and productive work for 8 hours a day is a myth (the author refers to her earlier posts on both). The expectation of constant energy creates false expectations, leading to disappointment and frustration — and frustration is exactly what takes energy away. Her recommendation: admit your imperfection and humanity and plan realistically from the start; then there is enough energy both for important tasks — «не больше двух в день» (no more than two a day) — and for more relaxed and even pleasant activities [#78].
2. Myth 2: "Coffee is the best source of energy." Coffee is a source of energy only when used correctly — like any tool ("unless you are one of those who tries to drive screws with a hammer"). The danger: in search of energy people start pouring coffee into themselves around the clock. This is not only inefficient for energy but leads to excessive arousal, anxiety and sleep problems, which "drain our batteries completely" [#78].
3. Myth 3: "The brain needs constant sugar to work." The author says she has been followed since childhood by the claim that under heavy mental load it is a great idea to eat something sweet. The problem is not only that sugar is so easy to abuse, but that a fast spike of energy is followed by an equally fast fall — of energy, mood and productivity (not to mention long-term effects on health and figure) [#78].
4. Myth 4: "Alcohol helps you fall asleep." The author used to think one sleeps sweetly under alcohol, "like falling into a black hole, away from all problems". Besides the fact that this is escapism instead of solving problems, it is proven (per the author) that alcohol worsens sleep quality: frequent (though unconscious) awakenings, strange dreams, disrupted sleep phases. With poor sleep, stable energy is out of the question [#78].
5. Myth 5: "Exercise takes energy; better to lie down." This can be true, but only in cases of total burnout and extreme exhaustion. If you are "not in that league yet", choosing to lie down instead of physical activity deprives you of «фонтана бодрости» (a fountain of vigor). Mechanism as she gives it: exercise improves blood circulation and oxygen supply, which gives a surge of energy and reduces the feeling of fatigue; it also releases endorphins (she calls herself a "running addict"), which improve mood, reduce stress and provide an energy surge [#78].
6. Myth 6: "Drink an energy drink to perk up." The author assumes her readers stopped drinking them long ago but writes "just in case". The "energy" sources in them are caffeine and sugar: the first is easy to overdo and exhaust yourself with, the second "proven" to take energy away after a short spike. So the drink may give a temporary effect, but you pay afterwards with fatigue — or worse: regular consumption leads to weight gain and mental, metabolic, cardiovascular and even dental problems [#95].
7. Myth 7: "Energy bars are great before/after training." Many bars contain sugar, but the bigger point: energy is stable when we eat in a balanced and regular way. Grabbing bars on the run breaks the eating routine and creates «иллюзию заботы о себе» (an illusion of self-care). Her health-coach caveat: a bar is better than eating nothing — but only as a one-off, and if it is sugar-free. Her rule: «А лучшая еда — та, на которой нет этикеток!» (And the best food is the kind that has no labels!) [#95].
8. Myth 8: "Lack of sleep on weekdays is easily compensated at the weekend." «тело — не кредитный счет» (the body is not a credit account) that you can top up at any time, occasionally paying interest for being late. Sleep cannot be stored in reserve; it needs to be restored daily. Going to bed and getting up at the same time is more important than "going to bed before 23:00" — she calls this the first and most important step to normalizing sleep. Principle: «Стабильность и цикличность дает наибольший прирост энергии» (Stability and cyclicity give the biggest gain in energy), as with nutrition in Myth 7 [#95].
9. Myth 9: "I'll take some vitamins to have more strength." The internet offers an endless assortment of vitamins, pills, herbs and powders that supposedly raise energy, but scientific evidence for most of these "miracle remedies" is scarce or absent. Many people (the author included herself) turn to supplements hoping for a quick fix. If you have a vitamin deficiency, supplements can give a temporary energy boost, but they can also cause side effects — she refers to her own experience of "recently breaking" her sleep with a supplement. Often supplements are «костыль, который уводит от истинных причин низкой энергии» (a crutch that leads away from the true causes of low energy) [#95].
10. Myth 10: "If there's no energy, lie down and wait." Clients often tell her: «у меня не было энергри, поэтому лежала и ждала, когда она появится» [sic: «энергри»] (I had no energy, so I lay and waited for it to appear). As with exercise, this strategy works only if you are in exhaustion or depression — and those people she always recommends going to a psychiatrist, not a health coach. For everyone else she offers a metaphor question: what do you do with a car that has stalled? You do not leave it "just to stand" — you push it, jump-start it, literally force it to drive little by little. Her closing question: «Нет энергии? — А что вы сделали, чтобы она была?» (No energy? — And what did you do so that there would be some?) [#95].

#### Where does energy go? — 9-point self-check
A checklist forwarded from the channel Матриарх на мосту, presented as «Проверьте себя» (Check yourself) and as a memo to save. Each item is a place where strength leaks [#200]:
1. Unmet basic needs — sleep, food, rest are not covered [#200].
2. Eating and drinking things away («Заедание, запивание») — and everything we do so as not to think, to «занять эфир» (fill the airtime) [#200].
3. A child position or an over-adult position — «я ничего не знаю, помогите» (I know nothing, help me) or, conversely, «я знаю, как всем надо жить» (I know how everyone should live) [#200].
4. Unfinished business — postponed tasks, unresolved arguments, hanging tasks, debts [#200].
5. Resentment — towards others and towards oneself [#200].
6. Fear of missing out on gain and the urge to control everything («Синдром упущенной выгоды и желание всё контролировать») [#200].
7. Fears — especially fear for the future; this includes fear of everything new [#200].
8. Lack of focus — switching between tasks [#200].
9. Always, «когда мы предаём себя» (when we betray ourselves) — from insensitivity to the body's signals to global denial of what the soul is drawn to [#200].

#### Three rules of energy from "Slow Living"
Source: the book "Slow Living: The Secrets of Slowing Down and Noticing the Simple Joys Anywhere" by Helena Woods, which the author recently read and whose concept she says she has been mastering for several years "with varying success". She shares three rules "about energy and resource" in a non-literal translation [#187, img #187]. Purpose: protecting energy by subtraction.

Rule 1 — Guard your boundaries, guard your energy. When we hear "boundaries", we picture a dull life deprived of pleasure, but «не стоит путать границы с ограничениями» (don't confuse boundaries with restrictions). Boundaries do not take away joy; they help focus attention and energy on what leads to flourishing: «Меньше внешнего, чтобы создать больше внутреннего» (Less external to create more internal). Saying "no" does not necessarily mean literally refusing someone (though that can be useful too); it means simplifying life by removing things that drain energy. What it can look like [#187]:
- On the commute, look out of the window or around you instead of at your phone [#187].
- Get up ten minutes earlier to enjoy your coffee without distractions [#187].
- Skip a trip to a crowded, noisy place in favor of a walk in a park, an empty museum or a quiet bookshop [#187].
- Organize more "air" in your calendar [#187].
- Spend one day a week without gadgets [#187].
- Accept only social invitations that give energy rather than drain it [#187].
- Unsubscribe from everything uninteresting or that triggers unwanted emotions [#187].

Summary line of the rule: «Энергия — форма валюты. То, за что вы платите жизненной энергией, должно быть стоящим.» (Energy is a form of currency. What you pay for with life energy should be worth it.) [#187]

Rule 2 — The "magical" 80/20 rule. Plan 80% of the day and leave 20% empty — for a feeling of freedom, the possibility of improvisation and spontaneity [#187]. The photographed book page (partly covered by the author's finger) gives the original wording: schedule 80 percent of your day while leaving 20 percent open for "last-minute changes, spontaneity, and in-the-moment decisions"; "room for flow by scheduling less"; slow down your need to say yes, "stop taking on more than you can" realistically do, "quit filling your calendar to the brim", and allow space in your day to do nothing [#188].

Rule 3 — Be unreachable. The modern world dictates being reachable every second, but to keep inner calm you need to be able to switch off: «У нас физически нет столько энергии и внимания.» (We physically don't have that much energy and attention.) What it can look like [#187]:
- Turn off all notifications [#187].
- Work on your anxiety [#187].
- Learn not to reply immediately [#187].
- Sometimes allow messages to stay unanswered for 24 hours [#187].
- Check email only twice a day: at the beginning and at the end [#187].
- Don't reply to work messages on weekends and on vacation [#187].
- Delete apps that distract too much and take up a lot of time [#187].
- Reply to social-media messages once or twice a week [#187].

Closing call: «Отключитесь и освободитесь.» (Switch off and set yourself free.) Get used to being unreachable and create more time and space for what really matters. The author rates the book «Книжка классная, рекомендую» (The book is great, I recommend it) [#187].

The legible parts of the book page under "Be Unreachable" add the book's reasoning (fragments only — the rest is covered by a finger and the page curve): the author of the book says being unreachable "is one of the best ways to simplify your life"; at any time of day someone can find us via email, text message, Instagram, Facebook or a phone call; we are easily accessible "now more than ever before"; with every notification we feel the need to respond right away; many young people feel social anxiety and live "in fear of checking their inbox"; too many things want our attention at once, but humans are not energetically capable of that amount of space for people. The book proposes "a little experiment": "Get comfortable with being unavailable. Train your brain" to be content in not responding right away, because "we've become so programmed over the years" to always be reachable and have trained our minds to always be "on" — "Turn it off, friend." Readable concrete suggestions: at dinner with your partner or friends, leave your phone at home; leave messages unanswered for a full twenty-four hours; start to only check your email twice a day; if going on vacation, tell people and stick to not communicating while on vacation [#188].

#### Weekend test: procrastination vs low charge
From Elena Rezanova's book «Никогда-нибудь», which the author calls a "great tip". Purpose: to tell laziness or procrastination apart from genuinely low energy. Method — «посмотрите на свои выходные» (look at your weekends) [#327]:
1. If at the weekend you "switch on" and spend time actively, you do have charge — the cause of not doing things is not energy [#327].
2. If even activities you used to love don't excite you and you prefer to lie on the sofa, you are most likely «в режиме энергосбережения» (in energy-saving mode) [#327].
The author's verdict is hedged: «Кажется, похоже на правду» (Seems to be true), and she asks readers what they think [#327].

#### Two arrows: anticipatory distress and expectation hangover
The author calls these «Два брата, которые жрут энергию и мешают нам быть счастливыми» (two brothers who devour energy and stop us from being happy). She found the first term in Daniel Goleman's «Измененные черты характера» (Altered Traits) — something she had long worked on herself and with clients without knowing it had a name [#649].

1. Anticipatory distress (антиципаторные страдания) — suffering we feel before something has happened: anxiety about upcoming pain that itself already hurts. «Мысли о том, как будет плохо — и вот уже плохо, хотя ничего еще не произошло» (Thoughts about how bad it will be — and it's already bad, though nothing has happened yet). Evidence the book describes: in a study, when a person expected a painful sensation, their pain matrix activated almost as strongly as from real pain, and after the pain subsided the matrix stayed active for a long time. So the brain suffered before the event, during it and after — sometimes much longer than the event itself lasted. «Чем сильнее тревога до, тем сильнее страдаем после» (The stronger the anxiety before, the more we suffer after) [#649].
2. Expectation hangover (похмелье от ожиданий) — the state after something went differently from what we expected ("that is, almost always"): it didn't happen; it happened but not that; that, but not like that. We are left disappointed, empty, angry. The author tells clients: "Disappointed? That's what you get when you have expectations." She clarifies it does not mean "don't want anything"; rather, suffering is born not from what happened in reality but from the gap between what happened and what we imagined. «В конце-концов реальность никому не обещала подчиняться» (After all, reality never promised to obey anyone) [#649].

Most often the two come together — "a killer cocktail". The author notes that we do all this without being conscious of it: we fully invent our source of suffering, then suffer devotedly, and on top of that get upset that we have no strength at all. She links them to the Buddhist concept of "two arrows" (which she wrote about earlier): the first arrow is what really happened, the second is what we added to it. Most of our suffering is second arrows — «теперь мы даже знаем их имена» (now we even know their names) — anticipatory distress and expectation hangover [#649].

#### What to do: meditation as noticing practice
The author's answer to the two arrows is short: «Что с этим делать? Медитировать.» (What to do about it? Meditate.) What the practice trains, as she lists it [#649]:
1. Training the ability to notice what is happening in your head [#649].
2. Separating facts from thoughts, thoughts from sensations, sensations from feelings, feelings from emotions [#649].
3. Noticing expectations before they have turned into a script/scenario [#649].
4. Stopping and asking: «это сейчас и правда происходит, или это история, которую я себе рассказываю?» (is this really happening now, or is it a story I'm telling myself?) [#649].
Her framing: «Звучит просто. Тренируется годами. Начать можно прямо сейчас.» (Sounds simple. Takes years to train. You can start right now.) She earlier called Goleman's book "an easy way to start meditating" [#649].

### Detailed insights

#### Zoom fatigue: the author's self-analysis
The author analysed the days on which she managed to film reels, make quality stories, write posts and texts — creative work in general — and found «Это были вторники. Дни, когда у меня обычно нет звонков.» (They were Tuesdays. Days when I usually have no calls.) Her explanation: meetings with people, "exchanges of energy", are a resource-heavy process. After COVID, video calls specifically have been actively researched, giving rise to the term "Zoom Fatigue": video meetings take even more energy because of the cognitive load from non-verbal signals, the interface and endless evaluation of yourself on video. This constant state of heightened vigilance and self-awareness leads to fatigue and reduces creativity and productivity (she links a PMC research article). She admits she had never thought about calls this way — for her it was "well, it's just a call" [#82].

Connecting it to circadian rhythms: human resource is finite, and within a day you cannot hope for resource-heavy tasks «больше пары раз» (more than a couple of times) — and both inventing/creating something and calls turn out to belong to this category. Her behavior change: now, when she can't squeeze out anything creative and meaningful, instead of kicking herself she looks at how many calls she had [#82].

Her advice: «Главный совет: Может быть, организуйте себе уже наконец тот самый, модный, день без встреч» (Main advice: maybe finally organize that very fashionable meeting-free day); and as a starting step, turn off your own self-view in video meetings — «сэкономите массу энергии» (you'll save a lot of energy) [#82]. The dictionary card defines Zoom Fatigue as «Пониженная продуктивность и креативность, вызванные видеозвонками» (lowered productivity and creativity caused by video calls), due to increased cognitive load from non-verbal signals, the interface and endless self-evaluation on video [#82].

#### A cautionary case: five hours of Zoom
"From an unsuccessful experience": the author talked on Zoom almost 5 hours in a row (2.5 hours with clients + 2.5 hours with friends — so social calls count too). It "knocked her out very strongly". A walk «без всего» (*"without anything"* — i.e. without devices/inputs) made it somewhat easier, but then there were more errands and shopping trips. As a result, for the first time since the end of January she had trouble falling asleep in the evening — «Очень возбужденное состояние» (a very agitated state). Verdict: «Не рекомендую» (I don't recommend it). Lesson: long call blocks drain energy and can overstimulate enough to disrupt sleep [#262].

#### Emotions and thoughts as leaks
One of the author's first findings from her energy diary: «эмоции и мысли часто сливают ресурс сильнее, чем усталость» (emotions and thoughts often drain resource more than tiredness). She wrote the post right after a total energy crash that came not from work and not from training, but from unmet expectations [#311]. Her examples:
- Guilt — «я должна больше» (I should do more) — «высушивает покруче ударного дня» (dries you out worse than an intense day) [#311].
- The thought "how hard it is — five hours of calls" starts pumping out energy before the calls have even happened [#311].
- Fear of not earning enough takes away prosperity and self-belief «на берегу» (*on the shore*, i.e. before anything has started) [#311].

She checked research for the post (see *Evidence and numbers*) and concludes that stress and anxiety really take more energy than it seems and can exhaust no less than a hard day's work. She calls the idea "rather banal" but says she had not realized how much it affected her; she now carries "this baggage" to therapy "with even more joy". She chose a CBT approach because it is the most scientifically grounded (she links a review) and because professionally she often encounters thoughts and beliefs that block change. She also notes that almost every woman in her channel's interviews talked about therapy [#311].

### Evidence and numbers

- Neuroticism and fatigue: a meta-analysis (Scientific Reports, linked by the author) confirms, in her words, that people with high neuroticism, prone to worry, feel fatigue more often even at minimal physical load [#311].
- Pessimism and energy expenditure: another meta-analysis she read states that pessimistic attitudes lead to greater expenditure of energy, both physical and mental, compared with a positive approach [#311].
- CBT is described by the author as the most scientifically grounded therapy approach, citing a PMC review [#311].
- Video calls: a research article she links (PMC) supports that the constant heightened vigilance and self-awareness of video calls leads to fatigue and lower creativity and productivity [#82].
- Anticipated pain: a study described in Goleman's book found that expecting a painful sensation activates the pain matrix almost as strongly as real pain, and the matrix stays active long after the pain stops [#649].
- Alcohol and sleep: the author states it is "proven" that alcohol worsens sleep quality (awakenings, strange dreams, disrupted phases); she links her own earlier post rather than a study [#78].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The "lie down / skip exercise" advice has an explicit exception: lying down and resting is a valid strategy in total burnout, extreme exhaustion or depression; for depression/exhaustion the author recommends a psychiatrist, not a health coach [#78, #95].
- Supplements may help if there is a real vitamin deficiency, and an energy bar is better than nothing as a one-off (sugar-free) — the myths are not absolute bans [#95].
- The author retracts her own earlier advice "go to bed before 23:00" in favor of consistent bed and wake times [#95].
- The weekend test is presented with a hedge ("seems to be true") and offered for discussion [#327].
- The myth series is unfinished: both parts end "To be continued…", and no further part is in this topic [#78, #95].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the "Be Unreachable" book page in [#188] is partially covered; only legible fragments are used.
- (!) UNCLEAR: the 80/20 rule here is about scheduling (80% planned, 20% open), not the Pareto principle in the usual sense; the source does not connect it to Pareto [#187].
- The nine-point leak checklist is forwarded from another channel (Матриарх на мосту) without the author's commentary [#200].
