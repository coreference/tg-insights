## Work-life balance in practice

### What this covers

How to combine work, family and the rest of life in practice, as taught through the channel's own posts and the posts it forwards: a founder-mother's principles for combining career and three children, calendar-based planning (calendar ninja levels, the ideal week, scheduled rest, protected Mondays and Fridays), the 8+8+8 rule, a coach's critique of "work-life balance", a high-season routine of a coach-strategist, Kate Hall's argument that overwork kills agency, and the misery tax. The sources treat balance not as a fashionable term but as a necessity: without managing state and energy, people "can't carry" their roles, burn out, lose agency and end up paying for disliked work with constant compensatory spending [#35, #191, #212, #258].

### Core ideas

- Balance is a matter of management, not of choosing between career and family. The FounderWoman author says the question of choice "never arose" for her; the real question is who you want to be, and then you build work, children, education, sport and self-care into the schedule (see *Seven principles*) [#35].
- The calendar is the main balancing tool. Planning the whole day — including meals, sport, routines and "doing nothing" — lets you manage time rather than be managed by it; rest that is not in the calendar does not happen (see *Calendar ninja levels*, *Ideal week*, *Protect Mondays and Fridays*) [#50, #201, #394].
- "Work-life balance" can be the wrong frame. Coach Sasha opposes the idea because it implies work is not life; she argues instead for clarifying what "the rest of life" is, checking energy, and dropping guilt and inflated expectations of hobbies [#74]. A forwarded video is titled "Work-life balance is bullshit" [#79].
- Structure the day into work, sleep and personal time. The 8+8+8 rule is presented as a flexible checklist, not a rigid formula; the goal is to find a place for all components [#124].
- Rest protects productivity and agency. Kate Hall: working too hard kills creativity and big-picture thinking, and burnout is "the main killer of agency" [#212].
- Doing disliked work has a hidden price. The misery tax is the stream of treats, coffees, lunches, purchases and luxury holidays needed to keep going in a job you dislike; it questions whether staying "for the money" really pays [#258, #259].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Seven principles for combining family and career (FounderWoman)
Purpose: the author's answer to "the most popular question" — how to combine family and career. She frames it from her experience of running a company of thousands of people and raising three children [#35].

1. There is no choice to make — define who you want to be. The question was never "career or family" but "Кто я? Какой я себя вижу? Кем я хочу быть как личность?" (Who am I? How do I see myself? Who do I want to be as a person?). She knows she wants to be happy, fulfilled, developing, healthy, loving and loved — a leader, woman, mother and wife. Having decided this, she builds work, children, education and sport into her schedule, plus self-care "чтобы на все это хватало сил" (so that there is enough strength for all of it) [#35].
2. Study productivity, time management and above all energy. To fit in what she wants, she started early studying everything about productivity and time management, "и, главное, — энергию" (and, most importantly, energy). She says there are few tools she has not tried, and she knows what works best for her [#35].
3. Don't pity yourself and don't be afraid. Her example: in her first pregnancy, at week 5, she was hospitalised with a threatened miscarriage and put on bed rest for several months. She decided "я не буду лежать и бояться, все будет хорошо" (I will not lie there and be afraid, everything will be fine), went back to work, and drove herself to an ultrasound every day. Lesson as she states it: don't fear that your husband will leave, that you'll lose your job, don't think about how you'll cope — just do what you believe in [#35].
4. Everything is management. "все есть управление, управление собой, графиком, людьми, энергией" (everything is management — of oneself, the schedule, people, energy). Her reasoning: if she can manage a company of thousands, she can manage her priorities and raise three children. The same skills work in private life as in business: responsibility, prioritisation, delegation, and enjoying the process [#35].
5. Know clearly what you will and will not delegate. She does not delegate choice and whatever defines her lifestyle: choosing team members (she interviews every employee of the company), housing, design, hotels, restaurants, the children's schools. She does delegate implementation, paperwork and process matters [#35].
6. The more you want, the more you do; the more you do, the easier it gets. She always has many bold desires; desires become plans and plans become life. "Чем выше задираешь планку, тем больше шансов к ней прийти и «растянуть» себя" (The higher you raise the bar, the greater the chance of reaching it and "stretching" yourself) [#35].
7. With children, quality over quantity of time. The hour before bed, making lunchboxes, weekends and holidays are the moments when the children "enjoy mum". Example: one morning she was rushing to a meeting but found 15 minutes to build a Lego house and promised to "put all the little figures to bed" in the evening [#35].

Addendum: in your own business you have much more freedom to build the schedule — she can now pause the working day between meetings to pick the children up from school, which was impossible before (when employed) [#35].

#### Calendar ninja levels (four stages of calendar use)
Purpose: the author of *состояние потока* (flow state) describes her evolution of calendar use; for her, the calendar is "мощный инструмент для того, чтобы жить насыщенной, но сбалансированной жизнью" (a powerful tool for living a full but balanced life) and for realising desires without sacrificing herself. Readers are invited to rate their own level [#50].

1. Level 1 (one ninja) — no calendar. Everything kept in her head, a notebook, random phone notes. Her brain was a store for a pile of information never recorded systematically [#50].
2. Level 2 — calendar for appointments only. She began entering only scheduled meetings and events she had previously remembered or tracked in notes. This noticeably improved her basic time-management skills: she "delegated" the task of remembering to the calendar, and liked it [#50].
3. Level 3 — the whole day is planned in the calendar, including time without meetings and time alone. Blocks appeared for the morning and evening routine, sport, meals and simply "потупить" (zoning out/doing nothing). Effects: she began managing her time rather than the reverse, understanding where it goes, and seeing her workload visually. Bonus: she shared calendar access with her assistant and team [#50].
4. Level 4 — preparing the ideal week in advance (see *Ideal week method*). She already had March planned at the end of February [#50].

#### Ideal week method
What it is: "заранее спланированный календарь" (a calendar planned in advance) that she fills in according to how she wants her schedule to look — what time she wakes up, how much time goes to work tasks, when she does sport, when she meets friends. How it works: things may change along the way, and she analyses the changes afterwards. Goal: "чтобы разница между идеальной и реальной неделей была минимальна" (that the difference between the ideal and the real week be minimal) — this, for her, is what effective time management is [#50].

#### Six points on combining work and the rest of life (coach Sasha)
Purpose: written after questions in her survey and her own memory of crawling home from work "выжатым лимоном" (like a squeezed lemon). Points 1–2 are about the framing; points 3–6 are for those already in a situation where they have to "gnaw out" time and energy for themselves [#74].

1. Reject the "work-life balance" idea. She is against it because it makes it seem that "работа как будто это не жизнь" (work is somehow not life), leaving three hours a day for life. Her (self-ironic, marked with a clown emoji) conclusion: look straight away for work that lets you live [#74].
2. Define what "the rest of life" is. Ask where, why and for what you want to add your attention outside work [#74].
3. Drop the pressure that out-of-work life and hobbies must exist. The pressing thought "otherwise I'm a damn workaholic, and that's not fancy" makes people spend their scant remaining energy on guilt that they got no further than the office today. Her view: it is actually OK that there are periods when we go all-in on one thing [#74].
4. Check your energy. Combining may be hard simply because there is no energy — "а что вы сделали для того, чтобы она была?" (and what have you done so that you have it?). If you draw energy from non-work activities, treat them as restorative therapy and put them in the schedule like a doctor's appointment — not a whim but a way to get yourself in order: "dinner with friends at 7, full stop". Pitfall from her practice: people in pre-burnout sign up for, say, a cycling club at the weekend and are surprised they didn't rest at all, "even though the bike isn't work" [#74].
5. Assess resources, then restore or plan. Once you have assessed how many resources you have for non-work activities, restore them if they are low. If resources are fine, make a top-three list of non-work activities you want to do in the next one to three months. Why: it saves time on deliberation and guilt, and confirms there are resources for these activities [#74].
6. Lower inflated expectations of hobbies. She sees in practice expectations of hobbies like those of a "zirconium bracelet": if the hobby didn't make me lose weight, didn't rest me, didn't take me to a new level of development, or I didn't want to turn it into work, the hobby is thrown out. Her counter: "а что по удовольствию БЕЗ ПОЛЬЗЫ? уже можно" (what about pleasure WITHOUT usefulness? it's allowed now) [#74].

She adds that there are also many beliefs that prevent people from relaxing — and, even worse, from working normally; in her coaching she catches them in texts and live conversations and brings them to the client. #### 8+8+8 rule with 3F / 3H / 3S
What it is: a technique of "optimal work-life balance" that divides the day into three parts — 8 hours of work, 8 hours of sleep, 8 hours for yourself. Idea: distributing time in a balanced way optimises productivity and improves quality of life. The FounderWoman author stresses it is not a rigid formula but "больше чек-лист, гибкое руководство" (more of a checklist, a flexible guide) adaptable to your needs; the main thing is to find a place for every component [#124].

1. 8 hours of work — devoted to professional tasks or learning, without distractions and procrastination [#124].
2. 8 hours of sleep — to recover fully and be at peak productivity [#124].
3. 8 hours for yourself — the part of the day that should bring joy and enjoyment, subdivided into three categories [#124]:
   - 3F: Family, Friends, Faith — the people and beliefs that support and inspire you; time with family and friends strengthens relationships and social skills [#124].
   - 3H: Health, Hygiene, Hobby — activities that improve physical and mental health [#124].
   - 3S: Soul, Service, Smile — actions that nourish your inner world and give a feeling of happiness [#124].

She closes by asking which part is hardest to plan [#124].

#### High-season resource routine (eight practices)
Context: Olia Bastron (coach and strategist, twice an emigrant, mother of two, married 18 years) says balance for her is a necessity, not a buzzword: she attends to her state because otherwise "я просто не смогу, не вывезу свои роли" (I simply won't be able to carry my roles). Her goal: "быть человеком, который много может, потому что есть на это силы" (to be a person who can do a lot because she has the strength for it). What she does [#191]:

1. Digital detox and boundaries. Once a week a digital detox; after 21:00 she tries "very (very!) hard" to leave the phone charging in the kitchen — she admits it works only with difficulty. Why: it keeps the brain from revving up before sleep and lets the day end calmly [#191].
2. Structure of the working day. She tries not to start work before 9–10 a.m. (she has a 5-hour time difference with Europe) and finishes by 16:00–17:00; no more than 2–3 client meetings a day [#191].
3. Structure of the working week. Wednesday is a special client-free day: she quietly studies, does research, meets pleasant people and handles things she never gets to on ordinary days [#191].
4. Small pleasures. Keeping resources is not only about work: she puts recovery in the calendar in advance — massages, trips — and does not forget small moments of pleasure such as a bubble bath or a morning basket of strawberries [#191].
5. Working with specialists. She regularly works with a psychologist and a health coach; she plans to start with a neuro-integration coach so as not to fall into the "red and purple zones" (when everything becomes "фиолетово" — slang for "I don't care") and to try "sprints" on herself; and she will return to a nutritionist with whom she built nutrition for the whole family a year earlier, feeling it is needed again [#191].
6. Limits are OK. She cannot focus on everything at once, and that is normal: she doesn't always manage to make lunchboxes (then her daughter Vera eats hamburgers from the school shop) or read school chats. She accepts this as part of a conscious choice in favour of what really matters: "важно знать свою цену и не бояться не успевать всё" (it's important to know your worth and not be afraid of not getting everything done) [#191].
7. Quality of life through awareness. She tracks sleep (with an Oura ring), the balance of fibre, iron and protein in her diet, and physical activity, because this helps keep resources. From time to time she rents a hotel for a couple of nights to reset and spend time in silence and in contact with herself [#191].
8. Photography as meditation. She attended a photo club in spring, a self-portrait course in summer and resumed photo walks — observing people, the city, shadows and light — which helps her be present. For moving "from the head into the body" she uses body-practice meetups ("телозамес"), ecstatic dance and the breathewithme app [#191].

Her summary: "баланс — это вдох и выдох, интенсивность и расслабление" (balance is inhale and exhale, intensity and relaxation) [#191].

#### Protect Mondays and Fridays rule
Olya Chess adopts a personal rule voiced by Lera Sergeeva in an interview: protect Mondays and protect Fridays. Lera builds her schedule so that the most active work meetings, calls and decisions fall mid-week, leaving the start and end of the week "с воздухом для себя" (with air for oneself). Olya considers it a good way to balance everything and help yourself a little [#201].

It combines with Olya's own rule: she knows that if she does not plan rest — literally enter "обед", "чтение", "тренировка" (lunch, reading, workout) into the calendar — she will simply work. So she now plans not only those few hours a week but also to transform Mondays and Fridays [#201].

#### Kate Hall's anti-overwork boundaries for agency
Context: Katya highlights from Kate Hall's essay on becoming more agentic (translated into Russian by Reminder) the point Hall considers most important: don't work too hard in order to be more productive [#212].

Definition of agency (агентность): it is the ability to act by one's own choice and make independent decisions; the feeling and belief that our actions affect life and the world around us. Katya adds that research shows developing agency improves quality of life, because with it we tend to feel a greater sense of control and become more successful at setting and achieving goals (no specific study is named) [#212].

Hall's argument and rules (quoted in the post):
1. More hours is not more productivity. It took her almost 40 years to learn this, because instinct said more hours means more productivity — "just multiplication". It isn't: overwork, even if it gives good short-term results, kills creativity and the ability to think big [#212].
2. Burnout is the main killer of agency — use lost agency as an early warning. She learned to recognise the first signs of burnout by a drop in her agency: a "switch" clicks in her head and she realises some ideas or actions should be dropped as not working or not needed, rather than pushed to completion [#212].
3. Set boundaries that once seemed shameful. Almost every day she disconnects from the internet at 18:00; she strictly observes a Sunday sabbath where nothing with even "a taste of effort" is allowed. To some this is trivial; in some communities she belongs to, it is a "mortal sin" [#212].
4. Never take advice on how much to work from someone who has never burned out. Very few people take this as seriously, she says [#212].

#### Misery tax (налог на страдания) and how to count it
Definition: "Налог на страдания или Misery Tax" — how much we pay for working in a job we don't like, perhaps a well-paid one. Mechanism: in that situation we constantly need something to hold on, lift our mood and "treat ourselves". So the budget fills with expensive coffee from the café by the office, endless snacks, restaurant lunches and after-work cocktails, not to mention expensive purchases, spas and lavish holidays "as if for the last time" [#258].

The idea's author, essayist Thomas Bevan (quoted in the post): many things we do for pleasure are really ways to cope and endure, more like "смазку для машины, чем на бальзам для души" (grease for a machine than balm for the soul). These indulgences are harmful less because of their content than because of why we resort to them: we relieve suffering on the surface because we lack the time, inclination or even courage to tackle the problem fundamentally. "Возможно, поэтому заработная плата часто называется "компенсацией"" (Perhaps that's why wages are often called "compensation") [#258].

Katya's position: she is not calling for asceticism or giving up life's joys. But she recalls painfully the "bottomless pit" inside when she was burned out and worked without joy; now she gets so much pleasure from work and lifestyle that she no longer needs so many external pleasures on a constant basis. Her open question: it would be interesting to calculate how much doing disliked work really costs a person, since money is usually the main argument for staying — "Но так ли это выгодно?" (But is it really so profitable?) [#258].

How it is applied: a reader (author of *Эстетика Блаженства* (Aesthetics of Bliss)) calculated hers and found an exact sum. She had not known the theory and called it "налог на усталость" (fatigue tax), but the thought points the same way. Her count covered only monthly spending on coffee, taxis and food; adding holidays, expensive clothes and health makes it "even worse" [#259]. (!) ### Detailed insights

#### Coaching case: from "8th speed or nothing" to a schedule with rest
The author of the food blog *Разогрейте духовку* (Preheat the oven) wanted to get rid of the constant feeling of barely managing everything. Though she had stopped blogging for a year and a half, the freed time went to basics: falling asleep before midnight, no more weekend work, training 5 days a week — "базовый минимум, чтобы не поехать кукухой раньше времени" (the basic minimum so as not to go crazy prematurely). She still had no time for series, books, solitary walks, museums and theatres — things she needs in order to create [#394].

Working with Katya on her schedule revealed [#394]:
1. Two states only: "я в Москве на 8 скорости" (I'm in Moscow at 8th speed) and "я в отпуске не делаю ничего" (I'm on holiday doing nothing) — no everyday middle ground.
2. Core problem — inability to hold focus on what matters. Small tasks constantly arrive that "must be solved immediately" and eat all free time, so important work (a recipe not yet shot, a table setting invented two weeks earlier) waits.
3. Agreed fix — a rest day in everyday life, with a solitary walk without any purpose, looking around in silence, listening to her thoughts; she says her best ideas always come this way.
4. Meals must also go into the schedule. Breakfast is fine, but lunch she remembers around 16:00 on the way between tasks, when any junk at hand will do instead of a balanced plate.

Her goal for the following months: gradually find a schedule in which she works without burnout and has time to rest [#394].

## Women's fatigue and social expectations

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's posts on why women tire more and get depressed more often than men, and on the social expectations behind this: the superwoman ideal, internalized misogyny and everyday sexism, beauty burnout, the "third shift", the cycle, care work and guilt, Seligman's analysis of women's depression, and Kate Manne's "Individuals vs Donors" model applied to the question of war. The author, health coach Katya Strelnikova, says she works mainly with women and within an anti-patriarchal approach; in four years of work she sees "the same thing" over and over and concludes it is "not chance but a system" [#240, #452]. Her stated aim is to help women see that exhaustion and depression are often logical consequences of social patterns — which, being learned, can be changed [#452, #517].

### Core ideas

- Women have it harder, so women should support each other. The author starts from acknowledging inequality and frames supporting women "both with deeds and with money" as "our big mission" [#240].
- Women tire more because of a system, not personal weakness. The superwoman ideal says "you can do everything" and silently adds "and you must"; the result is fatigue from too many roles, constant vigilance, beauty standards, living against the cycle, caring for others and guilt (see *Six reasons women tire more than men*) [#452].
- Beauty burnout is a real state of fatigue, anxiety and overload from pressure to meet beauty standards, with six recognisable symptoms and three coping moves [#301].
- Women get depressed roughly 1.5–2 times as often as men, and simple explanations (hormones, genes, money, complaining more) do not account for it; Seligman points to learned helplessness, rumination and the cult of thinness — all learned and therefore changeable [#517].
- The current system splits people into Individuals and Donors, and women are mostly Donors; the author argues Donor values (care, empathy, support) could ground a world with no room for war, and that change starts with oneself [#562].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Anti-patriarchal approach (acknowledge inequality first)
The author describes her working stance: she "very much loves men" but works mostly with women and within an anti-patriarchal approach whose first step is to acknowledge the current inequality and all the difficulties connected with it that women have to overcome [#240]. She shared it in response to an "angry comment" claiming "это всё придумали" ("it's all made up"), pointing to fresh statistics from a Russian (not Western) study as evidence — she stresses that research about Russia specifically is rare [#240].

Her practical version of support for women, as she lists it [#240]:
1. She chooses to pay women generously.
2. When shopping, she checks who owns the business.
3. She praises women.
4. She gives compliments.
5. She teaches.
6. She supports.

Reasoning: her behaviour "takes nothing away from men or from me", but she always has "a little more attention and time" for women — to replenish the energy women have to spend on "невидимый бесплатный труд и движение против течения" (invisible unpaid labour and moving against the current) [#240].

#### Six reasons women tire more than men
Source: the sixth post in the author's series "I'm 40 and I'm fed up, or why are we all so tired?" [#452]. She opens with a disclaimer: she is against speculation and "competitions in suffering", but after four years of working with women she keeps seeing the same thing and thinks it is a system [#452].

Background — the superwoman ideal: over the last hundred years women went from "housewife" to "superwoman", gaining freedom, education, the right to earn, choose and build a career. Now a woman must be successful, kind, groomed, caring and "preferably not tired". "Можно все. Но никто не сказал, что это все совместить не получится." (Everything is allowed. But nobody said that it's impossible to combine it all.) In the author's account, the superwoman image appeared in the mid-20th century together with active advertising: after the war the world faced a new, independent woman and tried to return her to the kitchen, and marketing quickly found a way to bring women back under control — "продав нам идеал" (by selling us an ideal). Women were told "You can do everything!", with the unwritten addition "and you are obliged to do everything" — be a CEO and mother of three, cook like a restaurant, volunteer at a shelter, be in great shape and grateful for everything, all with ease and a smile. "Но давайте уже признаемся: это утомительно." (But let's admit it already: it's exhausting.) [#452]

The six reasons:
1. Too many roles. A woman is a professional, a partner, a mother, and a manager of logistics, finances, relationships and "the weather in the house". Even when there is a partner, the emotional and organisational management of the family is "almost always on us". Each task costs not only time but also attention and energy. The author adds her own opinion that motherhood is a full-time job [#452].
2. Constant switched-on-ness (включенность). The author states that the female nervous system is built to pick up signals — crying, emotions, intonations, micro-movements. What makes women empathetic also makes them vulnerable to sensory overload. Being with children is not rest but full engagement; even when someone else is looking after the child, "ты все равно «боковым зрением» на стреме" (you are still on alert "with peripheral vision") [#452].
3. Beauty standards and chronic self-improvement. Women now pursue "male" attributes of success and have also set up an internal race: to be mindful, groomed, resilient, beautiful — but not anxious, not angry and preferably radiant. She quotes Naomi Wolf's *The Beauty Myth*: the quality called beauty supposedly exists objectively and universally; women must want to have it, and men must want women who embody it. This is the third shift: women work, they care for others, and then they also "work on themselves" — body, skin, grooming, nutrition, sport, inner balance. "Женщины выгорают от внутренней конкуренции: между собой и собой идеальной." (Women burn out from internal competition: between themselves and their ideal selves.) [#452]
4. Living against the cycle. The author holds that female energy is tied to the menstrual cycle: there are weeks of activity and weeks of decline, productive days and days when you need to ease off. But women live "as if every day were ovulation" — resourced, in shape, in a good mood, "ready for achievements" — and when they aren't, they keep forcing, pressing and overriding their nature. The cycle becomes a nuisance "хотя должен быть компасом" (although it should be a compass). The price: fatigue, hormonal disruptions and guilt about one's own physiology [#452].
5. Constant care — not for oneself. Caring for loved ones is "in our DNA", which the author calls wonderful but energy-consuming; given this predisposition, women easily give themselves away completely. She cites a heroine of her "На балансе" interviews: "Пока в кувшине нет воды, сложно ее разлить" (While there's no water in the jug, it's hard to pour it). Many women pour from an empty jug for years — sometimes not from great love but from the habit of being needed [#452].
6. Guilt. Guilt for anger, irritation, wanting to be alone, not rejoicing in every day, not being "in resource". "Мы не просто устаем, мы еще и виним себя за усталость. И устаем еще больше." (We don't just get tired, we also blame ourselves for being tired. And get even more tired.) — a self-reinforcing loop [#452].

The post ends by asking readers whether they agree and what they would add [#452].

#### Beauty burnout - 6 symptoms
A post forwarded from the channel "Ко-Лаб" (shared by the author) says the success of the film *The Substance* recalled the term beauty burnout, which arrived a few years ago together with social-media trends that are often inapplicable to real life. Definition: a state in which constant pressure to meet beauty standards causes fatigue, anxiety and a sense of overload; women spend time, money and energy maintaining their appearance but experience stress instead of pleasure [#301].

Symptoms [#301]:
1. Content fatigue — the constant flow of beauty information causes overload.
2. Financial burden — spending on procedures and products becomes onerous.
3. Trend fatigue — new trends seem endless and exhausting.
4. Social isolation — getting ready for events becomes so complicated that it is easier to decline.
5. Appearance anxiety — panic before going to work or a meeting if one's look isn't "perfect".
6. Hyper-awareness — constant attention to one's own appearance causes discomfort.

#### Beauty burnout - 3 coping steps
The same post gives three ways to cope [#301]:
1. Revisit priorities — determine what really matters to you and what is imposed by society.
2. Simplify the routine — drop superfluous procedures and products.
3. Focus on health — instead of chasing an ideal, take care of your physical and mental condition.

The post closes by asking readers whether they have felt this and how they coped [#301].

#### Seligman's three factors behind women's higher depression rates
Source: the author read Martin Seligman's *What You Can Change and What You Can't* over the holidays (she notes he is "that same" Seligman of learned optimism and character strengths). The text is over twenty years old, yet some parts sound "frighteningly relevant" to her [#517].

The claim: women suffer from depression more often than men — "not slightly", but stably and systematically. Seligman, using late-20th-century data, writes that depression in women occurs about twice as often as in men. This is not explained by women seeing therapists more often, by them "complaining more", or by differences in income or education; even when men and women with the same living conditions are compared, the gap remains [#517].

Simple explanations Seligman rules out, as summarised by the author [#517]:
- Not hormones — they have an effect, but not enough to explain such a gap.
- Not genes — the genetic contribution to depression exists in both sexes.
- Not work or money — rich women are still more depressed than rich men.

The three factors that remain [#517]:
1. Learned helplessness. From childhood girls are more often taught to be accommodating, careful and dependent. Their efforts are more often ignored, their anger isn't encouraged, their initiative isn't always noticed. Growing up, a woman meets a world where her role is constantly devalued — in the family, at work and in public space. And helplessness, according to Seligman, is a direct path to depression.
2. Rumination. When something goes wrong, women more often start to *think* — to chew it over, analyse, look for the cause in themselves. Men more often *act* or at least distract themselves; sometimes destructively (alcohol, working to the limit, aggression), but they still get out of looping thought.
3. The cult of thinness. In cultures where thinness is the ideal, women have more depression and more eating disorders; conversely, where this ideal is absent, there is no gender skew in depression.

Seligman's key point, per the author: these factors are "not carved in stone or set in concrete". They are not "female nature" but learnable, socially supported patterns — which means they can be changed [#517].

#### Individuals vs Donors (Kate Manne, via the Nagoski sisters)
The author found this concept in the Nagoski sisters' book *Burnout* (read in January); it belongs to philosopher Kate Manne and, she says, explains much about the current system [#562]:
- Individuals (Индивидуумы) — those who have the right to realize their human essence: achieve, conquer, take up space, go toward a goal. Their values: competition, achievement, power, autonomy.
- Donors (Доноры) — those obliged to provide this right to others: give themselves, support, care, serve — and to do so sincerely, wholeheartedly, expecting nothing in return. Their values: care, empathy, support, self-giving.

"Угадайте, люди какого пола Доноры по большей части?" (Guess which sex the Donors mostly are?) [#562]

The author's interpretation: this goes deeper than men vs women — it is a system where some hold power and others submit, some realize themselves and others make that possible. She calls the system "male" not to build a new division but because men built it over millennia, and women were allowed to play in it "only recently, to put it mildly"; to advance in the game you have to play by its rules [#562].

The Donor syndrome. The authors (the Nagoskis) call it a virus that you catch with your first breath. Its most insidious symptom is the conviction that it isn't a disease but the norm — common sense, "the way the world works". The author's suggestion: you can at least start asking questions [#562].

Cultural archetype example. Joseph Campbell, author of *The Hero with a Thousand Faces*, when asked about the heroine's journey, reportedly answered that women don't need to go anywhere — the woman is already where she needs to be, she is the destination the man must reach. The author laughs at it (🤡) but argues it is not just one scholar's sexism but an archetype sewn into culture — fairy tales, language, upbringing. Boys are taught to conquer and achieve; girls to be a convenient "destination". A girl who wants her own journey, goals and voice has one option: join the Individuals' race, adopt their values, play by others' rules and prove she is "no worse" [#562].

Author's own case. She did exactly that for roughly the first ten years of her conscious life — honestly trying to achieve, compete and conform — "Пока не поняла, что выигрываю в игре, в которую не хочу играть" (Until I understood that I was winning a game I don't want to play) [#562].

#### Steps toward a world built on Donor values
Context: under her Instagram post about war (seen by almost 50,000 people) the author ran a poll: if women ruled the world, would interaction between people be more empathetic, ethical and peaceful? She expected men to come and explain why she was wrong, but it was women who answered "no": "Власть портит всех" (Power corrupts everyone). She grants they are "not wrong", but clarifies she does not mean the same game with a player of the other sex at the top ("те же яйца, вид сбоку" — the same thing, viewed from the side). She means a world built on Donor values, which "could become the basis of another world — one in which war might simply have no place" [#562].

Her hedges: nobody knows for sure; but a world where all children — not boys, not girls, but all — are taught compassion as systematically as mathematics is a different world, in which the question probably wouldn't arise. She admits her reasoning is philosophical and perhaps realizable only after humanity's self-destruction or with "very strict face control" for a Mars colony 😅 [#562].

What to stop [#562]:
1. Stop militarizing children — remove the cult of strength as the only language of respect.
2. Stop setting people against each other in the media.
3. Stop provoking states of greed and scarcity.
4. Stop sowing panic.

What to do instead [#562]:
1. Teach empathy.
2. Promote mindfulness — not as a trendy wellness trend but as the skill of noticing yourself, noticing another, and stopping before causing harm.
3. Develop compassion as "the most difficult and rare form of courage".
4. Above all, start with yourself ("начать с самой себя").

The post's image card carries a Jiddu Krishnamurti quote that supports the last step: nothing more is needed — look at yourself, observe yourself, explore yourself, because in the state we are in we will create a monstrous world; you can fly to the Moon, Venus, Mars, but you will always carry yourself there; change yourself first, and to change, look at yourself, go deep into yourself — observe, listen, learn; it is not a teaching or a message, you can do it yourself if you want [#562].

## Relocation and staying stable through moves

### What this covers

Two personal-experience posts from Katya Strelnikova, written about one month after she and her husband moved to Belgrade (her fourth emigration in six years, second in one year, after the US and then a Spanish residence-permit refusal). The first post explains, point by point, why Belgrade suits her better than the US, Spain/Europe or Moscow, and what the downsides are. The second describes her rider (райдер), the portable set of routines, objects and habits that she says lets her change countries without losing her footing, and names the privileges that make it work. The source frames this as personal experience (both posts are tagged #личныйопыт_набалансе, "personal experience"), not universal advice; her summarizing thesis is that you take yourself wherever you go, but place still matters [#734, #737].

### Core ideas

- You take yourself with you, but place still decides. Inner stability travels with you, yet the external environment (familiarity, pace, costs, safety, food, distance to family) strongly shapes how well she feels. See *Six reasons Belgrade feels better* [#734].
- Stability moves with you if your way of life moves with you. Her explanation for coping with frequent emigration is that she moves "not alone, but with my (husband and) way of life": countries change, the supports stay the same. See *The relocation rider* [#737].
- Resilience is built over years; packing takes minutes. The deepest item of the rider is mental practice (daily meditation, impermanence practice, self-compassion), which takes years to build but then goes anywhere [#737].
- Comfort, not challenge, is her growth condition. She says she does not like challenges and "drowns" in them; to flourish she needs comfort, which is why lower financial pressure and an unhurried culture matter so much to her [#734].
- The rider rests on a foundation of luck. No children, control over her own schedule, a financial cushion, a husband who handles immigration paperwork, and moving only to places they have already visited and liked [#737].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Six reasons Belgrade feels better (Katya's place-fit criteria)
Purpose: her account of why, one month after arriving, she feels as if she has "lived here all my life", and what a place gives her that the US, Spain and Moscow did not. She stresses that it is "Для меня. По крайней мере, пока." (For me. At least for now.), and that she does not know whether it is her trained experience of moving or Belgrade itself; the best description she has is "город для жизни" (a city for living). She calls the move a "психологический и физический ретрит" (psychological and physical retreat) sent by the universe, after the decision a year earlier to leave the US and Spain's refusal of a residence permit that summer. General pluses she lists before the six points: comfortable prices, tasty food, lots of great people, Russian or English spoken almost everywhere, and everything close: she walks almost everywhere in the city even if it takes 45–50 minutes, and nature and mountains are an hour or two by car (she has already been hiking with people from a Telegram chat and was going to a spa hotel with friends) [#734].

1. It is familiar ("Здесь знакомо"). She immediately reads a familiar context: cigarette butts on the pavement, panel blocks, the "Slavic gaze", Cyrillic script, birch trees, grandmothers at the market, a language you do not know but still partly understand. Why it matters: "Мозгу не приходится тратить кучу сил на адаптацию и привыкание" (The brain doesn't have to spend a lot of energy on adaptation and getting used to things) [#734].
2. You don't have to try hard ("Здесь не нужно стараться"). To live in the US "надо очень хотеть и очень стараться" (you have to want it very much and try very hard); there is a specific culture of achievement and the American dream, and she and her husband are, apparently ("or temporarily"), "too old for this shit". Europe fit ideally in this sense (a place to go and "live out" life) but it did not work out, and Serbia's vibe is no worse. "Полако" (slowly) is heard at every step; lunch can last a couple of hours, people budget an hour for coffee with a friend, and nobody rushes. She ties this to her long-standing thesis that rest is not a reward for productivity but a physiological necessity: here it is simply a way of life and nobody needs to explain it. Her husband's joke: with their vibe they should definitely be admitted to the EU [#734].
3. She (almost) doesn't worry about money ("Здесь я (почти) не тревожусь о деньгах"). Her cost comparisons for living the way that is comfortable and joyful for her: Spain about 1.5 times more expensive than Belgrade ("life a bit to grow into"), Moscow about twice, the US more than twice (and there she was "going mad with anxiety"). The thought "умру под мостом" (I'll die under a bridge) visited her daily in the US, fairly often in Barcelona, and is a very rare guest in Belgrade. Her reasoning: "А я не люблю челленджи, я в них тону. Мне, чтобы цвести, нужен комфорт." (And I don't like challenges, I drown in them. To flourish, I need comfort.) [#734].
4. She feels safe ("Здесь я чувствую себя в безопасности"). In the US she felt unsafe on many levels (financial included). In a country where anyone may have a gun she feels not calmer but more scared, constantly in an "alert" state; she recalls being told on arrival in San Francisco not to leave valuables in the car. She mentions the well-known robbery problem in Barcelona. In Belgrade friends laugh at her because, as a person with PTSD, she guards her iPhone anxiously; a friend in a public sauna, asked where her phone is, answers she doesn't know, probably on a chair over there. For Katya this is still extremely unusual [#734].
5. The food is incredibly good ("Здесь невероятно вкусные продукты"). She calls the product quality in Serbia mind-blowing; compared with the US and Moscow, Spain was good too, but she does not remember salmon or raspberries tasting like this. Their favourite pastime is going to the market (one "like from childhood, with jeans on a piece of cardboard"), buying something, being pleasantly shocked by the prices, then slowly savouring "another masterpiece" at home. She is happy to live where kajmak is sold and eaten everywhere [#734].
6. She doesn't feel cut off ("Здесь я не чувствую себя отрезанным ломтем"). In the US she permanently felt torn away from everything dear to her: a flight to Moscow took 20 hours "if lucky"; from Europe about 10 hours; from Belgrade there is a direct 3-hour flight. This matters because her mother is in Russia and she plans to visit; knowing her mother is a few hours away rather than a day of travel is "a completely different feeling" [#734].

The downsides she names ("И да, минусы тоже есть", *And yes, there are downsides too*):
- Smoking: the most tangible for her; people smoke a lot and everywhere, including cafés and restaurants.
- Difficulties with Schengen visas (with the aside "but where don't Russians have them now?").
- Not the most popular airport: no direct flight to Bangkok.
- Electronics and deliveries are not simple; Amazon does not work in Serbia.
- Financial instruments are not as luxurious as in Europe or the US ("a problem for future us").
- Belgrade is probably not for those seeking a world-class cultural programme or career heights; she describes herself as more of a quiet type ("тихой сапой").
Her verdict on these: "Но если бы вы знали, как мне на это все похер, вы бы заплакали." (But if you knew how little I care about all this, you'd cry.) [#734].

Conclusion. She had a similar state on Bali, which she still considers the best time of her life; she is afraid to "jinx" it but wonders whether she can come close to it again. Since arriving "мне все хочется" (I want to do everything), which is not always the case for her; she notes that she is a different version of herself in different countries (a topic she plans for a separate post). Her signature line: "куда бы ты ни ехал, ты берешь с собой себя. Но место все же решает." (wherever you go, you take yourself with you. But place still decides.). She hopes for a three-year residence permit, and adds that if it is refused she no longer doubts they will cope "in every sense" [#734].

#### The relocation rider (portable supports for staying stable through moves)
Purpose: the answer to the question of why she copes with frequent emigration. Context: Claude, after analysing her energy diary three weeks after the move to Belgrade, told her "Ты неплохо держишься для второй эмиграции за год" (You're holding up pretty well for a second emigration in a year). She did not get ill or bedridden; she got a bit lost and lost inspiration, but says that happens to her without emigration too. Over six years she has lived in Moscow, Bali, Java, Istanbul, Miami, Raleigh, Barcelona and now Belgrade (not counting moves within places); this year Spain refused them a residence permit in summer and then a Schengen visa, so they packed and moved to Belgrade. Clients and friends tell her this is unusual, especially without "losing your mind". Her secret: "переезжаю я не одна, а со своим (мужем и) образом жизни. Меняются страны, а опоры остаются те же." (*I move not alone but with my (husband and) way of life. Countries change, but the supports stay the same.*) The rider has ten items, following the post's headings [#737]:

1. Working with the mind ("Работа с той самой кукухой"). She calls this the main item. What: she has meditated every day for the past year and has practised since 2018. How: she keeps taking programs by the teacher Lobsang (most recently Cultivating Emotional Balance); she practised impermanence for a year and believes that is why she got through the Spanish residence-permit refusal "without hysterics"; she treats herself with compassion rather than criticism when something goes wrong. Why: "Чемодан я уже могу собрать за сорок минут… А вот устойчивость собирается годами, зато потом едет с тобой куда угодно." (I can already pack a suitcase in forty minutes… But resilience is assembled over years, and then it travels with you anywhere.) [#737].
2. A normal relationship with food ("Нормальные отношения с едой"). What: she trained herself to eat a clear, simple set of foods that can be bought in any country: tomatoes, cucumbers, greens, bread, eggs, salmon. Why: no hunting for special cottage cheese, cheese or sauce across every supermarket of a new city. Caveats: she says this was neither easy nor quick given a history of an eating disorder (рпп в анамнезе); in some places these foods are less tasty and cost more, while in Belgrade she really enjoys her product list [#737].
3. Home workouts ("Домашние тренировки"). What: since 2021 she trains at home in Anastasia Lunegova's club. How: she carries small balls and resistance bands, and on arrival immediately buys dumbbells, a foam roller and a Pilates ball. Why: she doesn't need to find where to go right away, coordinate schedules or overcome a language barrier; "Я просто всегда знаю, что мне делать" (I just always know what to do) [#737].
4. Sport hobbies that are easy to find anywhere. What: padel, Pilates, yoga, hiking; every city has good places, studios and coaches. How: she explores them gradually, after the home routine is in place. Why: it gives the feeling of integrating into a community [#737].
5. Spacious, bright, quiet and cosy housing. What: they pay enormous attention to housing and look for flats with enough light and space. Cost: sometimes they pay more than they'd like. Why: they rent not only a home but an office, since both work from home. Rule learned "the hard way": "экономить на месте, где проводишь 90% времени, плохая идея" (saving on the place where you spend 90% of your time is a bad idea) [#737].
6. Rituals that are easy to reproduce anywhere. The list: meditation, microcurrents, face massage, dry-brush massage, skincare, a bath, a book with a cup of coffee, a TV series, studying. She always gets up one to two hours earlier than her husband (and goes to bed earlier): "Это мое время, и его не отменяет никакой переезд" (This is my time, and no move cancels it) [#737].
7. Acquaintances around the world. What: she has acquaintances and friends in almost every part of the world, some met online, and she keeps meeting new people (a "non-obvious benefit of the blog"). How: first thing, she writes to all locals she knows and asks where to live, where to go, how things work there, and their favourite wellness places [#737].
8. Small household items carried along. The list: a light alarm clock, a pillow, an aroma lamp, pyjamas, favourite slippers, a laptop stand and lamp, a tripod. Why: "Эти мелочи превращают чужую квартиру в мою за один вечер" (These small things turn someone else's flat into mine in one evening) [#737].
9. Fast search for basic places. What: in every new city during the first month she finds a bakery, a coffee shop and tea shop, a grocery, a Korean cosmetics shop, a pharmacy with collagen (which requires trying and testing), and similar. Why: so as not to feel anxiety when something runs out and marketplaces like Ozon and Amazon do not work (Amazon doesn't work in Serbia, and in Spain its selection was "disgusting") [#737].
10. A vacation for a romance with the city ("Отпуск для романа с городом"). What: for the second time she takes vacation for the first two weeks after a move. Trade-off: on one hand she "loses vacation" (and, for self-employed people, money); on the other she has never regretted it. Why: while her head is not occupied with work she has time to calmly set up daily life, wander, zone out and "start a romance" with the new city. She walks a lot and loves exploring space; this is how she makes herself fall in love with a city and finds places even her local friends don't know [#737].

Note: the post gives no count for the rider; in the source text items 3–6 run together without line breaks, and the ten-item numbering above follows its bold-style headings [#737].

#### Foundation under the rider (preconditions that make it work)
She stresses the rider works because there is a foundation under it, and "я знаю, как мне повезло" (I know how lucky I am) [#737]:
1. No children.
2. She sets her own work schedule.
3. A financial cushion. "We're not multimillionaires", but they have a reserve "for a step left and right": to pay for housing in a new country, pay helpers with documents, buy a coffee machine, set up daily life and keep money for a rainy day.
4. Her husband technically handles most immigration questions: in the US they lived on his visa, in Spain they applied via his startup, in Serbia via his sole proprietorship (ИП).
5. They only move to places they have already visited and liked — a rule adopted after the US. How they choose a place she says deserves a separate post.
