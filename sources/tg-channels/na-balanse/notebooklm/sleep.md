## Sleep as the base

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's posts arguing that sleep is the foundation ("база") of energy and an active life, and explaining what happens when it is neglected: hormonal and metabolic disruption, prediabetic glucose levels, weakened immunity, cardiovascular risk, gene-expression changes, mood problems and visible ageing of the face. It also covers how daytime behaviour (morning exercise, heavy dinners, smoking, evening screens), alcohol and light at night shape sleep, simple self-tests for whether you sleep enough, the practical difference between sleep time and time in bed, and an honest note that much of sleep science is still uncertain. The source frames this as decisive: without sleep, nothing else that people try for energy works [#64, #87, #265].

### Core ideas

- Sleep is the base of energy; nothing substitutes for it. Katya Strelnikova states that without sleep no coffee, energy drink, "magic vitamin", breathing practice or planning will save you; without full sleep it is impossible to live actively, get everything done, reach goals and be happy [#64].
- Quantity and quality both matter. The recommended 7–9 hours is the target, but what happens during those hours (passing through complete REM/non-REM cycles) determines whether sleep is restorative [#23].
- Chronic deprivation, not one bad night, is the problem. Even a single short night shifts hormones, but the real danger is chronic loss, which within about a week can push glucose regulation into the prediabetic range and disrupt hundreds of genes [#87, #265].
- Feeling fine is not evidence. Alcohol improves perceived but not actual sleep, and chronically sleep-deprived people stop noticing their own cognitive decline — subjective impressions are unreliable [#23, #265].
- Daytime behaviour and night-time environment shape sleep. Morning activity and regular exercise help; heavy dinners, smoking, evening screens, alcohol close to bedtime and even small amounts of light at night hurt [#13, #23, #75, #81].
- Time in bed must be longer than the sleep you need. To get 7–9 hours of actual sleep, plan at least 30 minutes extra in bed [#143].
- Sleep science is still uncertain. How much sleep a given person needs is not known exactly, and some data from Walker's *Why We Sleep* reportedly did not hold up [#265].

#### Daytime behaviour and sleep quality (4 findings with mechanisms)
Purpose: Marina Sidorich's summary of a recent large study on how behaviour during the day relates to sleep. Study design as reported: 2,050 participants, of whom 1,500 were women, mean age 34, mostly with higher education ("Нам актуально!" — *relevant to us!*); results are based on observation and self-report, i.e. associations rather than proven causes [#13].
1. Morning physical activity — specifically morning (not just any) activity was associated with longer sleep and fewer awakenings. Why (Marina's explanation): it improves synchronisation of the internal biological rhythms with the natural day–night cycle; bright light during morning exercise may help suppress melatonin (the hormone regulating sleep and wakefulness) in the morning and strengthen its effect at night [#13].
2. Dinner as the largest meal — those who named dinner their biggest meal took longer to fall asleep and slept less. Why: energy intake, nutrient processing and wakefulness belong to the daytime, when melatonin is low; sleep and recovery processes belong to the night, when melatonin is high. A large meal before sleep shifts the circadian rhythm [#13].
3. Smoking — associated with longer time to fall asleep and shorter sleep. Why: nicotine stimulates release of neurotransmitters such as dopamine that disrupt sleep, so smokers may stay awake longer, wake more often at night or even develop insomnia compared with non-smokers [#13].
4. Screens before bed — reports showed a linear relationship between screen time before bed, time to fall asleep and sleep duration; using screens for ≥2 hours before bed increased the likelihood of poor sleep quality. Why: internal circadian rhythms depend on regular alternation of light and dark; evening artificial light affects melatonin secretion and therefore sleep pattern and quality; in addition, incoming information can excite the nervous system and raise alertness exactly when the body should be preparing for sleep [#13].
Marina's addition: in practice "чужой опыт ничему не учит" (other people's experience teaches nothing), so if you want to fix your sleep, carefully observe your own routine and how it connects to your sleep [#13].

#### How alcohol changes the night's sleep architecture
Purpose: answers "Хорошо ли спится под алкоголем?" (Do you sleep well after alcohol?) — it always seems that you fall asleep faster and sleep more soundly [#23].
- Baseline: adults need 7–9 hours for full recovery, but quality matters as much as quantity. During a normal night sleep alternates between REM and non-REM phases roughly every 90 minutes; ideally a person passes through 5–6 full cycles per night, which is equivalent to 7.5–9 hours [#23].
- Alcohol's action: it is a nervous-system depressant with a sedative effect that causes drowsiness [#23].
- Perception vs reality: in the same daytime-behaviour study (see above), participants tracked how alcohol affected their sleep; most reported falling asleep faster and sleeping more soundly. The study's conclusion was that alcohol improves *perceived* sleep quality, not sleep itself — a cognitive error. Under alcohol people did not notice their night awakenings but remembered falling asleep faster than usual [#23].
- Effect on REM: some studies cited in the post show alcohol disrupts sleep cycles and substantially shortens REM sleep, which the post describes as responsible for nervous-system recovery and critical for cognitive function and emotional state; lack of REM is said to weaken immunity and slow the growth of new healthy cells and tissues [#23].
- Hypnogram on the card (normal sleep vs under alcohol, over an 8-hour night, stages: wake, stage 1–4, REM) annotates four effects [#23]:
  1. Alcohol shortens the time needed to fall asleep.
  2. Alcohol increases deep sleep (stage 4) in the first half of the night.
  3. Alcohol suppresses REM sleep in the first half of the night.
  4. Alcohol increases the number of awakenings and lengthens the REM phase in the second half of the night.
- Conclusion: with alcohol sleep may be long but not restorative ("может быть и долгий, но не восстановительный"). The minimum recommended step is not to drink 2–3 hours before sleep [#23].

#### Matthew Walker's 5 domains of sleep-deprivation damage
Purpose: Marina Sidorich's summary of insights from Dr Matthew Walker, professor of neuroscience and psychology whose lab studies the effects of insufficient sleep on health, answering "what happens if you neglect sleep?" [#87].
1. Hormones. Even one night of insufficient sleep can affect testosterone, oestrogen and insulin levels. After a week of chronic sleep shortage, blood-sugar regulation can be disrupted to the extent that people could be classified as prediabetic. Short sleep can upset the balance of leptin and ghrelin (the hormones regulating hunger and satiety), leading to increased appetite and cravings for unhealthy food [#87].
2. Immune system. Sleep restriction leads to a significant reduction in cells important for fighting cancer. Insufficient sleep before a flu shot lowers the antibody response and the vaccine's effectiveness. Chronic sleep deprivation raises the likelihood of colds and flu [#87].
3. Cardiovascular system. In the US it was calculated that when clocks move one hour forward to daylight saving time, heart-attack risk rises by 24%; conversely in autumn, when people get an hour more sleep, heart-attack risk falls by 21% [#87].
4. Mental and emotional wellbeing. Sleep shortage leads to increased irritability, moodiness and negative emotional reactions [#87].
5. Genes. One week of poor sleep can throw the activity of more than 700 genes into disarray: some genes important for immunity are pushed into the background, while others linked to chronic inflammation and cardiovascular problems start working overtime [#87].
Key caveat: "Одна ночь плохого сна — не конец света, хроническое лишение сна — вот где таится проблема." (One night of bad sleep is not the end of the world; chronic sleep deprivation is where the problem lies.) [#87]

#### Sleep time vs time in bed (3-step calculation)
Purpose: Katya Strelnikova's "kind reminder", prompted by comments under an earlier post on light alarm clocks: the 7–9 hours recommended for adults refers to actual sleep, not total time in bed [#143].
- Definitions: sleep time ("Время сна") is the actual time spent asleep; time in bed ("Время в кровати") includes falling asleep, night awakenings, lying awake, and the morning minutes before you "burst into the day" [#143].
- Principle: to reach the desired recovery you need more time in bed than the sleep target [#143].
Steps:
1. Measure how long you take to fall asleep. On average adults need 10–20 minutes [#143].
2. Track how long you lie in bed after waking — and check whether your gadget counts that as sleep [#143].
3. If you wake at night, count how long you can spend awake [#143].
Rule of thumb: on top of 7–9 hours of sleep, add at least 30 minutes for all the above; e.g. if you plan to sleep 8 hours, time in bed may be 8.5 or even 9 hours [#143].
Pitfall/warning: taking too long to fall asleep, being unable to wake up, or constantly waking at night may be a sign of a sleep disorder; Katya points such readers to a sleep guide and to a consultation [#143].

#### Two-question sleep sufficiency test
Purpose: a simple practical method, attributed to Matthew Walker's *Why We Sleep* and shared in a forwarded post from "dreamers become doers", to tell whether you usually sleep enough [#331].
1. After waking in the morning, could you fall asleep again at 10 or 11 a.m.? If "yes", you are probably short of sleep and/or your sleep quality is insufficient [#331].
2. Can you work at full capacity without spurring yourself on with caffeine at least until noon? If "no", you are most likely self-medicating chronic sleep deprivation [#331].
The forwarded author credits the book for her data of the last week, adding jokingly that if it keeps going like this she is ready to read it for a year. Her Sleep Score chart (scores over September–May in the range 48–92, monthly averages around 60–80) and a later chart with a value of 92 accompany the post; the nightly screenshots are listed under *Sleep time vs time in bed* [#331, #332].

#### Sleep vacation method for finding your personal sleep need
Purpose: from a New Scientist piece retold in a forwarded post (channel BitchyK, rubric "#переменныйЗОЖ"): since how much sleep each person needs is unclear, determine it yourself [#265].
- How: arrange "сонные каникулы" (sleep vacation) — go to bed at the same time every day but set no alarm; whatever time you wake up naturally is your answer. Condition: during the day you must be in excellent shape and not irritable [#265].
- Author's objection: she does not fully agree — she wakes without an alarm even when she has not had enough sleep, so spontaneous waking is not proof of sufficiency for everyone [#265].

### Detailed insights

#### Light at night and blood glucose
Marina Sidorich: blackout curtains and diabetes are not directly linked, but according to a study mentioned by Andrew Huberman, even a small amount of light at night can raise morning blood-glucose levels. Her recommendation: sleep in complete darkness ("Спите в полной темноте"), because stable glucose is necessary to keep health and longevity in balance [#75].

#### Regular exercise and insomnia
A forwarded study summary (forwarded from Misha Petrov's channel): people who did at least 2 hours of physical activity per week were 42% less likely to have trouble falling asleep and 22% less likely to suffer from insomnia. It was a 10-year study of about 4,300 people; the forwarded author notes as a curiosity that the most physically active participants were from Norway and the least active from Spain and Estonia [#81].

#### Sleep deprivation and appearance
A forwarded post (from BIOHACKER): the mattress company Simba AI-modelled the faces of people with insomnia. Sleeping under 7 hours is said to give more wrinkles, dryness and "panda-like" dark circles under the eyes; according to the company, this happens because cells do not have enough time to recover. The comparison image ("ABOVE 7 HOURS SLEEP" vs "UNDER 7 HOURS SLEEP") labels the under-7-hours face with: weary facial expression, dehydrated skin, under-eye bags, dark circles and light wrinkles [#88, #89].

#### What sleep science does and does not know (New Scientist retelling)
The forwarded BitchyK author calls sleep one of the hardest topics: apart from "sleep is wildly important", almost nothing can be said for certain, and some data on which the "great" *Why We Sleep* relies apparently have not been confirmed. Points she took from New Scientist [#265]:
- How much sleep is needed is unclear [#265].
- Genes that allow sleeping little without harm are extremely rare [#265].
- The sleep-vacation method (see *Sleep vacation method*) [#265].
- "The world is built for larks" — she, a lark herself, rejects this crudely; if you are an owl, it is important to get bright sunlight straight away in the morning so that falling asleep in the evening is easier. She uses Lumiette 3 light glasses for this [#265].
- The worst part: people who do not get enough sleep quite quickly stop noticing that they are getting duller. They think "at first I wasn't used to it, three days of adaptation, now I'm back to normal", while all tests show they keep getting duller. This is her answer to the claim "я приучил себя спать мало" (I trained myself to sleep little) [#265].
- Microbiome: people who are "active sleepwalkers" have little butyrate (which vegans have a lot of) and many inflammation-causing bacteria; *Bifidobacterium longum* 1714 improved sleep versus placebo over 8 weeks. Cause vs effect remains an open question [#265].
- Hormones/neurotransmitters: short sleep can disturb growth-hormone production (athletes build muscle not in the gym but during sleep); when people in an experiment were made to sleep 5 hours, their insulin sensitivity in some cases went from "healthy" to "prediabetes" in 3 days, and the way back to healthy took 6 to 10 days [#265].
- Sleep and learning: she calls sleep the cheapest "medicine" for learning and memory, though it takes a lot of effort — for example, switch off bright lights everywhere at 21:00, put the phone in airplane mode and skip two episodes of something, so that you wake without an alarm [#265].
- Personal note with a lesson: she dreams of averaging at least 7.5 hours instead of her current 6.5, imagining what she could achieve with an extra hour [#265].

### Practical playbook

- Move your physical activity to the morning where possible, ideally in bright light [#13].
- Do not make dinner the largest meal of the day [#13].
- Limit screens before bed; ≥2 hours of evening screen time was linked to poor sleep [#13].
- Do not drink alcohol within 2–3 hours of bedtime [#23].
- Sleep in complete darkness [#75].
- Aim for at least 2 hours of physical activity a week [#81].
- Owls: get bright light immediately after waking; evening routine: bright lights off at 21:00, phone in airplane mode [#265].
- Observe your own routine rather than relying on others' results [#13].

## Sleep practice and gear

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel author's hands-on sleep practice: the physical tools she relies on (pillow, sunrise light alarm, earplugs, e-reader), the habits that form her "sleep base", her self-experiments (sleeping 01:00–09:00 for six weeks; repairing stress-broken sleep in five steps), the behavioural trap of revenge bedtime procrastination, and the thesis of her sleep practicum — that between people and good sleep stand evening decisions and beliefs rather than lack of information. According to the author, energy level depends directly on sleep quality, and sleep quality depends on many factors, among which the pillow "играет не последнюю роль" (plays no small role) [#43]. She describes herself as having a historically "problematic" sleep and a large personal story with sleep (there was a time when she did not sleep for several days in a row, with lasting PTSD), which is why she keeps experimenting and why the topic comes up with almost every coaching client [#386, #656].

This part adds two kinds of material to the existing topic. First, a participant's report from the author's free sleep practicum (run with BioSisters): it transcribes the practicum's two written exercises — "Make the decision" and "Change the belief" — and shows a filled-in handwritten worksheet, plus the participant's observation that fixing sleep pulled movement and food along with it [#684, #685]. Second, a coaching case: client Olya, who knew "practically everything" about sleep and still slept 5–6 hours, and the insights from her work with the author [#742]. Both extend the existing section *Why we choose not to sleep — the practicum model and four belief types*: that section reported that participants "planned one concrete intention" and found their beliefs but marked the method as (!) INCOMPLETE; these posts supply the actual exercise questions and a real case [#684, #742].

### Core ideas

- Choose a pillow with your body, not with internet advice. The main rule is to lie on a pillow for at least 5 minutes in a store before buying; sleeping position and materials then narrow the choice (see *Pillow selection criteria*) [#43, #386].
- Regularity beats an early bedtime. The author argues that what matters most is going to bed and getting up at the same time and sleeping enough — not going to bed before 23:00; her six-week 01:00–09:00 experiment supports this for her (see *Experiment: feeling good while going to bed at 1 a.m.*) [#158].
- Light and noise control protect sleep quality. A sunrise light alarm helps waking and sets circadian rhythms; earplugs smooth background noise and reduce unnoticed micro-awakenings; but no gadget compensates for chronic sleep deprivation [#121, #386].
- Broken sleep can be repaired with several small changes at once — checking deficits, ending screen time earlier, bedtime meditation, separate bedrooms, and not reaching for a screen in the first 20 minutes of sleeplessness [#264].
- Replace rather than ban. Banning the phone in bed fails; replacing it with a more "ecological" screen (a Kindle) and with the right kind of book works [#437].
- Not sleeping is usually a choice driven by beliefs. Revenge bedtime procrastination is a symptom of a day with too little of yourself; and in general the barrier to good sleep is not physiology or information but nightly decisions and the beliefs they grow from (four belief types) [#564, #656, #658, #662].

- Not going to bed is often a missing decision. The practicum slide states that we often do not go to bed because we have not made the decision to go to bed; hence the first exercise is literally "making the decision" (see *Practice "Make the decision"*) [#684, msg #684].
- Beliefs, not knowledge, keep people sleep-deprived — and they can be rewritten. The second exercise finds the interfering belief, checks where it came from and whether it is true, and reformulates it into a helpful one (see *Practice "Change the belief"*) [#684]. The author restates the thesis through Olya's case: knowledge rarely changes behaviour by itself; behind sleep deprivation there are almost always stories we tell ourselves [#742].
- Sleep is systemic. The participant found that sleeping well also requires actively living one's day; working on sleep unexpectedly improved her sport and then her eating — "change one thing and the other elements change" [#684]. Olya's first results likewise went beyond sleep (less urge to eat, more urge to move, less stress) [#742].
- Habits are built by repetitions, not unbroken streaks, and sleep deprivation can have a hidden "benefit" that must be named honestly (see *Olya's case*) [#742].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Pillow selection criteria
Purpose: choosing a pillow that actually suits you. The author researched the internet and talked to ChatGPT, and shares what she considers most important [#43].

0. Main rule — lie on it. You can theorise as much as you like, but you should not choose a pillow based on internet advice. To find the right one you need to lie on it for at least 5 minutes ("__полежать хотя бы минут 5__" — *lie on it for at least 5 minutes*). Her practical advice: use Google (or Yandex) Maps and plan a visit to the nearest Askona store to test what suits you. Browsing marketplaces convinced her that "__точно надо идти и лежать, потому что иначе совсем сложно.__" (you definitely have to go and lie down, because otherwise it's really hard) — there are thousands of pillows for any budget and need. She repeats this in later posts: when buying her new pillow two years later she again lay on different ones in the store and felt them, and she stresses "когда выбираете подушку, на ней лучше полежать" (when choosing a pillow, it's better to lie on it), noting that her chosen model comes in at least 4 heights [#43, #378, #386].
1. Favourite sleeping position. Side sleepers need a thicker but soft pillow, to fill the space between the head and the mattress. Stomach and back sleepers need a thinner and firmer pillow [#43].
2. Materials — filling. After sleeping on a good anatomical (orthopaedic) pillow with special memory foam inside, you are unlikely to want to return to feathers — and natural fillings can also be allergenic — let alone to uncomfortable polyester [#43].
3. Materials — surface fabric. Modern fabrics with a cooling element help you avoid restlessly flipping the pillow in search of the cool side [#43].
4. If in doubt — double-sided. If you are still unsure about filling or fabric, manufacturers of less expensive pillows came up with a solution: double-sided pillows (her pillow in Moscow was one) [#43].

Why it matters, per the author: a correct pillow means a well-fixed neck and free breathing. If your neck or shoulders go numb or stiff, or your jaw aches, she would look in the direction of the pillow. On an uncomfortable pillow you start tossing and turning — and catch micro-awakenings or real awakenings [#386].

#### Replace, don't cancel — getting the phone out of bed
Purpose: stopping smartphone use in bed. Several years ago the author realised the phone in bed is a catastrophe: in the evening it prevents falling asleep, in the morning it immediately puts you into anxiety and does not let you start the day calmly and mindfully [#437].

Her sequence:
1. First cuts: she stopped looking at social networks, turned off notifications and unsubscribed from "news" [#437].
2. Recognised that reading on the phone also fails: reading Bookmate on the phone did not work — the finger still reaches for the coveted buttons [#437].
3. Bought an e-reader (Kindle) — almost ten years ago; she still considers it one of the best purchases of her life. It still works and keeps all her books and notes, which is valuable because she is in her second emigration and aiming for a third [#437].
4. The principle: although she was not yet a health coach working with behaviour, she reasoned that "«отменять» телефон плохая идея. Надо заменять." ("cancelling" the phone is a bad idea. You need to replace it.) If you want to look at a screen — fine, but what if it is a more ecological one? [#437]
5. Two types of books, "evening" and "morning":
   - *Evening books:* only what helps her exhale and switch off — not difficult, not work-related, in her native language, not stimulating a wish to go write something down or urgently send something to someone — yet interesting enough, because "книга конкурирует с сериалом или соцсетями." (the book competes with a series or social media.) For her this is fiction, e.g. recently Pelevin's "A Sinistra", Liu Cixin's "Of Ants and Dinosaurs", several books from Ursula Le Guin's Hainish cycle. Key criterion: interesting but not anxiety-provoking. Counter-examples: reading Thomas Mann's "The Magic Mountain" or "The Gulag Archipelago" at night did not promote good sleep [#437].
   - *Morning books:* the opposite — books with short chapters that give food for thought and help tune into the day, e.g. Leo Tolstoy's "A Calendar of Wisdom" («Круг чтения»), "The Daily Stoic" («Стоицизм на каждый день»), Oliver Burkeman's recently read "Meditations for Mortals". She can read one such chapter right after waking (literally on the toilet), and the morning vibe is completely different afterwards [#437].

#### Revenge bedtime procrastination — symptom and three questions
Definition (from the post and its card) of Прокрастинация сна (Revenge Bedtime Procrastination): "откладывание сна ради личного времени, которого не хватает днем" (delaying sleep for the sake of personal time that is lacking during the day). It often arises in people with a high workload, a deficit of autonomy and a constant "must" mode. "Это не лень, а попытка вернуть себе контроль ценой сна и восстановления." (It is not laziness, but an attempt to regain control at the cost of sleep and recovery.) [#564, img #564]

The scenario: the day is over, energy is zero, you have to get up early tomorrow, but you still turn on a series or keep scrolling frantically — not because you do not know sleep is important, but because this is the only time that is supposedly "for yourself". The author's comment: logically, if we can procrastinate everything, why not sleep? [#564]

Mechanism: a fresh study the author cites names the main cause — a day lived in "must" mode. When the whole day is children, husbands, clients, calls, other people's expectations and deadlines, in the evening an inner part wakes up with the slogan "А теперь мое" (And now it's mine). And it will not be a book, a bath or sex (because after a day on willpower there is no energy for these) but mindless reel after reel — "Зато «свобода»" (But "freedom") [#564].

Why it is self-deception, per the author: we call it "time for myself", but in fact it is time without yourself — without energy, without presence, without real choice. It is not care; it is a rebellion of an inner part that we suppressed so successfully all day (and by evening there is no energy left even to suppress it). Moreover, the body does not read this as rest — it reads it as one more load. In the morning we wake up not only unrested but with guilt: "опять все испортила" (I ruined everything again) [#564].

What to do:
1. Reframe it as a symptom — a symptom that during the day there is too little of you and for you, too little choice, too little silence, too much "must" [#564].
2. Do not treat it with willpower but with questions:
   - where in my life is there space for myself?
   - where am I living on autopilot?
   - where am I constantly giving but hardly ever choosing? [#564]
3. Self-compassion: "Если вы читаете это в 00:47 — с вами все ок. Вы просто очень хотите отломить себе хоть кусочек жизни." (If you are reading this at 00:47 — you're OK. You just really want to break off at least a little piece of life for yourself.) [#564]

#### Why we choose not to sleep — the practicum model and four belief types
The author ran an open, free sleep practicum with the BioSisters / FemtechForce community titled "Почему мы выбираем не спать. И как все-таки начать высыпаться раз и навсегда" (Why we choose not to sleep. And how to finally start getting enough sleep once and for all). She deliberately chose a different angle from numbers and specifics: she leaves "boring things about melatonin, circadian rhythms and sleep hygiene" to others, since the internet is full of them; as a practitioner she sees that often the issue is not that we lack knowledge [#656].

Core thesis: the practicum briefly covers what is proven to affect sleep, but that seems not to be the main thing (or not always). The main thing is that between a person and normal sleep there most often stands not physiology but the decisions they make every evening, and the beliefs from which those decisions grow. Guiding questions: where does the decision to stay up "just a little longer" come from? Why do many of us at some level believe that not sleeping is normal, even valiant? That there are things more important than sleep? [#656, #658]

Three discussion points of the practicum [#656, #658]:
1. Why "I know I need to sleep" does not work, and what actually governs our evening decisions.
2. Which beliefs about sleep interfere most, and where they come from.
3. How to find your personal pattern and take a first real step toward change.

Format and intended outcome: not a lecture but working through participants' own stories; participants leave not with yet another list of "musts" but with an understanding of what specifically keeps them in this cycle, and with one real step they can try that very evening [#656, #658].

What the practicum showed (author's recap): people saw that their problems are not about information. Between "I know" and "I do" stands something else — an inner voice saying "ну еще немного" (just a bit more), "я еще не додала себе" (I haven't given enough to myself yet), "сначала доделаю" (I'll finish first), and the beliefs from which this voice grows. In the session they worked out what really affects sleep, where exactly the wish to get enough sleep breaks down, found their own beliefs, and planned — not a list of "musts" but one concrete intention. A participant's feedback: "Очень глубоко и многогранно, очень вдохновляюще во многом из-за того, что все выглядит очень даже doable" (Very deep and multifaceted, very inspiring largely because everything looks quite doable) [#662].

The four belief types ("ЧТО ВЫ РАССКАЗЫВАЕТЕ СЕБЕ?" — *What do you tell yourself?*), the ones the author hears most often — "Choose your poison" [#662, img #662]:
1. "Sleep is a waste of time / weakness / shameful" (Сон — это потеря времени / слабость / стыдно). Typical self-talk: "Пока я сплю, другие работают" (While I sleep, others are working); "Successful people sleep little"; "It's a pity to spend time on sleep"; "I don't need that much sleep"; "What am I, a granny, going to bed at 22:00?"; "It's awkward to leave earlier than everyone" [#662].
2. "First I must finish everything" (Сначала надо всё доделать). Typical self-talk: "Мне нужно сначала всё доделать, потом лягу" (I need to finish everything first, then I'll go to bed); "I can't go to bed until…"; "I haven't earned rest yet"; "Others don't sleep — why should I" [#662].
3. "Night is the only time 'for myself'" (Ночь — единственное время «для себя»). Typical self-talk: "It's the only time that belongs only to me"; "During the day I can't be myself and do my own things" [#662]. This is the belief behind *Revenge bedtime procrastination*.
4. "I know better / I don't control my sleep" (Я знаю лучше / Я не контролирую свой сон). Typical self-talk: "Я сова, я по-другому устроена" (I'm an owl, I'm built differently); "I know better myself when to sleep"; "I'm built this way, nothing can be changed"; "I won't fall asleep anyway" [#662].

(!) INCOMPLETE: the content of the practicum beyond these points (what is proven to affect sleep, how to find the personal pattern) is only in the recording, which is not part of the source [#662].

#### Practice "Make the decision" (ПРАКТИКА: ПРИНИМАЕМ РЕШЕНИЕ)
Purpose: turning the vague wish "I should sleep more" into an explicit decision, because — per the practicum slide — "ЧАСТО МЫ НЕ ЛОЖИМСЯ СПАТЬ, ПОТОМУ ЧТО НЕ ПРИНЯЛИ РЕШЕНИЕ ЛОЖИТЬСЯ СПАТЬ." (Often we don't go to bed because we haven't made the decision to go to bed.) [#684]. The participant presents the questions as things the author invited everyone to explore and find their own answers to [#684].

1. "Как я хочу спать? Во сколько ложиться, во сколько вставать, как себя чувствовать утром?" (How do I want to sleep? What time to go to bed, what time to get up, how to feel in the morning?) — define the concrete target: bedtime, wake time and the desired morning state [#684]. Worked example from the participant's worksheet (my reading of the handwriting; the OCR is garbled): she wants to fall asleep at 22:30, be in bed at 22:00 with perhaps a book, and get up at 7:30 — about 9 hours in total [#685].
2. "Почему это важно для меня?" (Why is this important to me?) — name your personal reason, which gives the decision motivational weight [#684]. Worked example: it matters to her because she loves mornings and wants to feel rested, "like a charged battery", and to feel benevolent and friendly [#685].
3. "Что изменится в моей жизни, если я начну высыпаться?" (What will change in my life if I start getting enough sleep?) — picture the concrete consequences of the change [#684]. Worked example: she will feel good not only in the morning but throughout the day, and will feel content/satisfied [#685].

#### Practice "Change the belief" (ПРАКТИКА: МЕНЯЕМ УСТАНОВКУ)
Purpose: dismantling the belief that blocks the decision made in the first exercise (the belief types themselves are listed in the existing section *The four belief types*) [#684].

1. "Найдите установку, которая мешает вашему решению" (Find the belief that interferes with your decision) [#684]. Worked example (reading of the handwriting): in the evening she feels good, as if she is not tired, and has a false sense that tomorrow will be the same; and she believes she knows better when and how she should sleep — she acts from the present moment rather than from the whole day. This corresponds to belief type 4, "I know better / I don't control my sleep" [#685].
2. "Подумайте, откуда она взялась. Проверьте её на правду, найдите контрпример" (Think about where it came from. Check it for truth, find a counterexample) [#684]. On the worksheet, the counterexample (marked "Контр.") is: she feels good when she has slept well and enough; she loves people and is benevolent (i.e., her well-being and friendliness actually depend on sleep) [#685].
3. "Сформулируйте её по-новому, так, чтобы она помогала, а не мешала" (Reformulate it so that it helps rather than hinders) [#684]. The worksheet also contains a section heading "how to support myself before going to sleep" and a closing intention: in future she wants to do sport, and the morning is exactly the right time for it — with the words sleep / sport / food written alongside [#685].

#### Three pillars of well-being — sleep, movement, food
The participant's conclusion: "Вот они три кита хорошего самочувствия: сон - движение - еда." (Here they are, the three pillars of good well-being: sleep — movement — food.) During the practicum she realised that to sleep well you also need to actively live your day; so, while solving her sleep issue, she unexpectedly took up sport, and then improved her eating. She calls it a "Системный подход" (systemic approach): change one element and the others change too. Her post is titled "How I ended up in the gym" [#684].

#### Olya's case — four insights for changing sleep behaviour
Context: the author presents interim results of her coaching work on sleep with client Olya (Olya described it in her own channel, in two parts). Olya knew practically everything about sleep — circadian rhythms, light, rituals, keeping the phone outside the bedroom — and still slept 5–6 hours, "Потому что знания редко меняют поведение сами по себе." (Because knowledge rarely changes behaviour by itself.) Behind sleep deprivation there are almost always stories we tell ourselves; the author lists four: "сон — потеря времени" (sleep is a waste of time), "сначала надо все доделать" (first I have to finish everything), "ночь — единственное время для себя" (night is the only time for myself), "я так устроена, все равно не засну" (I'm built this way, I won't fall asleep anyway). By Olya's own words she had "bingo" — all four. These are the same four belief types as in the practicum. The author adds that several clients have already told her they recognised themselves in Olya's story [#742].

The insights the author highlights:
1. Repetitions, not streaks. "Привычку укрепляет количество повторений, а сорванная серия ничего не обнуляет." (A habit is strengthened by the number of repetitions, and a broken streak resets nothing.) A missed night does not cancel the progress already made [#742].
2. Ask the uncomfortable question. "что хорошего в том, что я не сплю?" (what is good about me not sleeping?). Olya's honest answer: sleep deprivation gives the right to demand less of oneself — i.e., the problem has a hidden pay-off that keeps it in place [#742].
3. Move "my time" from night to morning. The belief "night is the only time for myself" can be transferred into a slow morning — the need for personal time is kept, its slot is moved [#742].
4. Check what is actually under your control. Olya schedules her evening Zoom calls herself — which means they can also be moved [#742].

First results: less desire to eat, more desire to move, less stress and a more even state. The work is continuing [#742]. The author also links this case to the previous week's post about client Sasha, where they discussed that sometimes what is missing is not energy itself but the things that should give it [#742].

### Detailed insights

#### Light alarm: personal experience
The author normally wakes without an alarm, but after a vacation she had to readjust and needed one. She took her smartwatch (which she had recently stopped wearing) because she "despises" sound alarms — only gentle vibrations, no hardcore. On the second day she no longer needed it, and realised that for the previous months she had thought she woke without an alarm, but she was in fact waking with one — a light alarm: "последние месяцы думала, что вставала без будильника, но вставала-то с будильником" (for the last months I thought I was waking up without an alarm, but I was waking up with one) [#121].

After 7 months of use her verdict is that it works: it gently dims in the evening and prepares you for sleep, gently wakes you in the morning, without additional alarms. Their model is a SkyView 2, but there are many at more democratic prices [#121].

Mechanism (her summary of her 2022 article): light alarms imitating sunrise stimulate cortisol production, which affects how fast you wake up and synchronises the biological clock — and as a consequence affects energy, mood and health [#121].

Limits: a light alarm is definitely not an option for people with chronic sleep deprivation. Nor does it work if it is set not to your own biological clock. She and her husband need different amounts of sleep — she 7.5–8 hours, he more like 8.5–9 — so his alarm, set to her wake-up time, usually does not wake him (she turns it off and he sleeps another hour) [#121].

#### Experiment: feeling good while going to bed at 1 a.m.
Claim being tested: for years we have been told from every direction that we must go to bed before 23:00 to feel great, be full of energy and not harm our health. The author increasingly meets information that this is not so, because we no longer depend so strongly on light cycles — we can organise them ourselves with light alarms and blackout curtains. "И самое главное — это ложиться и вставать в одно и то же время и спать достаточно." (And the most important thing is to go to bed and get up at the same time and sleep enough.) [#158]

Set-up: when she flew from the US to Russia she decided not to shift her schedule, living roughly on American time — late to bed, late up. Hypothesis: if she kept her rhythm, with a stable bedtime and wake time and enough sleep, she would feel great. It looked counter-intuitive: regular US schedule 5 a.m.–9 p.m. versus test schedule in Russia 9 a.m.–1 a.m. People who knew her predicted she would shift to earlier rising within a week; she did not [#158].

Result over six weeks: apart from the first couple of adaptation weeks, when morning puffiness bothered her (and neighbours' drilling — a bonus of getting up at 5 a.m. is that no one drills), she noticed no problems at all: plenty of energy, easy waking and falling asleep. Her conclusion: "Получается, если мы стабильно спим нужное количество часов, ложимся в одно и то же время, и у нас нет жалоб, скорее всего, все в порядке — и здоровью мы не вредим." (So if we consistently sleep the needed number of hours, go to bed at the same time and have no complaints, most likely everything is fine — and we are not harming our health.) She calls it an empirical conclusion sufficient for herself and lets readers decide [#158].

Travel "lifehack": if you fly somewhere for a short time, you do not have to rebuild your sleep schedule [#158].

Side benefits she noticed from the late lifestyle: going to parties without suffering, drinking coffee after lunch, not worrying that sunset interferes with falling asleep. Upon returning to the US she quickly fell back into her rhythm, noting that flying east to west is always more pleasant [#158].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The 01:00 bedtime experiment is a six-week n=1 self-experiment; the author explicitly calls her conclusion empirical and "sufficient for me" and tells readers to decide for themselves. It also included two weeks of adaptation with morning puffiness [#158].
- Light alarms: the author repeatedly states they do not help with chronic sleep deprivation, and they fail if set to someone else's biological clock [#121, #386].
- Her 5-step sleep repair combined several changes at once (plus supplement course for deficits); the source does not isolate which step had which effect. She also states she does not recommend taking supplements, even vitamins, without indications, tests and a doctor's consultation [#264].
- Step 5 of the sleep repair (not grabbing a screen for 20 minutes) has its own exception: if anxiety becomes severe, it is better to distract yourself [#264].
- The cause of revenge bedtime procrastination is attributed to "a fresh study" the author cites (Talker Research, on Americans losing hours to late-night scrolling); the source does not give the study's numbers or method [#564].
- Mild tension: the author advises against choosing pillows by internet advice, while her own selection criteria were compiled from the internet and ChatGPT; she resolves this by insisting the final test is lying on the pillow [#43].
- The author says her own sleep is still not ideal and historically problematic; she measures improvement by her daytime energy [#386].
