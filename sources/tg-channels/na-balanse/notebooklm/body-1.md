## Fitness independence and sustainable movement

### What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel teaches about keeping movement in your life regardless of gym membership, location, schedule or mood: where long-term motivation comes from, Katya Strelnikova's concept of "fitness independence" and the concrete steps behind it, the honest claim that workouts never become fully automatic, evidence-checked advice on post-workout recovery, the argument that endurance training builds career-relevant traits, and lifelong-fitness principles from Jane Fonda. According to the source it matters because movement is framed as a lifelong part of health and of an active old age — Katya plans to live actively "minimum to 100, better 120" — and because most people's attempts at sport end after a couple of months when built on the wrong goals [#42, #229].

### Core ideas

- Motivation must be long-term and internal. Motivation borrowed from "this is how it's done", from what others like, or from social-media ideals does not carry you far; the coach advises assuming sport will be part of your life until the end and finding your own reason for it (see *Gavrish's three principles*) [#42].
- The wrong goals explain repeated quitting. Katya started gyms, yoga, CrossFit, stretching, tennis and badminton hundreds of times and always quit within a couple of months because her goals were appearance, trendiness or external motivation; things changed only when the goal became being "fitness-independent" — doing movement for herself, with pleasure, without coercion [#229].
- Fitness independence = want + know + be able. Wanting and choosing to move in any circumstances, knowing which kinds of movement fit those circumstances, and having the possibility to do them (see *Definition of fitness independence* and *Steps*) [#229, #230].
- Workouts never fully become automatic — and that is normal. Because the brain economises energy, resource-intensive activity is never treated as a "great idea"; what gets easier with time is ignoring the self-sabotage, not the effort itself [#238, #239].
- Recovery rests on basics, not gadgets. Sleep, nutrition, water, protein and rest work better than any expensive procedure; ice baths, cryo chambers and similar are optional bonuses [#322].
- Endurance trains character, not just muscles. Katya, drawing on the book *Rest* and studies she links, argues that endurance training improves the brain, transfers stress tolerance to life and builds self-belief [#477].
- Health in old age is laid down in youth. Jane Fonda: in youth we take health for granted, but that is when the foundation is built; keep the body flexible and strong and the heart healthy with cardio [#285].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Fitness coach Yuri Gavrish's three principles for starting and not quitting
Purpose: to find motivation that actually lasts, presented in an interview post by Marina Sidorich titled «Годовой план можно сделать только за год» ("A yearly plan can only be done in a year"). The framing: often the hardest part of taking up sport is finding motivation; motivation because "that's the norm" or "others like it" won't take you far, and neither will an inadequate striving for social-media ideals [#42].

1. Accept your body in its current state. Start with «принятия своего тела в его текущем состоянии» (accepting your body in its current state), understanding your own features and limitations. Why: this creates realistic expectations. Accept that no super-changes in the body will happen within a month — or even a year. The older we are, the more we have to invest for a smaller return. Yet in any case the body and your state will become noticeably better — «И этому надо радоваться!» ("And that is something to be glad about!") [#42].
2. Distribute the load correctly. «годовой план можно сделать только за год» (a yearly plan can only be done in a year): sport is a gradual increase of load and the organism needs adaptation. A second benefit of going gradually: it lets you exhale and observe the process, which makes it easier to notice what you like most — the club, the trainer, how you feel and your mood, your energy level after workouts — and so what will motivate you most [#42].
3. Define your long-term motivation. Assume from the start that sport will be part of your life until the end, and ask why. Examples the coach gives: wanting to live longer, actively and with enjoyment; getting rid of bad habits, because sport disciplines; or a concrete passion — running wine marathons in Bordeaux, taking part in amateur padel-tennis or powerlifting competitions [#42].

#### Definition of fitness independence (three components)
Katya Strelnikova says she coined the term «фитнес-независимой» ("fitness-independent") after noticing that her activity survived constant change of circumstances. Her definition, verbatim: «В любых условиях и обстоятельствах хотеть и выбирать двигаться. Знать, какие варианты движения тебе подходят в этих условиях и обстоятельствах. И иметь возможность их сделать.» (In any conditions and circumstances, to want and choose to move. To know which movement options suit you in these conditions and circumstances. And to have the possibility to do them.) [#229]

1. Wanting and choosing to move — motivation that is internal and holds in any setting.
2. Knowing your options — a repertoire of movement that fits whatever conditions you are in.
3. Being able to do them — practical access and capacity.

She explicitly states that this goal "is not about achievements, muscle definition, overcoming, community and fun. It is only about your state and health" — and asks "isn't that the main thing?" She discusses with clients that cultivating fitness independence is one of the most important goals on the path to health and self-care, and stresses that even someone who currently does no workouts at all "can end up where I am" [#229].

#### Steps to fitness independence (announced as six, seven listed)
Katya's post is titled «Шесть шагов к фитнес-независимости» (Six steps to fitness independence) but lists seven items, the seventh added "on a hint from the comments". These are the steps that helped her get there and help her stay there [#230].

1. Understand why you train. What training gives you; revise your goals. This refers back to her diagnosis that she used to set the wrong goals (weight loss, splits, a defined body, fashionable sport, community, fun, forgetting herself, external motivation — often subconsciously, "covered with care for health") and replaced them with moving for herself because she needs and enjoys it [#230, #229].
2. Change your perception of what a "workout" is. For example, a purposeful walk at a confident pace now counts as a workout for her, because it is movement she chooses in order to work her body and improve her state [#230].
3. Keep a repertoire of favourite workouts within easy reach. Hers: the Nike Training and Nike Running apps and Anastasia Lunegova's workouts; she also regularly tries new options [#230].
4. No attachment to workout duration. If there are only 5 minutes (or only the desire for 5 minutes), it will be 5 minutes — «а там посмотрим» ("and then we'll see") [#230].
5. No attachment to workout type. If she feels her body is already "clogged"/tight, she does stretching or yoga instead of strength training, even if strength was planned [#230].
6. No attachment to time of day. Today she may have time in the morning, tomorrow at lunch, the day after in the evening. When planning a day she always thinks ahead about where movement can be integrated [#230].
7. Emphasis on everyday activity (added from comments). Wherever possible walk, bend, reach, stand a little longer at the stove or sink — she perceives this not as a chore but as good load for the body [#230].

#### Recovery methods compared — ice bath, cryo chamber, banya
Expert: Ekaterina Khomenko, movement and nutrition specialist. Context: workouts should give energy, but after a break or when you overdo it, the next morning the body may feel "on strike" and getting out of bed feels like a new workout. She separates what really helps from "a pretty wrapper with doubtful effect" [#322].

Ice bath — "the hardcore method".
- Protocol: immerse legs or arms in water with 20–40% ice, no longer than 5–7 minutes (unless you are a "walrus", i.e. a winter swimmer); do it within 30 minutes after the workout.
- Pros: tones muscles; reduces inflammation and fatigue; costs zero roubles and is available at home.
- Cons: can cause cramps or hypothermia; immersion above the waist risks the genitourinary system; contraindicated with joint problems; effectiveness not proven ("water conducts cold, but... no miracles").
- Verdict: suits the experienced and careful — "or those who want to impress their household" [#322].

Cryo chamber (криокапсула)
- Protocol: exposure to cold down to −130° for 2–3 minutes; can be done within 24 hours after the workout.
- Pros: powerful anti-inflammatory effect; works well for immunity; gives a release of endorphins, light euphoria and a "second wind".
- Cons: comparable in effect to an ice bath but many times more expensive; cools worse than water.
- Verdict: an excellent recovery option if you want a feeling of "luxury" and vigour, but it does not replace the basics [#322].

Banya (bathhouse/sauna) — "beloved classic, with caveats".
- Protocol: if you lost a lot of fluid during the workout, better postpone it; use the banya on a rest day.
- Pros: relaxes; improves microcirculation; helps remove lactic acid.
- Cons: fat is not burned in the banya ("alas"); the main weight loss is water; with heart problems — use caution; "removing toxins" («выведение шлаков») is a myth not supported by science.
- Verdict: excellent for relaxation, but sleep and nutrition influence recovery more [#322].

#### Simple recovery basics available to everyone
Khomenko's "simple and working methods" [#322]:
1. Hot bath or shower — add sea salt, magnesium, essential oil; relaxes and improves circulation.
2. Light exercises the next morning — bends, rotations, squats; even 5 minutes of movement makes it easier.
3. Massage or self-massage — with hands, a roller or a brush; important to direct the movements along the lymph flow.
4. Quality sleep and nutrition — "sounds boring, but works better than any expensive spa". Without recovery muscles don't grow; only fatigue accumulates.
5. Pressure chamber, Indiba, endosphere, cryo — can be added as a bonus. But first the base: routine, water, protein and rest.

#### Soreness rule — when to train and when to rest
- If after intense load there is light pain in the body the next day that passes after a warm-up, you can train, just a bit more gently.
- In other cases you can give the body 2–4 days and everything will pass.
- Don't hurry to spend money on a cryo chamber if you ignore balanced nutrition and sleep 5 hours; start with basic things, and the rest is "by desire, possibilities and... the whim of the soul" [#322].

#### Four mechanisms by which endurance training is said to help career
Katya Strelnikova's argument, which she frames around running but believes applies to all endurance activity (cycling, swimming, rowing). Trigger: the book *Rest: Why You Get More Done When You Work Less*, which describes how aerobic endurance training affects purposefulness, concentration and even career success; her summary: «выносливость — не только про мышцы, но и про характер» (endurance is not only about muscles but also about character) [#477].

1. Brain renewal via irisin and BDNF. Harvard scientists (a study she links) found that during prolonged cardio (e.g. running or swimming) muscles produce the protein irisin, which stimulates the brain to produce BDNF, a neuron growth factor. This helps the brain "renew": memory, attention and ability to learn improve. Running turned out to be especially effective — it stimulates neurogenesis, the formation of new nerve cells (second linked study) [#477].
2. Physical endurance trains cognitive endurance. When you train the body to withstand load, the brain learns to do the same: hold focus, tolerate boredom, not give up at the first resistance. "Roughly speaking, running 10 km and finishing a big project are processes of the same type." Murakami wrote that finishing a book is closer to physical labour than to inspiration, and that he runs marathons because they help him keep concentration and not "fall apart over the distance" [#477].
3. Retuning the nervous system to stress. Running and other endurance training (linked article) reconfigure the nervous system, teaching the body to withstand stress and recover faster — "a training camp for the psyche". We create controlled short shake-ups; the organism learns to recognise that this is not danger but load, and that it copes. Over time the skill transfers to life: negotiations, deadlines, moves [#477].
4. Endurance builds self-belief. When we regularly cover a distance, even a short one, the brain records «я могу» ("I can"); this quiet inner "I can" then appears everywhere — at work, in relationships, in life (linked study) [#477].

#### Jane Fonda's training and ageing principles
Katya rewrote Jane Fonda's W magazine interview (given on the release of the VR program The Routine Reboot Challenge) as if the conversation were with her. Fonda, 80, Oscar winner, is described as the first fitness influencer: her 1982 video cassette "Jane Fonda's Workout" opened home workouts — previously considered a purely male prerogative — to women worldwide and even influenced fashion (the 80s leggings with leg-warmers) [#285].
1. Train daily when at home, alternating load: upper body one day, lower body the next.
2. Key word — slower: she does the same exercises as before, just performs them more slowly.
3. Favourite cardio — walking; depending on the weather a treadmill or exercise bike instead.
4. Age is no barrier to new technology: «Я думала, что слишком стара, чтобы быть частью виртуальной революции, оказалось это не так.» (I thought I was too old to be part of the virtual revolution; it turned out not to be so.) You don't need to understand technology to enjoy a workout.
5. Ageing depends on health, built in youth: when you age, everything depends on how healthy you are; in youth we take health for granted, but that is exactly when the foundation for the future is laid.
6. Body flexible and strong, heart healthy: heart health requires cardio.
Her (non-fitness) New Year resolutions: judge less, be kinder and show more empathy [#285].

### Detailed insights

#### Katya's movement history: activity that survives every change of circumstances
Five years before the post Katya practically did no sport, or did it unsystematically. Four years before she started running regularly. Two years before, her activity was (almost) daily surfing and regular home yoga. Three months before it was tied to a fitness club (strength training and group yoga). Then she lived "literally in the forest"; now she has moved back to a city without knowing where she'll settle in the next 3–6 months. Her observation: it seems there is less activity, but it hasn't disappeared — she still walks and runs, does short yoga and strength sessions of 10–30 minutes. This persistence across changing settings is what she named fitness independence [#229].

#### The turning point: "what if I force nothing and just enjoy it?"
In quarantine in Jakarta in early 2021 she realised she no longer wanted to depend on anything or anyone, and wanted to simply do something — even a little — for herself because she needs and likes it. Her question: «А что если ничего не заставлять себя делать, а просто получать удовольствие? Что из этого выйдет?» (What if I don't force myself to do anything and just enjoy it? What will come of it?). 3.5 years later she has done 800 workouts in the Nike apps alone — about 229 training days a year and about 19 days a month, "only in two free apps" [#229].

Her current state: no more doubts or resistance — «Я просто делаю то, что хочу, когда хочу. И могу.» (I just do what I want, when I want. And I can.) And she can almost every day because after any workout she feels great — morally (satisfied and proud of herself) and physically (a bright surge of energy and good mood). A second reason: she plans to live actively at least to 100, better 120; if she already "creaks like a worn saddle" and doesn't get off the couch, the probability of a magnificent old age "somewhere in the Alps with poles in hand" tends to zero [#229].

#### Why workouts never become automatic
Katya wrote this to correct the impression that after becoming fitness-independent training is "hyper-easy" for her. Her thesis: «некоторые. привычки. никогда. не станут. автоматическими.» (some. habits. will never. become. automatic.) — "a scary thing, especially from a coach" [#238].
- She pushes back on the popular claim that more than 40% of behaviour is automatic and that practically any habit can be taken into or out of autopilot ("if it hasn't happened to you, you just don't know how to cook them"). "Not so fast": habits that require serious resources don't always fit that category [#238].
- Mechanism (her logic): on one hand, the brain is geared to save energy — that explains automatisms. On the other hand, the brain is geared to save energy — so any resource-intensive activity that doesn't help survival in the moment or reproduction won't be considered a great idea. Translation: workouts will most likely forever stay in the category «надо бы немножко поднапрячься» ("I should strain a little"), especially in low-energy situations [#238].
- Case: her mother, 71, has always more or less exercised (morning exercises, skiing) yet constantly says how hard it sometimes is to push herself out to the pool, to Pilates, even to do exercises at home. Katya, 38, regularly training for several years, catches herself doing the same [#238].
- What does get easier: effort isn't needed every single time, and what becomes easier is «уговорить себя не реагировать на этот предательский самосаботаж» (persuading yourself not to react to this treacherous self-sabotage) [#238].
- Lesson: if you've trained for "…teen months or years" and still constantly have to make an effort, nothing is wrong with you — «Вы просто человек.» (You are just human.) Supporting example: in a podcast episode on controlling the inner voice, Andrew Huberman admits he too regularly has to motivate himself, though he has done sport since 16 [#238].
- A forwarded subscriber reply confirms it: her colleague, a lifelong athletic "jock" who runs 1–2 times a day, honestly says that every time he feels lazy and has to force himself, and the main thing is to start the workout. She (running slower than toddlers learning to walk, a kilometre at most, which is a huge win for her) says the thought helps her understand that it is normal, everyone has it, they somehow cope, and she can too [#239].

#### Running and Katya's own trajectory
Katya was prompted by Murakami's story — he became a writer only at 33 after owning a bar and attributes this to running. People are surprised by her systematicness, resilience and ability to "hit one spot no matter what"; her mother called her "so stubborn" — and she notes it wasn't always so. In 2020, at the peak of an existential crisis, she started running regularly; in 2021 she understood what she actually wants — "coincidence?" She hedges: her changes started before running became constant, but whether they'd have happened at this scale is "an open question". She suggests she keeps going, not giving up, changing her life again and again and believing she can succeed in a new profession on her own terms perhaps because every time, even when she doesn't want to or seems to have no strength, she takes one more step and a small effort over herself [#477].

#### Lunegova's program and the reality behind a fitness business
Katya has trained with Anastasia Lunegova since March 2021, when she decided in Jakarta to become fitness-independent and, through a friend, found Lunegova's "Здоровое тело" (Healthy Body) program; she has since completed it twice more and is now in the club "Легкость и сила" (Lightness and Strength). Her verdict: she grunts, puffs and curses Nastya, «Но, блин, после этих тренировок реально чувствую себя супер.» (But, damn, after these workouts I really feel super.) [#470]

She listened to the PR-Агент Купер podcast episode "Fitness and business: what's behind building an online sports product", where Lunegova — founder of a large online platform with a team of 20+ — speaks not in the glossy language of "successful success" but honestly about strategy, mistakes, system and team, and about combining the work of trainer and entrepreneur without giving up on life. Katya says the episode "sobered and invigorated" her, and names five things she found useful to learn from it:
- what stays behind the scenes of "healthy" online products — her one-word answer: a lot of work («очень много работы»);
- how not to lose the balance between "I'm a trainer" and "I'm an entrepreneur";
- how to build a team that holds quality;
- which strategies work now and which don't;
- how to stay in contact with a huge community.
Her takeaway: it is important to have examples of honest, sustainable business nearby rather than magical "million-rouble launches" [#470].

## Minimal effective dose of movement

### What this covers

This topic gathers the author's series on "movement on the minimum settings" (`движение на минималках` — "movement at minimal settings") [#393]: how many steps per day bring most of the health benefit (about 7,000), why short workouts of 10 minutes count, and why ordinary household and walking activity ("zero zone", NEAT) is what actually makes a person "active". It also covers two books the author recommends for falling in love with movement — Kelly McGonigal's *The Joy of Movement* (with its explanation of why nature walks make us happier) and Haruki Murakami's *What I Talk About When I Talk About Running*. According to the source, this matters because people chronically underestimate the movement they already do, punish themselves with an "an hour in the gym or nothing" mindset, and miss that most of the benefit comes from modest, regular, enjoyable movement [#371, #373, #393].

### Core ideas

- About 7,000 steps a day captures most of the benefit. A meta-analysis the author cites shows benefit rising steeply up to 5–7 thousand steps and only modestly after 7–8 thousand; more is not dramatically better. See *Dose–response of daily steps* [#371].
- Total weekly active minutes matter, not the length of a workout. Any bout of moderate-or-vigorous activity counts, even very short ones; 10 minutes is a legitimate workout. See *Weekly active minutes rule* and *Start-small workout ladder* [#373].
- Everyday "zero-zone" activity is what makes a person active. The author's view is that constant, low-level base movement (cooking, cleaning, walking instead of taking a taxi) — not gym sessions — defines an active lifestyle, builds the habit and identity of a moving person, and supports recovery and health. See *Zero-zone activity* [#393].
- Movement should be joyful, and nature amplifies it. Following McGonigal, physical activity "rewires" the brain, treats anxiety and depression, and trains the capacity for joy; outdoors, the brain's default network switches from self-centred rumination to open "soft fascination". See *Two scenarios of the Default Mode Network* [#360].
- Books can be a low-effort entry point to movement. The author presents McGonigal's and Murakami's books as an "easy way to start moving" — reading them produces the desire to put on sneakers [#360, #454].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Dose–response of daily steps
Purpose: to answer "how many steps are enough" and to relieve people of the pressure of 10,000+ steps. The author titles the post "7 000 шагов тоже норм" (7,000 steps is fine too) and opens with "Ребята, выдыхаем и расходимся" (Guys, exhale and go home) [#371].

1. The study. A meta-analysis published "the other day" (August 2025) in *The Lancet Public Health* — 57 studies, more than 160,000 participants — compared how different daily step counts affect life expectancy [#371].
2. Steep zone: up to 5–7 thousand steps. Benefit grows sharply as steps increase up to 5–7 thousand a day [#371]. In the follow-up post the author restates the gradient in plain terms: 3 thousand is better than 2 thousand, 5 thousand is better than 3 thousand, 7 thousand is better than 5 thousand — "дальше все уже не так драматично" (after that it's all not so dramatic) [#373].
3. Plateau zone: after 7–8 thousand. Gains still exist above 7–8 thousand steps but are "no longer critical" [#371].
4. What ~7,000 steps buys (as reported by the author from the meta-analysis): risk of premature death reduced by 47%, dementia by 38%, depression by 22%, diabetes by 14% [#371].
5. Lesson against "gigantomania". The author admits she always "suffered from gigantomania" and thought more is always better; the data changed that view [#371].
6. Practical translation. 7,000 steps is roughly an hour of moderately brisk walking. In the city she easily logs 15–20 thousand; in her subtropical village at +33°C she is happy if a morning walk yields 7 thousand, and with walking around the house the day reaches about 10 thousand. Her step-tracker screenshots show a day of 10,305 steps and a peak day (Sun, Jun 8, 2025) of 24,080 steps [#371, img #371, img #372].
7. Seasonal permission. She loves walking and goes all out when she can, but in certain seasons one can focus on other directions of fitness — strength, balance, mobility — "без зазрений совести" (without pangs of conscience) [#371].

#### Weekly active minutes rule
Purpose: to judge whether one's training "counts". Thesis (bold in the source): "Для здоровья важна не длительность тренировки, а суммарные активные минуты за неделю." (For health, what matters is not the length of a workout but the total active minutes per week.) And "«малыши» тоже считаются" (the "little ones" count too) [#373].

1. US guidelines dropped the 10-minute minimum. The new US recommendations removed the statement about "at least 10-minute blocks": now any activity of any duration counts, provided it is of moderate or high intensity [#373].
2. WHO target. Accumulate 150–300 minutes of moderate activity per week, or 75–150 minutes of vigorous activity — and not necessarily in one piece [#373].
3. Micro-bursts. According to data the author cites (a Health.com article), even very short "bursts" of a minute or two, as long as they noticeably raise the heart rate, give benefits for longevity and risk reduction [#373].
4. Intensity condition. The counting rule applies to moderate or vigorous effort; for the micro-bursts the criterion is a perceptible increase in pulse [#373].

#### Start-small workout ladder
Purpose: for someone who has no hour for training — or, like the author, finds it hard to make herself train for an hour [#373].

1. Choose 10, 15 or 20 minutes — "наш вариант" (our option) when an hour is not available or not realistic [#373].
2. Build gradually. This is how the author herself gradually reached a comfortable 30 minutes [#373].
3. Accept the short day. "Но если сегодня получается только десять — отлично, десять и делаем. Завтра добавим еще." (But if today only ten works out — great, we do ten. Tomorrow we'll add more.) The point is that a short session is a success, not a failure [#373].
4. The author mentions she has described other "hacks" for getting herself to train in an earlier post on "fitness independence"; (!) INCOMPLETE: those hacks are not part of this topic's source [#373].

#### Formal vs informal movement
Purpose: to see the movement you are already doing. Movement is either formal (workouts) or informal (household activity), and it is the second that we chronically underestimate and count as "under-moving" [#393].

- Case — the client and the Oura ring. A client told the author she "doesn't move enough". It turned out she simply wasn't recording all her movement: she took off her Oura ring while cooking and working (she works with her hands), scolded herself for "too little movement", yet was exhausted by evening. As an experiment she kept the ring on — over the day she accumulated "a whole kitchen" of steps and bending, and the rings on the tracker closed without effort. Lesson: self-assessment of activity based on incomplete tracking can be wrong; unrecognized informal movement is real movement [#393].
- Longevity observation. "При этом среди долгожителей мало атлетов, зато много тех, кто заботится о семье, ходит пешком, копается на огороде." (Meanwhile among long-livers there are few athletes, but many who take care of family, walk, dig in the garden.) [#393]

#### Zero-zone activity
Purpose: to define the base layer of movement beneath formal training and to replace the all-or-nothing mindset [#393].

1. Definition. Fitness usually talks about Zone 2 and Zone 3; zero zone is ultra-light activity *below* Zone 1 — a pace at which you breathe calmly and can talk [#393].
2. Examples (complete list from the source): walking back and forth ("ходить туда-сюда"); cooking; cleaning; carrying bags; watering plants; taking a walk after dinner; walking somewhere instead of taking a taxi. "Не спорт — но движение." (Not sport — but movement.) [#393]
3. What makes someone "active". The author's opinion: "По мне именно это базовое постоянное движение делает человека «активным»." (To me it is exactly this basic constant movement that makes a person "active".) [#393]
4. Who it is for. Not only beginners for whom "grown-up" sport is still scary or uncomfortable; it is also an excellent mode between hard training days [#393].
5. Benefits claimed. Helps recovery, supports mental state, sleep, blood-sugar regulation and lowers heart risks; the author points to reviews of the topic in The Conversation and The Guardian [#393].
6. The main benefit — habit and identity. Zero zone helps create the habit of moving and forms the identity of an active person. It is the background, the base — the NEAT activity (non-exercise activity thermogenesis) that erases the belief "или час в зале, или ничего" (either an hour in the gym or nothing) [#393].
7. Limit — not a replacement for training. The author is not talking anyone out of training: zero zone does not replace strength and interval work if the goals are strength, speed or volume. It is about health, recovery and the habit of "being in motion" [#393].

#### Author's everyday NEAT habits
The author's personal practices for informal movement [#393]:
1. Wash dishes and/or unload the dishwasher after a meal — she says she knows that standing and bending after eating stabilises blood sugar.
2. Never ask someone to bring her something — she gets up and takes it herself.
3. Always prefer a walk to a ride on transport.
4. Train less on days with an evening of cooking ahead — she deliberately lowers training load when she knows she will be on her feet cooking.

#### Two scenarios of the Default Mode Network
Purpose: explains, per McGonigal's book as retold by the author, why walking in nature makes us feel happier ("high") [#360].

- Background. Only about two decades ago scientists realised that when a person lies in an MRI scanner "doing nothing", the brain does not switch off: the so-called Default Mode Network (`сеть пассивной работы мозга` — network of passive brain work) activates. It is the inner "background" where we replay past scenes, rehearse future dialogues and argue with ourselves [#360].
1. Urban, self-centred scenario (in Russian: Городской, я-центричный). Within four walls the passive network easily couples with the reward system: each obsessive thought delivers a pinch of dopamine, the brain whispers «да-да, крути ещё» (yes-yes, spin it more). The result is a loop of self-criticism, anxiety and chewing over grievances [#360].
2. Natural, open scenario (in Russian: Природный, открытый). Outdoors the network switches into *soft fascination* — gentle, non-strained absorption in the current moment. Self-digging fades; zones of spatial imagination and social navigation activate; we feel sheltered and at the same time look at life «с птичьего полёта» (from a bird's-eye view) [#360].
- Evidence cited. Psychologists analysing people's written notes after park walks found the most frequent words were "love, life, time, world, God" [#360].
- Evolutionary explanation. In the "stone jungle" the brain must quickly assess social status and potential threats — hence self-fixation. On a trail (even in a city park) it is more useful to open up to the surroundings and notice birds, berries, wind direction. Nature switches the second branch of the passive mode back on — and instead of rumination we get a sense of perspective and a surge of calm joy [#360].

#### McGonigal's movement advice
The closing advice of *The Joy of Movement*, which the author quotes in full (Russian translation) and says she wants to read aloud "like a poem". Its framing: "Общей формулы не существует; нет единого пути или метода. Просто делайте то, что приносит радость." (There is no general formula; there is no single path or method. Just do what brings joy.) Then the advice, item by item [#360]:
1. Move. "Любое движение в любом количестве сделает вас счастливыми." (Any movement in any amount will make you happy.)
2. Move the parts of your body that can move, and be grateful that you can.
3. Move alone and in company.
4. Move at home and in nature.
5. Move to music and in silence.
6. Set goals that matter to you personally.
7. Go toward the goal in small steps, then take on serious challenges.
8. Seek new experiences, expand the boundaries of your personality.
9. Pay attention to sensations — how do particular exercises affect you, how do they change you?
10. Listen to your body.
11. Allow yourself to do what brings pleasure.
12. Look for metaphors and meanings.
13. Seek people, places and communities that inspire you and make you feel at home.
14. Follow the guiding star of joy and do not leave that path for as long as you can.

### Detailed insights

#### Movement as a brain "rewiring", and biophilia
The author describes *The Joy of Movement* as an attempt to answer why physical activity makes us happier, but "in fact a real manifesto of movement": movement doesn't just "pump" endorphins, it rewires the brain — treats anxiety and depression, trains the capacity to feel joy, helps feel the taste of life, "especially if you leave the room" (she crosses out "make the mistake of" leaving the room as a joke) [#360].

The most interesting discovery for her: her pull towards forest walks and "talking to squirrels and birds" has a scientific name — biophilia (`Биофилия — любовь ко всему живому` — love for all living things). McGonigal collected dozens of studies showing that people who are regularly outdoors rate themselves as more energetic and satisfied, with more meaning in life and less despair. The author says she really feels "high" during a walk [#360].

#### The author's own result from reading
The book produced the kind of resolve the author last felt ten years ago reading Allen Carr. Concretely: after eight months she finally reactivated ClassPass and went to her first group yoga class, and plans to continue. She challenges readers: if after this book you don't want to put on sneakers, come back and tell her — she finds it hard to believe [#360]. The book's cover gives its full subtitle: "how physical activity helps find happiness, meaning, self-confidence and overcome difficulties" [#361].

#### Murakami: running, goals and conscious ageing
The author places Murakami's *What I Talk About When I Talk About Running* in the same category as McGonigal's book — an easy way to start moving: a book "of incredible simplicity, sincerity and depth" after which you want to love movement [#454].
- Biographical lesson. Murakami became a writer only at 33 and before that owned a bar; he believes this happened exclusively thanks to running [#454].
- What the book is. An incomplete autobiography which Murakami himself calls "зарисовками о беге, но никак не секретами здорового образа жизни" (sketches about running, but by no means secrets of a healthy lifestyle). The author's reading: whoever knows how to read will find the latter there too, and also a tribute to conscious ageing [#454].
- Goal over metrics. Murakami quote chosen by the author: time, other metrics and appearance are inessential and secondary; the most important thing is reaching the goal he set himself, running to it on his own legs, and then realising that what he had to do he did, and what he had to endure he endured [#454].
- Radical routine change. After selling the bar and becoming a writer, the first thing he and his wife did was radically change their daily routine: go to bed as soon as it gets dark and get up with the sun — it felt natural, how "self-respecting people" should live. Second, they decided to meet only people they enjoy meeting and, where possible, avoid unpleasant ones [#454].
- Need for a routine. Life must have a routine by which you spend time and energy; if by a certain age you have not developed one, your life will soon lose clarity and contrast [#455].
- Unfairness. Life is unfair — an indisputable fact; but in almost every "unfair" situation one can find some element of fairness. It will probably take time and effort — and it is equally likely that the time and effort will be wasted [#456].
- Ageing as a first experience. For him, as for everyone, life is the first and only experience of ageing, so everything he feels about it, he feels for the first time [#457].
- Independence. Emotional pain is the price we pay for our independence [#458].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The health-risk percentages for 7,000 steps (47%, 38%, 22%, 14%) are reported by the author from the meta-analysis; the source gives no confidence intervals or population details beyond 57 studies and 160,000+ participants [#371].
- The zero-zone benefits (recovery, mental state, sleep, sugar regulation, heart risk) are stated with reference to press reviews (The Conversation, The Guardian), not specific studies [#393].
- The claim that standing and bending after meals stabilises blood sugar is presented as the author's own knowledge ("знаю"), without a cited source [#393].
- Zero zone is explicitly not a substitute for strength and interval training when the goals are strength, speed or volume [#393].
- The DMN mechanism and park-diary word analysis come from McGonigal's book as retold by the author; the original studies are not named [#360].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the author's "hacks" for making herself train (post on "fitness independence") are referenced but not contained in this topic's source [#373].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the OCR of the book cover contains garbled fragments ("Алоко Хатчинсок!", "*Endure") that cannot be reliably read [#361].
