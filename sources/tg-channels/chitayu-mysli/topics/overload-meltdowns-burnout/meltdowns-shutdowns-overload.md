# Meltdowns, shutdowns and acute overload

## What this covers

This topic gathers the author's posts on the acute end of neurodivergent overload: what a meltdown and a shutdown are, how they look in adults (often unlike the "classic" child picture), why acute stress lasts longer in autistic, ADHD and traumatised people, and the "empty head" state. It also contains her practical prevention kits — for going to a new place, for emotional overload from films/books, and for airports and flights. According to the author, recognising these states matters because what relatives call "hysterics", laziness or weakness is often an overload reaction with neurophysiological causes [msg #84, #688, #689].

## Core ideas

- **Overload resolves either as a meltdown or a shutdown.** When a person with ASD gets overloaded (sensory, emotional, by the number of people; the source leaves this list open-ended), it can pour out as a breakdown (meltdown) or a stupor (shutdown) [msg #281].
- **Adults, especially women with high intelligence, look different.** Meltdowns and shutdowns in adults — especially level-1 support and broader phenotype — can differ greatly from how they "classically" look in children [msg #84, #281].
- **Neurodivergent nervous systems digest acute stress more slowly** and need different recovery tempos; this is not weakness or manipulation [msg #688].
- **An empty head is a signal, not a defect** — "Это не надо чинить, это надо заметить" (*This doesn't need fixing, it needs noticing*) [msg #689].
- **Unpredictability is a core trigger**, which is why previewing places, knowing spoilers and preparing scenarios help [msg #221, #337].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### What a meltdown is and why it happens
1. **Definition.** A meltdown is basically a "срыв" (breakdown) in autism spectrum disorder [msg #84].
2. **Mechanism (author's explanation).** In ASD, brain networks are, in her words, much smaller and less well differentiated than in other people, so arousal is often more active and chaotic. Added to this are heightened sensitivity to certain sensory stimuli and fast fatigue from emotions, other people, communication and similar. All this causes a strong overload of the nervous system and leads to the breakdown [msg #84].
3. **How it looks.** The "classic" image shown of autism — children who hit themselves or others, scream and cannot stop — are indeed meltdown manifestations, but meltdowns can be far less obvious, "Особенно у взрослых, особенно с сохранным и высоким интеллектом, особенно у женщин" (*Especially in adults, especially with preserved and high intelligence, especially in women*) [msg #84].
4. **How to detect it in practice.** The author says one often has to ask directly "а у вас бывают истерики"? (*do you have tantrums/hysterics?*). She hates the term but everyone understands it in everyday terms. What relatives call hysterics may be a meltdown: outbursts of irritability, aggression, crying, self-harm, throwing things. Her conclusion: "человек не "истеричка", а просто у него перегрузки" (*the person isn't a "hysteric", they simply have overloads*) [msg #84].

### Non-obvious signs of a shutdown
Purpose: recognise a shutdown in adults when it doesn't look like the textbook description [msg #281].
- **Textbook definition:** freezing, or an unbearable desire/attempt to leave the current uncomfortable situation. The author says life is a bit more complex [msg #281].
- **Frozen-phone analogy:** imagine your phone has hung — it either opens the app you need very slowly, or opens some things and not others, or hangs on one screen entirely. The same happens to a person with ASD: in reality it looks and feels like **loss of part of one's functions** [msg #281].
- **Concrete signs:** (1) you want to say something and physically cannot; (2) someone speaks to you, you hear them but don't understand what is said, or don't understand the text in front of your eyes; (3) a persistent desire to turn around and leave, up to pronounced physical discomfort [msg #281].
- **Author's personal trigger:** it happens to her every time she stands before a person behind a counter or "glass", especially at control points or borders — and regardless of the language spoken; "на русском я буду тупить так же" (*in Russian I'll be just as slow*) [msg #281].

### Preparing to go to a new place (hypercontrol kit)
Problem: people with ASD often happily go to a place they like and know well, but a new place can cause a real stupor, up to being unable to leave the house at all. The author's counterintuitive remedy is "гиперконтроль" (*hypercontrol*) [msg #221].
1. **Preview the place on maps.** Look at how it looks on Google Maps, view pictures or street photos; with Yandex or Google Maps you can view practically the whole route as panorama. This can lower anxiety and tension from meeting the new [msg #221].
2. **Read/watch reviews** of the place online — especially good if it is a video review [msg #221].
3. **Carry a touchable safety object** — something you associate with safety: a keychain, fidget, toy, anything; use it when you feel discomfort [msg #221].
4. **Pre-play alternative scenarios.** If you fear things will go off-plan, disappoint you and lead to a shutdown/meltdown, think through different variants of events and prepare "a little" for each; this usually reduces the frustration from the mismatch with reality [msg #221].

### Acute stress in neurodivergent people
**What acute stress is (author):** not just "worried and let go" but an inner siren that suddenly switches on and drowns out everything — body, attention, ability to think, sometimes literally. It is a physiological reaction to a threat (or what the brain judged a threat): the sympathetic nervous system starts, hormones are released, then "бей, беги, замри" (*fight, flee, freeze*) — or go silent, repent, or blame; it differs between people [msg #688].

**What changes with neurodivergence (4 items):**
1. Autism: the ability for fast sensory adaptation is often impaired → the brain stays overloaded longer [msg #688].
2. ADHD: the self-regulation system can be "дырявой" (*leaky*) → harder to bring oneself back to balance [msg #688].
3. Chronic traumatisation (including complex PTSD): acute stress almost always activates the past rather than the present [msg #688].
4. Hypersensitivity to stimuli: the depth and duration of the reaction increase [msg #688].

**How it can look (5 items):** loss of speech for 10 minutes; trembling, tears, a feeling of "no air"; feeling that you have "fallen apart"; obsessive rumination over the situation; inability to return to tasks even the next day [msg #688].

**Framing:** it is not weakness and not "overacting" or manipulating — it is pronounced stress that a neurodivergent organism cannot digest as fast as a neurotypical one [msg #688].

**What can help (5 items):**
1. Remove stimuli — light, noise, speech, people [msg #688].
2. Anchor yourself in the body [msg #688].
3. Feel something simple and real [msg #688].
4. If you have strength, name it aloud: "я сейчас в реакции стресса" (*I'm in a stress reaction right now*) [msg #688].
5. If not, simply let yourself lie down and recover, without demands or explanations [msg #688].

The author's stance: neurodivergent people don't have to "cope faster"; they truly live load differently and truly need different recovery tempos [msg #688].

### Four causes of an empty head and what helps
**The state:** sometimes the author and many neurodivergent people have literally nothing in their heads — no thoughts, ideas, directions; "Как будто внутренний экран погас" (*As if the inner screen went dark*). No access to resources, creativity, focus — even to desires and needs. She says it is much more common in ASD and ADHD than assumed and has neurophysiological causes; it is not "bad mood", weak character or laziness [msg #689].

**What may be behind it:**
1. **Sensory and emotional overload** — after difficult events, communication, trips, decisions the brain goes into a protective "energy-saving mode" [msg #689].
2. **Task switching in ADHD** — a context change can leave a "hang" where the old task is done and the new one isn't loaded; "Это не прокрастинация — это реальный «глюк переключения»" (*This isn't procrastination — it's a real "switching glitch"*) [msg #689].
3. **Neuro-vegetative rollback** — after long tension, the self-regulation system can't immediately switch back on, like after an adrenaline wave [msg #689].
4. **Autistic burnout or shutdown** — after holding on "in a mask" for a long time, trying to be understandable, social, organised — and suddenly you can't do anything, even think [msg #689].

**Key rule:** notice it, don't fix it. Emptiness is sometimes the brain's way of saying: "я исчерпал ресурс. Мне нужен покой, а не новая цель." (*I've exhausted my resource. I need rest, not a new goal.*) [msg #689]

**What can help (4 items):**
1. Simple sensory stimuli — warmth, a scent, a texture, a favourite drink [msg #689].
2. Safe silence [msg #689].
3. Breathing — without trying to "catch your breath", just in the background [msg #689].
4. A notice to yourself: "это временно. это не про меня как личность. это просто пауза" (*this is temporary. it's not about me as a person. it's just a pause*) [msg #689].

The author adds that the neurodivergent brain often works in spurts, non-linearly, and "emptiness" is part of the process even if hard to accept [msg #689].

### Airport and flight meltdown kit (7 items)
Context: the author had a meltdown in Phuket airport — not her first airport meltdown but the first this strong — crying while standing in the check-in queue. She notes it is easier to know it's a meltdown than to think you're just a strange adult woman crying in a queue, but it still feels very vulnerable because you can't leave the queue. She shares that she has to apply the skills herself [msg #801].
1. **Headphones** — she never enters an airport without them; ideally in direct reach, e.g. hanging on the neck to put on instantly [msg #801].
2. **Comfortable clothing**, ideally with a big hood to pull over yourself as far as possible and narrow the visual field [msg #801].
3. **Something for stimming** to hold, also in direct reach — great if it can hang right on the backpack. This time she took nothing and regretted it [msg #801].
4. **Eye mask / dark glasses** — can help a lot, especially the mask once inside the plane [msg #801].
5. **Something to distract yourself** — e.g. writing a post (as she did), a book, a film [msg #801].
6. **Food** — if you know a food that calms you, take it in advance or find it at the airport; she asked her husband to buy her hot chocolate [msg #801].
7. **Maximum isolation on boarding** — combine everything above: hood on head, mask on eyes, headphones on top. She carries a super-soft, warm travel blanket everywhere and wraps herself in it first thing; "Таскать его бесит, но он помогает всегда!" (*Lugging it around is infuriating, but it always helps!*) [msg #801].

### Extra travel tips for unfamiliar places (4 items)
1. Look at the airport/station plan in advance [msg #801].
2. Delegate tracking the process and choosing queues to a companion, if you have one [msg #801].
3. Pack a bag/cosmetic pouch with all the kit items above straight away [msg #801].
4. Half-joking: don't fly through Phuket airport — seriously, in her experience airports in China were more organised and less crowded, and lacked the many, in her words, "мало вменяемых соотечественников, которые лезут везде без очереди" (*barely sane compatriots who cut in line everywhere*) [msg #801].

## Detailed insights

### Emotional overload from films, series and books
The author realised (after discussing it with her husband) where emotional overload from films/series/books comes from: they often contain unpredictable plot twists that you don't know even if you guess, and the reaction is to unpredictability — like a "sharp change of plans". That is why on a 2nd, 3rd or 4th viewing/reading the overload is gone, and also when you know the main plot spoilers; she sometimes uses this (not deliberately) when she has roughly understood what will happen in a book and then reads it calmly with pleasure. Her advice: if you worry and have ASD, "используйте маленькие спойлеры" (*use small spoilers*) [msg #337].
