# Building and using your sensory profile

## What this covers

How the channel author (an autistic psychologist writing about autism and ADHD, #РАС) explains the sensory profile: its three response types (hyper-, hypo-, mixed), the sensory systems it covers (including the less obvious interoception and temperature), a method for mapping your own profile, and concrete, system-by-system adaptations for interoception, vision, touch and smell. It also covers sensory seeking, mixed sensitivity, the extra costs of living with these features ("autism tax") and a reframing of so-called "VSD" symptoms. According to the author, the sensory profile is one of the B criteria for diagnosing ASD and strongly affects wellbeing and quality of life; knowing your own parameters lets you regulate them (e.g. in summer heat) instead of just suffering [msg #475, #198].

## Core ideas

- **A sensory profile is an individual map of hyper-, hypo- and mixed reactions** across all perception systems, not only light, smells and sounds. See *Sensory profile components* and *The seven sensory systems* [msg #475, #480].
- **Sensory features are near-universal in ASD**: the author cites research that over 90% of people with ASD have sensory particularities [msg #475, #673].
- **You can and should map your profile yourself** — observe for a week, use questionnaires, rate each system in a table, look for patterns, then optimize environment, planning and strategies (see *Four-step method*) [msg #480].
- **Hyposensitivity is as important as hypersensitivity.** It underlies sensory seeking, self-regulation and safety (not noticing pain, hunger, cold, injuries) and must be actively "topped up", not ignored [msg #669, #562].
- **Mixed sensitivity is the most common profile**, and it varies by system, within a modality and by state (overload → hyper, exhaustion → hypo); adaptation must therefore be individual, "by needs, not by diagnosis" [msg #673, #565].
- **Sensory features cost extra effort and money** — the "autism tax" [msg #507].
- **Many bodily "VSD" symptoms in autistic people are really manifestations of anxiety, overload, interoception problems or shutdown/meltdown**, requiring regulation work rather than "VSD treatment" [msg #702].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Sensory profile components (hyper / hypo / mixed)
The author defines the sensory profile as «индивидуальная схема восприятия сенсорных стимулов» (*an individual scheme of perceiving sensory stimuli*) made of three aspects [msg #475]:
1. **Hypersensitivity (гиперчувствительность)** — an acute reaction to stimuli that seem insignificant to most people, for example loud sounds or bright light [msg #475].
2. **Hyposensitivity (гипочувствительность)** — an insufficient reaction, because of which the person needs more stimulation to notice a stimulus [msg #475].
3. **Mixed reactions (смешанные реакции)** — a combination of hypo- and hypersensitivity depending on the type of stimulus (expanded in *Mixed sensitivity* below) [msg #475].

Sensory sensitivity shows up as heightened or lowered reaction to sounds, light, smells, textures and other stimuli; researchers study it to understand how it shapes behaviour, perception and quality of life in ASD [msg #475].

### The seven sensory systems of the profile
"Roughly speaking", the author says, the sensory profile consists of our perception systems [msg #480]:
1. **Visual system** — light, colours, visual movement.
2. **Auditory system** — loudness of sounds, tonality.
3. **Touch (осязание)** — textures, temperature, touch.
4. **Smell and taste** — odours and taste sensations.
5. **Proprioception** — the sense of your body in space.
6. **Vestibular system** — balance, the sense of movement.
7. **Interoception** — sensations of your own body: hunger, thirst, fatigue. [msg #480]

Two refinements from other posts: **temperature sensitivity** is, strictly speaking, one of the sensory systems in its own right; it can be lowered or heightened, and it can concern heat, cold or both [msg #198]. And **touch** usually includes pain and pressure, since all those receptors sit in the skin — but a person can have different sensitivity levels for touch, pain and pressure separately [msg #560].

### Four-step method to compile your sensory profile
Purpose: to understand your own features system by system; the author proposed reading the principles, then running a "week" of different sensory systems with observations shared in comments, and recommends making yourself a table and studying each system separately [msg #480].
1. **Observe yourself for a week.** Notice which stimuli cause discomfort and which, on the contrary, bring satisfaction. Example questions: Which sounds are pleasant to you and which irritate you? Do you like soft or hard textures in clothing? How do you react to bright light or dim lighting? Write the observations in a diary so that patterns stand out [msg #480].
2. **Use ready-made questionnaires.** Use special questionnaires for assessing the sensory profile, e.g. the *Sensory Profile Questionnaire* (W. Dunn), and questionnaires adapted for children and adults with ASD [msg #480].
3. **Rate each sensory area.** Create a table with categories (vision, hearing, touch and the other systems listed above — the source writes «и т.д.») and mark how strongly each stimulus evokes positive or negative emotions in you [msg #480].
4. **Look for patterns.** Once the table is done, note which stimuli most often cause you inconvenience. These can be zones of hypersensitivity (loud sounds, strong smells) or zones of hyposensitivity (you barely react to certain stimuli, for example cold) [msg #480].

### Three ways to use sensory-profile data
What to do with the results [msg #480]:
1. **Optimize the environment** — create a comfortable space, e.g. use dimmed lighting or noise-cancelling headphones.
2. **Plan activities** — take sensory preferences into account when planning the day, e.g. avoid crowded places if you are hypersensitive to noise.
3. **Sensory strategies** — develop self-regulation methods such as breathing exercises, sensory toys or rest breaks. [msg #480]

### Three effective support strategies (from the research overview)
Because understanding a person's sensory profile is important for individualized therapy and support, the author lists effective strategies [msg #475]:
1. **Sensory diets (сенсорные диеты)** — selecting specific sensory activities to improve self-regulation.
2. **Providing sensory safety** — creating a comfortable environment, e.g. noise-cancelling headphones.
3. **Sensory integration therapy** — working with an occupational therapist (эрготерапевт) to improve processing of sensory stimuli. [msg #475]

### Interoception — signs and adaptations
The author chose to start the self-study week with interoception as "the most difficult and unclear" system, "because it affects the receptivity of everything else". Definition: «Это понимание сигналов своего тела, эмоций и ощущений» (*It is understanding the signals of your body, emotions and sensations*) [msg #482].

**Signs of interoceptive HYPERsensitivity** [msg #482]:
1. You feel hunger and fatigue too vividly.
2. Any bodily discomfort is "hell" and is felt very sharply right away.

**Signs of interoceptive HYPOsensitivity** [msg #482]:
1. You poorly understand what you feel and what your body feels.
2. Fatigue, hunger and the urge to use the toilet seem to appear "suddenly" — when you can no longer hold out, or when you already feel marked discomfort and/or pain.
3. You need more time and/or information to understand your sensations.

Author's own example: she has interoceptive hyposensitivity — she can go a long time without eating until she starts to feel slightly nauseous or her stomach hurts, and does not realize she is tired until she gets a headache [msg #482].

**What to do with HYPERsensitivity** [msg #493]:
1. Use extra communication and sometimes warn people in advance that it is hard for you to tolerate hunger, cold and similar states.
2. Create visual prompts for yourself reminding you that if something feels very uncomfortable, you are not dying — it is just your body's heightened sensitivity.
3. Where possible, reduce the level of discomfort: always carry snacks, find out where the toilets are along your route.
4. Plan frequent breaks to recover.
5. Learn self-regulation skills. [msg #493]

**What to do with HYPOsensitivity** [msg #493]:
1. Also plan breaks — forced ones, "even if you don't want to, even if you aren't tired".
2. Schedule meals by timetable and alarm clock, even small portions and even if you are not hungry.
3. Use mindfulness skills to increase contact with and understanding of your sensations "at least a little".
4. Think through ways to avoid dehydration. [msg #493]

### Vision — signs and adaptations
The author notes that people most often complain about bright light, especially white, but visual features have many more manifestations [msg #499].

**Signs of visual HYPERsensitivity** [msg #499]:
1. Light irritates — especially white, especially bright, especially in the evening.
2. It is much easier to focus on details.
3. Lots of different patterns, textures, details and colours around you irritate.
4. Bright light can cause up to a feeling of pain in the eye.
5. Rapid fatigue in the bright time of day and/or in summer.

**Signs of visual HYPOsensitivity** [msg #499]:
1. Distance to objects is not read very well.
2. It is hard to search for objects; you don't notice them.
3. Difficulty describing differences between pictures and objects.
4. Low lighting irritates.
5. Rapid fatigue in the dark.

**What to do with visual HYPOsensitivity** [msg #503]:
1. Put all objects — especially those you need — in visible places; deliberately design a storage system.
2. Look at how you light your space, especially the workplace: use several light sources, possibly differing in intensity and colour temperature.
3. Again: a storage system — less clutter makes it easier to find things.
4. Use brighter colours around you; "don't be afraid to stimulate yourself with this".
5. You need large bright windows, plus extra walks outside — "никаких темных плотных штор!" (*no dark heavy curtains!*). [msg #503]

**What to do with visual HYPERsensitivity** [msg #503]:
1. Reduce lighting brightness, especially white and/or fluorescent light.
2. Minimalism — both in interior (the author calls a beige interior "top") and in the number of things lying in open space.
3. Fewer patterns, different textures and colours around you.
4. Instead of overhead lighting — soft, warm, low lighting from several sources, so you can combine them for different needs.
5. Blackout curtains are the best choice.
6. Sunglasses: buy several pairs with different tint levels for different situations. «Ходить в метро в очках - нормально!» (*Wearing glasses in the metro is normal!*). Stash them in all your bags if you tend to forget them. People who wear prescription glasses can order photochromic ("chameleon") lenses. [msg #503]

### Touch — signs and adaptations
Touch usually includes pain and pressure (all skin receptors), but sensitivity to each can differ in one person [msg #560].

**Signs of tactile HYPOsensitivity** [msg #560]:
1. High pain threshold.
2. Preference for tight, close-fitting clothing.
3. Dense plaids, heavy blankets, several blankets have a good effect.
4. Preference for lots of strong hugs and contact with close people in general.
5. Liking to touch things and to contact different textures.
6. Liking massage and contact interactions.

**Signs of tactile HYPERsensitivity** [msg #560]:
1. All tactile sensations irritate (combing hair, long hair, hairstyles).
2. Only certain textures and fabrics can be worn.
3. Clothing tags irritate.
4. Hard to wear anything that presses, e.g. tights.
5. Water can be irritating.
6. Contact with many textures is tiring.
7. Low pain threshold.
8. Everything heavy is tiring.
9. Wanting to avoid unnecessary contact with other people.

The author describes herself as apparently mixed in touch: high pain threshold and love of everything heavy (hypo), but tags and various textures are "just hell" (hyper) [msg #560].

**What to do** — the author first reminds that this must be looked at individually, especially with mixed manifestations [msg #565].

With tactile HYPOsensitivity [msg #565]:
1. Load yourself with different textures, especially ones you like; assemble a little box of different pleasant toys.
2. Find activities/hobbies where you touch something pleasant.
3. Buy a weighted blanket, a weighted scarf ("yes, these exist too"), or simply wrap yourself tighter in a plaid.
4. Ask partners, close people and friends to make tactile contact with you more often and to hug you tightly.
5. Go for massages and to activities where you feel your body; you can train with wrist/ankle weights.
6. Choose denser, close-fitting clothing.

With tactile HYPERsensitivity [msg #565]:
1. No wool, synthetics or prickly materials.
2. No tags or pronounced seams.
3. If hair and hairstyles irritate, cut your hair shorter, to the length at which it stops irritating.
4. Look for suitable seamless underwear.
5. Look for suitable bed linen.
6. Try to have less contact with textures you find unpleasant.
7. Choose light oversized clothing.
8. Pick a light, pleasant blanket and pillow.
9. Stock up on clothes you are definitely comfortable in, in several copies.
10. Audit everything you touch that might make you avoid things or get tired. [msg #565]

The author's own must-haves (a mixed profile): for hypersensitivity — giving up towels in favour of a pleasant, wide waffle bathrobe, only disposable cotton face wipes, specific bed linen, no tags; her curly hair causes the most trouble and she has not yet found an ideal solution. For hyposensitivity — she adores clay, has stocked up on hand putties («жвачками для рук», *hand gums*), looks for soft-touch items, touches everything, sleeps under a heavy blanket or two, and carries her "legendary" travel plaid for flights. She stopped wearing skinny jeans entirely; she is sometimes drawn to body-hugging dresses but never wears them for long during the day [msg #565].

### Five ways to protect yourself from smells
For olfactory hypersensitivity in ASD, prompted by a "life hack" video the author came across (she sets aside its deodorant part) [msg #302]:
1. **Vaseline under the nose** — the most accessible and simple method. Buy vaseline and apply it under the nose before going out or whenever something stinks; it protects from odours. If you have a favourite scent you are willing to smell all the time, do as in the video: melt the vaseline and drip in essential oil or perfume.
2. **A mask** — a mask also removes part of the smells.
3. **Scent beads on the mask** — as an add-on, you can buy scented capsules on AliExpress (like those in cigarettes) and crush one on the mask before putting it on.
4. **Pathologists' ointment** — "elusive, but said to be great"; applied under the nose the same way. (!) UNCLEAR: the source does not name the product.
5. **Little jars of perfume / aromatic oils** you like, to carry with you. [msg #302]

Rule: for "super-protection" — first apply something under the nose, then put a mask on top [msg #302].

### Sensory seeking — examples and five reasons it matters
Definition: «**Сенсорный сик** (от англ. sensory seeking) — это активный поиск определённых ощущений» (*Sensory seeking is the active search for particular sensations*). A person with ASD may literally crave sounds, touch, movement, smells in order to feel alive, engaged, collected. «Это не каприз и не привычка — это нейрофизиологическая потребность нервной системы» (*It is not a whim or a habit — it is a neurophysiological need of the nervous system*). The author notes that hypersensitivity ("too loud", "bright light hurts", "can't stand touch") is what people usually hear about, while hyposensitivity and seeking stay in the shadow — wrongly [msg #669].

Examples of sensory seeking [msg #669]:
1. Rocking, spinning, jumping.
2. Seeking tactile sensations: stroking, fidgeting with, feeling things.
3. Listening to loud music, repetitive sounds.
4. Chewing inedible things, pressure on the jaw.
5. Strong hugs or hitting the body.
6. All the examples already discussed under lowered sensitivity (see the hypo lists above).

Why hyposensitivity and seeking matter [msg #669]:
1. **The basis of self-regulation.** Without the needed stimuli a person may be in a "sensory pit" (сенсорная яма): sluggish, irritable, unable to start an activity. Seeking helps them switch on and pull themselves together.
2. **It affects behaviour.** Much "incomprehensible behaviour" is an attempt to get needed sensations; ignoring it means fighting symptoms rather than the cause.
3. **A safety issue.** With hyposensitivity a person may not feel pain, hunger or cold and may not notice injuries.
4. **Key to adapting the environment.** Understanding what a person seeks lets you organize sensory breaks, use sensory stimuli as motivation, and create a suitable space for rest and engagement.
5. **Improves quality of life.** Seeking can and should be taken into account in education, therapy and daily life; this improves wellbeing, reduces anxiety, helps focus and "be yourself". [msg #669]

Takeaway: «Гипочувствительность — не «ничего страшного». Она так же значима, как и гиперчувствительность.» (*Hyposensitivity is not "nothing to worry about". It is just as significant as hypersensitivity.*) and sensory seeking is "not an oddity but an important path to balance and comfort" [msg #669].

### Mixed sensitivity — three forms, why it matters, how to support yourself
Many people think a person with ASD is either hypersensitive or hyposensitive, but in practice the picture is more complex and for most autistic people it is mixed [msg #673].

Three forms of mixed sensitivity in one person [msg #673]:
1. **Across different systems** — e.g. hypersensitivity to sounds and hyposensitivity to pain.
2. **Within the same modality** — e.g. panic at loud sounds yet not noticing quiet speech or rustling.
3. **Depending on context and state** — in overload a person may become more hypersensitive, and when exhausted, on the contrary, drop into the "sensory pit".

Hearing example: a reaction of horror or pain to a vacuum cleaner, siren or slammed door (hyper), and at the same time not noticing being called from across the room, or a habit of always playing loud music (hypo). «*Это не противоречие* — это отражение нестабильной нейросенсорной обработки, когда мозг то перегружается, то «проваливается».» (*This is not a contradiction — it reflects unstable neurosensory processing, when the brain now overloads, now "falls through".*) [msg #673]

Why it matters [msg #673]:
1. You cannot draw conclusions from a single reaction: if a person doesn't react to your voice, it doesn't mean they don't suffer from noise on public transport.
2. Adaptation must be flexible and personalized, especially in schools, clinics and offices.
3. It explains behavioural "paradoxes" — when the same stimulus at different times provokes opposite reactions.

How to support yourself [msg #673]:
1. Keep a sensory diary — note reactions and changes.
2. Make sensory adaptations according to needs, not according to the diagnosis.
3. Learn self-regulation skills (breathing, grounding, sensory breaks).
4. Use sensory profiling that accounts for both hyper- and hypo-fragments.

Closing line: «Смешанная чувствительность — это не "ошибка системы", а её сложность.» (*Mixed sensitivity is not a "system error" but its complexity.*) The deeper we understand this, the fewer "oddities" and the more compassion — especially toward oneself [msg #673].

### Four sources of "VSD"-like autonomic symptoms
Answering a reader's question, the author states that VSD (vegetative-vascular dystonia) is an outdated, unscientific diagnosis not used in international classifications. Autistic people do not have a separate "VSD" condition, but may have autonomic symptoms linked to [msg #702]:
1. Sensory overload.
2. Anxiety and panic attacks.
3. Interoception difficulties (reading body signals).
4. Dysregulation of the autonomic nervous system (especially heightened sympathetic activity).

What gets called "VSD" — tachycardia, tremor, dizziness, a feeling of not getting enough air — is in fact often a bodily manifestation of anxiety, overload or shutdown/meltdown within autism. This calls not for "treating VSD" but for work on regulation, the sensory environment and psycho-emotional support [msg #702].

## Detailed insights

### What research says about the sensory profile (author's overview)
The author's research summary, in four points [msg #475]:
1. **Neurophysiological mechanisms.** Sensory disturbances in children with ASD may be one of the main problems hindering their social adaptation and integration; studies show deviations in the filtering and processing of sensory information in children with ASD.
2. **Impact on daily life.** Sensory features often lead to difficulties in social interaction, learning and adapting to the environment. Examples: hypersensitivity to sounds can cause fear of public places; hyposensitivity to pain — increased risk of injury. Understanding the profile is important for individualized therapy and support.
3. **Age-related changes.** The sensory profile can change with age. In children with ASD sensory features are more often more pronounced than in adults, but in some people they persist for life. Early identification and correction can improve adaptation and quality of life.
4. **Gender differences.** Studies show sensory features may differ in men and women with ASD; women more often show hyposensitivity and compensatory strategies in daily life, which underlines the need to account for gender in therapy approaches. [msg #475]

The author frames sensory features in ASD as «не просто симптомы, а важная часть индивидуальности человека» (*not just symptoms but an important part of the person's individuality*); deeper study of the profile helps understand the inner world of autistic people and build a more inclusive environment [msg #475].

### Temperature sensitivity and heat
Everyone knows heatstroke rules (hats, drink more water), but bodies of neurodivergent people can react differently to heat, because temperature sensitivity is itself a sensory system that can be lowered or raised, and can concern heat, cold or both. The author tolerates both heat and cold badly, heat worse: after a day outdoors in the shade at 38 °C on holiday she felt "as if I had been unloading train wagons" rather than doing nothing at the sea, and spent the next day in her room sleeping until evening. Conclusion: it is very important to know your sensory profile parameters not only for light, smells and sounds but also your reaction to temperature, so you can regulate it, especially in summer [msg #198].

### Weighted blankets and tactile "topping up"
With lowered tactile sensitivity the sense needs extra loading (донагружать): tighter clothing, heavy blankets, wrapping tightly in a plaid. The author adds a mechanism: increased tactile stimulation can activate our safety system, so these methods help lower the sense of threat and relax. Pressure-touch is read by the brain as hugs, releasing oxytocin and other hormones that help the body understand that it is safe [msg #562].

Her experience as a case lesson [msg #562]:
- She always sleeps under a blanket, even in summer (a sheet alone feels cold and makes relaxing impossible), often under two blankets just for more weight.
- **Mistake: too heavy.** Years ago she and her husband bought a double weighted blanket of 13 kg. She liked sleeping under it, but it was objectively very heavy — sometimes it felt like it was "flattening" her — and very hard to move on and off the bed without her husband's help. It ended up unused in a basket by the bed.
- **Second attempt: 6 kg.** Because anxiety and the need for tactile loading remained, she tried a 6 kg single weighted blanket — "a much more pleasant weight" under which she really relaxed, slept more deeply, and had fewer nightmares lately. She valued its smooth Tencel fabric, which does not irritate the skin; the combination of pleasant fabric and weight gives a hug effect that adds to relaxation.
- **Construction claim (from a sponsored post):** the brand says its blanket has 11 layers versus 5–7 in cheap analogues, which distributes the weighting filler as evenly as possible so it does not roll into a corner, so the blanket presses evenly and gently on the whole body. This is advertiser material, not independent evidence.
- **Who it's for, per the author:** people with hyposensitivity to touch/weight, high anxiety, stress and trouble relaxing; she "highly recommends" the practice of sleeping under a weighted blanket.

### The "autism tax" (налог на РАС)
By analogy with the term "ADHD tax", the author argues ASD also involves extra costs in both energy and money. Her example: she needs sport, but training alone is unrealistic (ADHD), group classes are very hard — many people, fast fatigue and overload, arms and legs get mixed up (she repeatedly does an exercise wrong because she can't work out "arm one way, leg the other"), little benefit, plus the stress of being touched by a near-stranger. So she can only train individually, with a person vetted over time. Other items in the "autism tax": buying only certain clothes, searching for suitable earplugs, and suitable food. (Context: she was training despite a week of migraine and overwork because "behavioural activation won't do itself".) [msg #507]

## Evidence and numbers

- Over 90% of people with ASD have certain sensory features (research cited by the author; "up to 90%" in the later post) [msg #475, #673].
- About 45–65% of autistic adults describe having hypo- and hypersensitivity simultaneously (Robertson & Simmons, 2015; Tavassoli et al., 2014, as cited by the author). Mixed sensitivity most often appears in the auditory, tactile and vestibular modalities; during overload the hyper part dominates, during exhaustion the hypo part [msg #673].
- Weighted blanket weights from the author's experience: 13 kg double — too heavy; 6 kg — comfortable [msg #562].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- (!) CONTRADICTION (minor): #475 says "more than 90%" of people with ASD have sensory features; #673 says "up to 90%" [msg #475, #673].
- (!) UNCLEAR: #475 says women more often show hyposensitivity and compensatory strategies; the specific studies are not named [msg #475].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the "pathologists' ointment" for smells is not named, and the author marks it as hearsay ("говорят классная" — *they say it's great*) [msg #302].
- The weighted-blanket post (#562) is a paid advertisement (the ad label is visible on the accompanying photo); the 11-layer construction claim comes from the brand. The author's personal experience (13 kg too heavy, 6 kg comfortable) is anecdotal [msg #562].

- (!) INCOMPLETE: the self-study week covered interoception, vision and touch (and smell protection in an earlier post); auditory, proprioceptive and vestibular adaptations are not covered in these messages [msg #480].
