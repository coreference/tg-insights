# ADHD and autism in women: phenotype, masking and hormones

## What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel taught about why neurodivergence (ADHD and autism, Russian СДВГ and РАС) in women is "invisible": the myths clinicians repeat about overdiagnosis, the large DIVA-5 study on sex differences in adult ADHD, how masked autistic women hide rigid rules even from themselves, and how female hormones (estrogen across the cycle and after menopause) change ADHD symptoms and medication effects. According to the author, it matters because women with ADHD are under-assessed and under-estimated, and late diagnosis means years of misunderstanding, anxiety, burnout and guilt, while a diagnosis helps a person understand what is happening, reduce shame and accept their traits [msg #46, #618].

## Core ideas

- **Rising diagnosis is not overdiagnosis.** The author argues that given prevalence data and the lifelong nature of neurodevelopmental differences, many people remain undiagnosed, so the growth in diagnoses reflects need, not fashion [msg #46].
- **Women are missed because their symptoms are quiet and masked.** In childhood girls show less visible, socially acceptable symptoms; in adulthood women confirm as many or more symptoms than men but hide them behind lists, schedules and perfectionism, at the cost of burnout and low self-esteem [msg #618].
- **Masking hides rigid rules even from the person.** In adult women with masking, autism criterion B (rigid rules) is among the hardest to assess, because the rules feel simply "correct" [msg #288].
- **Hormones modulate ADHD in women.** Estrogen affects dopamine neurons, so ADHD symptoms and stimulant response fluctuate across the menstrual cycle and worsen after menopause [msg #118, #706].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Five main conclusions of the DIVA-5 sex-differences study
Source: a study (Frontiers in Global Women's Health, 2025) summarised in a post forwarded from the NO STIGMA channel, titled «Успешная, но истощённая: как женщины маскируют СДВГ» (*"Successful but exhausted: how women mask ADHD"*). It examined how adult men and women with ADHD describe their symptoms and difficulties using the DIVA-5 diagnostic interview. Sample: 2,257 adult patients in the Netherlands undergoing ADHD diagnosis in real clinical settings; 61% women. Method: statistical analysis of how often symptoms and the life examples offered in DIVA-5 were endorsed, focusing on sex differences. The authors did not just count "who has more symptoms"; they analysed in detail (a) which symptoms patients confirm in childhood and adulthood, (b) which life examples resonate with them (e.g. "I lose keys", "I interrupt the other person"), and (c) how ADHD interferes with life — work, family, leisure, self-esteem — with particular attention to male–female differences [msg #618].

1. **Women endorsed more adult symptoms.** Women confirmed adult ADHD symptoms more often than men, especially (inner) hyperactivity — "feels restless", "talks too much" — and inattention — "loses things", "easily distracted". The summary calls this unexpected, since such symptoms (hyperactivity and impulsivity) were traditionally thought to be stronger in men. Per the post, this refutes the myth of "milder ADHD in women" and shows women can have marked symptoms while using masking strategies [msg #618].
2. **In childhood, symptoms were more often reported by men.** Men more often confirmed childhood symptoms, especially those visible to teachers: "leaves seat", "interrupts", "disrupts order" — hyperactivity and conduct problems. Girls could have symptoms, but they were less visible and socially acceptable, so they were often ignored and diagnosis was missed in childhood [msg #618].
3. **Women suffered more from self-esteem problems and perfectionism.** Self-esteem problems: 89% of women vs 81% of men in adulthood; 77% of women vs 64% of men in childhood. Women report more inner distress, anxiety and a sense of "not measuring up"; the post links this to social expectations to "be perfect" / of the "ideal woman", which conflict with the nature of ADHD [msg #618].
4. **Many women mask symptoms through compensation.** Frequently endorsed female answers: "strictly uses lists", "cannot relax even on weekends", "tired of constant busyness". This reflects perfectionism and anxiety rather than overt impulsivity — women hide symptoms behind hyper-organisation (lists, clear schedules, perfectionism). These strategies conceal the real difficulties but often lead to burnout [msg #618].
5. **DIVA-5 does not always reflect the female experience.** Some examples (about fights, trouble with the law) do not fit women and are almost never endorsed by them. The authors recommend revising the instrument to account for female presentations of ADHD [msg #618].

### Examples of hidden rigid rules in level-1 autism (criterion B)
Purpose: helps recognise rigid rules — part of DSM criterion B — when diagnosing autism (support level 1) in adults, especially masked women. The author considers this one of the hardest criteria: people build their routine so well, making themselves many super-small rules, that they don't notice the rules are rigid — «Ну правильно же так, в чём проблема» (*"Well, that's the right way, what's the problem"*). Such rules are very hard to recall on the spot in an interview, especially if you don't live with people who notice these "oddities" [msg #288].

The author's list of what such rules can look like:
1. Dishes lie in a specific order — e.g. the author is irked if a big plate is placed on a small one, or a glass is not in its place [msg #288].
2. Getting dressed in a specific order [msg #288].
3. Greeting every person you see [msg #288].
4. Always reading to the end whatever you started [msg #288].
5. Arranging objects this way and no other [msg #288].
6. Drinking tea only from one mug and coffee only from another (the source ends this item with «и т.д.», marking it as one pattern among many similar ones) [msg #288].
7. (Author's personal example from a trip) cash must ALWAYS be arranged in ascending order of denomination, and if it isn't, it must be rearranged immediately; she recalled it had always been so, even in childhood when she took money from her parents' wallets and played with it [msg #288].

Key qualification: there can be «миллиард миллионов» (*"a billion million"*) such small rules whose violation causes discomfort, without the person thinking about them. What makes them *rigid* is that they are too strict — they persist regardless of context. Example: you are visiting someone and cannot drink coffee from the same cup you drank tea from the day before (and this bothers you) [msg #288]. The childhood recollection matters for the criterion because it shows the pattern has always been present [msg #288].

## Detailed insights

### Myths about neurodivergence diagnosis and why they are wrong
In a forwarded DocPsyFest announcement for her talk «Невидимые: женские проявления СДВГ и РАС» (*"Invisible: female presentations of ADHD and ASD"*), the author lists phrases she has heard from colleagues, shown on cards: «А раньше такого не было» (*"There was no such thing before"*) [msg #46, img #47]; «это просто способ почувствовать себя не таким как все!» (*"it's just a way to feel different from everyone!"*) [img #48]; «РАС это просто модный диагноз!» (*"ASD is just a fashionable diagnosis!"*) [img #49]; «СДВГ сейчас у каждого второго!» (*"Every second person has ADHD now!"*) [img #50]; and «постановка диагноза ничего не изменит» (*"making a diagnosis won't change anything"*) [msg #46].

Her counter-arguments [msg #46]:
- Many specialists are worried by the increase in diagnoses and some call it overdiagnosis and «блажью» (*"a whim"*). She is glad that the field has moved from a few years ago, when ADHD supposedly could not be an adult diagnosis, to arguing about prevalence.
- A diagnosis does change things: it helps a person understand what exactly is happening to them, lower their level of shame and accept their traits.
- Statistics: per official US statistics, up to 10% of children have ADHD and 1 in 36 children has autism.
- These are developmental disorders / neurodevelopmental differences, so the brain's structural features persist for life — children with them become adults with them.
- Most people may go undiagnosed until a certain point — namely until social demands start to exceed their limited capacities.
- Hence there is a huge population of neurodivergent people who need help and competent diagnosis.

### Why late diagnosis hurts
Per the forwarded post, women with ADHD are often under-examined and under-estimated: their symptoms look "acceptable" or "not serious", although they may experience deep inner suffering. Late diagnosis means years of misunderstanding, anxiety, burnout and guilt [msg #618].

### Estrogen, the menstrual cycle and ADHD
The author (post for 8 March) explains why female specifics must be considered in ADHD [msg #118]:
- Unlike men's, women's hormonal background changes over the month and over the life course, and research has begun on how this affects ADHD symptoms.
- Mechanism: it has long been known that estrogens participate in the development and plasticity of midbrain dopamine neurons. Since ADHD specifically involves the dopamine system, researchers began studying how monthly changes in estrogen concentration affect ADHD.
- Finding: compared with men of the same age and the same ADHD severity, women's symptoms fluctuated depending on the cycle phase. Moreover, amphetamine-class medications acted on them with different intensity depending on the phase.
- Menopause: after menopause estrogen levels drop sharply, and as a result — together with age-related changes — ADHD symptoms worsen.
- Hedge: there have not been many studies yet; the author hopes their number will grow so the female phenotype can be taken into account and women helped better.

A reader's Q&A [msg #706] adds a first-person case: the reader finds medication works much worse in the second phase of her cycle, so she gradually increases the dose and then lowers it again when the phase ends. The author confirms: yes, because of fluctuations of estrogens and other hormones the amount of dopamine changes, which also affects ADHD symptoms in women [msg #706]. (!) UNCLEAR: whether the dose-titration practice is endorsed by the author is not stated; it is the reader's own report.
