# Health policy and healthcare systems

## What this covers

In autumn 2024 the author, a practicing psychiatrist, took a course on health policy at MSU (МГУ) and posted what she learned in the lectures. The posts cover: whether health can be measured; the history of insurance medicine in Russia and why infectious epidemics sank it; the link between public drug spending, healthcare budgets and life expectancy, with a lecture slide; and two later posts that apply health-system thinking to current debates. One debate is whether letting GPs prescribe antidepressants would make psychiatrists cheaper. The other is the "mentoring" law for medical graduates. She notes that parts of the course make her sad ("местами на меня навевает грусть это всё" — "in places all this makes me sad") and that she finds it "Очень конечно все интересно, но запутанно" ("all very interesting, of course, but confusing"). For her, the topic matters because it shapes how psychiatric care is measured, financed, priced and staffed. [msg #336, #342, #343, #349, #355, #578, #769]

## Core ideas

- **Health cannot be measured directly.** Everyone tries to count health indicators, yet "health" has several competing models and cannot be computed, so it is unclear what world medicine is actually measuring. [msg #336]
- **Russians have a right to health protection, not to health.** This is one of many contradictions in health policy. [msg #336]
- **Infections killed early insurance medicine in Russia.** Insurance coverage reached people who were already getting care and missed the poor, where epidemics start. Russia therefore used the state Semashko model until the 1990s. [msg #342, #343]
- **Public drug and health spending tracks outcomes.** Countries that supply more outpatient medicines at public expense have lower mortality from basic diseases and higher life expectancy. Russia (with Bulgaria) spent the least on such drugs. [msg #349, #355]
- **Market logic in psychiatry depends on the health-system model.** In centralized Russia, letting GPs prescribe antidepressants would not lower psychiatrists' prices the way the market does in the decentralized US. Russian GPs already prescribe them anyway. [msg #578]
- **The mentoring law will probably make the workforce crisis worse.** The author expects fewer medical applicants, fewer specialists and no better primary care. [msg #769]

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Three types of healthcare system
The author gives this classification as background for why market competition sets medical prices in the US but not in Russia. Historically, healthcare systems have fallen into three types: [msg #578]
1. **Centralized (центральная).** Examples: Russia, France and others. The state covers and organizes most of the population's healthcare needs. As a result, market competition has relatively little effect on how medical services are priced. [msg #578]
2. **Decentralized (децентрализированная).** Examples: the US, Germany and others. The private sector covers and organizes most needs. Market and competition therefore drive pricing more than in Russia. For the same reason, in the US "без страховки, ты платишь безумные деньги, т.к. государство практически не покрывает стоимость" ("without insurance you pay insane money, because the state covers practically none of the cost"). [msg #578]
3. **Mixed (смешанная).** Named as the third type. (!) INCOMPLETE: the source does not describe how the mixed type works and gives no examples. [msg #578]

### Three nuances against "let GPs prescribe antidepressants so the market lowers psychiatrists' prices"
**The proposal.** In a comment-thread discussion, someone put forward this idea: "если антидепрессанты начнут назначать врачи терапевты, то поток к психиатрам снизится и они начнут снижать цены. Условно рынок порешает, как в США" ("if GP physicians start prescribing antidepressants, the flow of patients to psychiatrists will fall and they will start lowering prices. Roughly, the market will sort it out, as in the USA"). The author grants that at first glance the idea seems very sensible, especially from a commercial and competitive point of view. "Но это медицина и тут есть нюансы" ("But this is medicine, and there are nuances here"). [msg #578]
1. **Nuance 1 — this already happens in Russia.** In Russia you can already go to a GP and ask them to extend your prescription if you can't get to a psychiatrist in time or can't get an appointment at all. GPs also prescribe antidepressants and anti-anxiety drugs themselves. They do this especially when a patient is very ("ОЧЕНЬ") afraid of going to a psychiatrist and the GP can see that the patient really needs treatment. Her conclusion: "Так что так можно" ("So that is possible"). The proposed change would therefore not create a new channel of access. [msg #578]
2. **Nuance 2 — a GP lacks psychiatric expertise.** A GP deals mainly with other kinds of disease and physically cannot know the nuances of psychiatric disorders or do good differential diagnosis of them. In her words, a doctor's value is not in being "машиной для выписки рецепта" ("a prescription-writing machine"). It is in understanding what is going on and choosing treatment methods to match, taking the person's individual characteristics into account. [msg #578]
3. **Nuance 3 — Russia and the US have different health-system models (the one she finds most interesting).** Russia is centralized and the US is decentralized (see *Three types of healthcare system*). That is why market competition shapes medical prices much more in the US than in Russia. Importing the US logic ("the market will sort it out") ignores this difference in structure. [msg #578]

The author ends with "Такие ~~пирожки~~ круассаны🥐 Что думаете?" — a joke, roughly "Such are the ~~pies~~ croissants — what do you think?". It invites discussion and draws no further conclusion. [msg #578]

### Three expected consequences of the medical-graduate "mentoring" law
**The law, as the author describes it.** The new law on "mentoring" (наставничество) for graduates of medical universities works like this. After finishing medical school and before getting accreditation, the graduate — whom she mockingly calls "личинку врача" ("a doctor larva") — is sent to compulsory work ("на отработку") somewhere. Only after that can they take accreditation and then enter residency (ординатура) or go to work in an outpatient clinic (поликлиника). [msg #769]

**Her view of the law itself.** She feels very sorry for young specialists. She thinks they will most likely not work as doctors and build medical experience. Instead, in reality they will mostly stand in for nurses and feldshers (paramedics). What frightens her more than the bill is how people outside medicine react to it: they rejoice and even gloat, "как будто это решение решит какие-то их проблемы" ("as if this decision will solve some of their problems"). [msg #769]

**The next round of problems she expects:** [msg #769]
1. **Fewer medical students, and so fewer doctors.** Probably fewer people will choose to enter medical universities in the first place, which means even fewer doctors in the end. [msg #769]
2. **An even bigger shortage of narrow specialists.** Specialists are already in short supply, and there will be fewer still. Not everyone will get the chance to complete residency, and the age at which people finish residency will rise sharply. [msg #769]
3. **No better primary care.** The quality of primary (therapeutic/GP-level) care will not improve, "особенно в разрезе принудительности" ("especially given its compulsory nature"). [msg #769]

**Bottom line.** She concludes that medicine most likely won't get easier in the coming years, "т.к. в целом подход к переработкам и дефициту кадров не меняется" ("because, overall, the approach to overwork and staff shortages isn't changing"). [msg #769]

## Detailed insights

### Measuring health: what are we actually counting?
The author's main takeaway from a lecture: everyone tries to calculate health indicators, but nobody can say what health is. There are completely different models of health, and the indicator itself cannot be computed. So what are "we" — meaning world medicine — counting? The lecture left it unclear which exact indicator would be a good measure. [msg #336]

A related contradiction: Russians have a social right to the protection of health ("право на охрану здоровья"), but not to health itself. She says there are many such contradictions in health policy. [msg #336]

She applies this to her own field. What would health indicators in psychiatry even be? How could that factor be fully understood, and how might it affect the way care is delivered? She says plainly that she has no big conclusion, "пока во всяком случае" ("at least for now"). She is sharing the questions because they interest her. [msg #336]

### History of insurance medicine in Russia and why infections sank it
**The sequence, according to the lecture.** Insurance medicine was first attempted in Russia by the Bolsheviks. The author is unsure whether to call the state the Russian Empire or the USSR at that point ("Рос.Империи? СССР?"). The attempt failed for two reasons: it broke down on infectious diseases, and it could not make medicine accessible. Russia then moved to the state Semashko model and kept it until the 1990s. [msg #342]

**Before insurance.** Zemstvo (земское) healthcare was for a time replaced by factory healthcare, in which factories and plants paid for their own workers' treatment. Insurance medicine was then tried as the next step. [msg #343]

**Infections were the dominant problem.** Epidemics and outbreaks — typhus, tuberculosis and cholera, "я уже не говорю про сифилис и остальные типа гриппа" ("not to mention syphilis and others like flu") — were the scourge of the 19th and early 20th centuries. The First World War, the growth of cities and other factors of the time made outbreaks harder to bring under control. Infections were therefore one of the global problems of healthcare. [msg #343]

**Why insurance failed.** When Russia tried to move from factory healthcare to insurance medicine, a problem appeared. People who could pay insurance contributions were already getting fairly good treatment. People who could not pay ended up with even less access to medical help. [msg #343]

**The epidemiological mechanism.** Infectious outbreaks usually start in poor, crowded districts. People there eat poorly and work hard, so their immunity is low. From there, infections often spread to all layers of society. An insurance model that left out exactly these people could not contain epidemics. [msg #343]

**Conclusion.** In the author's summary, infections kept insurance medicine from developing in Russia for almost another century, until 1990. [msg #343]

She adds jokingly that her nerdiness about history ("душность с историей") and her passion for infectious diseases had finally come in useful. [msg #343]

### Public spending on outpatient drugs, mortality and life expectancy
**Statistic from the lecture.** The more a country spends on medicines for outpatient treatment — drugs the state hands out to citizens, as opposed to drugs people buy for themselves — the lower its mortality from basic disorders (cardiovascular and similar) and the higher its life expectancy. [msg #349]

**Russia's position.** On what she calls a sad chart, Russia ("мы") and Bulgaria spent the least of all countries on such drugs. [msg #349]
