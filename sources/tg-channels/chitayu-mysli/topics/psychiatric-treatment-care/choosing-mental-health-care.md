# Choosing specialists and evidence-based mental health care

## What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel teaches about getting into mental health care and getting the *right* care: practical strategies for finding a psychiatrist versus a psychologist, the legal limits and real uses of an online psychiatric "second opinion", the economics behind private psychiatric prices in Moscow, the argument that psychotherapy for psychiatric disorders is medical treatment and must follow clinical guidelines, a comparison of specialists for fear of death and grief (including the death doula), and two warnings about how medicine treats psychiatric symptoms — somatic doctors fearing them, and psychiatrists failing to trust patients' reports. According to the source, this matters because people with psychiatric disorders get sick "and sometimes die" in this field, because the choice of specialist is currently a heavy burden placed on patients, and because people with psychiatric symptoms are "такие же люди, как и все остальные" (the same people as everyone else) [msg #4, #219, #350].

## Core ideas

- **Finding care is hard, and that is reality, not a failure.** A psychiatrist is best found through trusted clinics; a psychologist is the hardest choice because several independent conditions must coincide. Expect not to find the right one the first time [msg #600].
- **Assessing professionalism should not fall on the patient.** Forwarded author Masha argues the field puts too much responsibility for choosing a specialist on clients; the solution is a more consolidated professional community that maintains quality [msg #219].
- **An online psychiatric consultation is consultative, not medical** in Russia: no prescriptions, certificates or diagnosis reports — but it can give a second view of diagnosis and treatment, psychoeducation and a plan [msg #242].
- **Private psychiatry is expensive for structural reasons**: long appointments, scarcity of psychiatrists, and the costs of private clinics; state evidence-based psychiatry is improving as an alternative [msg #575].
- **Working with a psychiatric disorder moves psychotherapy from psychology into medicine**, where it is a "non-drug method of treatment" and must follow clinical guidelines; this requires a basic education in psychology or medicine [msg #350].
- **For death-related fears and grief there are three kinds of specialists** — psychiatrist, psychotherapist and death doula — differing in education, methods and focus [msg #519].
- **Psychiatric symptoms are just symptoms.** Somatic medicine's fear of psychiatric patients is unjustified, hallucinations are not an automatic reason for antipsychotics, and patients' reports should be believed and interpreted in context [msg #4, #767].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Author's algorithm for finding a psychiatrist
Purpose: answering the frequent question "how to find a good psychotherapist/psychiatrist/psychologist among so much choice, preferably not at a huge price". The author admits "я всегда точно так же мучаюсь" (I always struggle in exactly the same way) [msg #600].
1. **Keep a list of clinics you trust.** The author has a set of clinics she trusts and books a random specialist there — the clinic's quality control substitutes for evaluating the individual doctor [msg #600].
2. **Or ask colleagues for a specific name.** She pesters colleagues to recommend a specific person — but notes this is one of the few privileges of being a doctor, so it is not available to most readers [msg #600].
3. **General rule for patients:** "идите в проверенные места" (go to proven places), accepting that it will never be cheap [msg #600].
4. **Why it is worth paying for a good one:** a psychiatrist is a relatively one-off specialist — you do not see them every week — so it is better to go to a good one [msg #600].

### Four factors that must coincide when choosing a psychologist
The author calls choosing a psychologist "САМЫЙ сложный процесс" (THE most difficult process), because success depends on all of the following at once [msg #600]:
1. **The specialist is good** (professionally competent).
2. **They work in the right approach** (therapeutic modality) for you.
3. **They work with your specific problem.**
4. **They have openings now and you click personally** — the personal match needed to establish a therapeutic relationship (availability and personal fit are given together in the source).
Why it takes so long even for professionals: after a consultation, the author often spends a lot of time on recommendations, because even for a patient she has seen and understands, matching a specific specialist and arranging it with them "требует очень много сил и ресурсов" (takes a great deal of effort and resources) [msg #600].
Practical consequences: searching on your own is always difficult and long; tune in to the fact that you may not find the right person the first time — "Это не баг, это реальность" (This is not a bug, it's reality). Aggregator platforms are "достаточно сложно" (quite problematic), so it is better to go to centers or use friends' recommendations — "Но и это не гарант" (But that is no guarantee either) [msg #600].

### Online psychiatric second opinion — what it is and when to take it
Source: post forwarded from the Psycho_pharmacologist channel, written because people kept emailing "какую услугу выбрать" (which service to choose) [msg #242].

**Legal status.** In Russia the law on medical activity still states that prescribing drugs, writing prescriptions and providing medical services in general happen at an in-person appointment in a clinic holding a medical license. Therefore any online consultation with a psychiatrist is not a medical service but has a consultative character. No certificates, diagnostic reports or prescriptions are issued; for any documents you must see a doctor in person in a clinic [msg #242].

**What the service can give** (six items) [msg #242]:
1. An additional opinion from another psychiatrist on the diagnosis.
2. An assessment of the current treatment the client is receiving for their psycho-emotional difficulties.
3. Answers to all the questions you have that, for some reason, your treating doctor cannot answer.
4. Psychoeducation and support on the difficult road to recovery.
5. Help building a plan of further treatment steps, including taking your place of residence into account.
6. Information on the most effective psychotherapy approaches for the specific case, plus a list of psychotherapists who work with that problem.

**When to take it** (four situations) [msg #242]:
1. **Relatives and loved ones of people with psychiatric disorders** — e.g., to understand what to do next when the loved one is currently in a hospital or rehabilitation center.
2. **You live in another country** and want an opinion on your condition in your native language — but you will still have to see a local doctor to get prescriptions.
3. **You live in another Russian city** and doubt your diagnosis, treatment or your doctor's competence — caveat: you may be asked to come in person to the city where the second-opinion doctor practises, or to take the new information to your local doctor.
4. **You do not understand what is happening to you or where to go** with your complaints — no idea where to start: psychologist or psychiatrist, whether pills are needed or you can manage without for now, and if a psychologist, which approach.

### Three reasons private psychiatry is expensive
Context: questions to Karina about "premium" prices at the center and future clinic. The author notes she is both an owner and specialist and also a patient — she sees psychiatrists, has had a therapist weekly for 3 years and has taken antidepressants regularly for about 3 years, "так что знаю эту сферу со всех сторон" (so I know this field from all sides) [msg #575].
The price problem: private psychiatric visits in Moscow cost 10, 13, 15 thousand rubles and upward, "и хочется всплакнуть" (and you want to cry). When the clinic Чайка raised her price to 13 thousand, she inwardly cried too, because it is hard to accept that people must pay that much for her consultation. For that reason she tries to see everyone within one hour, although many initial psychiatric consultations need 1.5 hours (at МНС she always did 1.5-hour first visits) — 1.5 hours would be "совсем космическая сумма" (a truly astronomical sum) for many people. Other doctors charge 3–5–7 thousand, which makes people think psychiatrists have gone crazy [msg #575].
1. **Time.** A psychiatrist's appointment lasts an hour, while many other specialties do initial visits in 20–30 minutes. Recalculated per hour, the other doctor costs the same — they just do not need an hour [msg #575].
2. **Scarcity — supply and demand.** There are not many psychiatrists. Their number grows every year, but when the author began practice and entered residency, psychiatry was still quite stigmatized both in society and among doctors, so there was no big competition for places and no large graduating cohorts. Meanwhile many people need help, so psychiatrists are in an advantageous position: departments in Чайка and other multi-specialty clinics try to hire psychiatrists and expand, and practically fight for specialists. "Поэтому вступает в игру рынок, спрос и предложение" (So the market, supply and demand come into play) [msg #575].
3. **Private medicine costs.** To reach even minimal break-even rather than working at zero or a loss, clinics — especially non-chain ones — have to set a price of about 10 thousand rubles in Moscow, because rent, taxes, administrators' pay and other expenses are practically not covered at a lower price [msg #575].
Conclusion of the author: private psychiatry is an expensive pleasure, especially when combined help (psychotherapy + pharmacology) is needed, and it is sad that more vulnerable social groups need such costly treatment programs. The positive side: in recent years not only private but also state evidence-based psychiatry has been developing actively — psychotherapy departments and centers at hospitals are appearing, there are many good young doctors in ПНД (psychoneurological dispensaries), and many new expensive medicines are available there. So there is an alternative; "частная медицина никогда не сможет быть супер бюджетной, доступной и качественной одновременно" (private medicine can never be super-budget, accessible and high-quality at the same time) [msg #575].

### Psychiatrist vs psychotherapist vs death doula — whom to see about death and grief
Purpose: in mental health, death comes up often — fear of one's own death, death of loved ones, living through loss, and thoughts of ending one's life. The post compares three specialists who work with this [msg #519].
1. **Psychiatrist** — a doctor specializing in diagnosing and treating psychiatric disorders. *Education:* medical — must have a medical diploma and a specialization in psychiatry. *Methods:* can prescribe medication, apply psychotherapy or other medical interventions. *Focus:* treating psychiatric disorders [msg #519].
2. **Psychotherapist** — a specialist working with mental and emotional problems through talk therapy. *Education:* may be a psychologist or a doctor, but not necessarily; the main requirement is professional training in a psychotherapeutic approach. *Methods:* talk therapy, self-regulation techniques, awareness, working through trauma. Cannot prescribe drugs but can refer to a psychiatrist when needed. *Focus:* emotional and psychological support, help changing behavior, overcoming crises, searching for meaning [msg #519].
3. **Death doula** — a non-clinical specialist who accompanies a person at the end of life and supports loved ones through the dying process, and also supports people in other losses. *Education:* no single standard; doulas train in specialized courses (the source names INELDA, in English, and Death Foundation, in Russian). *Methods:* emotional, spiritual and practical support — helping prepare for a loss, helping with grieving, creating rituals, farewells, helping talk about death and other losses. Can work with one person or the whole family system. Cannot prescribe drugs but can refer to a psychiatrist if necessary. *Focus:* helping people live through loss. A loss is not always someone's death: it is "любое событие, включающее утрату привычного уклада жизни" (any event involving the loss of one's habitual way of life). Grieving is "переход из жизни до потери в жизнь после неё" (the transition from life before the loss to life after it) [msg #519].

### Nine types of loss a death doula works with
The full list from the source [msg #519]:
1. An impending or already-happened death of a loved one.
2. One's own terminal diagnosis and preparation for one's own death.
3. Perinatal losses — miscarriages, stillbirth, infertility.
4. Death of pets.
5. Divorce — loss of family wholeness and of the role of husband/wife.
6. Moving — loss of a habitual way of life.
7. First motherhood — loss of the former free way of life.
8. Loss of faith, of a sense of safety, of meanings.
9. Any other losses — "когда старый мир рухнул, а в новом ещё не ясно, как теперь жить" (when the old world has collapsed and in the new one it is not yet clear how to live now).
The promotional part of the post adds, as description of who the training suits, that a death doula course is aimed at people who want to professionally accompany people and their loved ones at the end of life and in grieving (graduates choose which losses to work with), at specialists already working with end-of-life — psychologists, psychotherapists, psychiatrists, nurses and doctors, palliative and funeral-sector workers — and at people who want to understand their own attitude to death and loss and gain inner stability. Dates and link are dropped as logistics [msg #519].

### Two main problems of the psychology field
Trigger: after Natasha's post about recruiting for the center, a comment storm mixed together claims that higher education is unnecessary for a psychologist, treating psychoses with psychoanalysis, and that the evidence-based approach is wrong because evidence base supposedly translates differently. The author names, in her opinion, the two main problems of the field [msg #350]:
1. **Absence of regulation of activity in psychology.**
2. **Specialists not understanding the difference between relative norm and pathology.**
The reasoning: counseling — helping with family problems, existential questions and similar — is one thing, and there many other methods "работают отлично" (work excellently). But once you work with a client who has a psychiatric disorder, you are no longer in the field of psychology but in the field of MEDICINE, where psychotherapy is a non-drug method of treatment — "ЛЕЧЕНИЯ! Чувствуете разницу?" (TREATMENT! Feel the difference?) [msg #350].
Medicine is strict: every doctor of every specialty has written clinical guidelines based on international research; consequences of deviating from them differ by country, but globally nobody is praised for it. Being part of medicine, psychotherapy likewise must follow clinical guidelines — unfortunately not yet at the legislative level — because research has already been done and written up on what treats the patient/client more effectively according to studies and international experience [msg #350].
Why it matters: in psychotherapy we work with the fact that people get sick, and sometimes die. Therefore working with eating disorders (РПП), suicidal risk, depression and similar conditions without a clinical basis — without being able to recognize these states and do differential diagnosis — and without following what works most effectively is a very big risk for both client and therapist. Hence the importance of both a basic education in psychology or medicine and following clinical guidelines [msg #350].
The author pre-empts objections with her credentials: 8 years of medical school, residency in psychiatry, retraining in clinical psychology, and study of psychoanalysis and other approaches. She acknowledges the post may seem harsh compared with her usual ones, but "медицина не то место, где мы можем позволить себе вольности, отвечая за жизнь других людей" (medicine is not the place where we can afford liberties when we are responsible for other people's lives) [msg #350].

## Detailed insights

### The burden of choice should be lifted from patients (forwarded post by Masha)
Masha (channel Маша does therapy) took part, with colleague Liza Fedorova, in an episode of the project "Справиться проще" with Natasha and Karina, discussing difficulties in the field: the state of diagnostics, problems in training specialists, pricing of these services. She notes each of these topics could be discussed separately because of its complexity, ambiguity and the huge room for interpreting answers, and that naming problems does not mean there is a quick solution [msg #219].
Her main point, addressed to people seeking help: too much attention is currently given to the idea that the client is responsible for choosing a specialist. She grants that fit matters — psychotherapy is a special kind of interaction, and even a good specialist (by professional criteria) will not suit everyone. But evaluating professionalism criteria "это большая нагрузка на клиентов" (is a big burden on clients) [msg #219].
She sees it as important to lift this burden of difficult choice from clients and patients. People with pathological doubts often write to her about how hard choosing a specialist is — a specialist is not always easy to find, and the choice adds an extra aggravating factor. Colleagues have already done a lot to ease this choice: associations are being created and good clinics with quality control are opening. But in her view the real solution is a more consolidated professional community whose task is to create an environment where specialists can maintain the quality of their services, develop together and develop the field, in order to help clients and patients [msg #219].
As illustration of this conviction she describes her own path: she noticed an interest in therapists' professional development and training and recognized her core value of belonging and tribe (with a nod to RO DBT); she developed an educational project at the МНС clinic, where a community of colleagues was "главным бустом моего развития" (the main boost of my development) as a specialist; after leaving that clinic she and Liza began the professional community project "ПРАКТИК" for specialists' development and support. (The invitation to follow the project is dropped as promo.) [msg #219]

### Somatic medicine's fear of psychiatric symptoms
At a case-based medical conference (covering medicine, ethics and management, with interactive elements), the author was angered by a case where a patient had hallucinations and the doctors wanted to immediately treat them with antipsychotics or even hospitalize them in a psychiatric inpatient unit. Her view: "огромная часть врачей боится психиатрической симптоматике [sic] и наших клиентов как огня" (a huge share of doctors fear psychiatric symptoms and our clients like fire) — a big problem of somatic medicine [msg #4].
Concrete examples she and colleagues regularly meet in the clinic: people with depression, bipolar disorder (БАР) and other disorders are refused surgery or a caesarean section without certificates (справки) from a psychiatrist; once a certificate was even requested for laser hair removal [msg #4].
Her reasoning: the prevalence of psychiatric disorders is huge, and most people do not even suspect they have them because they do not seek help — and they go to somatic doctors just the same. "Это такие же люди, как и все остальные. И галлюцинации - не повод сразу давать нейролептик" (These are the same people as everyone else. And hallucinations are not a reason to immediately give an antipsychotic.) She draws no further conclusion than that psychiatric symptoms are just symptoms, people with them are just people, and we should treat each other as people who are simply having a hard time, as with other disorders [msg #4].
A side note from the same post: the author says she generally dislikes conferences because her concentration lasts only part of a talk, but a case-based, interactive format kept her engaged [msg #4].

### Trust patients and take a context-aware history — the "pink dog" story
On 10 October the author told her favorite story from residency: a patient in a psychiatric hospital complained that he saw a pink dog. He was treated this way and that, the antipsychotic dose was increased, but the dog remained — until a doctor looked out of the window and saw a dog covered in pink paint, because a nearby building was being painted. The moral, in her words: "верьте пациентам и внимательно собирайте анамнез, с учётом контекста" (believe patients and collect the history carefully, taking context into account) [msg #767].

## Practical playbook

- Do not request "certificates" from a psychiatrist as a default precondition for ordinary procedures; the author considers this fear-driven practice unjustified (see *Somatic medicine's fear of psychiatric symptoms*) [msg #4].
- If you need prescriptions or documents, an online second opinion is not enough — plan an in-person visit to a licensed clinic [msg #242].
- If private prices are out of reach, look at the state sector: psychotherapy departments at hospitals and ПНД, where the author says good young doctors and new expensive medicines are now available [msg #575].
- A psychotherapist or death doula can refer you to a psychiatrist if medication may be needed; only the psychiatrist prescribes [msg #519].

## Evidence and numbers

- Prices for private psychiatric visits in Moscow: 10, 13, 15 thousand rubles and upward; the author's own price at Чайка was raised to 13 thousand; other specialists charge 3–5–7 thousand [msg #575].
- Appointment lengths: psychiatrist about 1 hour (initial consultations often need 1.5 hours; the author did 1.5-hour first visits at МНС), other specialties 20–30 minutes for an initial visit [msg #575].
- Minimal break-even price for a non-chain private clinic in Moscow: about 10 thousand rubles per visit, per the author [msg #575].
- The author's credentials: 8 years of medical education, psychiatry residency, retraining in clinical psychology; as a patient — weekly therapy for 3 years and antidepressants for about 3 years [msg #350, #575].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The legal point about online consultations refers specifically to Russian law at the time of the post (2024) [msg #242].
- Price figures and break-even estimates are the author's view for Moscow private clinics in 2025; she has a stake as a clinic owner and specialist, which she discloses [msg #575].
- (!) UNCLEAR: in the four-factor list for psychologists, availability ("чтобы сейчас были места у него") and personal fit are written as one clause; the split into separate factors is not explicit in the source [msg #600].
- The two "main problems of the field" and the requirement to follow clinical guidelines in psychotherapy are the author's opinion; she notes guideline adherence in psychotherapy is not yet legally mandated [msg #350].
- Post #519 combines the specialist comparison with a promotion of the Death Foundation course, so its description of doulas comes from a promotional context [msg #519].
- Masha's answer — a consolidated professional community — is offered as her personal view ("как мне кажется"), and she stresses naming problems does not imply quick solutions [msg #219].
