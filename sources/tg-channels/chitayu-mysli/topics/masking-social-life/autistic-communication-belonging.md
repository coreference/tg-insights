# Autistic communication, belonging and being misread

## What this covers

This topic gathers the channel author's posts on how autistic people communicate and belong: the double empathy problem (communication breaks down on both sides, not just because of an autistic "deficit"), the mismatch between inner feelings and outward nonverbal signals that leads to being misread, why a trusted person makes public places manageable, why a small safe "tribe" is a real need, and how to tell social anxiety from autistic limited social energy when work demands a lot of talking. The author treats these as painful, everyday issues for people with ASD: constantly thinking about how one is perceived is described as "большое напряжение" (a great strain) [msg #867], and rejection is described as felt by the brain almost like death [msg #431].

## Core ideas

- **Autistic people often enjoy solitude but still want company** — liking a lot of time alone does not mean not wanting time with others [msg #212].
- **Communication problems are two-sided** (double empathy problem): autistic and neurotypical people misunderstand each other; the theory challenges the view that all communication problems are an autistic deficit [msg #382].
- **The need for a tribe is universal and evolutionary**, and neurodivergent people are more likely to face rejection and fail to find their people — yet they need a tribe no less [msg #431].
- **Inner experience and nonverbal expression often do not match in autism** — the author calls this one of the official criteria — so close people should ask rather than interpret [msg #867].
- **Autistic social energy is limited regardless of practice**, unlike social anxiety, which can be worked on; this forces real choices about work and life [msg #852].
- **Nonverbal-heavy content can be harder to process** — the author's hypothesis for why she dislikes sitcoms and romance but enjoys detectives and any book [msg #790].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Double empathy problem — six research findings
Definition: the double empathy problem is a theory that when autistic and non-autistic people communicate, understanding and skills are lacking on both sides. The usual framing is that people with ASD have difficulty understanding context and other people (verbally and nonverbally); the theory asks whether the other side of the communication also has difficulties. According to the author, it is increasingly discussed, and scientific data are appearing showing the problems are bidirectional: autistic people misunderstand neurotypicals just as neurotypicals do not understand autistic people, "И коммуникация не ладится от слова совсем" (And communication simply doesn't work at all) [msg #382].

Caveat stated by the author: far from all scientists and specialists support the theory; it largely challenges the view that all communication problems of people with ASD come from their deficit in communication [msg #382].

What studies conducted so far show, as listed by the author (no specific studies or authors are named — (!) INCOMPLETE: no citations given):
1. **Facial expressions are misread both ways.** Autistic people misread neurotypicals' facial expressions, and neurotypicals likewise misread and misinterpret autistic people's facial expressions [msg #382].
2. **Hasty, wrong judgments by non-autistic people.** Non-autistic people tend to make quick (and incorrect) judgments about autistic people that interrupt or spoil communication with them; they also more often suspect them of insincerity or deception [msg #382].
3. **Difficulties shrink among autistic peers.** The social and communication problems of autistic people are less noticeable or significant when they communicate with other autistic people [msg #382].
4. **More openness among autistic peers.** People with autism are more open and tell more about their lives when talking with each other [msg #382].
5. **Similar trait scores → closest friendships.** People with similar scores on an autistic-traits test rate their friendship as the closest [msg #382].
6. **Implications for diagnosis and therapy.** The double empathy problem may explain shortcomings of diagnostic tools and therapy methods [msg #382].

Author's view: she likes that communication problems are now examined from both sides, because any communication is a two-way process and we need to learn to find common ground from each side. Her own observation: it is sometimes much harder to talk with neurotypical people because she often has to explain why she behaves one way or another, whereas with friends who are also on the spectrum this happens "гораздо гораздо меньше" (much, much less) [msg #382].

### Two reasons a trusted companion helps in public places
Context: a common belief is that a person with ASD is better off alone. Many autistic people do say they like spending a lot of time by themselves, but this does not mean they don't want to spend time with others. It especially matters for going out to public places or parties with little-known people: in such cases many people with ASD would rather stay home unless someone from their closest circle goes with them [msg #212].

Why it is much easier to be in such places with a partner or friend:
1. **The companion takes over part of the communication**, especially the necessary bits — for example buying a coffee or asking/finding something out [msg #212].
2. **The environment feels less unpredictable and unsafe.** A crowd or just a public space will not feel as unpredictable and unsafe, because a more predictable person is right there [msg #212].

Recommendations:
- If you are autistic and need to go somewhere you don't want to go alone, try to take someone you feel good with [msg #212].
- If you are a friend or close person of someone with ASD, don't refuse to go with them, even to places that seem the strangest to you [msg #212].

### Social anxiety vs autistic limited social energy at work
Question from a reader: how to cope with a lot of communication at work as an introvert, when one often needs to initiate discussions and negotiations but does not want to talk to anyone more than necessary [msg #852].

The author's answer, in order:
1. **First decide what your "introversion" is linked to.** The label hides different causes, and the remedy depends on which one it is [msg #852].
2. **If it is social anxiety** — this is something one learns to face and to build up different experience with; i.e. it can be worked on through exposure to new experience [msg #852].
3. **If it is autism** — understand that you have a limited reserve of energy for social communication in any case. The options are then:
   - choose a job where you can roughly "вывозить" (handle, cope with) the amount of communication;
   - or put together a combination of different jobs, or reduce your working hours/rate (уменьшать ставку);
   - or give up other communication outside work [msg #852].
4. **Accept that it is a hard choice.** The author says this is unfortunately often a very difficult choice, but at some point we will have to make it anyway [msg #852].

### Two-context approach to being misread (work vs close people)
Question (#867): a subscriber diagnosed last year with ASD, ADHD and GAD asks how to deal with being misunderstood — her words counted as rude or interpreted wrongly, with other emotions read into them. Her examples: since childhood her mother has said she is being rude (she says she just becomes too emotional when worried); that she "бычу глаза" (glares) — a phrase still painful for her to even write; her mother trained her to look in the eyes, although she felt more comfortable listening without looking, because the mother believed she wasn't listening if she didn't look. Recently, while they were baking kulich together, she told her mother that an analysis had found a pathology in her colon, and the mother said she said it too cheerfully — while she was simply focused on something else and of course was not glad about the illness. She asks whether she can work on this or explain that it is her trait, when the person likes to say she "нести флаг" (waves the flag of her diagnosis too much), and worries that strangers at work or study may perceive her the same way and form a bad impression [msg #867].

The author's answer: she calls it a very painful and relevant question for people with ASD, and first offers moral support — living with this and constantly thinking about how you are perceived is a great strain. Then she splits what to do by situation [msg #867]:
1. **Work-type situations (strangers, colleagues, study).** Here one learns — most often individually in therapy — how to synchronize inner experience with outer expression and how to give people social signals correctly. Adjusting is treated as a skill to learn in these settings [msg #867].
2. **Situations with close people.** Constant adjustment here is an excessive load. Ideally, talk to close people and explain that your inner state and your nonverbal expression often may not match, since this is one of the manifestations of autism (the author states it is even one of the official criteria). Let them instead clarify in the moment what exactly you are feeling — so you can at least relax a little, not analyse everything constantly, and simply be yourself [msg #867].

## Detailed insights

### Belonging: the need for a tribe
The idea comes from the author's therapy group on overcontrol (hypercontrol), which discussed people's need for a tribe. In short: in evolutionary terms people could not survive alone, so we are social animals, and that is why rejection by society is so painful for us — for our brain (which is "ещё там далеко в прошлом", still far back in the past), rejection by the tribe is practically equal to death [msg #431].

Being neurodivergent makes it much more likely to face rejection and not find one's "tribe"; with ASD it can be even harder. The author acknowledges there are autistic people who are not interested in society and suffer little from lack of communication and belonging — but she says such people are far fewer in the ASD population than those who suffer because they cannot find "their" people [msg #431].

Her conclusion, "грустная, но правда жизни" (sad but the truth of life): it is harder for us to find our tribe, but we need it no less than other people. So it is important to keep looking for people with whom you feel good, calm and safe — "даже 2 человека - это уже племя!" (even 2 people are already a tribe!) [msg #431]. (!) ### Nonverbal communication and media preferences (author's personal hypothesis)
The author describes a heated argument with her husband about why she loves watching thrillers and shows like "Очень странные дела" (Stranger Things); he dislikes thrillers, she has loved them since early childhood. Their hypotheses — "это все твоя травма" (it's all your trauma), watching detectives with her grandmother from infancy, having graduated from medical school — led nowhere. She can hardly watch sitcoms, comedies and especially very romantic content: unrealistic happy endings irritate her, she gets stressed by embarrassing situations, gets emotionally overloaded, or it is simply boring [msg #790].

Her later hypothesis — simpler than trauma or adrenaline-seeking (which she still doesn't see as a problem anyway):
- In detectives, thrillers and mysticism the focus is on what is happening and on finding the truth/logic/explanation of events [msg #790].
- Even in sitcoms, without the heavy emotional load of a romantic drama, everything is built around relationships between characters — awkward situations, sad ones, and frequently played-out misunderstandings (she thinks Turkish and Korean series are built only on these) — and all of it through largely *nonverbal* communication, which is harder to perceive [msg #790].
- Books do not have this problem: she can read anything, all genres are fine, because books have almost no nonverbal communication — everything has to be written out, so everything is clear at once [msg #790].

She hedges: she doesn't know how far this is the ultimate truth, but there is clearly a grain of truth in it; and in any case her "dark and sad soul" loves watching and reading gloomy, sad things [msg #790].

- The nonverbal-content explanation of media preferences is explicitly a personal hypothesis with "a grain of truth", not an established finding [msg #790].
- The author acknowledges a minority of autistic people genuinely uninterested in social contact; the tribe argument applies to the majority who suffer without "their" people [msg #431].
