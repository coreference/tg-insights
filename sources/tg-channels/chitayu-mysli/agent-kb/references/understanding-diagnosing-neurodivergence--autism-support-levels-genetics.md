# Autism spectrum: support levels, genetics and history

## What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel taught about how autism spectrum disorder (РАС, ASD) is classified today — three levels of support need, replacing older labels — and why even the lowest support level remains a disability. It also covers the genetics of autism (twin concordance, sibling recurrence, de novo mutations, autistic traits in parents), what this means for autistic people planning children, the tensions between parents of autistic children and autistic self-advocates, autistic parenting, overlooked history and pioneers, and resources for adults just encountering the diagnosis. According to the author, many adults with undiagnosed mild-spectrum ASD spend years cycling through diagnoses and treatments that do not help, so understanding the spectrum correctly matters clinically [msg #149].

## Core ideas

- **Support levels replaced subtypes and severity grades.** The field has abandoned Asperger/Kanner subtypes and also mild/moderate/severe grading, describing ASD through three levels of support need instead; see *ASD support levels 1–3* [msg #150].
- **Low support needs ≠ no support needs.** Autism is a disability for many, including people at level 1, because it limits them; see *Low support needs are still a disability* [msg #67].
- **Undiagnosed mild-spectrum autism causes real harm.** The author regularly sees exhausted adults with 5–6 comorbid diagnoses for whom treatment does not work because nobody diagnosed ASD; part of their problems are solved by acceptance, environment building and work with sensory overload [msg #149].
- **Autism is highly heritable but unpredictable in its form.** An autistic parent has an elevated chance of an autistic child, but the child's support level is independent of the parent's, and over 1000 genes are implicated [msg #602, #642, #717].
- **Autistic parenting cuts both ways.** Autistic parents may understand autistic children better, but may find neurotypical children harder, especially due to sensory overload from children [msg #718].
- **Autism is not new.** Historical anecdotes and early clinical work (Sukhareva in the 1930s) predate the usual Asperger/Kanner origin story [msg #539, #547].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### ASD support levels 1–3
**Purpose.** The current way to describe the difficulties and functioning of autistic people. The author explains that the world community has abandoned dividing autism into Asperger syndrome, Kanner syndrome and similar subtypes, and that a mild/moderate/severe rating also fails to describe difficulties and functioning in a way that matches what actually happens to people with ASD [msg #150].

**Why functioning labels fail.** Functioning is not stable: "вот сейчас вы бодры и активны, вербальны, а потом хоп, нагрузка, истощение и твоя степень тяжести и функциональности меняется" (*right now you are alert and active, verbal, and then — bam — load, exhaustion, and your degree of severity and functioning changes*). For the same reason the autistic community dislikes the terms "высокофункциональный" (*high-functioning*) and "низкофункциональный" (*low-functioning*) [msg #150].

**Important reference point.** Support levels compare autistic people with other autistic people, not with allistic people (see *Low support needs are still a disability*) [msg #67].

The three levels, with the features listed in the channel's Russian text and the attached cards (Verywell card [img #150]; Healthline + PsychCentral carousel [img #151], [img #152], [img #153]):

1. **Level 1 — Требуется поддержка (Requiring support).**
   - Problems understanding and following social rules (card: "Trouble understanding and following social rules") [msg #151, img #151].
   - Rigid or inflexible behaviour [msg #151].
   - Stress during changes/transitions (the card says "Some stress during transitions") [msg #151, img #151].
   - May benefit from therapy or life-skills training/coaching [msg #151].
   - Verywell card adds: difficulty initiating social interactions; organization and planning problems can hamper independence [img #150].
2. **Level 2 — Требуется существенная поддержка (Requiring substantial support).**
   - Atypical social behaviour, for example walking away in the middle of a conversation [msg #151, img #152].
   - High interest in specific topics [msg #151, img #152].
   - Noticeable distress/anxiety when faced with change [msg #151, img #152].
   - May need school accommodations such as reading help or social-skills support [msg #151, img #152].
   - Verywell card adds: social interactions limited to narrow special interests; frequent restricted/repetitive behaviours [img #150].
3. **Level 3 — Требуется очень существенная поддержка (Requiring very substantial support).**
   - Severe communication deficits, for example being nonspeaking ("неразговорчивость") [msg #151, img #153].
   - Repetitive behaviours such as rocking or spinning [msg #151, img #153].
   - Extremely pronounced distress when asked to switch tasks [msg #151, img #153].
   - May need one-on-one time with an education assistant and may use augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) tools such as picture symbols [msg #151, img #153].
   - Verywell card adds: severe deficits in verbal and nonverbal social communication skills; great distress/difficulty changing actions or focus [img #150].

**How the levels relate in life.** The author notes that people at levels 1, 2 and 3 "часто живут в разных мирах" (*often live in different worlds*), yet none of them has fewer problems because of it — particularly when a level-1 parent has a child at level 2 or 3, or a child with ADHD or another condition [msg #149].

### Two approaches to autism: parents vs autistic self-advocates
**Purpose.** Answers a reader's question about the perceived tension or conflict between parents of autistic children (who prefer "person-first" terminology) and adult autistic self-advocates — even though, as the reader notes, these are overlapping groups. The author confirms the tension is real and exists not only in the Russian-speaking space [msg #717, #718].

1. **Parents' approach.** Parents may focus on various therapies aimed at "correcting" autistic behaviour, which can be perceived as an attempt to suppress individuality [msg #718].
2. **Self-advocates' approach.** Autistic self-advocates argue for accepting autism as neurodiversity and consider attempts to "cure" autism potentially harmful [msg #718].

**Consequence.** This difference leads to conflicts, especially over choosing therapy methods and lifestyle for autistic children — which, the author says, is what is frequently seen online [msg #718].

## Detailed insights

### Low support needs are still a disability
A text by autistic author zebulontheplanet, translated by Anna Green Moon and forwarded from the channel "Аутичные переводы", argues angrily against the claim that level-1 autism is not a disability [msg #67]:
- Autism is a disability for many, including those with low support needs. People with low support needs can call their autism a disability because it limits them — "низкая потребность в поддержке не означает ОТСУТСТВИЕ потребности в поддержке" (*low support need does not mean ABSENCE of support need*) [msg #67].
- Concrete examples of needs at low support levels: help with instrumental activities of daily living (ИФА — shopping, cooking, using the phone, housework, laundry, cleaning), help with finances; some cannot drive a car; some cannot cook a complex dish [msg #67].
- Denying this erases the real difficulties of low-support autistic people: communication difficulties and sensory differences (the source itself leaves this list open-ended: "и так далее и тому подобное"). Autism creates limitations for most and this must be taken into account [msg #67].
- The author concedes: low-support autistic people are less limited than high-support ones, and society treats them more leniently; it can be hard when your autism is less limiting. But "Это не соревнование" (*This is not a competition*) [msg #67].
- Key reasoning: support needs were not designed as a comparison with allistic people (neurotypical or non-autistic neurodivergent), but to compare the needs of autistic people with each other. Therefore one cannot say low-support autistic people have no autism-related limitations, nor that they should be moved into a "no support needs" category [msg #67].
- The call to action: stop spreading misinformation, study, learn from autistic people with high and low support needs, and listen to them [msg #67].

### The clinician's view: stigma and missed diagnoses on the mild spectrum
Writing on Autism Awareness Day, the author describes how exhausting it is to work in a world of stigma where you "не выглядишь как аутист" (*don't look autistic*), where you are told you "везде просто видишь нейроотличия" (*just see neurodivergence everywhere*), and where half of one's colleagues openly say one is dealing with nonsense and non-existent diagnoses [msg #149].
- She constantly sees tired, worn-out people with 5–6 comorbid disorders whom most treatment schemes do not help, who have gone "через все круги ада и все диагнозы МКБ, группы ДБТ, препараты, ограничения" (*through all circles of hell and all ICD diagnoses, DBT groups, medications, restrictions*) because no one ever diagnosed ASD [msg #149].
- Part of these problems turns out to be solved by acceptance, building a suitable environment and working with sensory overload [msg #149].
- She takes comfort in how attitudes to ADHD have changed and are changing, and hopes the milder spectrum of ASD will go the same way [msg #149].

### Genetics and inheritance: answer to an autistic future parent
A reader with diagnosed ASD level 1 asked whether their future child is likely to have ASD and whether the child could be level 2 or 3; the author says the question troubles her too [msg #602]:
- ASD is a genetic disorder, so if you have ASD the probability your child will have it is fairly high [msg #602].
- The child's support level cannot be known and does not depend on the parent's level in any way; the child may also not get ASD at all — but the probability remains elevated [msg #602].
- Children with ASD are also born to neurotypical parents [msg #602].
- Gene variability is huge: the current gene base for ASD includes more than 1000 different genes, so many outcomes are possible [msg #602].

### Autistic traits in parents of autistic children
Answering whether there is data on what share of parents of autistic children are themselves autistic (diagnosed or self-diagnosed), the author — beyond the known high heritability — cites three studies [msg #717]:
1. **Pilot study in Italy (2025, Frontiers in Psychiatry).** So far found that 29% of parents of autistic children meet criteria for the broader autism phenotype: 12% of fathers and 17% of mothers. A significant correlation was also found between fathers' autistic traits and their children's [msg #717].
2. **Systematic review of 41 studies (2018, Springer).** Between 9% and 45% of parents of children with ASD show signs of the broader phenotype — significantly higher than parents of neurotypical children [msg #717].
3. **Washington University study (2014).** If both parents score high on an autistic-traits scale, the child's risk of autism rises by 85%; if only one parent does, by 52% [msg #717].

### Autistic parents with autistic vs neurotypical children
The author calls this a trick sub-question: the most important thing to remember is that every autistic person, adult or child, is individual [msg #718].
- In general, research and personal accounts show autistic parents may understand their autistic children better; some say their own experience helps them intuitively understand their autistic child's needs, which eases parenting [msg #718].
- Few articles exist, but the Avaz blog piece she recommends emphasizes that autistic parents can create structured, understanding environments, especially when their children are also autistic, which can foster mutual understanding and support [msg #718].
- However, raising neurotypical children can be **harder** for autistic parents because of differences in perception and communication; it may require extra effort to understand and adapt to neurotypical children's needs [msg #718].
- Many autistic parents struggle with sensory overload from children, especially sounds and touch — so the experience can also be extremely negative [msg #718].

### History: autism is not new
- **Anecdote "for those who say it didn't exist before".** Found while digging into autism's history: Франческо Анне described early mentions of autism, including a story from India. A sheikh named Chilli fell in love with a girl and asked his mother how to make her love him back. She advised him to sit by the stream and, when the girl came for water, "просто бросить в нее камешек и улыбнуться" (*just toss a pebble at her and smile*). The sheikh took the words literally and threw a boulder at her, breaking her head. The author shares it humorously as an illustration of literal interpretation of speech, aimed at people who claim "а раньше такого не было!" (*this didn't exist before!*) [msg #539].
- **Grunya Sukhareva.** Psychiatrist after whom Moscow's Children's Psychiatric Hospital No. 6 is named. She was the first to describe children whose behaviour did not fit manifestations of schizophrenia; her paper was translated into German a year later but then somehow got lost. This was in the 1930s, whereas Asperger's and Kanner's first papers date from 1943–44. The author highlights that such women's contributions are often omitted from articles that focus on men [msg #547].
- **Lorna Wing.** British physician who studied autism in parallel with Kanner and Asperger and almost constantly championed the idea that autism should not be viewed as separate narrow forms or rigidly categorized. She first introduced and popularized the term "Asperger syndrome" for high-functioning autistic people, and from the 1970s proposed the term ASD, which came into use with DSM-5 in 2013. She founded the National Autistic Society in England and raised a daughter with ASD [msg #547].

## Evidence and numbers

Figures the author gives on prevalence and familial risk (no source named for this post) [msg #642]:
- Concordance in identical (monozygotic) twins: **70–90%**; in fraternal (dizygotic) twins: **0–10%**.
- Younger siblings of family members diagnosed with ASD face increased risk, especially younger male siblings.
- In **20–25%** of children or adults with ASD, genetic causes can be identified as de novo mutations (mutations that appeared for the first time).
- For parents of one child with ASD, the risk of the next child having ASD is about **5–10%**.
- The risk is higher (about **7%**) if the child is a girl, and lower (about **4%**) if a boy.
