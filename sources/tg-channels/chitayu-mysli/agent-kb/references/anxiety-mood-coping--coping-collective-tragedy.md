# Coping with terror attacks and collective tragedy

## What this covers

A cluster of posts from late March 2024, right after the terror attack at Crocus City Hall near Moscow (22 March 2024). It contains a forwarded first-aid post on what to do when the news is making you feel bad, the channel author's own reflections after giving a media comment, and a forwarded six-card interview with clinical psychologist Anastasia Afanasyeva for the outlet «Черта» on the psychological consequences of the attack for individuals and society [msg #130, #134, #135]. The source treats it as important because attacks raise anxiety in society "several times over", and how people and society react decides how fast the shared trauma heals — fighting with each other slows it down and, in the author's words, plays into the aim of the attack itself [msg #134, #135, #137].

(!) UNCLEAR: in #134 the channel author says she gave a media comment that day on society's reactions to tragedies like Crocus; the forwarded interview (#135–#140) is with Anastasia Afanasyeva. The source does not say outright that the author and Afanasyeva are the same person. The interview text is therefore attributed to Afanasyeva below, and the #134 reflections to "the author".

## Core ideas

- **Terror attacks create threatening unpredictability, and that drives anxiety.** Like large natural or man-made disasters, an attack shows that ordinary actions can get you or your loved ones killed. This raises anxiety and the sense of being unsafe across society (see *Chain of societal reactions*) [msg #135, #136].
- **People react in different, opposite ways, and the main harm starts when the groups collide.** Some get angry and radicalise, others shut themselves in at home. When these groups meet in the same space, the result is conflict and fragmentation, and the shared trauma heals more slowly [msg #135].
- **Calls for the death penalty, bans and xenophobia are defensive reactions: an illusion of control.** They try to remove unbearable uncertainty, but Afanasyeva says they do not actually reduce crime [msg #136].
- **Dividing people is the point of terror.** The author: attacks are carried out "to frighten, to anger and above all to disunite", so turning our anxiety and anger on each other plays into that aim [msg #134].
- **The pace of healing depends on authorities, society and media.** Witness trauma heals over months to decades. How fast it heals depends on how the authorities and society respond and how the event is presented. Loud spreaders of anxiety and demands for restrictions slow normalisation [msg #137, #138].
- **Limit the information flow; doomscrolling and online fights don't help.** Both the forwarded first-aid post and Afanasyeva say this, and the first-aid post gives concrete steps [msg #130, #139].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### First-aid steps when news of a tragedy overwhelms you
Source: a post the author forwarded from the channel Psycho_pharmacologist on the day of the attack. Purpose: what to do right now if reading the news is making you feel bad. Its starting point is that "думскролинг может скорее навредить, чем помочь" (*doomscrolling may harm rather than help*) [msg #130].
1. **Stop reading.** "Если вы сейчас читаете новости и вам от этого плохо - остановитесь 🛑" (*If you are reading the news now and it makes you feel bad — stop*). Cutting off the stream comes first [msg #130].
2. **Use cold water.** Wash your face with cold water. If it is very hard, fill a basin with water and dip your face into it ("ныряйте в него, пока не станет легче", *dive into it until it gets easier*). Repeat until you feel some relief [msg #130].
3. **Switch to everyday tasks.** Distract yourself with household chores. The post adds a key reframe: "это не избегание ситуации, а сохранение своего ментального здоровья" (*this is not avoiding the situation but preserving your mental health*). Stepping back from the news is presented as a legitimate act of self-care, not a failure to face reality [msg #130].
4. **Connect and comfort yourself through your senses.** Hug your loved ones, play with your dog or cat, put on music [msg #130].
5. **Overall stance: support yourself as much as you can.** "Все, что сейчас происходит, очень страшно и нам важно поддержать себя насколько это возможно" (*Everything happening now is very frightening and it is important for us to support ourselves as much as possible*) [msg #130].

### Chain of societal reactions to a terror attack
Purpose: Afanasyeva's explanation of what happens to a community psychologically right after an attack, and why society splits [msg #135, #136, #138].
1. **Threatening unpredictability.** Terror attacks, like major natural or man-made catastrophes, create "ощущение угрожающей непредсказуемости" (*a sense of threatening unpredictability*). Unpredictability "всегда вызывает очень сильное чувство тревоги и небезопасности" (*always causes a very strong feeling of anxiety and being unsafe*). The forwarding text says anxiety in society rises "в несколько раз" (*several times over*) [msg #135].
2. **Very high uncertainty in daily life.** It turns out that "самые обычные бытовые действия могут привести к тому, что ты или твои близкие могут умереть" (*the most ordinary everyday actions can lead to you or your loved ones dying*). That is a very, very "высокая степень неопределенности" (*high degree of uncertainty*), and people want to get rid of it somehow [msg #136].
3. **Divergent reactions, depending on how each person lives through the tragedy.** Some get angry. Some start to generalise, look for enemies and radicalise; Afanasyeva notes that talk of bringing back the death penalty started right away. Others do the opposite: they don't go out and stay at home [msg #135].
4. **Collision of the groups → conflict and fragmentation.** These different "layers" of society start to collide in the same space, which produces the next reaction: conflicts and "разрозненность" (*fragmentation, disunity*). According to the forwarding text, this collision is "главная проблема" (*the main problem*), because the shared trauma then heals even more slowly [msg #135].
5. **Amplifiers.** Some people are always more prone to anxiety, and they start to spread it "очень громким и высоким голосом" (*in a very loud, high-pitched voice*), online or in public. Afanasyeva says research found that this is what most often sets other people off too. (!) INCOMPLETE: she does not name or describe the studies [msg #135].
6. **Effect on the rest of society.** Fear and anxiety reach some part of society anyway. The question is how much that part acts on the rest. If it actively spreads anxiety and demands bans or restrictions, this slows normalisation and deepens traumatisation, "Потому что на самом деле чем больше ограничений, тем больше тревоги" (*because in reality the more restrictions, the more anxiety*) [msg #138].

### Timeline of witness trauma after a terror attack
Purpose: Afanasyeva's answer to how witness trauma settles in and lives on over the long term [msg #137, #138].
1. **Time periods, loosely defined.** As with any trauma there are phases, but they are "довольно размытые" (*rather blurred*): a month, then several months, a year, decades. The wound slowly scars over. How fast depends on how the authorities and society respond to the tragedy and how it is presented [msg #137].
2. **First reaction: a heightened sense of being unsafe, and attempts to cope with it.** Her example: after the series of Chechnya-related attacks "15 лет назад" (*15 years ago*), people who saw a woman with her head covered in the metro simply got up and left the carriage, because they were afraid. This phase can last from several months to a year [msg #137].
3. **Delayed and prolonged reactions in anxious people.** People who already feel very unsafe have a longer phase, and for them it can lead to mental disorders: depressive episodes, anxiety, avoidance [msg #137].
4. **Long term for direct survivors: PTSD.** People who went through the attack directly may develop PTSD (ПТСР) [msg #138].
5. **Sensitisation even without PTSD.** When they meet a similar situation later, their reaction will be sharper and stronger [msg #138].
6. **Re-activation of past attacks.** A new attack immediately pulls up similar memories and adds to the sense of being unsafe. Her personal example: when this attack happened, the first thing she and a friend remembered was past attacks. Neither of them had been caught in one, "но это было все равно очень близко" (*but it was still very close*) [msg #138]. The channel author describes the same effect in #134: after the interview she remembered every attack of recent years that she had read about or that happened in her lifetime [msg #134].

## Detailed insights

### Radical proposals as an illusion of control
Asked whether talk of the death penalty and xenophobia is also a defensive reaction, Afanasyeva answers "Ну, конечно" (*Well, of course*). Because the situation is so frightening and unpredictable, people want to remove the uncertainty. When they propose radical actions — banning something, punishing someone, bringing in the death penalty — "это просто такая иллюзия того, что такие ситуации можно контролировать в дальнейшем и снизить их количество" (*it is simply an illusion that such situations can be controlled in the future and their number reduced*). She adds that "в реальности это не помогает снизить количество преступлений" (*in reality this does not help reduce the number of crimes*). It is only an attempt to protect oneself and to feel in control of what happens next [msg #136].

### Why this attack (Crocus) was especially shocking
Afanasyeva says this attack differed from earlier ones: it was not an explosion but a cold-blooded shooting of unarmed people. Many people, especially the older generation who grew up with the attacks of the 1990s and 2000s, had a ready script: "если ты видишь людей в автоматах, скорее всего, тебя возьмут в заложники" (*if you see people with assault rifles, you'll most likely be taken hostage*). Most people tried to react according to that script, but the scenario was completely different and unpredictable. This made it a big surprise and raised stress in the moment even further. On top of that, defenceless people were being killed in front of you, and you could not understand what was happening [msg #140].
Her view on preparation: "Я, конечно, считаю, что к этому невозможно подготовиться" (*I of course believe it is impossible to prepare for this*). Even so, people carry "зашитая информация" (*built-in, hard-wired information*): what to do in a panic, during an attack, if taken hostage. Here, "все перебираешь, и все не подходит" (*you go through everything, and none of it fits*). So stored scripts can fail when the scenario is new [msg #140].

### Media, news flow and online fights
- **The media and the news shape reactions.** Asked whether people get "разгоняется" (*wound up*) by media, especially by TV talk of the death penalty, Afanasyeva says "Конечно" (*Of course*). That is why "ограничение этого невероятного потока информации очень-очень важно" (*limiting this incredible flow of information is very, very important*) [msg #139].
- **The amount of information is far greater than 15 years ago.** At the time of the earlier attacks there was not so much information. Now TV, Telegram, YouTube, TikTok — "куда ни зайди, ты получишь огромную порцию информации" (*wherever you go, you'll get a huge portion of information*) [msg #139].
- **Flame wars under unrelated posts.** She noticed people starting "адские срачи" (*hellish flame wars*) under completely innocent posts that had nothing to do with the attack [msg #139]. The author noticed the same thing: in places the internet turns into a "портал ад" (*hell portal*), for example under ASMR videos [msg #134].
- **Venting online does not really help.** People on social media try to vent ("вентилировать") and somehow live through these emotions, "Но это не очень помогает" (*but this doesn't help much*). There are "более рабочие и экологичные навыки регуляции эмоций" (*more effective and healthier emotion-regulation skills*). (!) INCOMPLETE: the interview does not say which skills [msg #139].
- **Fights are contagious.** Flame wars strongly wind other people up too, because at some point you naturally want to join in and dump your own anger, anxiety and resentment [msg #139].

### The author's reflections after giving a media comment
The channel author gave a media comment on society's reactions to tragedies like Crocus. She says the interview stirred up many emotions and memories, and lists four of them [msg #134]:
1. **Sadness at how routine such interviews have become.** In recent years interviews about what to do in emergencies and how to cope with emotions and anxiety have become too frequent; "Я бы предпочла отказаться от этой части своей жизни" (*I would prefer to give up this part of my life*) [msg #134].
2. **Recall of past attacks.** She naturally remembered all the attacks of recent years that she had read about or that happened in her memory. This is the re-activation effect described in the trauma timeline [msg #134].
3. **Tragedies as a lever on public opinion.** While explaining the theory of how such tragedies act on us as a community, she began to wonder how far such events can really be used to steer public opinion and lead society in a direction someone wants. She raises this as a question, not a conclusion [msg #134].
4. **Displacing stress onto others.** Because of how we react to this stress and try to cope with it, we often pour out anxiety, anger and resentment on other people: loved ones, friends, strangers on the internet. She feels torn: on one hand she wants to hug and comfort everyone, on the other "именно для этого же и совершаются теракты. Чтобы напугать, разозлить и главное разобщить" (*this is exactly what terror attacks are carried out for. To frighten, to anger and above all to disunite*) [msg #134].
Her closing point, which she explicitly says is not an "enlightened" conclusion: we are all people with our own reactions, but "ругаться и обвинять друг друга не самый помогающий способ справиться с таким стрессом" (*quarrelling and blaming each other is not the most helpful way to cope with such stress*) [msg #134].
