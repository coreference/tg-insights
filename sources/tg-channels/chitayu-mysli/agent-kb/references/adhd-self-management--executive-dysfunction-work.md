# Executive dysfunction, environment and work

## What this covers

This topic gathers what the author (a specialist who runs ADHD trainings and wrote a book about ADHD) teaches about executive dysfunction: what the term means, the four brain circuits behind it, the related autistic phenomenon of "inertia", and the everyday consequences — the "ADHD tax" and unrealistic planning — illustrated with her own stories. It also covers why neurodivergent people often have an unstable sense of self (brain networks, especially the DMN), why changing environment and context is a key self-management skill, and her basic criteria for choosing a profession and work format with ADHD and/or ASD. According to the author, environment can either help overcome executive dysfunction or worsen it, so understanding the mechanism and shaping the context are practical necessities, not luxuries [msg #64, #332].

## Core ideas

- **Executive dysfunction is the default state in ADHD/ASD, only its intensity varies.** In general it is a state where working memory, motivation, emotional regulation and will "leave" you; in ADHD and ASD they leave "by default", sometimes more, sometimes less. See *Four neural circuits* for the mechanism [msg #64].
- **Symptom profiles differ because the four circuits are damaged to different degrees.** Some people have more working-memory deficits, others more emotional-regulation or time-estimation problems [msg #66].
- **Autistic inertia is a difficulty with starting AND stopping**, not laziness or stubbornness [img #425].
- **ADHD inevitably costs you something ("ADHD tax")**, even with strong hypercontrol, but planning reduces it [msg #60].
- **Long-term planning of time and energy fails in ADHD**; the author's own overbooked weeks and her book written to the wrong outline are cases in point [msg #54, #204].
- **The sense of self is less stable in ADHD/ASD for neurological reasons** — the DMN is not properly suppressed or runs in parallel [msg #733].
- **Environment and context are levers**: leaving home, removing the bed and endless chores from view, makes concentration much easier [msg #332, #534].
- **There is no single right profession for ADHD/ASD**; only basic criteria (flexibility or clear rules, people load, special interest, no overwork/night shifts) plus routine, food, sleep and planned rest [msg #544, #695].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### What executive dysfunction is
The author defines executive dysfunction as a state in which your "normal" cognitive functions have left you, listing:
- working memory (рабочая память);
- motivation (мотивация);
- emotional regulation (эмоциональная регуляция);
- will (воля) — the author ends the list with «и т.д.», so it is open-ended in the source.

In the general population this happens occasionally; in ADHD and ASD these functions leave "by default — just sometimes more, sometimes less" («при СДВГ и РАС, они покидают тебя по умолчанию»). She notes there is a lot of confusion around the term and how it relates to ADHD: everything depends on what we mean by "executive functions" and how they relate to self-regulation. As a result, when people use the term today they often describe an extremely pronounced state in which "your prefrontal cortex refuses to do its job" [msg #64].

Her illustration: you wake up in the morning and realise you literally cannot do anything. Yesterday you were jumping around, having fun and briskly doing your work; today — "рррраз и всё" (bam, and that's it). There will be no work. You simply cannot get out of bed, because you need to "pull yourself together" and organise the process of getting up, having breakfast and the rest of the morning — and you can't. "What work can there be if you can't manage to get out of bed and brush your teeth" [msg #64].

She attributes this to "incorrect" functioning of four neural circuits, joking that the list — "What", "When", "Why", "Who" — sounds like the truly Russian question «кто виноват и что делать» (who is to blame and what is to be done) [msg #64].

### Four neural circuits of executive dysfunction ("What", "When", "Why", "Who")
Purpose: to understand where ADHD symptoms come from and why they differ between people [msg #64, #66].

1. **The What circuit (Контур «Что»).** Runs from the frontal lobe to the basal ganglia, in particular to a structure called the striatum (полосатое тело). It is tied to working memory: it is in this circuit that what we think starts to determine our actions — especially when it comes to plans, goals and the future. A deficit here shows up as working-memory problems [msg #66].
2. **The When circuit (Контур «Когда»).** Runs from the same prefrontal area back to the cerebellum, at the very back of the head. It is the brain's timing scheme: it coordinates not only how smooth behaviour is and its sequence, but also the timeliness of your actions and when certain actions are performed. A malfunctioning "When" circuit in a person with ADHD explains why we so often have trouble managing time [msg #66].
3. **The Why circuit (Контур «Почему»).** Also starts in the frontal lobe and passes through the central part of the brain (the anterior cingulate gyrus, передняя поясная извилина) to the amygdala — the gateway to the limbic system. It is often called the "hot" circuit because it is connected to emotions: here what we think controls what we feel, and vice versa. It is the final decision-maker in all our plans: when we are considering several things we could do, this circuit ultimately picks one based on how we feel about them and on their emotional and motivational properties [msg #66].
4. **The Who circuit (Контур «Кто»).** Runs from the frontal lobe to the very back of the hemisphere. This is where self-awareness happens — where we become aware of what we are doing, how we feel (both inside and outside) and what is happening to us [msg #66].

How to use the model: looking at ADHD through these four circuits lets you understand where symptoms come from. Depending on which circuits are most and which are least affected, each person shows different symptoms: some have a larger working-memory deficit; others have more problems with emotion regulation or with estimating time, but fewer with everything else. "But they are all connected to these neural circuits" [msg #66].

### Three brain networks behind self-identification (DMN, Salience, Executive)
Definition first. The author defines self-identification as «осознанное внутреннее ощущение себя, основанное на переживании собственной уникальности, принадлежности и личного опыта» (a conscious inner sense of oneself based on experiencing one's own uniqueness, belonging and personal experience). It is not only "who I am" but also "how I understand myself", "what I relate myself to" and "how this influences my choices, feelings and behaviour" [msg #733].

Why it matters for neurodivergent people: self-identification can become an important support, especially given late diagnosis, being visibly different from others, and repeated experiences of being misunderstood. Through self-identification we can find words to describe our experience, build a stable connection with ourselves, rethink our path and learn gentle acceptance of our traits [msg #733].

Why it is harder: the process is not just a psychological construct but is conditioned by how the brain is built and works — which is why people with ASD and ADHD have problems with self-identification more often than neurotypical people. In the conventional "norm" several functional brain networks interact [msg #733]:

1. **Default Mode Network (DMN) — the network of the brain's passive mode.** Activates when a person is not focused on external tasks but turned inward: thinking about themselves, the past, the future, other people. It plays the central role in forming the sense of "I" and the continuity of self over time [msg #733].
2. **Salience Network — the network of significance.** Helps determine which stimuli are important and switches attention between internal and external tasks. It participates in deciding which aspects of experience a person includes in their identity and which they filter out [msg #733].
3. **Executive Network — the ventral prefrontal network.** Takes part in self-reflection and conscious control, including making sense of one's behaviour, goals and values [msg #733].

What goes differently in ADHD/ASD: in neurodivergent people these networks work in their own way, especially the DMN. The author repeats what she says she has explained before: in ADHD the DMN is not inhibited as it should be when the other networks activate, or even works in parallel with them. Because of this, our experience, the sense of self and of wholeness can be disrupted, provoking exactly the problems with forming a stable self-identification [msg #733].

### Environment management
The author calls environment management (управление средой) a skill that neurodivergent people need extremely, whether they have ASD or ADHD: environment can either help us overcome executive dysfunction or make it worse. On her ADHD training this is taught directly [msg #332]:

1. **Learn to notice what in your environment destabilises you** — what distracts you, what drains you, what takes your strength away («отвлекает/истощает/забирает ваши силы») [msg #332].
2. **Learn how to change the environment to suit yourself** [msg #332].
3. **Prioritise the workplace** — the author says it is especially important [msg #332].
4. **Start with small steps, not with "declutter the whole apartment".** Do not set yourself the task «разобрать всю квартиру» (sort out the whole flat); begin with your physical desk or the desktop on your computer [msg #332].

Example from abroad: in Western countries, for instance the UK, there are separate specialists who visit families with children with ADHD, inspect their rooms and write recommendations for parents on changing the space so that the child with ADHD can both study and rest more effectively. The author stresses that for adults this is no less relevant than for children [msg #332].

Her own case: one day she, Liza and Masha went to work and write books at a coworking space in Peredelkino. Result: the book plan was written, a chapter for a new anthology was drafted, everything was sent to the editor, and other work tasks were partly done. She is sure that had she stayed home that day, in that weather, she would have spent most of the time sleeping or procrastinating [msg #332].

### Basic criteria for choosing a profession and work format with ADHD/ASD
Context: a reader asked how to tell which profession suits someone with ASD+ADHD, what criteria it should meet, and whether it should be tied to a special interest to live in balance without sliding into hyperfocus and fixating only on that field. The author answers that there is no right or obvious answer: neurodivergent people differ — different symptoms, predispositions, the conditions they grew up and live in (which also affect career choice) and different life values. So, in her view, only basic things can be offered [msg #544]:

1. **Potential flexibility of the job** — with the caveat that many people with ASD do well with work that has a clear schedule and clear regulations [msg #544].
2. **Check how many people tire you.** Your option may be remote or hybrid work so you are not in the office every day. "Опенспейс - ад для всех" (open space is hell for everyone) [msg #544].
3. **A job built around your special interest would be ideal** if you find one [msg #544].
4. **No overtime and no night or 24-hour shifts** — "you simply won't have time to recover" [msg #544].

The overriding rule, whatever job you look for or find: schedule, food, sleep and planned rest — plus tracking your individual sensory profile and overload from people [msg #544].

On office vs remote: when a reader wrote that productivity drops in the office but at home it takes enormous effort to make oneself work, the author answered that it is important to pick what fits your particular presentation of neurodivergence — some find it easier in the office ("oh god, such people exist"), some only remotely, some need hybrid. The question is more about the field of work and the team atmosphere combined with the workload [msg #695].

## Detailed insights

### Autistic inertia
A series of comic cards from watamota.com, reposted by the author, defines autistic inertia (аутичная инерция) as «ЗАТРУДНЁННАЯ СПОСОБНОСТЬ НАЧАТЬ ИЛИ ПРЕКРАТИТЬ КАКОЕ-ЛИБО ДЕЛО» (an impaired ability to start or to stop any activity). It is "a feature of perception and behaviour, and not a person's 'whim', stubbornness or laziness" [img #425]. The card's character says «А Я УЖЕ ВСЁ? ТОЛЬКО НАЧАЛ!» (Am I already done? I only just started!) — showing the stopping side [img #425].

The cards show the paired situations where inertia appears — each a transition that is hard in both directions:
- **Waking up / going to bed** — with the character stalling with "just a sec, just a sec, one second..." (ЩАС, ЩАС, СЕКУНДОЧКУ) [img #424];
- **Taking a break / returning to the task** — on returning, the character says «ЭТО О ЧЁМ ВООБЩЕ? НИЧЕГО НЕ ПОНЯТНО» (what is this even about? I don't understand anything) [img #426];
- **Going into the shower / getting out of the shower** [img #427];
- **Leaving the house / coming back** [img #428];
- **Starting work / finishing work** — at the start "I don't want to! I just sat down to rest!", at the end "I don't want to! I just got into the work!" [img #429].

The lesson of the series: both starting and stopping are hard; once switched into a state (rest, shower, home, work), the person resists leaving it [img #424, #425, #426, #427, #428, #429].

### The "ADHD tax"
The author describes the "ADHD tax" (налог на СДВГ): with ADHD, inevitably — even if you have very strong hypercontrol on top — you start losing or forgetting things or failing to cope. Examples she lists: lost things, abandoned higher-education programmes that were already paid for, plane tickets for flights you were late for, "до бесконечности" (endlessly). Unfortunately, this is the "payment" for having ADHD and the overload that comes with it. Her own examples: she "paid" for a trip to a congress with her wonderful glasses, and for a conference in St Petersburg with her watch — and she has no idea where she lost them [msg #60].

Remedy: a well-planned schedule, a rest schedule and a list of items you check can greatly reduce "our tax" [msg #60].

### Failure of long-term planning (the author's cases)
**Overbooked conference trip.** The author describes what the inability to plan long-term in ADHD looks like: you travel to a conference in St Petersburg; at the same time you have a talk at another conference on Saturday, and the deadlines for submitting recorded lectures for two other courses are burning. You still put the talks and workshops of the conference you plan to attend into your calendar and think: fine, I'll write one lecture on the Sapsan train, finish the rest later, and do "100500" other things in parallel. Outcome: from 8 a.m. she went out only for 15 minutes to a grocery store and back to eat something, spent the whole day writing, giving, writing and recording lectures, still had two left — and did not attend the conference at all. Her closing line: «Мозг, а можно мне НОРМАЛЬНО рассчитывать время и силы?» (Brain, can I please estimate time and energy NORMALLY?) [msg #54].

**Writing a book to the wrong plan.** She calls this her "most ADHD story" of recent years. She was offered a book deal with a very short deadline (about 3 months) and wrote all summer in all her free time from two management jobs and clients — after which burnout hit her, which she says she is still fighting. When she finished the first draft by September and sent it to the editor, it turned out she had been writing to the wrong plan the whole time: her brain had fixated on the first plan, not the final one — although the correct plan sat at the very top of the document she was writing in. "But who are we to look at it and check" [msg #204].

What saved her: the book's original concept — any chapter can be opened and read, not necessarily in order — so the book was quickly reassembled and the needed parts were added. The lesson she draws: a modular structure made the planning error recoverable [msg #204].

Then problems with the publisher and a change of editor stalled the process; when it resumed she began procrastinating desperately: "there was everything" — ADHD combined with already being burned out, shame, impostor syndrome. In the end she completely rewrote the third part of the book, which is how an important (to her) part about women's specifics appeared. She says she still does not quite believe the book exists — in her head it is still somewhere between the wrongly written plan and the long procrastination — and shares the story so readers find it easier to believe that a long project like this can be completed [msg #204].

### Context and leaving home: lessons from a writing trip
During a writing trip the author shared progress (two chapters done, slower and less efficiently than she wanted) and her conclusions [msg #534]:
- **Context matters enormously, especially with ADHD.** Leaving and removing the familiar home setting — with its endless household chores you get distracted by anyway, plus the attractive bed always waiting for you — is a great idea; concentrating becomes much easier [msg #534].
- **Clearing only client hours is not enough to write nonstop and fast.** The rest of the work constantly distracts — both the work itself and thoughts about it — and other tasks that also require writing throw you off badly. As a consequence, she missed a class with students because she forgot to put it into her schedule [msg #534].
- Half-jokingly: there is little oxygen in Moscow, and a runny-nose virus also spoils things [msg #534].

Later, answering a reader who remembered she also found it hard to work during the nature trip, she said that at least back then she was actively writing and made very good progress — whereas now she is not writing it [msg #695].

## Practical playbook

- Reduce the ADHD tax with three tools: a well-planned schedule, a rest schedule, and a checklist of items to verify (see *The "ADHD tax"*) [msg #60].
- Use a modular structure (chapters readable in any order) for long projects so a planning mistake can be fixed by reassembly [msg #204].
- When you need concentrated output, take a dedicated block of time away from home and clear not only client hours but other work obligations too; still put every commitment into the schedule [msg #534].
