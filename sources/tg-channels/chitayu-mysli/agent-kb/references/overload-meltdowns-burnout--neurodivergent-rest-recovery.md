# How neurodivergent people rest and recover

## What this covers

This topic gathers the author's teaching on rest for neurodivergent people (ADHD, autism, also depression and executive dysfunction): what counts as rest, the three kinds of rest and how to combine them, how to learn the hardest one (doing nothing), why everyday rest has to be planned and done "through I-don't-want-to", how to recover after social overload and holidays, how to return to work, the concept of resilience as flexibility and recovery, and the author's annual year-review ritual with a full workbook. The author argues that rest is "unfairly little talked about" and that people with neurodivergence are likely not able to rest well by default, so rest must be learned like a skill [msg #168, #174].

## Core ideas

- **Neurodivergent people genuinely struggle to rest, and procrastination is not rest.** Rest is defined by its goal — restoring strength — not by the absence of work [msg #168].
- **Rest comes in three kinds that must be combined according to one's needs**; the "resource" kind most people call rest is itself tiring and only works on top of the two basic kinds (see *Three kinds of rest*) [msg #168].
- **Doing nothing is a skill learned in tiny increments** and at first brings anxiety, guilt and shame (see *Graduated practice of doing nothing*) [msg #174].
- **Everyday rest has to be planned, especially with ADHD, depression and executive dysfunction**; planning clarifies values and does not kill spontaneity (see *Seasonal rest lists*) [msg #272].
- **After intense socializing, recovery may require silence and solitude** [msg #463].
- **Return to work gradually after any long break** and plan rest and self-reinforcement from day one [msg #470].
- **Resilience means bending and recovering, not enduring**; the Russian "устойчивость" carries a heroic "stand to the end" meaning that loses vulnerability, temporary setbacks and the need for rest [msg #817].
- **A structured yearly review done with a close person** helps "tame" ADHD and focus on what matters, including rest and recovery from burnout [msg #813, #814, #815].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Three kinds of rest
Purpose: to stop treating "not working" (or procrastination) as rest and to deliberately combine different kinds of rest according to current needs. The author frames this "conditionally" ("условно мы можем выделить") [msg #168].

1. **State of stillness, or doing nothing (Состояние покоя или ничегонеделание).** Lying still without sleeping. The author calls it "the hardest exercise if you are not asleep". How: learn from pets — watch how your cat lies and sluggishly looks out the window or simply dozes, and imitate that [msg #168]. How to train it is described in *Graduated practice of doing nothing* below.
2. **Process activity (Процессуальная деятельность).** Activity where "the samurai has no goal, only the path" ("у самурая нет цели, есть только путь"). Examples given: knitting, assembling a diamond mosaic, re-reading books you already know, walking a familiar route on "autopilot". The principle: motor activity works while the brain works minimally. Why: according to the author it helps switch on the brain areas responsible for rest and "unloading" the brain [msg #168].
3. **Resource activity (Ресурсная активность).** The things people often consider rest: going to potter about at the dacha, going hiking, visiting an exhibition, reading a difficult book, watching a new educational film. It gives moral strength, but the author stresses it is "rest after which you need more rest" ("это отдых, после которого нужен ещё отдых") and cannot be "natural". Order rule: first you need the previous two kinds; resource activity comes on top of them [msg #168].

The post ends: this is how we learn to relax, slow down ("тормозиться") and rest [msg #168].

### Graduated practice of doing nothing
Written as a follow-up after many readers wrote about how hard "doing nothing" is [msg #174].
1. **Expect discomfort.** Learning it is really hard; at the start it can cause a lot of anxiety, guilt and even shame, because of the belief that one must be useful and effective non-stop [msg #174].
2. **Start tiny, not with an hour.** Do not try to begin with an hour or another long stretch. The author's comparison: that is like sitting down to meditate with Buddhist monks for a full day without ever having tried meditation; she adds that some people actually get psychoses going off to retreats ("это не шутка" — "this is not a joke"). Start with 30 seconds, 1 minute, 2 minutes, increasing gradually [msg #174].
3. **Handle distraction gently.** Do not scold yourself for getting distracted; simply and softly bring yourself back to the activity [msg #174].
4. **Watch your pets rest.** Observe how they rest — this is both a good mindfulness practice and a way to learn the skill [msg #174].
The attached meme shows the familiar paradox: a caption reads "ничего не сделал" (*did nothing*) all day, followed by "я так усталь" (*I'm so tired*, with a deliberate typo) [img #174].

### Seasonal rest and resource-activity lists
Purpose: to make sure rest and resource activity actually happen, since for everyone — and "especially" people with ADHD, depression and executive dysfunction — rest and resource activity have to be planned and done through "I don't want to" ("через "не хочу"") [msg #272].
- **How:** the author writes lists by season — a plan for summer, for autumn, for the winter holidays, continuing through the year [msg #272].
- **Realistic expectation:** she admits that far from everything gets done, but the lists give a feeling that life is not passing by and that she manages at least something that fills her life besides work [msg #272].
- **Example:** she caught "the last carriage of summer" with a friend at a beautiful picnic, and now has a warm memory that the summer contained such a picnic — even though that morning she did not want to go anywhere and felt "сдалось мне это всё" ("who needs all this"). Lesson: sometimes (or often) this is work for the long term [msg #272].
- **Objection — "then it is no longer rest / where is the spontaneity?":** the author calls this a trap. Making plans and a rough idea of what you want in a given period helps you understand yourself, your values and needs; *how* to carry them out is a separate question. The picnic itself happened spontaneously: "let's go on a picnic tomorrow, since you're on vacation, I have a daytime break between work, and summer isn't over yet" — which she calls maximal spontaneity that she and her friend (who is also autistic) keep training [msg #272].
- Conclusion: plan, plan and plan again everything you can [msg #272].

### Gradual return to work after a long break
For anyone sad about having to start working after the holidays [msg #470]:
1. **Ease in.** The author says scientists advise returning to work after any long break gradually, not throwing yourself straight into a 10-hour workday where possible. Start with planning and prioritization — she stresses that this is also an important part of WORK [msg #470].
2. **Schedule rest and rewards right away.** Immediately plan rest/resource activities and how you will reinforce yourself; she reminds that self-reinforcement is "ОЧЕНЬ ОЧЕНЬ ВАЖНО" ("VERY VERY IMPORTANT") [msg #470].
3. **Expect possible post-vacation blues.** Coming out of a vacation can trigger an almost depressive state: lowered mood, wanting to cry, wanting to quit. It is usually called "post-vacation syndrome or blues". Response: give yourself time to adapt and plan daily and weekly rest — the author says this helps [msg #470].

### Year-review ritual and workbook
Purpose (author's own): to "rein in" her ADHD a little and help focus on what is really needed, including rest and recovery, since her burnout "is not going to leave by itself" [msg #813].

**How the author does it:** for the second year in a row she and a close friend do the year review together at year's end, following the workbook "by the book", writing it "long and tediously". It always takes no less than 3 hours, but helps see the past year more objectively rather than only emotionally. She laughed reading what she wrote to herself a year earlier [msg #813]. The workbook is "nothing amazing" but has many questions, which they like. What she values most is that someone does it with her: alone she could not sit for 3 hours, and a person who knows her well helps recall achievements and difficulties and adds an outside view. She strongly recommends doing it with someone close [msg #814]. The workbook itself also suggests doing it with friends over tasty drinks, sharing memories and building plans, as a way to strengthen friendship and create new warm memories [file #815].

**Her resulting focus for the new year:** besides work tasks, (1) structuring life, including planning, because that skill dropped sharply during the chaotic previous year; (2) driving — she wants to really try driving, although in the moment it causes strong stress. Her closing thesis: without thoughtful goal-setting it is very hard to get where you want [msg #813].

**Workbook contents (2024/2025, by @juu.be)** — stated purpose: to effectively sum up the outgoing year and plan the next one, a tool for pleasant reflection and analysis [file #815].

- **Task 1 — Events and achievements.** Recall and write down no fewer than 10 main events or achievements of the year in each life sphere, including successes that seem insignificant. Spheres: work; family/relationships; friends/social life; rest/impressions; self-development and spirituality [file #815].
- **Task 2 — Wheel of balance (Колесо баланса).** Rate different life spheres on a 1–10 scale: draw a circle, divide it into sectors matching the spheres, rate each. The analysis reveals imbalance and helps plan next year's actions toward more harmony. Example template sectors: hobbies, health, self-realization, family, finances, love, career, friendship [file #815].
- **Task 3 — Letter to the future (Письмо в будущее).** Described as a creative tool of self-reflection and motivation, "a dialogue with your future version". Steps: (1) start with the date of writing and the date you plan to read it; (2) describe your current state — goals, feelings, worries, what you are proud of; be frank with yourself; (3) ask yourself several important questions whose answers you want to know in a year; (4) share your predictions about what will happen in the next year — realistic, but not afraid to dream; (5) give yourself a few pieces of advice you believe will help reach your goals and cope with difficulties. Afterwards put the letter in a safe place where you won't lose it — an envelope, a desk drawer or a special place for important things [file #815].
- **Task 4 — Questions of the year (Вопросы года)**, meant to analyze the year more deeply and draw valuable conclusions [file #815]:
  - How would you describe the year in one sentence?
  - What did you do for the first time?
  - Which achievement of the year are you proudest of?
  - What was the brightest event of the year?
  - What advice would you give yourself a year ago?
  - Which of your actions would you like to repeat in the new year?
  - How has your attitude toward yourself changed?
  - What new did you learn about yourself?
  - What is your main achievement in study or work?
  - Which set goals did you achieve?
  - What do you need to get rid of or let go?
  - What new appeared in your life?
  - Which life spheres need developing?
  - What valuable lesson did you take from the most difficult situation?
  - What can you praise yourself for?
  - 10 warm memories?
  - Best experiment of the year?
  - Which goals should be carried over to next year?
  - What is the most valuable gift you received?
  - What would you do differently, and why?
  - Which goals lost relevance this year?
  - What new skills and abilities did you acquire?
  - Which decision or words would you repeat again?
  - How have your views on life changed?
  - Which events do you remember with a smile?
  - Whom do you need to thank?
  - Which book or film had the greatest impact on you?
  - What is your main victory and your main failure?
  - What success factors can you identify?
  - What three best decisions did you make this year?
- The workbook closes by wishing the answers serve as a compass toward happiness in the coming year and encouraging the reader not to forget goals and dreams [file #815].

## Detailed insights

### Social recovery after holidays
After a "crazy" December 31st, the author spent 2 days lying in the dark without communication, "degrading with my phone"; it was (and still is) hard even to go through the congratulation messages. Her reminder: if you were socially active at the end of December, you may simply need to sit quietly at home, possibly even alone. She returns to social life slowly while unsure it is time yet, and for holiday outings she chose quieter Zvenigorod and advised against going into central Moscow at that time. She closes by wishing readers to rest and do resource activity [msg #463]. Similarly, after the next New Year break she says she was incredibly happy to sit at home "like an owl" ("как сыч") all those days and was sorry it ended [msg #813].

### Resilience as flexibility, not endurance
- **Definition:** resilience (резильентность), literally, is the ability of a system (a person, psyche, community) to adapt to stress, loss or adverse conditions, recover from them and keep functioning without irreversible damage. The author notes this is not new to psychology but is important for building one's life, especially with neurodivergence [msg #817].
- **Translation problem:** it is usually translated as «устойчивость» (stability), «стрессоустойчивость» (stress tolerance), «психологическая устойчивость» (psychological stability), but none of these conveys the original meaning. The author attributes this to a difference in mentality: in English, resilience is about flexibility, about "bouncing back" — recovering, changing under the influence of the environment, not just enduring. In the Russian cultural code, "устойчивость" means holding out no matter what, not breaking, not showing weakness, "standing to the end" like a bogatyr [msg #817].
- **What gets lost:** flexibility and recovery. Originally resilience includes vulnerability, the possibility of a temporary setback and the need for rest — rather than dying on the battlefield like a hero. Hence: the "stable person" in our idea is the one who doesn't fall and doesn't give up; the "resilient" one is the one who can fall and then recover [msg #817].
- In her skills-training group, participants tried to translate the term into one word; her favourite joke option was "терпила" (roughly "a pushover / one who just puts up with everything"), which she acknowledges is not what it means — yet it captures the Russian endurance connotation [msg #817].
- Her invitation: adopt the concept and the term to learn to take better care of yourself and allow yourself a step back, instead of being a "steadfast tin soldier" all the time [msg #817].

### A rest experience for hyper-controllers: the art retreat
- Context: Moscow's pace "hit her on the head" so that she was glued to her chair all week; out of desperation she signed up with an ADHD buddy-coach to do something about her schedule, also seeing it as useful client experience from the other side of ADHD help [msg #557].
- She describes the art retreat as the most pleasant rest experience in a long time: a feeling of being at a children's but comfortable camp. There is a schedule, you are fed well, taken for walks every day, and have classes in drawing, vocals, acting and similar. Everyone around behaves like teenagers and chats about trivia like "what colour do you like" rather than how many patients you saw or what business you opened. She notes she still dislikes the word "retreat", but there was no esotericism [msg #557].
- Acting class gave her several insights, some of which she wants to take into her own work. Her verdict: a super experience for hyper-controllers — people for whom it is hard to relax, feel their body and do strange, illogical things. Rated 10 out of 10 [msg #557].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The three-kinds model is offered "conditionally" (условно); the claim that process activity activates the brain's rest areas is the author's statement with no study cited [msg #168].
- "Scientists advise" a gradual return to work, but no study is named (!) INCOMPLETE [msg #470].
- The warning that some people develop psychoses on retreats is the author's assertion without a source [msg #174]; she also later strongly praises an (art) retreat — not a contradiction, since that one involved no intensive meditation or esotericism [msg #557].
- The author admits many seasonal plans go unrealized [msg #272] and that her own burnout persists [msg #813]; the outcome of the buddy-coaching is unknown [msg #557].
- The workbook PDF transcription is OCR'd; some words were garbled (e.g. Task 1 heading lost), but all listed content was legible [file #815].
