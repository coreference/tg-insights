# Emotional dysregulation, rejection sensitivity and relationships

## What this covers

This topic gathers the author's posts and Q&A answers about the emotional side of neurodivergence: what "rejection sensitive dysphoria" (RSD) is claimed to be and why the author doubts it is a separate condition; why people with autism, ADHD, trauma or high sensory sensitivity often feel emotions very intensely and what helps; why it is worth learning to notice emotions even when one barely feels them (autism with emotional hyposensitivity); and two relationship questions — a partner's anger outbursts and a man who blamed his infidelity on ADHD. Across these, the author keeps one line: emotional differences are real and not a weakness, but building regulation skills and making choices stays each person's own responsibility [msg #280, #681, #698, #707, #866].

## Core ideas

- **RSD adds nothing new.** Its signs are already explained by the CBT model of ADHD and the bio-psycho-social model; research is almost absent; the remedy is the same as for emotional dysregulation in general (see *Author's three objections*) [msg #280].
- **Neurodivergent emotional sensitivity is neurophysiological, not "character".** Dysregulation is common in ADHD and autism, and it is a feature of sensory and emotional regulation that can be learned to recognise and support (see *What helps*) [msg #681].
- **Emotions are a signal system, like pain sensitivity.** Both a muted and an over-weighted signal are harmful, so we learn to tell which emotions mislead and which report something important (see *Emotions as a signal system*) [msg #707].
- **Regulation is the person's own job.** A partner can show love, but learning anger control belongs to the person with ADHD; likewise ADHD does not decide whether someone cheats — the person does [msg #698, #866].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Proposed signs of RSD (8-item list)
The author opens by defining RSD: «**Дисфория чувствительности к отвержению (RSD)** — это когда вы испытываете сильную эмоциональную боль из-за неудачи или чувства отверженности» (*Rejection sensitive dysphoria (RSD) is when you experience intense emotional pain from failure or a feeling of being rejected*). It is most often linked to ADHD, and experts suspect it arises from differences in brain structure. The author then asks «есть она всё-таки или нет?» (*does it actually exist or not?*) and lists the signs that are offered as describing what people with RSD face [msg #280]:
1. They can very easily feel embarrassed or awkward [msg #280].
2. They show signs of low self-esteem and problems believing in themselves [msg #280].
3. They find it hard to contain their emotions when they feel rejected; this is often visible in children and teenagers with the condition [msg #280].
4. Some may react with sudden displays of anger or rage, while others may burst into tears [msg #280].
5. Instead of losing control of their emotions outwardly, some people with RSD may direct their feelings inward [msg #280].
6. They often "please people" («угождают людям») and try very hard to avoid others' disapproval [msg #280].
7. They may avoid starting projects, tasks or pursuing goals where failure is possible [msg #280].
8. They compensate for the fear of failure or rejection by giving their all or striving for perfectionism; the downside is that they often feel strong anxiety and cannot easily make self-care or downtime a priority [msg #280].

### Author's three objections to RSD as a separate condition
The author says several things personally bother her («Меня лично смущают несколько вещей», *a few things personally bother me*) [msg #280]:
1. **Existing models already explain every sign.** All these signs are explained by the CBT model of ADHD and by the bio-psycho-social model. These have a biological part (high impulsivity and difficulties with emotional regulation) and a part tied to experience of upbringing and failures, through which we acquire core beliefs («глубинные убеждения») about ourselves and the world. So no new entity is needed to account for them [msg #280].
2. **There is practically no research.** According to the author, PubMed returns no studies on this topic at all, other sources give vague references, and there is essentially one doctor, William Dodson, who has been pushing («педалирует») the topic since 1990 [msg #280].
3. **What is the function of singling it out?** The author grants that, most often, our experience of failure and rejection is extremely painful because of this, and that many people have emotional dysregulation problems — but far from all people with ADHD. It again depends on experience, upbringing and the combination of individual ADHD traits, so labelling it as a distinct condition does not add explanatory value [msg #280].

Conclusion: «ничего нового и революционного не происходит, расходимся» (*nothing new or revolutionary is happening, let's disperse*). What helps is still the same: individual therapy, emotional regulation skills, and accepting that people may reject and not understand us [msg #280].

### What helps with neurodivergent emotional sensitivity (4 supports)
Context: many people with autism, ADHD, traumatic experience or heightened sensory sensitivity are emotionally sensitive, «Иногда — очень» (*sometimes very*) — contrary to the myth that autistic people have no emotions [msg #681]. The author lists what helps:
1. **Normalisation** — telling oneself: «мне не нужно объяснять, почему мне сейчас тяжело — я просто так устроен(а)» (*I don't need to explain why it's hard for me right now — I'm just built this way*) [msg #681].
2. **Regular "safety pauses"** («паузы безопасности») throughout the day [msg #681].
3. **Support from loved ones that does not demand explanations** [msg #681].
4. **Self-regulation skills** — breathing, movement, simple sensory markers («простые сенсорные маркеры») [msg #681].
(!) INCOMPLETE: the source names the supports but does not describe how long safety pauses last or what counts as a "sensory marker".

The guiding idea: «Эмоциональная чувствительность — не ошибка, а сигнал» (*emotional sensitivity is not a mistake but a signal*); if you learn to interact with it rather than suppress it, it becomes not an enemy but a navigator. Personally, the author says what helps her recover is dogs — contact with them, their unconditional love and joy in the world — and of course walks [msg #681].

### Emotions as a signal system (pain-sensitivity analogy)
Answer to an autistic reader (diagnosed ASD, apparently emotionally hyposensitive) whose psychotherapist advised tracking emotions; she felt resistance because she usually feels «ровно-спокойно» (*even and calm*) and asked whether one needs to dig into emotions and why [msg #707].
1. **Starting fact:** people with ASD indeed most often have much lower understanding of their own emotions than non-autistic people [msg #707].
2. **Against the pop-psychology cult:** in modern psychological pop culture emotions are sometimes raised to the skies — everything must be felt, lived through and expressed, "and God forbid you don't" [msg #707].
3. **The body's view:** emotions are «просто одна из сигнальных систем нашего тела. Не больше и не меньше» (*just one of our body's signal systems. No more, no less*). Evolutionarily it does not pay to think everything through cognitively — "you'd be eaten while thinking" — and emotions let us react fast. They also have a social function, which the author says she will cover separately [msg #707].
4. **The analogy:** emotions are like pain sensitivity. It is bad when it is muted — you can get burned; it is bad when too much weight is given to it — it can seem to hurt and sting where everything is fine [msg #707].
5. **Purpose of tracking:** that is why we learn to distinguish emotions — to understand where they lie to us («подвирают») and where they report something important [msg #707].

## Detailed insights

### How neurodivergent emotional sensitivity shows up
The author cites three points as "what science says" [msg #681]:
- Emotional dysregulation (difficulties managing emotions) occurs in 30–70% of adults with ADHD [msg #681].
- Autistic people often show both heightened emotional intensity and a delay in becoming aware of their emotions [msg #681].
- Sensory overload directly affects emotional reactions, especially combined with anxiety or fatigue [msg #681].
Hence, she says, it is not "character" and not "winding oneself up" («накрутил себя») but a neurophysiological feature of emotion processing [msg #681].

Manifestations listed [msg #681]:
- A person may flare up sharply over a trifle — and not understand why themselves.
- Emotions "stick": the situation is over, but inside it is still noisy.
- It can be hard to put into words what exactly is being felt.
- Or the opposite: the feeling comes down like an avalanche, as if everything at once.
The author stresses there is no weakness in this: it is not "drama", "manipulation" or "infantilism" but a feature of sensory and emotional regulation that can be learned to recognise and support [msg #681].

### A partner's anger outbursts in ADHD
A reader wrote that her boyfriend with ADHD has anger problems: they fight often because he dislikes something in her words or actions, he is often irritated, and in fights he fixates on one topic and cannot move off it even when the discussion goes in circles. She walks "as if on a minefield", fearing the next outburst, though she knows he loves her and is ashamed of it. She asked whether this is normal for ADHD and how to behave and help [msg #698].
The author's answer: emotional dysregulation in ADHD is fairly common, and most often it shows exactly as bursts of irritability and anger. But fighting dysregulation is the person's own task: the partner can show that she loves him in any case, while «освоение навыков контроля гнева ответственность вашего партнёра» (*mastering anger-control skills is your partner's responsibility*) [msg #698].

### ADHD does not excuse infidelity
A reader saw a post in which a man justified cheating by having ADHD and asked whether it is really impossible with ADHD to stay faithful and build stable, lasting relationships [msg #866]. The author's answer:
- Cases like this are why many specialists and lay people start either devaluing or getting angry at people with ADHD [msg #866].
- "Of course not": even if you are impulsive and have regulation problems, «все равно только ты выбираешь как тебе поступать» (*it is still only you who chooses how to act*) [msg #866].
- Second, there are skills that help you build your life [msg #866].
- Her ironic close: «Так то удобно спихнуть измены на СДВГ» (*it's convenient, of course, to dump infidelity on ADHD*). She notes she gave a whole interview on this subject on another channel [msg #866].
