# Physical comorbidities: sleep, pain, migraine and eating

## What this covers

This topic gathers the channel author's posts and answers to readers on the bodily problems that often come with autism (РАС) and ADHD (СДВГ): pain felt in dreams and nightmares (and its link to PTSD), migraine, chronic muscle pain (sensory overload, hypermobility, fibromyalgia), connective tissue dysplasia, sleep problems in autistic adults, daytime sleepiness, ADHD sweet cravings and overeating, and sensory eating difficulties, including the author's own trial of medical nutrition drinks. The author presents these problems as common among neurodivergent people and often unnoticed by them ("могут о ней не догадываться" — they may not suspect it), so recognising them and getting to the right doctor matters [msg #543, #185, #472].

Much of the material is the author's own experience (nightmares with pain, migraine attacks, torn ligaments, being unable to eat during burnout) combined with her reading of research; she is open that research on several of these questions is thin or inconclusive [msg #8, #315].

## Core ideas

- **Pain in nightmares is linked to PTSD.** Nightmares with physical pain are most common in PTSD; normally pain is dulled in sleep; dream pain comes from the dream's content, not a real stimulus [msg #8].
- **Migraine is much more common in autistic adults** (42% vs 10%), especially autistic women, and autistic people, nonverbal ones in particular, struggle to recognise and describe its symptoms. Migraine is a chronic condition that needs a neurologist [msg #185].
- **Chronic muscle pain is common among neurodivergent people, but nobody knows the exact cause.** The author sorts it into three variants and advises tracking muscle pain as a sign that you are tired and need to recover [msg #315].
- **Connective tissue dysplasia may be linked to autism and mental disorders.** A reader's research summary and a 2022 study frame ASD partly as a connective-tissue problem [msg #543].
- **Autistic sleep problems have three sources**: a brain that won't rest at night, late melatonin, and sensory discomfort in bed. Usual advice doesn't work; regular hours, personal trial-and-error and a doctor do [msg #472].
- **Daytime sleepiness in ASD/ADHD** comes from high fatigability and from raised rates of sleep disorders [msg #541].
- **ADHD sweet cravings have two different causes** (the brain's need for glucose vs impulsive dopamine-driven eating), and each needs a different response [msg #607].
- **Eating difficulties in ASD/ADHD can be sensory overload**, not an eating disorder; when tired or overloaded the author can only tolerate soft, dense, mild-tasting food. Self-knowledge of one's sensory profile is lifelong [msg #838].
- **Medical nutrition drinks can carry you through bad days** when nothing else goes down [msg #839, #862].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Four findings on pain in dreams
The author woke up feeling physical pain after being killed in a nightmare, and searched for why we feel physical pain in sleep. She says she has been "замучали кошмары, особенно с физическими ощущениями" (worn out by nightmares, especially ones with physical sensations) and that not much research has been done on this [msg #8]. What she found:
1. **Pain nightmares are most frequent in PTSD.** She calls this the most obvious finding: nightmares with physical pain occur most often in PTSD [msg #8].
2. **Normally pain is dulled during sleep.** This dulling gives relief to people with chronic pain [msg #8].
3. **Dream pain has a different source from "real" pain.** Its source is the content of the dream, not a stimulus from real life. She adds that pain is mostly processed by the brain, not at the site of the injury, which makes dream pain logical [msg #8].
4. **What happens in the brain.** Nightmares are associated with loss of grey-matter volume in anterior limbic/paralimbic structures that are sensitive to PTSD, and with functional changes in areas involved in controlling REM sleep. In her reading this confirms a link between PTSD and disturbed sleep [msg #8].
Her conclusion, half-joking: "похоже придется и дальше просыпаться от того, что тебя убили во сне" (it seems I'll have to keep waking up from being killed in my sleep), meaning the findings explain the phenomenon but offer no fix [msg #8].

### Six signs of migraine in autistic people
Purpose: people with ASD, especially nonverbal people, often find it hard to understand and put migraine symptoms into words, so others (and they themselves) can watch for these behavioural signs [msg #185]:
1. Changes in eating habits [msg #185].
2. Nausea or vomiting [msg #185].
3. A sudden need to be in a dark place (photophobia, светобоязнь) [msg #185].
4. A sudden need to be in a quiet place (phonophobia, фонофобия) [msg #185].
5. Repeated touching of or hitting the head [msg #185].
6. Eye movements or problems that point to an aura: tunnel vision, blind spots or coloured spots [msg #185].
Next step: migraine is a chronic condition that needs treatment by a neurologist. The author urges readers bothered by headaches not to be afraid to go to a цефалголог (headache specialist) and get checked for migraine [msg #185].

### Three variants of chronic muscle pain associated with neurodivergence
Background: after asking her readers, the author was surprised how many neurodivergent people have chronic muscle pain. Her research gave two results: there are many such people, and nobody yet knows exactly what it is linked to [msg #315]. The variants:
1. **Pain from sensory overload.** Her proposed mechanism, marked by herself with a question mark: you get very tired from sensory load, brain and body become markedly fatigued, and pain receptors are activated. She says this isn't fully understood and she will keep looking [msg #315].
2. **Hypermobility syndrome.** Hypermobility itself puts a large load on the muscles, which causes pain. Here she cites Asztély et al.: 77% of neurodivergent women had chronic pain, and fibromyalgia was described in almost a quarter of people with ASD [msg #315].
3. **Fibromyalgia.** Not everyone has it (she gives "всего 2-3%", only 2–3%). The criteria are fairly strict: pain must last at least 3 months in a row. She also found a lot of data on people who complain of similar symptoms that do not fully meet fibromyalgia criteria, and it is unclear where to classify them [msg #315].
Practical takeaway: whatever the cause, track your muscle pain as a symptom that you are tired and urgently need to recover [msg #315].

### Three problems of autistic sleep and three things to do
From the post "Сон, аутизм и ода ночным совам" (Sleep, autism and an ode to night owls). The author compares sleep for an autistic adult to a trip to IKEA: you go in for one thing and come out three hours later, tired, but without a bed. Her point is that the problem is not choosing the wrong pillow [msg #472].

The problems:
1. **"Ночь — это моя зона комфорта" (Night is my comfort zone).** Many autistic adults complain that at night the brain doesn't rest but throws a party: new ideas, things to think about, an awkward situation from 7 years ago. You lie there counting the moments when you could have said something smarter instead of counting sheep. She says advice like "drink warm milk" or "read boring books" doesn't work here [msg #472].
2. **"Свет, камера, мелатонин!" (Lights, camera, melatonin!).** In many autistic people the brain "doesn't like" melatonin. The sleep hormone arrives late, so autistic people only start yawning when others are already asleep. Melatonin tablets are unreliable for some ("то сработает, то нет", sometimes it works, sometimes not), but she says recent meta-analyses show the problem really is melatonin [msg #472].
3. **Sensory hell.** For many autistic people "Which pillow do you like?" sounds like a provocation: the pillow is too hard, the blanket too hot, the bedding rustles too much. Night becomes a test drive of fabrics, temperatures and positions, and the sound of a fridge in the next room becomes the main villain of the night [msg #472].

What to do:
1. **A regular schedule helps, but…** Regular sleep times do help. The caveat is that life gets in the way: you plan to go to bed at 11 and end up watching videos about odd octopus facts until 3 a.m. [msg #472].
2. **Keep trying until you find your own remedy.** Some are helped by white noise (the author personally loves it), some by a warm shower before bed, some by reading the tax code. The key is to find what specifically "switches you off" [msg #472].
3. **See a doctor, sometimes a sleep specialist (сомнолог) directly.** The problem might have a simpler solution: for example, polysomnography can reveal sleep apnea, and that may turn out to be the cause [msg #472].
Closing advice: if insomnia seriously troubles you, go to a doctor to find out what it is actually linked to, and try melatonin [msg #472].

### Causes of daytime sleepiness in ASD/ADHD
A reader's question: a support group found that many members have attacks of daytime sleepiness; is ASD to blame, and is there a link between ASD/ADHD and hypersomnia? The author's answer lists possible causes [msg #541]:
1. **High fatigability.** Many people with ASD/ADHD tire very easily, so they may badly want to sleep during the day (and not only then; her favourite: "встал и уже устал", got up and already tired) [msg #541].
2. **Sleep disorders.** Neurodivergence is often accompanied by sleep disturbances, so there is a raised likelihood of narcolepsy, or of other sleep disorders (apnea, insomnia, restless legs syndrome) that lower the quality of night sleep, so the person is sleepier in the day [msg #541].

### Two-cause method for ADHD sweet cravings and overeating
A reader asks what a person with ADHD can do to stop overeating and snacking, since the brain sometimes demands sweets and fighting it is very hard. The author jokes "nothing", then gives a method: work out the cause of the overeating in each specific case, then respond to that cause [msg #607].
1. **Cause 1: the brain needs glucose.** A strong sweet craving may mean you are under heavy mental load right now and the brain is asking for glucose. Her reasoning: neurons are almost the only cells in the body that can't build up a glucose reserve, so they only have glucose from the blood; without glucose there is no energy and the cells suffer and die. **Response:** eat, and eat more than just sweets, so the brain gets enough nutrition [msg #607].
2. **Cause 2: impulsive overeating.** People with ADHD are prone to impulsive overeating because (a) food gives fast dopamine reinforcement, and (b) food is always a clear, pleasant activity when you are bored or procrastinating. **Response:** the STOP skill (навык СТОП) helps a lot to stop and switch to other activities, along with ADHD skills for coping with procrastination [msg #607].

### Sensory-overload eating difficulty (self-observed pattern and two conclusions)
The author's own case. When she was first considering that she might be autistic, Maria asked whether she had any difficulties with food, and she could only think of lifelong inability to eat mushrooms (because of the texture) [msg #838].

In fact she had nearly always had trouble accepting food, and years of therapy didn't fix it. Two things were going on: (a) the classic pattern of poorly sensing hunger and, ADHD-style, forgetting to eat; and (b) something she couldn't explain: simply being unable to eat. She stresses she never had an eating disorder (РПП) and likes a variety of foods, just not the food she herself put in the fridge yesterday or that morning. She could walk hungry around the fridge and be unable to make herself eat [msg #838].

The vicious circle she describes: you are hungry and don't eat → you start to feel nauseous → eating becomes even harder → you are even hungrier [msg #838].

The insight: as soon as she is tired or overloaded, she can't tolerate any food texture except something moderately soft and dense with a mild taste; for her that is soft curd snacks (творожки) and bananas. She identifies this as sensory overload plus tactile-gustatory hypersensitivity, and says it turned out to be "банально просто" (banally simple) [msg #838].

Her two conclusions:
1. You may think you already understand your sensory profile, but because of masking or reduced interoception you may still be missing some overload moments. Exploring yourself is truly lifelong [msg #838].
2. Food problems are very common in ASD and ADHD, but can be so unobvious that people are even diagnosed with and treated for an eating disorder. The topic needs to be explored very gently if there are difficulties [msg #838].
Open end: her next plan is to work out what else she can eat, since you can't live on bananas for long [msg #838].

## Detailed insights

### Migraine and autism: prevalence
According to the latest research and statistics the author cites, migraine is much more common in autistic adults (42%) than in the general population (10%). As in the general population, women are three times more likely than men to have migraine, so autistic women are especially prone to it. She wrote the post during a severe migraine attack that "paralysed" her for almost the whole day [msg #185].

### Connective tissue dysplasia and neurodivergence
A reader wrote that about ten years ago she and her daughter were diagnosed with undifferentiated connective tissue dysplasia (ДСТ), which at its most basic is a disorder of collagen synthesis. She remembered reading that people with ДСТ are more prone to mental disorders than the population average, and she noticed people with both ASD and ДСТ in the chat, although people usually don't know they have the diagnosis. Her search found [msg #543]:
- Two Russian-language papers, both in paediatrics. In one, children from a psychoneurological clinic (ПНД) were counted by disorder to see what percentage also had confirmed ДСТ; the total came to 51% of all of them [msg #543].
- The other Russian paper studied how ДСТ and ASD are specifically linked [msg #543].
- One English paper that tried to diagnose ASD from anomalies in lung structure [msg #543].
- All the papers recommended that paediatricians who suspect or confirm ДСТ also refer the child to neurologists and psychiatrists. The papers are relatively recent, from the 2020s [msg #543].

The author calls this a problem neurodivergent people often face without suspecting it, and links it to her chronic muscle pain post (see *Three variants of chronic muscle pain*). She says she has suspected ДСТ in herself since medical school: all her life she has torn ligaments, and in 7th grade one cervical vertebra slipped off the second when she just reached for a towel. She also mentions a 2022 study whose authors suggest looking at ASD as partly a connective-tissue disorder, with consequences for brain function [msg #543].

### Nutrition mixes for people who can barely eat
During a flare-up of autistic burnout the author almost stopped eating altogether, and readers wrote to her about low body weight from problems with food and water. In despair she tried an option Maria (whom she calls an excellent specialist in eating problems) had recommended [msg #839].

What "неешки" (non-eaters) can use:
- **Protein.** The obvious first option, but she personally can't stand (1) the aftertaste of protein and (2) a smoothie-like texture, which is also why kefir doesn't work for her. She jokes that bananas with animal protein would solve her problem [msg #839].
- **Medical nutrition mixes (liquid and dry)**, used for people with eating disorders, cancer and food problems in general. Most often these are Nutrilak products, but there are others. She ordered ones with a cooling ("с холодком") flavour, which appealed to her because they are made for people whose taste perception is impaired [msg #839].

Result of the test: she liked Nutridrink overall, especially the version for people with heightened or distorted taste perception. For days when nothing goes down and the body feels bad, she calls it a really good option, and keeps a small stock in the fridge for bad days. The photo shows the product: Nutricia Nutridrink Compact Protein, in a cool red-fruit/berry flavour (packaging text in Scandinavian languages, e.g. "Cool red frugtsmag") [msg #862, img #862].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The author notes that research on pain in dreams is limited [msg #8].
- The mechanism for sensory-overload muscle pain is her own guess, marked with a question mark; nobody knows the exact cause of chronic muscle pain in neurodivergent people [msg #315].
- (!) UNCLEAR: "всего 2-3%" for fibromyalgia: the source doesn't say 2–3% of whom (general population or neurodivergent people). This sits uneasily with the Asztély figure of almost a quarter of people with ASD having fibromyalgia, which appears in the same post [msg #315].
- People whose symptoms resemble fibromyalgia without meeting its criteria remain unclassified [msg #315].
- Melatonin: the author says tablets work only sometimes but meta-analyses point to melatonin as the cause; she still recommends trying it [msg #472].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: a third cause of daytime sleepiness was forgotten by the author [msg #541].
- The ДСТ material is a reader's summary of studies, not checked by the author; her own ДСТ is a suspicion, not a diagnosis [msg #543].
- The glucose explanation of sweet cravings is the author's reasoning; the STOP skill is named but not explained [msg #607].
- The eating pattern is the author's self-observation, and she warns such problems can be wrongly treated as eating disorders [msg #838].
- Nutrition mix verdict is a single personal trial [msg #862].
