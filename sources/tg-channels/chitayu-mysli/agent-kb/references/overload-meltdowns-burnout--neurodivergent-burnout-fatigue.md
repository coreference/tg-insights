# Autistic and ADHD burnout, fatigue and limited energy

## What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel teaches about chronic fatigue and burnout in autistic (РАС/ASD) and ADHD (СДВГ) people: the mechanisms that make them tire faster, the definition and course of autistic burnout and how to tell it from depression, a forwarded model of the ADHD work-burnout cycle with supporting studies, the author's candid account of living close to burnout herself, a concrete ChatGPT setup she used to offload executive functions while burnt out, and her advice to count energy instead of tasks. The source frames this as important because constant exhaustion is "the most frequent complaint" of neurodivergent people and is often misdiagnosed, sometimes leading to inappropriate psychiatric treatment [msg #88, #155].

## Core ideas

- **Neurodivergent people have a smaller "battery" and a larger nervous-system load**, so tiring more than others is normal, not a failing; they are people with special needs who tire faster and need more rest (see *Why neurodivergent people tire faster*) [msg #80, #88].
- **Fatigue is not laziness or poor organisation**: resources are objectively smaller, so the question is not "how to get everything done" but "what drains me most" [msg #805].
- **Autistic burnout is a distinct state, not depression**: it follows months of overload, involves temporary skill loss (especially communication), takes months to recover from, and medication helps little — so prevention matters (see *Autistic burnout*) [msg #155, #617].
- **ADHD burnout at work follows a repeating cycle** driven by dopamine-seeking novelty, praise, then depletion, shame and procrastination; executive-function deficits are the key mediator (see *The ADHD work-burnout cycle*) [msg #765].
- **There is no magic way** to be neurodivergent, super-productive by neurotypical standards and feel well — the author herself has not solved this and openly frames it as an unresolved dilemma between suffering from non-fulfilment and suffering from constant overload [msg #420, #770, #793].
- **Plan by energy, not by to-do count**; one big finished task beats five small unfinished ones [msg #805].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Why neurodivergent people tire faster
Purpose: explain the chronic exhaustion that neurodivergent people describe, so it is not misattributed. The author says constant, exhausting tiredness is the most common complaint you hear from neurodivergent people, and it leads to "a mass of interesting diagnoses" — ВСД, asthenia, vitamin deficiency, even depression — "whatever imagination allows" [msg #88].

1. **ADHD: constant switching between distraction and concentration.** With ADHD the brain keeps going "distracted – concentrates – distracted – concentrates". What costs other people a small amount of energy and actions is "huge work" for a person with ADHD — "literally". The author's image: you are not simply walking from point A to point B, but on the way you visit 30 more points far off the direct route, and not even in order [msg #88].
2. **Autism: processing a flood of high-intensity sensory information.** With ASD the brain simultaneously processes a mass of information that is highly intense for it: every touch of clothing on the skin, light reflecting off snow, the rustle of a bag, body and ambient temperature. If on top of that you have to communicate with people, the load on the brain multiplies instantly [msg #88].
3. **Result: tired without visibly doing anything.** From the outside you seem not to be doing much, yet you get exhausted "как собака сутулая" (like a hunched dog — a jokey idiom for being dead tired). The accompanying image says "Я ОЧЕНЬ УСТАЛ ПОСТОЯННО УСТАВАТЬ ОТ СВОЕЙ УСТАЛОСТИ" (I am very tired of constantly being tired of my tiredness) [msg #88, img #88].
4. **Conclusion: treat it as a special need.** It is important to remember that neurodivergent people are people with special needs: they tire faster, need more rest, and also need their non-obvious needs to be met [msg #88].

The author repeats the same point in #805: neurodivergent people spend a great deal of energy on sensory signals and task switching, which is why even cleaning is "a whole big task" [msg #805]. In #80 she phrases it as: the neurodivergent "battery" is often much smaller than other people's, and the load on the nervous system much greater, so tiring more than others is normal [msg #80].

### Autistic burnout — definition, course and differential from depression
Purpose: recognise autistic burnout and avoid confusing it with depression.

1. **Status of the term.** There is not yet an official rubric for it in the classifications; the term was singled out fairly recently, but there is already research and an understanding that this state differs strongly from other rubrics and cannot be assigned to them [msg #155]. In April 2025 the author added that over the last 5 years about 40 studies on it have appeared — she checked PubMed "literally yesterday" [msg #617].
2. **Definition and trigger.** It is a state that develops after prolonged — usually several months of — overstrain and overload in an autistic person [msg #155].
3. **Symptoms.** Not just apathy and fatigue: the person may suddenly and temporarily lose skills, especially communication skills. Example: any communication — even the idea of it — causes strong aversion, crying, despair, up to losing the physical ability to speak [msg #155].
4. **Key difference from depression.** It is often mistaken for a depressive episode. The main differentiating feature is the **absence of a persistently lowered mood for most of the day over more than 14 days** [msg #617].
5. **Why the confusion matters.** The author has seen people hospitalised in psychiatric hospitals during such a state, where "all the schemes in the world" were tried and did not work, because pills cannot help much in this case [msg #155].
6. **Recovery and prevention.** Getting out of autistic burnout is not easy and takes time — again, usually several months — so it is very important to work on preventing it [msg #155].

Related: a reader asked about "ВСД" under extreme stress or sensory load in autistic people (she had a year of accumulated stress plus a series of sad events). The author answered that if this means "внутренний сенсорный дисбаланс" (internal sensory imbalance), then (1) it is not a separate condition but an exacerbation of the person's ASD sensory profile against the background of stress, and (2) most likely the person has autistic burnout and must deal with that, not only with the sensory side. In general this means removing load as much as possible, especially social load, and minimising sensory load [msg #701].

### The ADHD work-burnout cycle (5 stages)
Source: a post forwarded from the channel NeuroDive | РАС и СДВГ. Purpose: explain why people with ADHD frequently burn out and change jobs, and whether this is an ADHD feature rather than a general "zoomer" trend — the post answers that it is indeed a tendency seen in people with ADHD [msg #765].

1. **Pleasant novelty period = a sharp dopamine release.** The person dives very actively into the new job, gets to know the team, is inspired by the tasks and often takes on far more load than needed and than they can handle (e.g. answering work chats at night). Why: at the least because they more easily catch hyperfixation on something new and interesting [msg #765].
2. **Reinforcement of the behaviour.** The person starts being praised for initiative, responsibility and the other "-ities" the company values so much. This gives new portions of dopamine [msg #765].
3. **Motivation drop, errors and anxiety.** As soon as the energy runs out (quite quickly in this mode), lateness, forgetting and careless mistakes begin, and anxiety rises sharply. For a while the person holds on through shame: they were just praised, now they are letting people down, and they "should be coping!" [msg #765].
4. **Procrastination.** It keeps growing; staying late in the evenings and working weekends become more frequent; less and less time is left for rest. Dopamine is already at zero, and forcing oneself to work and concentrate gets ever harder [msg #765].
5. **Burnout.** At some point everything starts breaking — both the tasks and the person — and shame and anxiety rise sharply. After a while this leads to burnout and disappointment in oneself / the job / the profession [msg #765].

**After the cycle:** then comes recovery — though more often just "eating/drinking the problem away" to hide it deeper, hoping it passes on its own — and the vicious circle closes. Many people with ADHD go through more than one such cycle. The end result: a constant feeling that one must be better, faster, stronger so as not to let others down, with the background "favourite records" of "high anxiety and shame" and "perfectionism is my middle name", which feed a hyperfocus on approval, a desire for quick success and unstable self-esteem [msg #765].

**Takeaways stated by the post:**
- Constantly maintaining the image of a good employee and overworking demand super-efforts from executive function, which in many people with ADHD is weakened to begin with [msg #765].
- Sensory sensitivity — especially if high — can "eat up" remaining resources, forcing a person to compensate by any means even when the body "can't pull it" anymore [msg #765].
- Burnout in people with ADHD requires constant prevention; one must pay very close attention to the quantity and quality of rest, which many people with ADHD find hard as it is [msg #765].

### ChatGPT project setup for offloading executive function during burnout (5 steps)
Context: in May 2025 the author was hit by autistic burnout plus a pile of health problems; the number of things she *could* do fell to a minimum while the number she *had* to do did not decrease, which she found very hard to bear. In the first week she switched off all personal chats for a week and cut all contacts except work ones; from the next week she brought everything back, but replying to people and entering communication, especially non-work, remained hard. In this situation she "discovered the potential of AI" — half a year earlier a friend had laughed at her for not using AI, and she did not understand why she would need it [msg #661].

What she started using it for [msg #661]:
- tracking completion of tasks against a to-do list, and messaging it whenever she remembers something on the go;
- asking it to track her load level and assess it from two sides: work efficiency and time management, and her personal state;
- keeping track of food, because executive functions are at zero, so understanding what to eat and remembering it is a problem.

Setup — done in several steps inside a separate **project**, so the shared information is processed together [msg #662]:
1. **Load the medical context.** Feed it all current lab tests, describe your state, diagnoses (the source closes this list with "и т.д.") [msg #662].
2. **Describe the current load.** Describe all current workload, what tasks you have and what needs to be done [msg #662].
3. **Evaluation chat with a strict two-role prompt.** In a separate chat enter the prompt: "я буду загружать тебе и описывать дела в течение дня, которые я делала, а ты должен максимально честно и строго оценивать с 2х позиций: управления, рабочей эффективности и тайм-менеджмента, и моего врача/психотерапевта на основе текущего самочувствия. После этого давать рекомендации по изменению нагрузки и планирования." (I will upload and describe the tasks I did during the day, and you must evaluate them as honestly and strictly as possible from 2 positions: management, work efficiency and time management, and my doctor/psychotherapist based on my current well-being. After that, give recommendations on changing load and planning.) [msg #662].
4. **Task-capture chat.** In another separate chat in the same project, give a simple prompt: you will send tasks as you go — ones to be done or already done — and it should systematise them and each time reply with an update in the form of a checklist [msg #662].
5. **End-of-day handover.** At the end of the day copy that checklist into the well-being evaluation chat, adding how you feel and how the day went [msg #662].

Verdict and notes: after two weeks of testing the author said she likes the experiment very much and "quite recommends" this use of the chat for people with similar difficulties. She deliberately tested GPT with tricky questions and restrictions so that it would not over-praise her, and it "seems to be holding up well". It also turned out it can "scold"; her friend joked that she had driven even GPT to that. Incidentally, her GPT speaks of itself in the feminine gender [msg #661, #662].

### Three things to check first with ASD + ADHD + high fatigue
A reader with autism + ADHD and high fatigue asked what to look at first and how to help herself, since she literally cannot get things done — it is "either cleaning or work". The author answered that, first, look not at "how to get everything done" but at what exactly drains you most, because resources are objectively smaller and this is not a question of willpower [msg #805]. Points to note:
1. **Fatigue is not laziness or bad organisation.** Neurodivergent people spend a lot of energy on sensory signals, task switching (the source adds "и т.д.") — so cleaning is a whole big task [msg #805].
2. **Sensory load often eats more energy than the tasks themselves** [msg #805].
3. **Multitasking with ADHD is an illusion.** We can do only one thing at a time; the faster we switch back and forth, the more tired we get. Better one large task a day than five small ones left unfinished [msg #805].

Bottom line: "считаем количество своей энергии, а не дел, которые вы собирались сделать" (count the amount of your energy, not the tasks you planned to do) [msg #805].

## Detailed insights

### The author's own experience of overload and near-burnout
- **Year-end ADHD crunch (Dec 2023).** "In the best ADHD traditions" she worked through the pre-New-Year rush: writing lectures until 3 a.m., consulting 12 hours a day, signing a mountain of documents and running job interviews — then lying face-down in the pillow while trying to clean the whole flat for New Year. Her lesson: be gentler to yourself and to each other, and remember that neurodivergent people's battery is smaller, so rest when you can [msg #80].
- **"Balancing on the knife-edge of burnout" (Dec 2024).** Asked by a commenter how she manages not to burn out, being so active and positive, she answered that for years she has been constantly balancing on the edge of burnout and cannot get into a more or less normal state. Concrete signs [msg #420]:
  - Her hours of individual appointments keep decreasing because she "simply can't cope".
  - In spring, seeing more than 100 new people in 2 months while opening a clinic nearly finished her off, and she wound that work down.
  - She had to hire an assistant to book appointments and answer booking questions, because she cannot handle that much even written communication.
  - When not working, she lies down; her therapist keeps working with her to reduce load and learn to rest.
  - Even sleep, always her stable support, fails her: either nightmares from working with PTSD, or her ADHD brain, overloaded, does not let her fall into deep sleep and keeps thinking.
  - Conclusion: there is no magic way to be a neurodivergent person, super-productive by the standards of neurotypical society, and feel good — so rest more and criticise yourself less.
- **Autistic burnout (May 2025)** — described in the ChatGPT section above: cutting personal chats for a week, then continued difficulty with non-work communication [msg #661].

### Autistic people with active social lives and careers: energy on credit
Patients ask her how autistic people who communicate actively and build careers survive — how can they be so active? Her answer: the universal reply is that everyone is different, and some also have ADHD. But the result is always the same — severe fatigue, up to being unable to reply to messages. You can borrow strength from your body for communication and career, but it is always strength "on credit", and the loan later has to be repaid. She admits she has no answer for her readers or herself about what to do, especially if you want a career; her therapist expects her to solve it somehow, but "radical acceptance" has not happened yet. This came after she lectured on ADHD and burnout [msg #770].

### Ableism toward oneself and the unresolved dilemma
For a very long time, in her personal therapy, she and her therapist have been trying to figure out how to normalise her load so she does not feel constantly tired; every week she also sees many clients with the same complaint. At every appointment she says "вам нужно подогнать по возможности условия среды под ваши особенности" (you need to adjust environmental conditions to your traits as far as possible), sincerely, knowing it helps — the problem is the "as far as possible": every neurodivergent person (like every neurotypical) has different possibilities, values, environment, profession [msg #793].

The open questions she poses: how to live this life fully without constantly feeling run over by a tractor; how to realise ambitions; how to earn a living often enough; how to have and raise children, especially without help. Her current view is that any choice produces suffering — either from non-fulfilment or from constant overexhaustion. This is how her therapist came to call it ableism toward herself and her neurodivergence. She firmly knows that to feel better you must accept your traits, but accepting yourself is one thing and the neurotypical world — often not kind even to neurotypicals — is another. She invites readers to share how they actually cope with living fully without dying of chronic fatigue [msg #793].

## Evidence and numbers

Cited in the forwarded NeuroDive post [msg #765]:
- One study found that executive-function deficits in employees with ADHD mediate the link between ADHD and occupational burnout — i.e. constant load on planning, self-control and organisation is the key factor of exhaustion.
- A review of adults with ADHD notes higher stress levels and a greater number of sick days; the average "excess" loss of working time is about 8.4 days a year compared with controls.
- The study "Sensory Over‑Responsivity as an Added Dimension in ADHD" found that 43% of women and 22% of men with ADHD have sensory hyper- or hyposensitive reactions — an additional "weight" on load.
- The recent article "Altered somatosensory processing in adult ADHD" showed adults with ADHD have increased tactile sensitivity and sensory overload, correlating with ADHD symptom severity.

From the author: about 40 studies on autistic burnout in the last 5 years (her PubMed check, April 2025); autistic burnout usually develops over several months and recovery usually takes several months; the depression criterion she contrasts it with is persistently lowered mood most of the day for more than 14 days [msg #617, #155].
