## Inner critic, schema therapy and soothing the threat system

### What this covers

The author's posts on the inner critic as seen from schema therapy plus a behavioural (function-of-behaviour) lens: where the critic originates developmentally, which critic modes are worked with and which one is removed, and why. It also covers practical ways to lower the brain's threat level — the RO DBT "safety skills" list, and gratitude / loving-kindness — and several book and experience reviews linked to self-criticism (family demands in a novel, narcissism, therapeutic language via RFT, an imagery audio play). According to the author, this matters because living with an abusive inner critic makes healing and healthy relationships, even with oneself, very unlikely [#231].

### Core ideas

- The critic has a function. Nothing in the psyche arises from nowhere; the critic originally helped a dependent child survive by anticipating and preventing the caregiver's displeasure (full model in *Developmental origin of the inner critic*) [#234].
- Different critics, different tactics. In schema therapy the demanding critic is negotiated with, while the punitive critic is the single mode that is deliberately eradicated [#231].
- Rigidity keeps the critic alive. A flexible psyche lets the critic fade once life circumstances change; a traumatized, inflexible psyche keeps saying "idiot" over a broken cup long after the punishing adults are gone [#234].
- Calm and fear are incompatible, so act calm. Safety skills work through feedback: doing things the brain associates with safety produces the feeling of safety [#256].
- Gratitude and loving-kindness are threat-reduction skills, not about "loving everyone" [#798].
- Family demands shape self-criticism even without violence, when delivered softly in the name of destiny and love (author's reading of a novel) [#177].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Schema-therapy critic modes and the tactic for each
Purpose: decide how to work with a self-critical voice. The author notes that many therapy modalities single out an "inner critic" part, but they relate to it differently. Commenters mentioned IFS, where one looks at the usefulness of one's parts. The author says she is closer to schema therapy combined with a behavioural view: one always examines the function of the behaviour of the "parts" — in schema therapy called "режимы" (modes) — and their short-term and long-term consequences [#231].

Schema therapy distinguishes different critics, and the tactic depends on which critic it is [#231]:
1. Demanding critic (требовательный критик). Tactic: negotiate with it — "с требовательным критиком мы договариваемся, снижаем уровень его требований (если они нереалистичные)" (with the demanding critic we negotiate, we lower the level of its demands (if they are unrealistic)). The conditional matters: demands are lowered when they are unrealistic, not abolished wholesale [#231].
2. Punitive critic ("Карающий"). Recognised by humiliating, devaluing and threatening (the author refers to a video from the previous day as an illustration). Tactic: get rid of it. "Буквально это единственный режим, который мы прямо стараемся искоренить из своей личности" (literally this is the only mode that we directly try to eradicate from our personality). Reason given: while you live with "a rapist and abuser inside", the chances of healing and of healthy relationships, even with yourself, are most likely extremely low [#231].

#### Developmental origin of the inner critic (survival-dependence model)
Purpose: understand why the critic exists and why it persists [#234].
1. Biological premise. Biologically, our species' task is to survive, and this applies to small individuals (children) too [#234].
2. Dependence. The human child's task is complicated: for years it needs an adult on whom its survival depends; the probability that a small child survives without an adult is extremely low. So the child has to adapt to its adult's rules [#234].
3. The critic appears as a memory of displeasure. The critic quickly learns what leads to the adult's displeasure and demands, and memorises it [#234].
4. The critic starts speaking itself — pre-emptively. It then begins to "whisper" on its own to prevent the adult's displeasure. Example: we call ourselves "идиотами" (idiots) inside while looking at a broken cup [#234].
5. Normal outcome: the critic fades. A normal psyche is characterised by flexibility, so as the child grows up the critic gradually "отмирает" (dies off) and recedes into the background, because life's circumstances and context change: we no longer depend on adults, we know our own values, our road, what we rely on, and where we are right or wrong [#234].
6. Traumatized outcome: the critic persists. A traumatized psyche, being inflexible, has no such easy path. We grow up, live in another city or country, there are no longer people nearby who would slap us over a cup — "Но ты стоишь над разбитой чашкой и внутри говоришь себе "идиот"..." (But you stand over a broken cup and inside say to yourself "idiot"...) [#234].

#### RO DBT safety skills
Purpose: brake the threat system when feeling unsafe. The author opens with T. Lynch's question from RO DBT: "Вы когда-нибудь замечали, что вы не можете чувствовать себя одновременно спокойным и испуганным?" (Have you ever noticed that you cannot feel calm and frightened at the same time?) [#256].

Mechanism: a feedback system. While in a state of unsafety, we begin doing things that the brain and body associate with safety — and we start to feel safety. Example: it is hard to imagine slowly savouring something tasty while a bear is attacking you; so when we sit down, wrap ourselves in a cosy blanket and eat a treat, the brain reasons "we can sit like this now, so there is no bear nearby, OK, let's relax" [#256].

The eight skills [#256]:
1. Use wide gestures with open hands instead of keeping the arms along the body [#256].
2. Move your facial muscles. Under threat or tension facial muscles naturally "freeze" and we lose the ability to communicate flexibly through facial expression. Deliberately moving facial muscles sends safety signals to the brain [#256].
3. Breathe deeper and slower, consciously. Use long, slow exhalations; purposely exhale longer than usual. Slow breathing to six breaths per minute; on the inhale focus on the movement of the belly rather than the chest [#256].
4. Consciously tense and relax large muscle groups. Start with toes and feet, move to the legs and torso, then arms and neck, and finally the facial muscles. Tense each group, hold the tension, then slowly relax and notice the difference [#256].
5. Use touch, pressure, massage and hugs. Touch or hug soft objects (fluffy pets, soft toys, pillows); lightly stroke the face and neck; massage the muscles of the face, head and neck [#256].
6. Chewing and eating. When anxious, the body is tense and ready for action. The brain associates chewing and swallowing food with rest and digestion rather than fight or flight. Chewing moves facial muscles linked to social safety. When we are truly upset, swallowing is difficult; chewing and swallowing food naturally calm the body [#256].
7. Listen to music and voices. Use music you find soothing and/or recordings of human voices you find soothing to activate the social safety system. Avoid listening to disturbing or exciting music [#256].
8. Look at photos of loved people, pets or landscapes. Viewing photos associated with warmth, heartfelt connection with close people, or a sense of calm can launch the social safety system [#256].

How to use them: you can do several skills at once, or choose one and practise specifically that one in moments of unsafety. The author flexibly picks those that are possible in a given situation. Her examples: she always keeps nature sounds downloaded in Yandex Music so she can listen during any trip, e.g. on a plane; or she packs some special food in her bag [#256].

#### Gratitude and loving-kindness as threat-reduction skills
The author arrived at "a concept of gratitude" while reflecting under a starry sky during a period of emotional swings (irritation at working on a laptop by the sea while writing a book and being distracted by work, alongside delight at the sun, sea, colours, greenery and animals she lacks in Moscow) [#798].
- Target of gratitude is secondary. She thinks it is not that important to whom the gratitude is directed — God, oneself, the universe [#798].
- Why it helps (author's view). At minimum it is the skill of seeing the good in life and "увеличение дофамина, которого нам так не хватает" (an increase of dopamine, which we lack so much). However hard life is (differently for everyone), the ability to keep going forward despite everything and to take care of oneself is, in her words, one of the most important skills improving quality of life [#798].
- Psychotherapy framing. From a psychotherapy standpoint there really are skills of loving-kindness and gratitude. They are not about simply loving everyone, but about lowering the threat level in the brain and feeling more safety and calm; the author says this "реально очень и очень хорошо работает" (really works very, very well) [#798].
- Practical prompt. Praise yourself and thank yourself for something good you have done for yourself and for the world this year [#798].
- Personal outcome. She concluded that by the end of a difficult year her emotional balance had returned to quite good shape, noting that her "Christ's age" year (33) was hard [#798].

### Detailed insights

#### Family demands as a source of self-criticism (novel "Мы все мертвы")
While ill, the author bought an e-reader and was reading "Мы все мертвы" (We Are All Dead; cover shows author Анастасия Вайолет) [#177], chosen because of a fun TikTok ad and the beautiful paper edition. As literature she had not yet decided how good it is — it depends on the ending. But stripped of its "wrapper", the book in her view shows very well the influence of family and family values on personality formation, the formation of self-criticism and the loss of self behind excessive demands — "даже если никто никого не бьёт, не насилует, а говорят это мягким голосом, ссылаясь на предназначение и любовь" (even if nobody beats or rapes anyone, but says it in a soft voice, referring to destiny and love). She found it so eerie and prosaic that she struggled through it, repeatedly putting it down to self-regulate. Recommendation: for anyone who thinks family demands have little influence [#177].

#### Narcissism through a non-demonising lens ("Хрупкие люди")
Book: "Хрупкие люди" by Юлия Пирумова (the cover subtitle is only partly legible; it reads roughly as: why narcissism is not a vice but a [cut-off word starting with осо-]; (!) UNCLEAR beyond that) [#380]. The author opens with a parable: "Маленькая Нарциссочка" (Little Narcissist girl) who, whenever something didn't work out, began scolding herself furiously; this helped her, first, not to relax and, second, to "spin the earth twice as fast" — but she never considered that not everything in life can be handled. Lesson: harsh self-scolding is used as a performance driver while ignoring the limits of control [#380].
Verdict: a quite good book about narcissists that does not see them as monsters from the outset and accessibly shows where the difficulties with self-esteem, grandiosity and relationship problems come from. She liked the depth and the validating atmosphere, but finds it "немного местами психоаналитично" (a bit psychoanalytic in places). She recommends it to all her clients with NPD (НРЛ) and narcissistic traits [#380].

#### Choosing professional psychotherapy literature; RFT ("Искусство терапевтической беседы")
- Selection rule. In psychology it is hard to tell if a book is worthwhile, especially when young in the profession; the author ended up with a huge NLP tome she never read. Now she is careful and selective with professional literature: she buys only what she will definitely read, what suits her, is significant and scientifically grounded [#625].
- RFT in simple words. "Искусство терапевтической беседы" (The Art of Therapeutic Conversation) is based on RFT — relational frame theory: "рамочную модель, объясняющую, как люди устанавливают и понимают отношения между словами и понятиями в контексте языка" (a framework model explaining how people establish and understand relations between words and concepts in the context of language). Understanding and using this in therapeutic conversation lets a therapist improve their skill; it is also interesting to see how the use of language helps change behaviour and influences the client's self-perception [#625].
- Verdict. Strongly recommended to psychotherapists, especially those working in ACT who want to understand RFT. Although the theory is convoluted, the book is written in fairly simple language with dialogue examples that illustrate the use of interventions well [#625].
- Publisher. She likes the publisher "Научный мир" (Scientific World): she has bought perhaps half its range in recent years, and says it is hard to find anything heretical or scientifically unfounded there [#625].

#### Imagery self-exploration: the NOOSPHERA audio play
The author took part in the immersive audio play НООСФЕРА — an audio fairy tale in which the main character is you, built around immersion into your inner garden and the road of your life. She finds it very similar to her favourite schema-therapy techniques aimed at emotional experience [#377].
- Her experience: she went in without expectations; the part exploring her life path and where she is now resonated immediately. After a turbulent year of sharp changes, stopping to see where she is and where she is going was very important. Her image: her road is a path in a big forest; though at times she feels lost, there is only one path, and above all she knows where she is going — to the mountains visible even through the forest [#377].
- Format details: about 40 minutes of contact with oneself outside a regular therapy session; afterwards you can describe and send your images to receive feedback on them; the plot was developed with a clinical psychotherapist and the head of an RSUH (РГГУ) psychology laboratory [#377].
- Verdict: she highly recommends it to those who want time alone with themselves and like imagination-based psychotherapeutic exercises; she calls it not only an audio tale but a self-knowledge tool [#377].
