## Self-diagnosis, myths and stigma around neurodivergence

### What this covers

How the author (a psychiatrist/psychotherapist working with autistic and ADHD adults) thinks about self-diagnosis from social media, the rapid rise in autism and ADHD diagnoses and the slogan that "everyone is neurodivergent", the clash between the medical and the social model, the myths and stereotypes she keeps hearing, whether a helper must share the condition, misinformation about ADHD from doctors of other specialties, and the 2025 Russian rule that bars people with an F84 diagnosis from driving. According to the source this matters because stigma — "especially among helping specialists" — is still frighteningly high, and because distorted images and blurred boundaries can devalue the real difficulties of neurodivergent people and keep others from the help they need [#709, #375, #546].

### Core ideas

- Self-diagnosis is acceptable as a stop-gap, not as an endpoint. Doing skills when you suffer and cannot reach a specialist is better than doing nothing, but social media create a loop of distorted self-recognition; use verified information and still go to specialists (see *The author's position on self-diagnosis*) [#375].
- We will not all become neurodivergent. The share of people with ASD and ADHD in the population is probably constant; diagnoses grow because we have become better at recognizing them (see *Two reasons why it feels like everyone is neurodivergent*) [#546].
- The social model without the medical one blurs boundaries. Only the social approach "без медицинского никуда не годится" (is no good without the medical one) because it can devalue real difficulties [#546].
- A specialist does not need the condition to help well, and having it guarantees nothing. What matters is expertise and continuous training [#379].
- Autism is a spectrum, and most autistic people are invisible. ASD does not equal speech and intellectual impairment [#146].
- Stigma is embedded in law and professional practice. The F84 driving ban has no medical basis and reflects traditional stigmatization of psychiatric diagnoses [#728].
- Real advocacy defends all disorders, not only the "liked" ones [#38].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### The social-media self-diagnosis loop
Purpose: explains why a study showing TikTok misleads about ADHD matters, even though the author is not against self-diagnosis as such. The author calls it a "патологический круг" (pathological circle) and notes it is in general normal when everyone starts encountering something new [#375].
1. One person shares. Someone on social media starts sharing their knowledge and symptoms — which the author considers good in itself [#375].
2. Many recognize themselves. Viewers see themselves in the description but do not go to a specialist [#375].
3. They become broadcasters. These people start carrying their own "knowledge" to the masses. The problem is here: it is not certain that this second person has ADHD or any other disorder [#375].
4. The next people build on the distortion. Each subsequent wave relies on a possibly distorted perception, so what we see online does not always correspond to the truth [#375].
Consequences named by the author: (a) it can devalue the real difficulties of people with ADHD; (b) it can prevent those who suspected ADHD but actually have a different disorder from getting the help they need [#375].
The trigger was a forwarded post by Liza (Psycho_pharmacologist) about a study — reported on psyandneuro.ru — showing that TikTok misleads about ADHD; she framed it as evidence "что по соц сетям не надо ставить себе диагноз и доказывать его врачам" (that you should not diagnose yourself via social media and then prove it to doctors), noting that strictly the study is about TikTok being misleading, and that she planned a separate post on the problems of self-diagnosis [#374]. #### The author's position on self-diagnosis
1. Self-diagnosis is not bad when you are suffering and there is no possibility to see a specialist: "Лучше делать навыки, чем их не делать" (better to do the skills than not), and in principle better to do anything that improves your life [#375].
2. Psychoeducation is "топ" (top, the best thing) [#375].
3. Self-diagnosis can be fine as a temporary solution [#375].
4. But: use verified information and still try to turn to specialists in the field [#375].

#### Two things to know before choosing a neurodivergent specialist
Context: someone voiced the opinion that a neurotypical person cannot be a good specialist in neurodivergence. The author agrees that many neurotypical people do not understand the inner experience of people with ASD and ADHD, which delays timely help, and that many people — especially in psychiatry and psychotherapy — look for specialists who are neurodivergent too. She calls this "a not bad strategy", with two caveats [#379]:
1. A specialist does not have to have a problem to treat it well. Just as a pediatrician need not have children. Example: her colleague Maria Novikova, with whom she runs many neurodivergence projects, has neither ASD nor ADHD, yet her sensitivity to symptoms and clients is rare [#379].
2. A specialist with ADHD and/or ASD has the same problems with attention and communication, so the "double empathy" story can apply to them too. This can make correct diagnosis harder or create limits and problems in psychotherapy. She cites a qualitative study (BJPsych Open) on autistic psychiatrists' experiences of recognising themselves and others as autistic [#379].
Conclusion: nothing guarantees that a specialist will be sensitive and attentive to your autistic (or other) symptoms; assuming so is closer to a cognitive error, as in the pediatrician example. The key is that the specialist knows their field and keeps raising their qualification. Doctors are human, not robots, and make mistakes — "Даже ИИ с этим не справляется" (even AI can't manage that) [#379].

#### Two reasons why it feels like everyone is neurodivergent
The author's answer — stressed as "мое личное мнение и не более того" (my personal opinion and nothing more) — to the claim that soon everyone will be neurodivergent [#546].
1. Real growth in diagnosed, self-accepting people. The number of diagnosed people who understand and accept their neurodivergence has risen sharply and will keep rising. Four factors:
   - improved diagnostics;
   - an expanded pool of people being diagnosed (for example adults, and especially women);
   - lower stigma toward psychiatry worldwide;
   - greater availability of medical care.
   Against this background it is logical that numbers grow and the feeling appears that "вокруг одни нейроотличные" (there are only neurodivergent people around) [#546].
2. A position of tolerance in society — the medical and social approaches collide here (see *Medical vs social model*). She says she was very surprised that a British professor said such a thing at all [#546].
Verdict: no, we will not all become neurodivergent — as many people with ASD and ADHD as there were, so many there will most likely be in the population; we just know better how to recognize them. There is no point in calling everything neurodivergence; she still disagrees with including everything indiscriminately, such as personality disorders and OCD [#546].

#### Medical vs social model of neurodivergence
- Medical approach: stricter, with concrete criteria [#546].
- Social approach: the brain simply works differently in different conditions, and we should accept that we are different — normal, but different [#546].
- Author's ("вредный", contrarian) view: only the social approach without the medical one is useless, because it really blurs boundaries and may "обесценивать" (devalue) the real difficulties of neurodivergent people [#546].

#### Six myths about neurodivergence the author hears most
The author says that when the wave of psychoeducation about neurodivergence began there were many more stereotypes, but some beliefs persist, resurface regularly and hurt "будто по живому" (as if cutting into living flesh) [#709]:
1. «У людей с РАС не должно быть эмпатии и эмоций» (people with ASD must not have empathy and emotions) [#709].
2. «У всех бывают трудности с вниманием / общением / усталостью — это просто жизнь» (everyone has difficulties with attention / communication / fatigue — that's just life) [#709].
3. «Мы тогда тут ВСЕ НЕЙРООТЛИЧНЫЕ» (then we're ALL neurodivergent here) [#709].
4. «Если у тебя есть друзья, то ты точно не аутичный» (if you have friends, you're definitely not autistic) [#709].
5. «При СДВГ не может быть успешной карьеры» (with ADHD you can't have a successful career) [#709].
6. Her "personal favourite": «Если тебе тяжело — значит, ты неправильно живёшь» (if it's hard for you, you're living wrong) [#709].
In an update after reading comments, she added the myth that someone is "too" warm, smart or organized to be neurodivergent: nobody thinks about what this "too" costs a person [#709]. She hopes such statements will become rarer, but the level of stigma, especially among helping specialists, is still frighteningly high [#709].

#### Analysis of the F84 driving ban (Russia, from 1 September 2025)
Forwarded from the channel Чистые Когниции: an interview with psychiatrist Anastasia Afanasyeva about the updated official list of diseases that bar driving. The list now includes ICD-10 section F84 «Общие расстройства психологического развития» (pervasive developmental disorders), which covers autism and Asperger syndrome — diagnoses that ICD-11 groups as autism spectrum disorders. Obtaining or renewing a licence becomes questionable for people with these diagnoses [#728].
1. What it means in practice. A licence requires a certificate from a medical board and from the ПНД (psychiatric dispensary); if the medical record contains an autism diagnosis, the certificate will not be issued. As a rule there are no other checks while driving: the traffic police (ГИБДД) do not check diagnoses themselves and act only on the medical certificate, so the current formal "filter" is the dispensary psychiatrist. Theoretically, concealing a diagnosis could lead to the licence being cancelled if discovered [#728].
2. Is there a medical basis. Studies show that adolescents and adults with ASD get licences less often, but when they drive their accident rate is not higher than that of neurotypical peers. There are specific difficulties — complex turns, reacting to non-verbal signals of pedestrians and other drivers — but they do not lead to a systematic increase in accidents. Hence there are no medical grounds for a total ban; the decision rather reflects the traditional stigmatization of psychiatric diagnoses in Russian law, where they often serve as grounds for restrictions despite current scientific data [#728].
3. Possible consequences. (a) Discrimination: the ban creates the impression that all people with ASD are dangerous drivers, which is untrue. (b) More secrecy: people will hide the diagnosis or not seek help when they suspect it, for fear of losing their licence — worsening access to qualified help. (c) Legal collisions with licences issued before the ban; how this will work is still unclear [#728].
4. Bottom line. The restrictions will hardly affect every person with ASD; there is no reason to panic — it is a bureaucratic change, not mass roadside checks. But the very appearance of such laws shows how important it is to talk about stigma and its consequences [#728].
Follow-up from the author in the discussion [#730]:
- Adults are diagnosed with ASD very poorly in Russia, so the probability that you will get this diagnosis at the dispensary or the licence exam is extremely low — "и это плюс" (and that's a plus) [#730].
- On the other hand, with the universal rollout of ЕГИСЗ, where all data sit, it is hard to predict how dispensaries will react [#730].
- On the wording about "chronic" conditions: everything relating to neurodevelopmental disorders are states we have ALL our life, so under that wording they fall into it automatically [#730].

#### Four objections to anti-ADHD posts by a pediatrician
Readers asked about pediatrician-blogger Fyodor Katasonov's (федиатрия) recent posts on ADHD. The author stresses this is "свое ценное мнение (и только мое)" (my valuable opinion, and only mine) [#843]:
1. Stay in your specialty. Pediatricians and somatic doctors should not meddle in psychiatry or other specialties unless they have been trained in them and worked in them for some time [#843].
2. Tired of "great male minds". In medicine and psychology many have their own unique, "only correct" view of a problem; ADHD in particular "никому жить не дает" (gives no one peace). On reading, half of the speculation is unsupported or far-fetched. In his article, half argues that scientific research is falsified, yet at the end he cites articles himself — only those that fit his opinion [#843].
3. Manipulation by generalization. On the claim «Официальная медицина, иногда заблуждаясь, а часто намеренно, годами и десятилетиями врет своим пользователям» (official medicine, sometimes mistakenly and often deliberately, has lied to its users for years and decades): it is "kind of true and kind of not" — this is "манипуляция обобщением". Yes, there are paid studies and studies with poor samples, as in any field of science; yes, our knowledge is often limited. But that does not lead to "НАМ ВСЕМ ВРУТ ФАРМКОМПАНИИ" (pharma companies lie to us all). The remedy: learn to read research (taught at university), follow results of large studies, and again, don't meddle in every field [#843].
4. No clear point, and treatment is not only drugs. It was hard to grasp the article's meaning or what ADHD is supposed to be in it (her spoiler: "явление" — a phenomenon, unclear what it includes, especially given the concept of neurodivergence), apart from the message that it need not be treated. She reminds that treatment is not only medication but also non-drug, which "за заговорами фармкомпаний видимо забылось" (was apparently forgotten behind the pharma conspiracies) [#843].

On World Autism Awareness Day (2 April) the author writes that each of us should keep showing again and again how much autism is a spectrum: ASD does not equal a child or adult with speech and intellectual impairments, it is not necessarily a person banging against a wall, and "нас очень и очень много" (there are very, very many of us). ## Autism spectrum: support levels, genetics and history

### What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel taught about how autism spectrum disorder (РАС, ASD) is classified today — three levels of support need, replacing older labels — and why even the lowest support level remains a disability. It also covers the genetics of autism (twin concordance, sibling recurrence, de novo mutations, autistic traits in parents), what this means for autistic people planning children, the tensions between parents of autistic children and autistic self-advocates, autistic parenting, overlooked history and pioneers, and resources for adults just encountering the diagnosis. According to the author, many adults with undiagnosed mild-spectrum ASD spend years cycling through diagnoses and treatments that do not help, so understanding the spectrum correctly matters clinically [#149].

### Core ideas

- Support levels replaced subtypes and severity grades. The field has abandoned Asperger/Kanner subtypes and also mild/moderate/severe grading, describing ASD through three levels of support need instead; see *ASD support levels 1–3* [#150].
- Low support needs ≠ no support needs. Autism is a disability for many, including people at level 1, because it limits them; see *Low support needs are still a disability* [#67].
- Undiagnosed mild-spectrum autism causes real harm. The author regularly sees exhausted adults with 5–6 comorbid diagnoses for whom treatment does not work because nobody diagnosed ASD; part of their problems are solved by acceptance, environment building and work with sensory overload [#149].
- Autism is highly heritable but unpredictable in its form. An autistic parent has an elevated chance of an autistic child, but the child's support level is independent of the parent's, and over 1000 genes are implicated [#602, #642, #717].
- Autistic parenting cuts both ways. Autistic parents may understand autistic children better, but may find neurotypical children harder, especially due to sensory overload from children [#718].
- Autism is not new. Historical anecdotes and early clinical work (Sukhareva in the 1930s) predate the usual Asperger/Kanner origin story [#539, #547].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### ASD support levels 1–3
Purpose. The current way to describe the difficulties and functioning of autistic people. The author explains that the world community has abandoned dividing autism into Asperger syndrome, Kanner syndrome and similar subtypes, and that a mild/moderate/severe rating also fails to describe difficulties and functioning in a way that matches what actually happens to people with ASD [#150].

Why functioning labels fail. Functioning is not stable: "вот сейчас вы бодры и активны, вербальны, а потом хоп, нагрузка, истощение и твоя степень тяжести и функциональности меняется" (right now you are alert and active, verbal, and then — bam — load, exhaustion, and your degree of severity and functioning changes). For the same reason the autistic community dislikes the terms "высокофункциональный" (high-functioning) and "низкофункциональный" (low-functioning) [#150].

Important reference point. Support levels compare autistic people with other autistic people, not with allistic people (see *Low support needs are still a disability*) [#67].

The three levels, with the features listed in the channel's Russian text and the attached cards (Verywell card [#150]; Healthline + PsychCentral carousel [#151], [#152], [#153]):

1. Level 1 — Требуется поддержка (Requiring support).
   - Problems understanding and following social rules (card: "Trouble understanding and following social rules") [#151, img #151].
   - Rigid or inflexible behaviour [#151].
   - Stress during changes/transitions (the card says "Some stress during transitions") [#151, img #151].
   - May benefit from therapy or life-skills training/coaching [#151].
   - Verywell card adds: difficulty initiating social interactions; organization and planning problems can hamper independence [#150].
2. Level 2 — Требуется существенная поддержка (Requiring substantial support).
   - Atypical social behaviour, for example walking away in the middle of a conversation [#151, img #152].
   - High interest in specific topics [#151, img #152].
   - Noticeable distress/anxiety when faced with change [#151, img #152].
   - May need school accommodations such as reading help or social-skills support [#151, img #152].
   - Verywell card adds: social interactions limited to narrow special interests; frequent restricted/repetitive behaviours [#150].
3. Level 3 — Требуется очень существенная поддержка (Requiring very substantial support).
   - Severe communication deficits, for example being nonspeaking ("неразговорчивость") [#151, img #153].
   - Repetitive behaviours such as rocking or spinning [#151, img #153].
   - Extremely pronounced distress when asked to switch tasks [#151, img #153].
   - May need one-on-one time with an education assistant and may use augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) tools such as picture symbols [#151, img #153].
   - Verywell card adds: severe deficits in verbal and nonverbal social communication skills; great distress/difficulty changing actions or focus [#150].

How the levels relate in life. The author notes that people at levels 1, 2 and 3 "часто живут в разных мирах" (often live in different worlds), yet none of them has fewer problems because of it — particularly when a level-1 parent has a child at level 2 or 3, or a child with ADHD or another condition [#149].

#### Two approaches to autism: parents vs autistic self-advocates
Purpose. Answers a reader's question about the perceived tension or conflict between parents of autistic children (who prefer "person-first" terminology) and adult autistic self-advocates — even though, as the reader notes, these are overlapping groups. The author confirms the tension is real and exists not only in the Russian-speaking space [#717, #718].

1. Parents' approach. Parents may focus on various therapies aimed at "correcting" autistic behaviour, which can be perceived as an attempt to suppress individuality [#718].
2. Self-advocates' approach. Autistic self-advocates argue for accepting autism as neurodiversity and consider attempts to "cure" autism potentially harmful [#718].

Consequence. This difference leads to conflicts, especially over choosing therapy methods and lifestyle for autistic children — which, the author says, is what is frequently seen online [#718].

### Detailed insights

#### Low support needs are still a disability
A text by autistic author zebulontheplanet, translated by Anna Green Moon and forwarded from the channel "Аутичные переводы", argues angrily against the claim that level-1 autism is not a disability [#67]:
- Autism is a disability for many, including those with low support needs. People with low support needs can call their autism a disability because it limits them — "низкая потребность в поддержке не означает ОТСУТСТВИЕ потребности в поддержке" (low support need does not mean ABSENCE of support need) [#67].
- Concrete examples of needs at low support levels: help with instrumental activities of daily living (ИФА — shopping, cooking, using the phone, housework, laundry, cleaning), help with finances; some cannot drive a car; some cannot cook a complex dish [#67].
- Denying this erases the real difficulties of low-support autistic people: communication difficulties and sensory differences (the source itself leaves this list open-ended: "и так далее и тому подобное"). Autism creates limitations for most and this must be taken into account [#67].
- The author concedes: low-support autistic people are less limited than high-support ones, and society treats them more leniently; it can be hard when your autism is less limiting. But "Это не соревнование" (This is not a competition) [#67].
- Key reasoning: support needs were not designed as a comparison with allistic people (neurotypical or non-autistic neurodivergent), but to compare the needs of autistic people with each other. Therefore one cannot say low-support autistic people have no autism-related limitations, nor that they should be moved into a "no support needs" category [#67].
- The call to action: stop spreading misinformation, study, learn from autistic people with high and low support needs, and listen to them [#67].

#### The clinician's view: stigma and missed diagnoses on the mild spectrum
Writing on Autism Awareness Day, the author describes how exhausting it is to work in a world of stigma where you "не выглядишь как аутист" (don't look autistic), where you are told you "везде просто видишь нейроотличия" (just see neurodivergence everywhere), and where half of one's colleagues openly say one is dealing with nonsense and non-existent diagnoses [#149].
- She constantly sees tired, worn-out people with 5–6 comorbid disorders whom most treatment schemes do not help, who have gone "через все круги ада и все диагнозы МКБ, группы ДБТ, препараты, ограничения" (through all circles of hell and all ICD diagnoses, DBT groups, medications, restrictions) because no one ever diagnosed ASD [#149].
- Part of these problems turns out to be solved by acceptance, building a suitable environment and working with sensory overload [#149].
- She takes comfort in how attitudes to ADHD have changed and are changing, and hopes the milder spectrum of ASD will go the same way [#149].

#### Genetics and inheritance: answer to an autistic future parent
A reader with diagnosed ASD level 1 asked whether their future child is likely to have ASD and whether the child could be level 2 or 3; the author says the question troubles her too [#602]:
- ASD is a genetic disorder, so if you have ASD the probability your child will have it is fairly high [#602].
- The child's support level cannot be known and does not depend on the parent's level in any way; the child may also not get ASD at all — but the probability remains elevated [#602].
- Children with ASD are also born to neurotypical parents [#602].
- Gene variability is huge: the current gene base for ASD includes more than 1000 different genes, so many outcomes are possible [#602].

#### Autistic traits in parents of autistic children
Answering whether there is data on what share of parents of autistic children are themselves autistic (diagnosed or self-diagnosed), the author — beyond the known high heritability — cites three studies [#717]:
1. Pilot study in Italy (2025, Frontiers in Psychiatry). So far found that 29% of parents of autistic children meet criteria for the broader autism phenotype: 12% of fathers and 17% of mothers. A significant correlation was also found between fathers' autistic traits and their children's [#717].
2. Systematic review of 41 studies (2018, Springer). Between 9% and 45% of parents of children with ASD show signs of the broader phenotype — significantly higher than parents of neurotypical children [#717].
3. Washington University study (2014). If both parents score high on an autistic-traits scale, the child's risk of autism rises by 85%; if only one parent does, by 52% [#717].

#### Autistic parents with autistic vs neurotypical children
The author calls this a trick sub-question: the most important thing to remember is that every autistic person, adult or child, is individual [#718].
- In general, research and personal accounts show autistic parents may understand their autistic children better; some say their own experience helps them intuitively understand their autistic child's needs, which eases parenting [#718].
- Few articles exist, but the Avaz blog piece she recommends emphasizes that autistic parents can create structured, understanding environments, especially when their children are also autistic, which can foster mutual understanding and support [#718].
- However, raising neurotypical children can be harder for autistic parents because of differences in perception and communication; it may require extra effort to understand and adapt to neurotypical children's needs [#718].
- Many autistic parents struggle with sensory overload from children, especially sounds and touch — so the experience can also be extremely negative [#718].

#### History: autism is not new
- Anecdote "for those who say it didn't exist before". Found while digging into autism's history: Франческо Анне described early mentions of autism, including a story from India. A sheikh named Chilli fell in love with a girl and asked his mother how to make her love him back. She advised him to sit by the stream and, when the girl came for water, "просто бросить в нее камешек и улыбнуться" (just toss a pebble at her and smile). The sheikh took the words literally and threw a boulder at her, breaking her head. The author shares it humorously as an illustration of literal interpretation of speech, aimed at people who claim "а раньше такого не было!" (this didn't exist before!) [#539].
- Grunya Sukhareva. Psychiatrist after whom Moscow's Children's Psychiatric Hospital No. 6 is named. She was the first to describe children whose behaviour did not fit manifestations of schizophrenia; her paper was translated into German a year later but then somehow got lost. This was in the 1930s, whereas Asperger's and Kanner's first papers date from 1943–44. The author highlights that such women's contributions are often omitted from articles that focus on men [#547].
- Lorna Wing. British physician who studied autism in parallel with Kanner and Asperger and almost constantly championed the idea that autism should not be viewed as separate narrow forms or rigidly categorized. She first introduced and popularized the term "Asperger syndrome" for high-functioning autistic people, and from the 1970s proposed the term ASD, which came into use with DSM-5 in 2013. She founded the National Autistic Society in England and raised a daughter with ASD [#547].

### Evidence and numbers

Figures the author gives on prevalence and familial risk (no source named for this post) [#642]:
- Concordance in identical (monozygotic) twins: 70–90%; in fraternal (dizygotic) twins: 0–10%.
- Younger siblings of family members diagnosed with ASD face increased risk, especially younger male siblings.
- In 20–25% of children or adults with ASD, genetic causes can be identified as de novo mutations (mutations that appeared for the first time).
- For parents of one child with ASD, the risk of the next child having ASD is about 5–10%.
- The risk is higher (about 7%) if the child is a girl, and lower (about 4%) if a boy.
