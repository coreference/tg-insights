## Scientific thinking, philosophy and reading lists

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's posts on how to think about science itself: the author's personal criteria for which sciences are "good" (they keep updating, they integrate with neighbouring fields, they stay scientific and open), an open-science ecosystem diagram she endorses, her answer to whether philosophy is a joke or a useful discipline, and a forwarded physicist's analysis of the failed LK-99 superconductor claim as a lesson in reproducibility. It also covers reading: the ten books that formed the author's worldview, books on economics and on humanity's future that she sought as a dialectical counterweight to optimism, and free portals for finding leading scientists and reading research. According to the source, this matters because the way we build our values depends partly on education and on the information we take from the wider world, so the quality, openness and self-correction of the sciences — and our own willingness to test our worldview — directly shape how we see the world [#109, #494].

### Core ideas

- A good science is defined by approach more than subject. The author judges the sciences "of the bright present and beautiful future" by whether they are updatable (Bayesian refreshing of concepts on new data) and integrated (open to neighbouring fields), while keeping the basic criteria of scientificity. Full detail under *Criteria of a "good" science* [#109].
- Openness produces better science. An open-science ecosystem, given the right framework and ecosystem conditions, yields collaborative outputs and outcomes including higher quality of science and democratised knowledge — see *Open Science co-creation ecosystem model* [#109, #110].
- Philosophy is not a science but a separate branch of knowing the world, whose main "why" is offering ways to think about life, the universe and everything; in the author's opinion it still owns the initial creation of worldview concepts and morality/ethics, and is for now "the cradle of knowledge and sandbox of modus operandi" [#121].
- Extraordinary claims fall when they do not replicate. LK-99 was claimed as a room-temperature superconductor, but groups that synthesised it from the original recipe saw no superconductivity; a result that exists only in one "magic sample" is questionable as real science [#208].
- Books can build a worldview, and the author's formative ten span genetics, evolution, scepticism, neurology, cosmology, the self, religion, science fiction, meaning without gods and personality theory [#116].
- Read dialectically and update. Optimistic narratives (Ridley's *Rational Optimist*) should be balanced by works showing how things can go wrong (Reinert on economics, Ord on existential risk); authors who revise their own estimates deserve extra credit; and one should separate a concept from the person promoting it [#494].
- Research is findable and readable for free via research.com (rankings of works and scientists), Edge (conversations with leading thinkers) and JSTOR (100 free articles a month) [#40, #58, #334].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Three sources from which we learn what we value
Purpose: the author's starting point for why the quality of the sciences matters. We understand what is valuable to us by collecting information from different sources [#109]:
1. Samples from our close circle — the models and examples of the people near us [#109].
2. Personal experience — specific events and the decisions we made in them [#109].
3. Broadcasts from the distant environment and the outside world — and education belongs to this third source [#109].

She notes that Russian-speaking Europe, now scattered around the world, still receives its education in a predominantly secular rather than spiritual format ("Хвала богам" — "Thank the gods"), and defines that secularity: "Светскость в этой сфере предполагает опору на научные данные и отделяет их, в первую очередь, от религиозной этики." (Secularity in this sphere means relying on scientific data and separating it, first of all, from religious ethics.) Because education rests on the sciences, she asks whether all sciences are currently good — useful and sufficient — and whether they can be accepted unconditionally or whether it is time for "reinforcement" [#109].

#### Criteria of a "good" science (updatable, integrated, basic scientificity)
Purpose: a personal-opinion checklist (tagged by the author as personal opinion) for judging which sciences help people and improve the world versus which are relics of the past "that even postmodernism cannot ironically outplay". What matters to her is not so much what a science studies as its approach [#109]:
1. Updatable (обновляемые). "обновляемые — те науки, что продолжают байесиански освежать свои концепты под влиянием новых данных" (updatable — those sciences that keep refreshing their concepts in a Bayesian way under the influence of new data). The point is that a science's concepts are not fixed dogma but beliefs revised as evidence arrives [#109].
2. Integrated (проинтегрированные). "проинтегрированные — ведь по-настоящему обновляться, на мой взгляд, они смогут только путём открытости и взаимопроникновения с ближними и дальними своими соседями, особенно социальных наук с естественными" (integrated — because, in my view, they can truly update only through openness and interpenetration with their near and distant neighbours, especially the social sciences with the natural ones). The reasoning links criterion 2 to criterion 1: integration is the mechanism that makes genuine updating possible [#109].
3. Basic scientificity preserved. "Ну и базовые критерии «научности» per se, конечно, должны быть сохранены, чтобы оставаться, знаете… науками" (And the basic criteria of "scientificity" per se, of course, must be preserved — to remain, you know… sciences). Openness and integration must not dissolve the discipline's scientific standards [#109]. (!) INCOMPLETE: the source does not spell out what these basic criteria of scientificity are.

#### Four key branches of academic philosophy
According to the author, the key directions of academic philosophy today are [#121]:
1. Metaphysics — questions of the existence of the human being and of reality [#121].
2. Epistemology — about whether phenomena in the world are knowable, and about belief in particular ideas [#121].
3. Ethics — how one should live, what good and evil are [#121].
4. Logic — how to draw conclusions from true premises [#121].

She finds it most interesting to track the differences in these areas by comparing the continental and analytic philosophical traditions. To a modern eye, she adds, there seem to be two main lines of thought: something resembling the natural sciences and something resembling the social sciences [#121].

#### What remains in philosophy's domain (two areas)
Purpose: the author's answer (explicitly "debatable" and her opinion) to "if the sciences are already formed and are slowly but surely investigating all necessary questions, why do we need philosophy — should we not be thorough materialists and eliminate it?" She names two things that still belong to philosophy [#121]:
1. The initial creation of concepts and beliefs that form the basis of a worldview, in places where we have only fragmentary ideas. She calls this "the very 'proto'" — i.e. the proto-scientific role Churchland describes [#121].
2. Morality and ethics — "the juiciest part". Its open questions: what should guide decisions — maximising benefit or minimising harm; for a specific person or for society; judging by consequences or by some deep nature of the act itself; and overall "стоит ли человеку быть птичкой, созданной для счастья, или только махать крыльями, чтобы не крякнуться сразу?" (should a person be a bird created for happiness, or only flap their wings so as not to crash right away?) [#121].

Why ethics stays human for now: perhaps in time it will be fully calculated by super-powerful machines and placed into a convenient table with assigned weights, where one sets the parameters of desired results over a given time span. But for now this is done by people for people and is debatable at every step; she cites as a vivid illustration the recent story of automating court decisions and the colossal differences between machine and human judgements in sentencing [#121].

#### Red flags of a non-replicable breakthrough (LK-99 / magic sample)
Purpose: the checks the forwarded author (of the physics channel Соня и наука (Sonya and Science)) applied to the LK-99 claim, usable for judging any sensational result [#208]:
1. Is it only a preprint? A preprint is a work posted openly before journal publication; it is not moderated and "may with some probability be full of obscurantism". LK-99 was announced in a preprint [#208].
2. Is the team size plausible for the claim? The three Korean authors described in detail what each did. For such experimental work she expects a team of 5–6, because the range of tasks is very wide: growing (synthesising) the material, setting up the experiment, interpretation. It is "very hard to believe" that three people made a breakthrough of the claimed scale. She notes the authors had another preprint with a different author list (hinting there are more people involved, though she declines to speculate) [#208].
3. What is the history? The authors had long held a superconductivity-related patent, the "99" in the name refers to 1999, the year LK-99 was first synthesised, and they had published in a Korean journal earlier in 2023; the resonance only arose at the end of July, when groups worldwide "started dragging the hype around" and testing it [#208].
4. Does it replicate from the recipe? The questions are mostly about methods: was the synthesis done correctly, is it reproducible, did they lie. The claimed physics was seen only by the original Koreans; six more groups worldwide got a negative result, and groups that synthesised LK-99 from the original preprint's recipe did not observe superconductivity. FIAN (the Lebedev Physical Institute) researchers' short answer: no — "Материал проявляет свойства изолятора и не левитирует." (The material shows the properties of an insulator and does not levitate.) [#208]
5. Is it a "magic sample"? Her term for a pattern: "у тебя есть ОДИН ЕДИНСТВЕННЫЙ образец, его головокружительная физика и публикации о ней" (you have ONE SINGLE sample, its dizzying physics and publications about it). As soon as you replace it with an identical sample, the physics dissipates and all the tasty effects stop being observed. "Насколько это можно считать тру наукой с таким уровнем воспроизводимости, думайте сами." (How far this can be considered true science with such a level of reproducibility — judge for yourselves.) [#208]

Besides serious checks, a wave of memes and conspiracy theories rolled through the scientific community on whether LK-99 was a prank or a ChatGPT product [#208].

#### Dialectical reading (read both poles and the grey zone)
Purpose: the author's approach to choosing books on how the world is going, from #494 [#494]:
1. Notice one-sided framing. Reading the annotation of Ridley's *The Rational Optimist* (life keeps getting better, medicines more accessible, technology more progressive; not everything is smooth, but we move forward and are resilient to various failures), she realised it lacked dialectics of the kind "А что, блин, если нет, вот так вот раз — и нет?" (What the hell if not — just like that, and no?) — and pointedly notes the book is from 2010 [#494].
2. See both poles and the grey zone, systemically. "Ведь важно видеть оба полюса и серую зону между ними." (After all, it is important to see both poles and the grey zone between them.) — and to understand systemically how the shift towards one or the other happens [#494].
3. Look for counter-authors field by field. In economics she found Erik Reinert; then she looked for similar authors among socio-anthropologists and found Toby Ord's *The Precipice* (details under *Books on economics and humanity's future*) [#494].
4. Prefer recent, self-updating work. She values that *The Precipice* (March 2020) is closer to the reality we have faced and that Ord revisited the book's data and updated his risk estimates in January 2024: "Такой подход в моих глазах выглядит оперативным и готовым к обновлению своей позиции, что, я считаю, дорогого стоит." (In my eyes such an approach looks prompt and ready to update its position, which, I believe, is worth a lot.) [#494]
5. Separate the concept from the person. Despite reservations about the author's movement, she reads the work because "стоит разделять концепцию и персону" (one should separate the concept and the person): good things can become a weapon in bad hands, and vice versa [#494].

### Detailed insights

#### What philosophy is, and Patricia Churchland's challenge
The question "Is philosophy some kind of joke or a useful scientific discipline?" was asked in Agmentek's writing challenge, and the author takes it up as a continuation of her post on useful and not-so-useful sciences, calling philosophy their "damned mother" [#121].

Her definition: "Философия как таковая — не наука, это отдельная ветвь познания мира." (Philosophy as such is not a science; it is a separate branch of knowing the world.) It has its own tools for gathering and processing information, which differ by school — Socratics, sceptics, materialists, Stoics, phenomenalists, voluntarists, with the list left open-ended in the source ("и пр.") [#121].

Its main occupation is the search for what Douglas Adams packaged into the number 42 — answers to questions about life, the universe and everything. "Она предлагает разные варианты того, как про это подумать, как посмотреть и какие гипотезы и выводы представить." (It offers different options for how to think about it, how to look at it, and what hypotheses and conclusions to put forward.) That, she says, is probably its main "why" [#121].

Patricia Churchland, described as a neurophilosopher of our time (the cover image of the Edge post shows her), agrees with the "something resembling science" part: she sees philosophy as a "прото-науку" (proto-science) that poses hard questions, primarily of a practical nature. As an eliminative materialist she further argues that intuitive and "folk" concepts such as "free will" or "consciousness" should be revised in a reductionist key as scientists (here neurobiologists) uncover more confirmed data on how the phenomenon (here the brain and thinking) is built and works. Put simply, "необходимо устранить все сущности, что рассогласуются с научными данными" (it is necessary to eliminate all entities that are inconsistent with scientific data) [#121, #58].

The author's conclusion: at this historical moment "философия видится мне колыбелью познания и песочницей" (philosophy appears to me as the cradle of knowledge and the sandbox) of modus operandi — both for beginning thinkers and for those who have travelled a long path of scientific knowledge and now want to assemble what they found into a coherent worldview concept. She closes by asking readers whether modern people need philosophy and, if so, in what form and why [#121].

#### The LK-99 case: background on superconductivity
The forwarded post opens by saying the world of modern physics was shaken by something stronger than the Barbie fever, and covers "the discovery (and closing)" of LK-99 [#208].

The claim. A couple of weeks before the post, three Koreans posted a preprint claiming to have discovered a substance that is superconducting at temperatures below 120 °C — i.e. a room-temperature superconductor ("this is not a drill") [#208].

What superconductivity is. A phenomenon in which a substance has zero electrical resistance and expels magnetic field from itself — hence the effect of a superconductor levitating over a magnet, the so-called "Mohammed's coffin". It was first discovered in mercury at -270 °C (the temperature of liquid helium) a little over a century ago; later research aimed at creating a high-temperature superconductor. Superconductors working at liquid-nitrogen temperature (-196 °C) are already called high-temperature, because nitrogen is cheap and easy to obtain. For a popular explanation she suggests an episode of the Russian cartoon Smeshariki (Смешарики) on the topic; a picture in her previous post was taken from it and shows Bardeen, Cooper and Schrieffer running to collect their Nobel Prize [#208].

Why high-temperature superconductivity matters. If current flows through a sample without resistance, there are no losses; the main applications relate to the electric power industry, magnetic-levitation trains, the quantum computer "and everything else". A room-temperature superconductor therefore was, and remains, a "blue dream" on the level of cold fusion. She also points readers to a post by "Katerina" (another channel) which she says lays out the facts [#208].

#### The ten childhood books that shaped the author
Context: the author defines childhood as something pervaded by carefreeness, freedom of discovery and calm, warm support, without anxieties for oneself and loved ones — and says "Моё «детство» началось, когда мне было 19." (My "childhood" began when I was 19.) For a long time she had been a support and mentor, friend and mother to others and a reflection of them, always knowing which face to create for the other person ("your needs are more important than mine"), while her own wants and values were an ellipsis; her personality was hidden under a thick layer of amalgam, with only endurance and will "like Demi Moore's G.I. Jane", apart from rare moments of solitude in creativity and sport. Only far from her former surroundings, washing off the tension with silence and calm support, could she look inward and gradually feel out what in the outer world resonated with her inner one. The following years brought discoveries that opened her world "from the size of a needle's eye to an almost infinite universe", including these books, which laid the foundation of her professional development and reflected and completed how she sees the world — in many ways to this day [#116]:
1. Genome — Matt Ridley. Inspiring popular science about the structure of human DNA; it captured her so much that in her first years of searching for a profession she wanted to become a geneticist [#116].
2. The Red Queen: Sex and the Evolution of Human Nature — Matt Ridley (Russian title «Секс и эволюция человеческой природы»; the post gives the English title as "The red queen theory: sex and evolution of human being"). How does the struggle between viruses, mutations and adaptation processes go on inside a human, and what role does reproduction play in all of it? [#116]
3. The God Delusion — Richard Dawkins. A book with well-raised questions for beginning sceptics (not necessarily atheists): "Но только начинающих!" (But only for beginners!) [#116]
4. The Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat — Oliver Sacks (she read it in English). What illusions a brain damaged in different areas can create, and how to live with that in ordinary life [#116].
5. The Large, the Small and the Human Mind — Roger Penrose, Stephen Hawking et al. Hard to describe in two words; she says it "очень помогает масштабировать представления о мире" (helps a lot to scale up one's ideas about the world) [#116].
6. The Ego Tunnel — Thomas Metzinger. A close acquaintance with the very brain program we are used to calling "ourselves", with illustrations of how changeable it actually is [#116].
7. Religion Explained — Pascal Boyer. A deep study of religion in the lives of different peoples — from its kinds and functions in different societies to how all of it is connected with the features of our brain's structure; spoiler: there are big and very big differences from what might come to mind at first [#116].
8. The Blindsight / Echopraxia duology — Peter Watts. Science fiction about intelligent superorganisms and the change in a person's inner picture of the world in the process. She agrees with Russian Wikipedia that Blindsight explores themes of personality, consciousness, free will, artificial intelligence, game theory, neurobiology and human evolution [#116].
9. The Age of Nothing (Эпоха пустоты) — Peter Watson (she warns not to confuse him with Peter Watts). Non-fiction about how people, including great ones, construct life meanings when every god has died for them [#116].
10. Personality Theories — Larry Hjelle and Daniel Ziegler. In her view "фундаментальнейший труд, который внёс порядок во всю массу подходов к исследованию личности" (a most fundamental work that brought order into the whole mass of approaches to studying personality). Her incomplete list of what it covers: whether there is free will or everything can be calculated; what is greater in a person — "nature" or "nurture", the inner impulse to act or reaction to the external, the constant or the variable; how and why different psychological schools view this; and how all of it can be measured [#116].

#### Books on economics and humanity's future
The post's occasion was a personal one (spending a remaining account balance), and it led her to her wish-list library [#494]:
- The Rational Optimist — Matt Ridley (2010). Her "beloved" Ridley's optimistic book, even by title: life gets better, medicines more accessible, technologies more progressive; not everything is smooth, but we move forward and are resilient to failures. She found it lacking dialectics (see *Dialectical reading*) [#494].
- How Rich Countries Got Rich and Why Poor Countries Stay Poor — Erik Reinert (not to be confused with the "useless pop" *Rich Dad Poor Dad*). Found a couple of years earlier; still her favourite for how clearly he points to economists' "sclerosis" and the needless acceptance on faith of the maximalist utopia of free-trade joys. Her summary of his argument: it has been shown several times over recent centuries that free trade starts to work only after a long period of a country getting on its feet through outside injections of funds (sponsorship, investment); otherwise it exhausts "newcomers" in futile attempts to play on equal terms with those who already have established production, lowered costs and can trade without fearing for their last pants. Reading economic history, as Reinert does with readers, shows how, why and where economics at some point came to dominate as a theory detached from life. She thinks this was not malice but partial knowledge, wishful thinking and a thirst for ease, reinforced by the dominant discourses of the late 19th–early 20th centuries ("opportunities should be equal for all" and the like), which affect how information is filtered — so "нужно иметь и кругозор, и нехилую смелость, чтобы проверять на прочность мировоззрения" (one needs both breadth of outlook and considerable courage to stress-test worldviews) [#494].
- The Precipice: Existential Risk and the Future of Humanity — Toby Ord (published March 2020). About which risks humanity should pay attention to given the achievements and accumulated problems of the 20th–21st centuries: from climate change to man-made pathogens and artificial intelligence. In January 2024 Ord published a review of the book's data with updated risk estimates. Her caveat: she plans to stay on guard because Effective Altruism, of which Ord is a supporter, is utilitarian — the end may justify the means, and the ethics of acts is defined by their usefulness. She still considers it important to read because, first, it is unknown whether the book touches ethical aspects at all or is simply a good historical-analytical review, and second, concept and person should be separated. She asks readers who have read it to share impressions [#494].

### Tools and resources mentioned

- research.com — "Хозяйственным учёным на заметку" (A note for thrifty scientists). A portal that, among other things, ranks the most influential works and people in science (method under *Evidence and numbers*). What it is useful for — quickly and easily finding: (1) quality materials in your research field; (2) top conferences and journals in your speciality; (3) lists of the scientists who know the question you are interested in most thoroughly. ## Biomedical research news: aging, epigenetics and medicine

### What this covers

A set of short science digests the channel posted between 2022 and 2023, some written by the channel author and some forwarded from other science channels (Безвольные каменщики, Блуждающий нерв). They cover sex differences in strength gains, mitochondrial transplants, transgenerational epigenetic inheritance (a mouse model and the aspartame study), cryopreservation of organs, parabiosis and aging, a manifesto for a digital twin of the worm nervous system, entropy as the reason life on Earth needs the Sun, the public-health lessons of COVID and a breakthrough in treating multidrug-resistant tuberculosis [#39, #146, #174, #178, #186, #187, #201, #210, #238, #255].

The posts matter, in the authors' framing, because they show how one generation's environment and habits can shape the next generation's biology [#174, #238], how close certain life-extension and preservation technologies are to becoming practical [#186, #201], how neuroscience may change through in-silico modelling [#210], and how good medicine can still fail when communication and policy fail [#178, #255].

### Core ideas

- Same training, same strength gains. Men carry more upper-body muscle on average because of testosterone, but with identical training and nutrition the absolute strength gain is statistically the same in both sexes; differences lie in accumulated averages, not in trainability [#39].
- The environment of parents can be inherited. Parental overweight, poor diet or stress can affect children and possibly grandchildren through epigenetic marks passed in eggs and sperm; new mouse models with targeted methylation make the mechanism studyable [#174]. Aspartame consumption in mice impaired spatial memory and the effect appeared in the offspring of exposed males [#238].
- Young blood extends old lives, mechanism unknown. Joining old mice to young ones for three months lengthened the old mice's lives by 6–9%, yet nobody knows why young blood rejuvenates [#201].
- Successful cryopreservation strengthens trust in long-term freezing. Frozen rat kidneys worked after transplant, which could fix donor-organ logistics and makes cryopreservation more credible [#186].
- Model a whole simple nervous system before mice. A manifesto argues for building a working digital twin of C. elegans' nervous system rather than a connectome [#210].
- COVID was handled badly despite medical success. The forwarded author argues that distrust, poor communication and impulsive policy cost lives, and that humanity would handle the next pandemic just as poorly [#178].
- MDR-TB treatment became shorter and far cheaper. The endTB regimen cuts treatment from 18 to 9 months and from 5000 to 300 dollars [#255].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Reasoning for changing COVID vaccination policy
The forwarded post explains why people were first forced to vaccinate en masse and later appeared to be left alone — something authorities failed to explain, so people could not tell whether they needed another booster [#178].
1. Early pandemic: vaccinate everyone, urgently. The strains were "злее" ("more vicious") and there was no collective immunity, so herd immunity had to be created quickly by vaccinating everyone [#178].
2. Later phase: regular universal vaccination is no longer obligatory. Strains now cause severe illness less often, and roughly 100% of everyone older than three years has immunity, so it is no longer necessary to vaccinate every single person regularly [#178].
The author's lesson: there is nothing strange in this change of policy; the failure was in not communicating the reasoning [#178].

#### Catalogue of failures in the COVID response
According to the forwarded author, despite huge pharmacological and medical success (effective, safe vaccines and reliable treatment protocols were developed within a year), the three years of COVID showed [#178]:
1. "пещерное антивакцинаторство" ("cave-man anti-vaccinationism") and distrust of medicine in general;
2. illogical and impulsive government policy;
3. poor informing of the public;
4. excessive tightening of restrictions — or, the opposite, refusal of any responsibility for citizens' deaths;
5. too slow a reaction at the start;
6. and at the same time too long a refusal to lift measures once everything was over [#178].

#### Vision of future neuroscience (digital-twin manifesto)
The forwarded author reads in the manifesto an image of science its authors foresee and bring closer: neuroscience will be fed by two sources [#210]:
1. Observation tools with grown capabilities that can see the activity and organization of living networks in the finest detail;
2. Machine learning resources that make it possible to extract correlations from petabytes of data, build models and experiment *in-silico* [#210].
The practical recommendation of the authors is to postpone mouse experiments for now and derive the principles of neural computation from a simpler model organism, C. elegans [#210].

### Detailed insights

#### Sex differences in strength and training (study by Paulo Gentil and colleagues)
The post, titled with a wordplay "Пчёлки работают попой, а пчелы рубят с плеча" (roughly "female bees work with their bottoms, male bees chop from the shoulder"), asks whether men and women differ in strength and physical capacity and what role testosterone and progesterone play [#39].

A study by Paulo Gentil and colleagues on upper-body strength showed two things [#39]:
- On the one hand, in the absence of physical training, testosterone lets men accumulate more muscle mass in the shoulders than women (as a statistical norm) [#39].
- On the other hand, with identical training and unchanged nutrition, physical (Newtonian) strength increases equally in both — the difference is within tenths, so small that it is recognized as statistically insignificant [#39].

Further points from the post: in women, strength gains occur predominantly when progesterone rises, which happens in the second phase of the menstrual cycle; and, according to another study mentioned in the accompanying video, women gain strength more easily in the lower body, whereas men gain it in the upper body [#39]. The author's reading of the article: what actually differs between the sexes is the average absolute muscularity and shoulder width accumulated by a certain age, not the response to training [#39].

The author closes with open questions rather than answers: what would happen — and surely does happen — if women had higher testosterone during puberty; at what point a female MMA fighter becomes able to pin an untrained man; and, jokingly, whether the study will lead to a new sport, "арм-тай-рестлингу (или по нашему — бёдрорукоборью)" (*"arm-thigh-wrestling (or in our terms, thigh-arm-wrestling)"*), combining the upper-body strength of men with the lower-body strength of women. "Так много вопросов, так мало ответов." ("So many questions, so few answers.") [#39]

#### Mitochondrial transplants for rare genetic disease
A one-line news item: children with rare genome mutations were treated with mitochondrial transplants from their mothers. A Russian translation of the news (on N+1) was suggested by a reader [#146].

#### Transgenerational epigenetic inheritance: a new mouse model (Takahashi et al., Cell)
Parental overweight, a diet low in nutrients, or stress can endanger the health of children and possibly grandchildren through the inheritance of "эпигенетических модификаций" ("epigenetic modifications") [#174].

- What epigenetic modifications are. Biochemical changes to DNA or to the associated histone proteins — for example adding methyl or acetyl groups — that influence gene expression, cell type and many other things without changing the DNA sequence [#174].
- How they arise and travel. They can appear in response to environmental signals and be passed to subsequent generations in eggs and sperm [#174].
- The open debate. It is currently disputed how epigenetic marks such as methyl groups on DNA can be transmitted from parents to offspring [#174].
- The model. In a paper in Cell, Takahashi et al. presented a mouse model in which methylation is induced at targeted DNA sites, and showed that the approach can be used to address pressing mechanistic questions about transgenerational epigenetic inheritance (TGI). Their targeted methylation lets researchers probe specifically the genomic features that may be linked to TGI [#174].
- Next steps named by the article. Determining how the molecular landscape (epigenetic modifications, three-dimensional chromatin organization, with the list left open by the source: "и т. д.") and the underlying DNA sequence together contribute to protecting edited genes from the reprogramming machinery [#174].
- Further use. The approach can also be applied to study the heritability of epigenetic marks on disease-linked genes [#174].
- Why it matters. Identifying the mechanisms that connect the accumulation of parental epigenetic marks with the development and health of offspring could strongly affect our understanding of hereditary diseases [#174].

#### Aspartame and spatial memory in mice (and their offspring)
Headline claim: the sugar substitute aspartame contributes to deficits in spatial memory and learning [#238].
- Exposure. Lab mice drank water with aspartame for 16 weeks, in an amount described as 8 ounces a day (roughly equal to 237 ml of soda) [#238].
- Effect. The experimental mice performed significantly worse in working memory than mice that drank plain water; during learning they were worse at holding and handling new spatial information [#238].
- Inheritance. The effect was seen not only in the exposed mice but also in the offspring of the males of the experimental group [#238].
- Interpretation by the author. This part of diet seems to belong to environmental factors whose influence is passed on "генетически" ("genetically") to 1–2 generations — another example of how the behavior and habits of one generation affect the brain organization of the next [#238].
- Dose context. The dose used was 7–15% of the amount currently classified as safe by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [#238].
The author ends half-jokingly that she will drink water and eat one piece of normal milk chocolate — with sugar [#238].

#### Parabiosis extends the life of old mice
A forwarded post (from Блуждающий нерв): a group of scientists extended the life of old mice by joining their blood vessels with those of young mice. The pairs were "stitched" together for three months and then separated; the old animals lived 6–9% longer, which is equivalent to six years for a human [#201].
- The authors of the Nature Aging paper write that the old mice's bodies became younger as judged by molecular markers [#201].
- What surprises the forwarded author: parabiosis has been studied for a long time (this group is far from the first), yet it is still not clear what in young blood rejuvenates the old organism — and the authors of the new study do not know either [#201].
- The team came from Duke, UCLA and Harvard and included Vadim Gladyshev, Sergei Dmitriev, Anastasia Shindyapina and Alexander Tyshkovskiy; the forwarded author notes with curiosity that he did not know the Belozersky Institute of Physico-Chemical Biology at Moscow State University (НИИ ФХБ МГУ) was connected with such projects [#201].

#### Cryopreserved kidneys transplanted in rats
Researchers at the University of Minnesota successfully restarted previously frozen organs (kidneys) in rats after transplantation [#186]. The channel author draws two implications:
1. Locally — donor-organ logistics. One of the hardest parts of transplantation is delivering the organ in time; mistakes are costly both financially and in human lives. Freezing could help solve this [#186].
2. Globally — long-term cryopreservation. It is an example of a successful technological solution for the idea of long-term cryopreservation. The author notes that life extension through freezing currently raises "вопросики" (*"little questions"*, i.e. doubts) at the present stage of technology, so successful practices like this help one count on the reliability of such procedures in the future [#186].
She closes with a joke about future wedding vows: "В богатстве и бедности, до заморозки и после…" ("For richer, for poorer, before freezing and after…") [#186].

#### Entropy: how Earth uses the Sun's energy to sustain life
The author frames Earth and the Sun as some of the largest "machines" known to us and recommends a video that explains, in simple language and from an interesting, non-pop angle, the concept of entropy: how Earth uses the Sun's energy to create and maintain life, and why life on Earth would be impossible without the Sun. The video is in English; the author suggests switching on Russian auto-subtitles [#187].

#### A digital twin of the C. elegans nervous system
A fresh manifesto by a group of authors including Ed Boyden and George Church, "To reverse engineer an entire nervous system" (arXiv, 2023), calls for building a working model of the nervous system of a worm — not a connectome, which "не дал понимания" ("did not give understanding"), but all the interactions of the cells; in effect, a "цифрового двойника" ("digital twin") of the nervous system [#210].
- The authors think mouse experiments should be postponed for now and the principles of neural computation derived from a simpler model [#210].
- Their stated promise: reverse engineering the causal interactions of C. elegans' nervous system would establish in-silico simulations as a way to accelerate neuroscience and teach how to run experiments that reveal how circuits causally work [#210]. For the broader picture of science the manifesto implies, see *Vision of future neuroscience* above.

#### COVID: the end of the emergency and what it showed
Forwarded from the channel Безвольные каменщики. WHO cancelled the global health emergency status for COVID. Practically, the author says, this changes little, because most countries had long since cancelled restrictions and support measures such as free testing; but as a symbolic declaration it was a "must" [#178].

- Death toll. Officially 6.9 million died of COVID, but WHO head Ghebreyesus voiced a consensus figure of 20 million. The roughly threefold gap is explained by massive under-reporting of deaths: estimated 12-fold in Egypt, 8-fold in Iran and 13-fold in Belarus [#178].
- The paradox. Instead of showing people the power of modern medicine, COVID sharpened distrust of it and strengthened anti-vaccinationists, partly because authorities failed to explain what was happening and why (see *Reasoning for changing COVID vaccination policy* and *Catalogue of failures in the COVID response*) [#178].
- Retreat of state control. Part of the reason for the "отката к средневеквым [sic] представлениям о медицине" ("rollback to medieval notions of medicine") is that states — including authoritarian ones such as Russia — abandoned strict control in healthcare [#178].
- Ideology over evidence. In Russia, Poland and some US states the attitude to vaccination is "we won't force anyone", while the attitude to abortion moves rapidly to the opposite pole, "ban everything". The author concludes that instead of implementing the results of years of research, health policy is increasingly defined by primitive ideas about what is "богопротивности" ("ungodliness"); in that light, banning abortions and refusing vaccination turn out to be logical and mutually consistent [#178].
- Outlook. The author welcomes the end of the acute phase and the transition to an endemic state, and that no new pathogen has appeared yet — but predicts that with a new one humanity will behave just as incompetently, and nobody guarantees it would take 20 million lives rather than, say, 220 million [#178].

#### Multidrug-resistant tuberculosis: the endTB breakthrough
- Definition. Multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (ТБ-МЛУ, MDR-TB) is a disease that ignores the strongest drugs in this field, isoniazid and rifampicin [#255].
- The result. Independent clinical trials of a new approach from the EndTB project (the post glosses the name as "Stop tuberculosis") succeeded; the presentation was met with a standing ovation [#255].
- What changed. Treatment of this form of tuberculosis now takes 9 months instead of 18 and costs 300 dollars instead of 5000 [#255].
- Advocacy. Брайан Грин (as named in the post) explains in a video how these results could change the lives of those who get access, and insists that WHO must recommend these protocols everywhere as the gold standard and increase their availability — without deferring to Johnson & Johnson and other pharmaceutical companies — otherwise organizations such as Nerdfighteria will push for it with tougher measures [#255].

### Evidence and numbers

- Gentil et al.: strength gains with identical training differ between men and women only within tenths — statistically insignificant [#39].
- Parabiosis: 3 months joined, old mice lived 6–9% longer, equivalent to ~6 human years [#201].
- Aspartame: 16 weeks, 8 ounces/day (~237 ml of soda); dose = 7–15% of the FDA safe level; effect seen in offspring of exposed males, inferred to pass 1–2 generations [#238].
- COVID: 6.9 million official deaths vs 20 million consensus (WHO head); under-reporting ×12 Egypt, ×8 Iran, ×13 Belarus; ~100% of people over 3 years old have immunity; vaccines and protocols developed within one year [#178].
- MDR-TB (endTB): 9 vs 18 months, 300 vs 5000 dollars [#255].
