## Sensory regulation, emotions and recovery for neurodivergent people

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's material on how neurodivergent (especially autistic) people live with their senses and emotions: what stimming is and what it does, the eight sensory channels through which we contact the world, how autistic adults recognize, express and regulate emotions (a detailed retelling of Anastasia Zmievskaya's DocPsyFest lecture plus its slides), and two practitioners' concrete sensory "menus" for relaxing and for restoring energy. [#365, #650, #727, #729, #732]

According to the source, this matters because autistic stress is often invisible from the outside and is noticed by the person themselves only late, so knowing one's sensory channels and having ready ways to relax and refill energy is how one can "direct the flow" rather than discover overload only after a breakdown; and because the people around an autistic person often misread their emotional reactions as exaggeration. [#659, #660, #727]

### Core ideas

- Stimming is universal, but in autism it differs in intensity, form and function. Everyone fidgets, taps or hums to relieve stress, concentrate or have fun; in neurodivergent people stimming can be more intense, more unusual or ritual-like, and it mainly serves adaptation, communicating needs and managing sensory/emotional load. See *Forms of stimming* and *Functions of stimming*. [#365]
- The author's hypothesis: the root difficulty is suppressing incoming detail. Autistic people seem to receive every detail separately and must make an effort to separate figure from ground; stimming helps by narrowing attention to one predictable stream. See *Detail-suppression hypothesis*. [#365]
- We have eight sensory channels, and each person's channels have different widths and flows. Besides the classic senses, neurologists now count vestibular sense, interoception and proprioception; knowing one's channels lets one direct the flow of energy. [#727]
- Autistic emotional life: more negative emotion, invisible arousal, late recognition. Survey respondents report frequent anxiety and anger, suppress outward expression, and often understand that something was wrong only after a breakdown. [#650, #651, #659, #660]
- Time is the key resource for recovery. Respondents put time at the centre of everything that helps them regulate; the author calls it perhaps the most valuable gift for a person with ASD. [#655, #661]
- Relaxation and recovery are different jobs. Relaxation removes what weighs on you ("emptying the vessel of muddy water"); recovery refills what was spent. Each has its own key question and its own sensory menu. [#729, #732]

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Forms of stimming in neurodivergent people (three types)
Definition first: stimming, or self-stimulation, is the process of repeating the same body movements, sounds, words, actions with objects. In all people it can be part of development and behaviour. The author's everyday examples, which "каждый из нас сможет припомнить" (each of us can recall): fiddling with or "getting stuck on" objects — twirling a pen or fidget spinner, drawing circles all over a sheet; repeating movements — tapping fingers or clicking the tongue in rhythm, throwing a ball against a wall, rocking on a chair while lost in thought; and humming or mooing songs, which scientifically is called "вокализация" (vocalization). Such behaviour is usually used to relieve stress, raise concentration, or simply because it is entertaining. [#365]

In neurodivergent people, especially with ASD, stimming differs in both content and motivation. It can include three types: [#365]

1. "Typical" actions repeated with greater intensity. Examples given: rocking with a high amplitude; fiddling with one's own body — strongly tensing and relaxing muscles, lengthy "grooming one's fur" while lost in thought (cleaning the face, filing nails, twirling locks of hair around a finger); listening to a song or watching a video many times in a row; clapping or stomping feet under the table — without a melody and without end; spinning around one's own axis for a long time, hopping down a corridor in a single rhythm; building complex construction-set towers with a repeating pattern of pieces. [#365]
2. Unusual, complex movements. Examples: flapping hands, clapping palms; chewing something because it crunches nicely; grinding or rubbing the teeth against each other rhythmically and for a long time; making interesting movements of the tongue inside the mouth (which can be done quietly, "чтобы только самому было в кайф" — so that only you get the kick out of it). [#365]
3. Ritual-like actions — but without fear that something bad will happen otherwise, as in OCD. Examples: lining up toys in a neat row (the author calls it the example that "became a classic"); arranging and straightening things so that they are even, in accordance with rules (for instance of composition, a golden standard or an optimal algorithm); looking at details on a wall long and carefully. The distinction from OCD is explicit: the ritual is not driven by fear of a bad outcome. [#365]

#### Functions of stimming in autism (three functions)
The author says this kind of stimming "чаще выполняет несколько иные функции" (more often performs somewhat different functions) than everyday fidgeting: [#365]

1. Adapting to a new environment and interacting with surroundings — squeezing, jumping, sniffing, tasting things as a way of getting to know a place. [#365]
2. Communicating preferences and needs — some repetitive actions can point to specific wishes and interests, so they carry information for those around. [#365]
3. Managing sensory and emotional load (including strong joy) — many people with ASD have heightened sensitivity in the sense organs and in the intensity of emotions. Stimming can help cope with or prevent overload: by focusing attention on one stream of impressions, a person can make their input predictable and get rid of "visual noise" and jitters ("мандраж"). [#365]

#### Detail-suppression hypothesis of autistic perception (author's hypothesis)
Under the heading "Авангардные мысли" (avant-garde thoughts), the author offers, explicitly as an assumption "исходя из моих знаний и опыта" (based on my knowledge and experience), an explanation for these difficulties and for why stimming matters: [#365]

- The core claim: the source of the difficulties — and of the importance of stimming — is that it is hard to suppress the details of incoming information. [#365]
- Neurotypical contrast: neurotypical people normally generalize data easily, can perceive things in broad strokes, and their attention is more selective. The author says this is why many visual illusions work, and why one can perceive an Impressionist painting as fairly realistic. [#365]
- Autistic perception: people with ASD seem to receive every detail separately, and it takes effort "«выделить фигуру из фона»" (to separate the figure from the ground) — i.e. to process only what is a priority in the given context. [#365]
- Historical link (also hedged with "возможно" — perhaps): this may be why autistic people were once considered to have an "ultra-male brain": what was then attributed to men was absolute left-hemisphere dominance, which, according to the Russian neurological school, provides detailed, step-by-step, sequential ("сукцессивной", successive) processing — as opposed to the holistic, silhouette ("симультанной", simultaneous) perception of the right hemisphere. The author promised a separate story on this in later posts. [#365]

#### Eight sensory channels and the channel metaphor
The author frames senses as tied to body parts that receive external signals, while stressing that sense organs are only "лишь первый рубеж длинной цепочки, имя которой — «чувственное восприятие»" (only the first frontier of a long chain whose name is "sensory perception") — hence insider jokes like "I hear you with my brain" or "I perceive you with my whole body". Staying at the level of everyday experience (not neural descriptions or direct electrical stimulation of the brain), we have 8 sensory channels for interacting with the world and sensing our place in it, "including literally". [#727]

The channels:
1–5. The ones known from childhood, written in the source as "Зрение, осязание, обоняние, запах и вкус" (sight, touch, smell, smell/odour and taste) — see the caveat about this wording below. [#727]
6. Vestibular sense — signals from the vestibular apparatus: balance, gaze stabilization, orientation in space. [#727]
7. Interoception — signals from internal organs: thirst, hunger, temperature, heart signals, plus further ones not named. [#727]
8. Proprioception — the position of body parts relative to each other: signals from muscles, tendons and joints about limb position, force and movement. This is what the neurologist's test "закройте глаза, дотроньтесь до кончика носа" (close your eyes, touch the tip of your nose) checks. [#727]

The author notes that neurologists added the last three "for practical purposes" only recently. [#727]

The channel metaphor (the author likes "channels" also as a metaphor):
- In each of us some channels may be narrower and shallower, others wider and deeper. [#727]
- At different moments a channel may carry either too turbulent a flow or too little — "до сухой земли" (down to dry earth). [#727]
- While inside the flow we cannot always stop and notice that something is flowing wrong, and sometimes we may not picture the landscape at all. [#727]
- This applies whether one is simply in the "modern race of Western civilization" or has sensory nuances on the autism spectrum: in each of us our unique sensory experiences both replenish and spend energy. [#727]
- The practical conclusion: "Чем больше мы будем знакомы с собой, тем больше в этих каналах мы сможем направлять течение" (the better we know ourselves, the more we can direct the flow in these channels). The author's hopeful image is a unique cascade where all streams merge into one big multisensory deep feeling. [#727]

The series (with psychotherapist Olga Gorbacheva) was announced as starting from how to get acquainted with one's sensory world and find "keys to all 8 locks". [#727]

#### Arousal-valence map of stress signs (high vs low arousal)
A lecture slide asks how autistic people notice that they are stressed. It places stress signs on two axes: pleasant–unpleasant (vertical) and arousal–calm (horizontal). Stress lives in the unpleasant half, in two zones: [#652]

- Unpleasant + high arousal ("возбуждение"): muscle tension, tachycardia, sweating, tremor, anger / irritation / aggression, tearfulness, anxiety, stimming, increased motor activity. [#652]
- Unpleasant + low arousal (toward "спокойствие"): fatigue / apathy / "nothing is wanted", difficulty thinking and perceiving, "туман в голове" (fog in the head), depersonalization / derealization. [#652]

The slide adds a cited observation (PMID: 19884152): a mother of an autistic child said she recognizes that her daughter is overexcited when she sees her tapping her fingers in a particular way — i.e. a specific stim can serve others as a stress marker. [#652]

In the author's text summary: high-arousal signs people usually manage to recognize are muscle tension, sweating, irritation, stimming; low-arousal signs are apathy, "fog in the head", derealization. Often people on the spectrum notice stress only by observing physical or behavioural changes after the fact. [#659]

#### Three-step pattern of delayed recognition of overload
From the survey of Zmievskaya's sample: physiological and sensory discomfort is easier to recognize (especially pain, hunger, noise, fatigue); discomfort in communication with people is harder, especially in the moment. The typical sequence: [#651]

1. Emotional signals are not recognized right away. [#651]
2. Overload accumulates — gradually, unnoticed. [#651, msg #660]
3. Only from "breakdowns" ("срывам") in state and behaviour can one understand what was wrong and how badly. [#651, msg #660]

What helps break the pattern: self-reflection and the experience of therapy. [#651, msg #660]

#### Causes of emotional dysregulation and typical trigger situations
Why emotions "knock you out" (emotional dysregulation) — four causes from the lecture, with the author's additions: [#660]

1. Recognition gap: recognizing physical (sensory) discomfort can be hard, but it is still easier than recognizing interpersonal or emotional discomfort — so people stay in a situation that is stressful for them longer than they should. [#660]
2. Slower processing of emotional information than in neurotypicals. The author adds from her own data: trying to "calculate" in time leads to overload; what is important is noticed late, and then it may come crashing down like an avalanche. [#660]
3. Sensory overload itself — too bright, too loud, a cluttered visual background, itchy, unpleasant in the mouth, plus similar inputs. [#660]
4. Co-occurring diagnoses — the author adds: migraine, epilepsy, chronic pain, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, mast cell activation syndrome, the spectrum of anxiety and depressive conditions, plus further ones not named. [#660]

Situations in which this often happens (six): [#660]
1. Changes in the environment.
2. Social interaction.
3. Sensory stimuli (hyper- or hyposensitivity).
4. Plans disrupted on someone else's initiative.
5. Rule-breaking and illogical/irrational behaviour in other people.
6. Encountering injustice — with a "nuance" the author flags by linking a translated Telegraph article "Аутизм и справедливость" (Autism and justice); its argument — that autistic people do not have an innate strong sense of justice — is written out under *The "nuance" about injustice* in Detailed insights. [#660, article #660]

#### Survey map of effective emotion-regulation strategies (time at the centre plus six clusters)
The lecture slide asks respondents "Какие способы регулирования эмоций вам помогают больше всего?" (Which ways of regulating emotions help you most?) and draws a mind map with time ("время") in the centre and six clusters around it. The author's post text adds the purpose of some clusters. [#655, msg #661]

- Time (centre). The key factor for recovery. The author adds: "пожалуй, это самый ценный подарок, который может быть для человека с РАС" (perhaps the most valuable gift there can be for a person with ASD). [#661, img #655]
1. Solitude ("уединение") — to reduce the number and strength of stimuli. [#655, msg #661]
2. Rest ("отдых") — stopping, sleep, nature; breaks. [#655, msg #661]
3. Body ("тело") — breathing, tapping/EMDR, walking, shower, food, self-massage, crying, maintaining physical health. [#655, msg #661]
4. Distraction ("отвлечение") — TV series, reading, drawing, games, shopping, collecting, animals, and doing tasks "даже через силу" (even by forcing oneself). [#655, msg #661]
5. Mindfulness ("осознанность") — journaling, psychotherapy, analysing triggers and states, rational thinking, writing out lists, DBT skills, meditation. [#655, msg #661]
6. Communication with loved ones ("общение с близкими") — supportive contact with close people. [#655, msg #661]

#### Fallback strategies when information and support are lacking
A slide on features of emotional regulation (citing PMID: 23800481) and the author's text: [#656, msg #661]

- Used less often: cognitive reappraisal ("когнитивная переоценка" — changing for yourself the meaning of what is happening). The slide's hedged reason: difficulties with cognitive flexibility and switching attention (with a question mark); the post says "возможно, из-за низкой гибкости" (possibly because of low flexibility). The author adds that for some situations reappraisal is simply not applicable: "как пересмотреть отношение там, где, метафорически, в ногу впивается заноза?" (how do you reconsider your attitude where, metaphorically, a splinter is digging into your foot?). [#656, msg #661]
- Used more often when information and support are lacking: [#661, img #656]
  1. Suppression.
  2. Withdrawal (detachment).
  3. Avoidance.
  4. Rumination — "мысленная жвачка об одном и том же без выводов и решений" (mental chewing of the same thing without conclusions or decisions).
  5. And fairly often: when stressors bombard the person, they block them by hyperfocusing on imaginary worlds. The slide illustrates this with a meme: a man labelled hyperfixation on a fictional universe holds a shield over a little girl labelled "я" (me) against meteors labelled stress, loneliness, study and anxiety. [#661, img #656]

#### Recommendations for autistic people (five)
From the lecture slide and the author's retelling: [#657, msg #661]

1. Use apps for tracking your state and raising awareness (a CBT diary, AI and similar). Why: this lets you get to know yourself, your cycles and your features better. [#657, msg #661]
2. Study your sensory profile and use a sensory diet. [#657, msg #661]
3. Use AI to make sense of your experiences/states and, if needed, to formulate messages to other people. The slide gives two example prompts:
   - (I have ASD. I feel bad, but I don't fully understand what happened, why I feel this way and what exactly I feel. Ask simple, concrete questions so that I can explore it).
   - (Help me formulate a message for a loved one describing what is happening to me and why, and how best to respond to it). [#657, msg #661]
4. Use an energy-accounting system — "Светофор" (Traffic light), "Теория ложек" (Spoon theory) — i.e. pacing; and use every available opportunity to build a comfortable way of life that takes your features and needs into account. [#657, msg #661]
5. Create an "Instruction" ("Инструкцию") / "manual" for loved ones — write the main things close people and those around you should know about your features and needs: what helps you, what doesn't, and how best to support you, especially in hard moments. Share it if that is safe and useful — this is self-advocacy ("самоадвокация"). [#657, msg #661]

#### Recommendations for people around autistic people
From the lecture slide and the author's retelling: [#658, msg #661]

1. Study the concept of neurodiversity and the features of autistic people — no to judgment and devaluation. [#658, msg #661]
2. Believe what an autistic person tells you about themselves: they are not lying, not exaggerating, not seeking attention, but informing you about their features. Specifically:
   - autistic people really do tire faster, especially from communication, and need a lot of rest and solitude;
   - they really experience the most intense stress from some stimuli (sounds, smells and similar);
   - they tolerate unpredictability with difficulty and feel stability and constancy thanks to rules/rituals (this reduces stress). [#658, msg #661]
3. Remember that the emotional sphere of autistic people really has particular features:
   - they often experience strong stress even if they look calm outside — emotional manifestations may be muted outwardly but intense inwardly;
   - they find it hard to notice discomfort at an early stage;
   - it can be extremely hard to put their experiences into words, especially in the moment and especially orally;
   - sometimes the intensity of emotions and discomfort is so high that an emotional breakdown happens (even if the person tried to regulate themselves), or the person needs solitude to come back to themselves — and at that time they simply should not be touched. [#658, msg #661]
4. Ask your autistic loved one what suits them specifically: touch? solitude? conversation? — i.e. what would help right now. [#658, msg #661]
5. Be understanding: periodic detachment, especially during strong stress, is not anger and not unwillingness to be close; sometimes they simply need space. [#661]

#### Relaxation vs recovery (emptying the vessel vs refilling it)
A two-part series by the author and psychotherapist Olga Gorbacheva. Part 1 (relaxation) removes what weighs on you — "освобождали сосуд от мутной воды" (freed the vessel from muddy water); Part 2 (recovery) is about refilling it. A third part, on resting by adding inspiration, beauty and taste, was announced but is not in this source. [#729, #732]

Part 1 — Relaxation. [#729]
- *Olga's definition:* "Мы уменьшаем нагрузку, отпускаем контроль, сбрасываем напряжение, перестаём решать задачи и справляться с требованиями, переключаемся, чувствуем безопасность" (We reduce the load, let go of control, release tension, stop solving tasks and coping with demands, switch over, feel safe).
- *Key question:* "Что поможет мне перестать напрягаться?" (What will help me stop straining?)
- *Margarita's addition — when it is useful:* when you need to let off steam, take a weight off your shoulders, or remove unpleasant agitation, "наэлектризованность" (being electrified). [#729]

Olga's relaxation list (11 items): [#729]
1. Soaking in the bath.
2. A walk in nature or in familiar (deserted!) places, or in a bookshop.
3. Lying curled up on something soft, under something soft, next to something (someone?) soft.
4. Drawing doodles and stream-of-consciousness writing.
5. Favourite tea in the right setting.
6. A set of psychotechniques for stopping thoughts and visualizations.
7. Certain music.
8. Favourite cartoons.
9. "Torturing" guitar strings (she says she cannot bring herself to call it playing).
10. Shooting a ball into a hoop / keeping up a ball / kicking at a goal.
11. Tennis.

Margarita's relaxation list (7 items): [#729]
1. Sitting or lying in a closed lotus pose with eyes closed.
2. Placing a palm under the neck between the collarbones and breathing deeply, with the exhale longer than the inhale.
3. Changing into comfortable clothes — often of soft fabric and/or tight-fitting.
4. Being in warm water: slowly washing the hair helps well; with strong fatigue, a full long shower.
5. Slowly massaging the face and body with gel or cream (best combined with the previous item).
6. Making tea or coffee in favourite crockery — so that it is beautiful for the eyes and warm for the hands — and immersing in contact with it: drinking slowly, inhaling and examining it, photographing it from different angles and refractions of light.
7. Swaying on waves of cyclical music — humming, purring and rocking in a light dance ("короче, вайблю" — in short, vibing): (a) to lo-fi such as Lofi Girl (in a Chinese musical scale or the original); (b) to music where the melody runs as a continuous thread or wave through the whole composition — her examples are by Opeth, "лучшей_группы_в мире_Opeth" (the best band in the world, Opeth): "Face of Melinda" and the heavier "Harlequin Forest". [#729]

Part 2 — Recovery. [#732]
- *Olga's definition:* recovery is "возвращение того, что было потрачено: физические силы, способность концентрироваться, эмоциональную устойчивость. Здесь важны сон, питание, паузы, то, что бодрит и дает силы" (the return of what was spent: physical strength, the ability to concentrate, emotional stability. Sleep, nutrition, pauses, what invigorates and gives strength matter here).
- *Key question:* "Что именно я потратил, чего мне не хватает, чтобы это восполнить?" (What exactly did I spend, what am I lacking, in order to replenish it?)
- *Margarita's framing:* notice states when you need not just to relieve stress and tension but to give yourself energy, strength and vigour — and she includes hope during hard periods of life. [#732]

Olga's recovery list (14 items): [#732]
1. At least 8 hours of quality sleep in a sensory-comfortable setting: darkness, fresh air, a comfortable mattress, a heavy blanket, clean white bed linen, favourite soft toys.
2. Swimming pool and swimming in general (ideally naked in a deserted lake).
3. Mountain lake – chalet – fireplace – glass of wine.
4. Favourite food / balanced food that takes into account nutritional needs at this moment or phase of the cycle (rich in proteins/fats/iron).
5. Certain music and sounds of nature.
6. Not having to talk.
7. No strong stimulation through the sensory channels that were overloaded.
8. Touching interesting surfaces.
9. Wearing favourite jewellery that fits today in meaning.
10. Wearing clothes that fit today tactilely (either tight or soft and loose) and feel organic to her.
11. Looking at subjectively beautiful interiors and architecture (in person and room tours).
12. Watching nature and animals.
13. Joint activity with kindred spirits, or different activities but in one space.
14. Hugging close, safe people.

Margarita's recovery list — physical (4 items): [#732]
1. Power nap: 30 minutes of sleep ending with a melodic alarm, to wake up "a new person"; it also works well as a reboot when one has "short-circuited" ("закоротило").
2. Meat with vegetables such as tomatoes and paprika — protein and fibre as "building material for muscles and a healthy treat".
3. Fast walking in comfortable shoes, best of all on a travelator, so it feels like gently soaring — essentially aerobic exercise with a sense of lightness.
4. Strength and endurance training — only if there is a base of energy but you need extra: static-tension exercises (planks, holds) and bodyweight work (push-ups, pull-ups, crunches, squats, calisthenics/gymnastics on parallel bars). The condition matters: this is for topping up, not for an empty tank.

Margarita's recovery list — refilling hope (5 items): for when she needs to fill up with faith in a bright future and that harmony is possible already now, in small steps, without special effort: [#732]
1. Deep conversations with people close in spirit — values and interests.
2. Being in her "places of power": cafés with the smell of coffee and Edison lamps, nooks with live music, streets with evening lanterns, perfume workshops, little markets and shops of beautiful textured things ("*щуп-щуп-аромат*" — touch-touch-aroma); when possible, aquariums, museums, large spaces of books.
3. Watching kind films — about good relationships, adventures and creative solutions.
4. Light creativity — one-line drawing or graphics, designing interiors, or "playing dolls" with herself and friends by choosing style, details, accents, look (she notes she has a certificate for this).
5. Soaking up videos about humanity's possibilities and achievements, progress in scientific research, before/after harmony of space and personality: (a) the Russian show "Квартирный вопрос" (The Housing Question); (b) shows like "Obsessive Compulsive Cleaners" (Russian version "Помешанные на чистоте"); (c) her recent "big love", the project "Never Too Small" about fitting out small homes full of comfort, high technology and unusual use of space with many variants.

### Detailed insights

#### Emotional background of autistic people
According to the lecture as retold by the author, autistic people often experience more negative and fewer positive emotions than neurotypicals. These may be emotions of strong arousal (anxiety, anger, irritation) or of low arousal (shutting down, depression, burnout). Of the survey respondents, 90% report frequent anxiety, 55% experience strong anger, and 74% feel as if negative emotions dominate. [#659]

#### Invisible nervous overexcitation
Increased heart rate and reduced heart-rate variability — i.e. stress indicators — in people with ASD are often invisible to others. 61% of respondents already know their distress is not visible from the outside, and 81% tend to deliberately suppress the outward expression of emotions. [#659]

An MIT study with a physiological-arousal tracker (a wristband device, shown on the slide as a black terry sweatband with two electrodes on the inside) showed that people with ASD have stress spikes simply from someone looking in their direction — before the person themselves becomes aware of it. The slide quotes a participant: in a room with many people her arousal level turned out to be really high; if she sat rocking and not looking at people it slowly decreased, but the moment one of them turned their head to look at her it suddenly jumped again — "even before the point of eye contact" and before she could feel a small difference in her stress. She concludes: "Эти устройства могут быть действительно полезны для изучения того, что меня напрягает, прежде чем это достигнет точки, когда я это замечу" (These devices can be really useful for learning what stresses me before it reaches the point when I notice it). The quote also illustrates rocking (stimming) lowering arousal in real time. [#654, msg #659]

#### Poorer emotion differentiation
A slide cites Erbas (2013), "Emotion differentiation in autism spectrum disorder": autistic people have less differentiated knowledge of their own emotional world and conceptually distinguish separate emotions less than NT people. The slide's joke picture shows a modern man listing "diarrhea, ARVI, fever, depression, allergy" to a man in old Russian dress who has a single word for it all — "Хворь" (ailment). In the sample, to the question how well they manage to understand which emotions/states they are experiencing, 48% answered "rather difficult" and 32% "it varies". The author's text summary: conceptual clarity of emotions is lower than in neurotypicals. [#650, msg #659]

#### Awareness and self-expression
Many find it hard to recognize and express emotions: 71% find it hard to describe their state in words; 74% find writing easier than speaking; 84% find it hard to notice and report their emotional state quickly; 58% find it hard to detect early discomfort; 42% cannot say what caused the stress. Hence printed/written communication often works better than oral. [#659]

#### Obstacles to expressing feelings
A slide asks what difficulties respondents face when trying to tell others about their feelings/states and sorts respondents' answers into two groups. [#653, msg #659]

External obstacles — I am not understood, I am judged, devalued, shamed, humiliated. Respondents' examples: [#653]
- People think there is no reason to feel this way.
- They consider it weakness, whims, being spoiled and infantile.
- Disbelief, "because I look normal".
- I'm often told that I exaggerate and dramatize.
- It is hard to describe overload / executive dysfunction to those who experience nothing of the kind; people do not believe it happens and cannot imagine it.

The author's text sums this up as fear of judgment, devaluation and misunderstanding, including because of common reactions ("ты преувеличиваешь", "драматизируешь", "нет причин так себя чувствовать" — you're exaggerating, dramatizing, there's no reason to feel this way); it is especially hard when the symptoms are not visible. [#659]

Internal obstacles — difficulty understanding one's state, putting it into words, starting to talk; stress from the conversation itself; emotional overload increases stress and worsens communication skills. Respondents' examples: [#653]
- The feeling that language simply lacks words to convey the exact shade of sensations; sometimes not even knowing the approximate name of what one feels.
- Formulating too long, over-explaining with lots of examples so as to be understood, and ending up confusing things even more.
- Discomfort when talking about negative feelings: troubles are easier to live through inside oneself.

In the post: difficulties finding words, starting a conversation or even understanding what you feel; language lacks precision for their inner experience; the conversation itself can worsen stress and overload. [#659]

The exception: "С друзьями аутистами такого нет" (with autistic friends there's none of this) — communication with other autistic people is much easier and there are fewer fears about expression. [#653, msg #659]

#### Meltdowns and shutdowns
As a consequence of all the above, two states occur: [#659]
- Meltdown ("мелтдаун", literally "melting down") — overstrain of the nervous system of a person with ASD, when they cry, shout and so forth — from overload, "а не потому, что хочет чего-то от кого-то" (and not because they want something from someone).
- Shutdown ("шатдаун") — the same overstrain, but closer to "freeze": wild fatigue falls on the person, all emotions wash off the face, and it is physically hard to speak and move.
The author points to two light translations on the subject in the Telegram channel autichnieperevodii ("autistic translations"); their content is not reproduced in this source. [#659]

#### The "nuance" about injustice: autistic people do not have an innate strong sense of justice
The lecture lists "encountering injustice" among situations that trigger dysregulation; the author attaches a "nuance" by linking a Russian translation (the Telegraph page's author field reads lozzagilbert and the name Anna Green Moon heads the text; (!) UNCLEAR which is the original author and which the translator — the text contains "от пер." translator's notes) of an essay titled "Аутизм и справедливость: почему аутичные люди не обладают «естественным сильным чувством справедливости»" (Autism and justice: why autistic people do not possess a "natural strong sense of justice"). The essay is an opinion piece by its author; its argument runs as follows. [#660]

1. The claim being challenged. "Аутичные люди обладают врожденным сильным чувством справедливости" (autistic people possess an innate strong sense of justice) has for a long time been spreading as a fact and as a positive aspect of autism. It is often generalized as something all autistic people have, and, according to the essay's author, it is promoted mainly by white autistic people. She disagrees that all autistic people have a deep sense of justice and cannot bear any injustice. [#660]
2. Definition used. Justice can be described as behaviour or an attitude that is honest and morally right. [#660]
3. The argument against ("Почему нет" — why not). If autistic people had a strong sense of justice by default and could not bear any injustice, there should be no autistic racists, ableists, or people supporting various kinds of discrimination and phobias — because everyone would share that "natural sense of justice" and dislike of lawlessness, which would motivate them to fight it, and there would be no hatred of marginalized groups. Since the community does contain members who are racists and who often promote the idea of "аспи превосходстве" (Aspie supremacy), the innate-justice claim fails. [#660]
4. The author's alternative account ("Мое мнение" — my opinion). Instead of a natural strong sense of justice, autistic people highly value what seems "right" or "fair" to them on the basis of their own beliefs and life experience, possibly paired with black-and-white thinking. This can make them passionate about it ("Несомненно" — undoubtedly) and they can fight for what they believe in ("Конечно же" — of course). But it does not mean that all their causes and beliefs are good or worth fighting for, nor that they by default have the high moral values people like to talk about. [#660]
5. Who spreads the claim. The essay finds it especially doubtful that the idea is spread mainly by white autistic people: "Вы не можете называть себя «активистами с сильным чувством справедливости», если вы активно не учитесь избавляться от расизма и эйблизма" (You cannot call yourselves "activists with a strong sense of justice" if you are not actively learning to rid yourselves of racism and ableism), fighting these things and centring those who belong to marginalized groups. The translator's examples of such groups: non-speaking autistic people, autistic people with support levels 2–3, autistic people with intellectual disabilities, autistic people from Indigenous peoples. [#660]
6. Activism is not universal. Some autistic people enjoy activism and others do not; one cannot generalize that all autistic people want to fight to make things better for everyone, because "некоторые из нас, по-настоящему, ужасные люди и активно способствуют дискриминации и угнетению" (some of us are truly terrible people and actively contribute to discrimination and oppression). [#660]

Conclusions of the essay ("Несколько выводов"): [#660]
- The online autistic community and the wider neurodivergent communities consist predominantly of white people. The translator adds that some members ignore or push aside people of other peoples and cultures and their experience — the example given: an African-American autistic woman shared that ABA therapy sometimes literally saves the lives of other African-American autistic children, because in some parts of the world they were in danger due to visible manifestations of autism (stimming, gait, communication differences), and she was hit by a flood of hatred.
- Autism does not make anyone less prone to racism, ableism or other kinds of discrimination and phobias.
- No one automatically becomes good, innocent and incapable of being bad just because they are autistic.
- "Аутизм – это не оправдание для нетерпимости" (autism is not an excuse for intolerance) and not the reason someone behaves that way; such people are simply bigots ("ханжи").
- The translator notes that some parts concern problems the Russian-speaking community faces less, but they remain a problem for the rest of the community; the translation was made for informational purposes and is not meant to offend anyone.

The lesson for this topic: a strong reaction to perceived injustice is a common trigger, but it reflects what the person personally considers "right" or "fair" (possibly with black-and-white thinking), not a built-in moral virtue shared by all autistic people. [#660, article #660]
