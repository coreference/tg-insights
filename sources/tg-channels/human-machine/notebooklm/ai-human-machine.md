## Large language models: capabilities, limits and risks

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's posts on large language models (LLMs) and adjacent generative AI between December 2022 and September 2025: the author's first, enthusiastic impressions of ChatGPT; its inability to add large numbers; the author's own painful experience of ChatGPT, Phind and Perplexity hallucinating content of a neuroscience paper; a study showing LLMs carry political biases; the APA's guide on citing ChatGPT; examples of an AI summarizer and of Midjourney's first video model; and a psychologist's explanation of why US states ban AI as a therapist. It matters, in the source's framing, because the same systems that write convincingly enough to pass a job interview also invent facts, believe misinformation from their own political side, and can amplify delusions — so the reader has to know which scenarios they are good for and which they are not [#136, #523, #204, #672].

### Core ideas

- Fluency is not reliability. ChatGPT produces strikingly realistic, dialogue-based output (code, poems, essays), yet fails at school arithmetic and invents content when summarizing — impressive writing does not imply correct reasoning or faithful reading [#136, #184, #523].
- Do not delegate serious source work to neural networks. The author's conclusion after catching three different AI tools hallucinating: read the sources and write yourself; neural networks "have not grown up" to serious articles and conclusions yet [#523].
- LLMs have political leanings that cannot currently be trained out. A study reported by MIT Technology Review placed 14 models on a political map; models believe misinformation from their own political spectrum and nobody knows how to fix this [#204].
- AI as a therapist is risky in specific, known ways, which is why several US states restrict it; but bans will not stop non-professional use, so the real task is AI literacy and carefully designed, research-backed professional tools [#672].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Risks of LLMs in psychotherapy (Maria Danina's list)
Context: in August (2025) a law came into force in Illinois restricting the use of artificial intelligence in mental health. It prohibits using AI as a therapist or counsellor and sets strict rules for companies offering digital services in this field. Illinois became the third US state with such restrictions, after Nevada and Utah; similar initiatives are being discussed in California, Oregon and Texas. The forwarded post (from the channel "Чистые Когниции") asks psychologist Maria Danina what risks arise from AI in psychotherapy and whether legislative bans can reduce them [#672].

Danina says it is "reliably known" (Достоверно известно) that LLMs have the following problems [#672]:
1. They handle suicide risk incorrectly — «некорректно работают с суицидальным риском» (work incorrectly with suicidal risk) [#672].
2. They support illusions and distortions of the client rather than challenging them — «поддерживают иллюзии и искажения» (support illusions and distortions) [#672].
3. They can give harmful advice when asked directly — «могут дать вредный совет в случае прямого запроса» (may give harmful advice in the case of a direct request) [#672].
4. They do not build a foundation for a long-term relationship, which is important for work with severe cases [#672].
5. "AI psychosis." Cases are known where people developed delusions together with LLM-based chats: the AI supported and amplified their ideas, helped them look for "evidence", and took the person's side. Danina hedges: "we cannot claim that the models generated the delusions on their own", but high trust in generated text, the models' persuasiveness and their unrestricted availability may contribute in some borderline cases [#672].
6. Confidentiality. Private information is at risk if the conversation with the AI takes place on the servers of the system's owners [#672].
7. Over-trust by inexperienced practitioners in supervision use (see *Two acceptable modes of AI-assisted clinical supervision*): a novice may believe what the AI suggests too strongly and implement it in practice without checking [#672].

Where the line runs: because of risks 1–4, if a client's request is even slightly broader than psychoeducation and self-knowledge, or more complex than moderate anxiety or depressive disorders, "unexpected problems" may arise "with which it is unclear what to do". Danina adds that with proper development of an AI-based tool some of these risks can be minimized, but this takes time and deeper understanding [#672].

#### Two acceptable modes of AI-assisted clinical supervision
Purpose: what a psychotherapist can realistically get from an AI when using it for supervision (reviewing one's own sessions). According to Danina, a model can do one of two things [#672]:
1. Feedback on formalized, clear-cut manifestations in a session — i.e. on observable, well-defined features of what happened, not on the whole case [#672].
2. Joint reflection and hypothesis generation — the AI as a thinking partner that proposes hypotheses for the therapist to consider [#672].

What it cannot do yet: a full-fledged analysis is currently impossible to obtain, especially in non-specialised applications. The pitfall is risk 7 above — an inexperienced practitioner adopting AI suggestions unchecked. Her recommendation for greater reliability: use specialised RAG models (retrieval-augmented generation), which generate an answer only on the basis of relevant information — for example, professional literature on supervision [#672].

#### Safe-use rule for AI summaries of sources
The author's rule after her hallucination experience (detailed under *Hallucinations when summarizing a scientific paper*) [#523]:
1. Beware of fakes — «Остерегайтесь подделок» (beware of counterfeits): an AI summary may look like the paper's content while containing invented claims [#523].
2. Read the sources — «Почитайте источники» (read the sources): go to the original text, as she ended up doing by re-reading 60 pages of the original [#523].
3. Write with your own hands — «Пишите своими руками» (write with your own hands) [#523].
Why: «До серьёзных статей и выводов нейросетки ещё не доросли» (neural networks have not yet grown up to serious articles and conclusions) [#523].

#### Hallucinations when summarizing a scientific paper
The author was writing a long-read on subtypes of autism spectrum disorder (РАС) and had to rewrite the remaining parts for the fifth day in a row by re-reading 60 pages of the original paper. The cause [#523]:
- She asked a neural network to "please highlight the key ideas in these paragraphs" — a simple request she had made a thousand times [#523].
- It "lied a whole heap" (наврала мне с три короба) — about brain regions that do not exist in the paper, connections that do not exist, and the authors' supposed opinion about them [#523].
- She caught it only when it got tangled in its own hallucinations and she asked what EXACTLY the original said, without additions, interpretations or speculation. The model answered something like "Oops" and shortened the text tenfold, leaving almost only the abstract [#523].
- By then she had already written five parts about the functions of those regions and speculated about possible consequences of such connectivity between the named brain areas — i.e. the hallucination had propagated into her own writing [#523].
- Even after she gave special instructions, ChatGPT and two other tools, Phind and Perplexity, mixed up the ASD subtypes and the parameters by which they are distinguished [#523].
Her emotional summary: «Боль. Агония. Фрустрация. Преодоление.» (Pain. Agony. Frustration. Overcoming.) [#523].

What the original actually says (the follow-up post shows an excerpt, headed by the author «Конкретное и чёткое описание регионов мозга, их функций и значений изменений в них» — *a concrete and clear description of brain regions, their functions and the meaning of changes in them*) [#524]: to understand how atypical functional connectivity in specific regions and networks underlies individual differences in ASD symptoms, the researchers first examined the resting-state functional connectivity (RSFC) correlates — connectivity score loadings — of each latent brain–behavior dimension, and found that each dimension described a distinct pattern of functional connectivity. Specifically, the verbal-IQ-related dimension was associated with connectivity between brain areas known to be important for language processing and reading ability, including corticothalamic, visual network and striatal connectivity — consistent with previous work indicating that reading ability is negatively associated with thalamic synchronization and functional connectivity involving these areas. The lesson implied by pairing the two posts: the original text is specific and clear, so the AI's invented regions and links were not a matter of an ambiguous source [#524].

#### Will bans on AI therapy work?
Danina's view: legislative measures have limited power — non-professional services will be used anyway despite restrictions, because some groups of people simply have no alternative. Many tools are available: if OpenAI starts blocking such content, one can still turn to Chinese DeepSeek or Qwen for "psychotherapy". What matters is to regulate the use of AI tools specifically in professional help, so that delegating tasks to AI does not lower the quality of psychotherapeutic services or put clients and therapists at risk. Her overall position: AI is a useful tool, but one must understand which scenarios it is effective in and which not; this requires AI literacy and thoughtful design of AI tools for professional help — with research and a worked-out methodology [#672].

## Human-machine interfaces and AI in science

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's posts on technologies that connect human bodies and minds to machines, and on AI used as an instrument of science. It covers wearables that read the face (Dolami VR mask) and silent inner speech (MIT AlterEgo), an AI that temporarily moves part of the user's body (Human Operator), round-the-clock driverless robotaxis, machine learning that designs new proteins and discovers antibiotics, a University of Tokyo robotics approach built on Jean Piaget's developmental theory, and William Gibson's novel/series The Peripheral as fiction about neural interfaces. The source frames these as signs of a human-machine future: devices that become part of everyday life, help patients without voice or movement, speed up drug discovery, and could be used in rehabilitation [#118, #156, #172, #182, #192, #206, #566, #734].

### Core ideas

- Generative AI can design matter, not only pictures. An architecture similar to DALL-E 2 was adapted to predict protein amino-acid sequences that reach a given structure, opening the way to proteins that do not exist in nature and to low-carbon materials; see *Generative protein design* [#172].
- AI compresses drug discovery. A neural network screened 7000 compounds, flagged 240, and lab testing yielded the new selective antibiotic abaucin; see *AI-guided antibiotic discovery* [#182].
- Interfaces are moving to the face, inner speech and the body itself. A mask carries facial expressions into VR, AlterEgo reads the neural signals of internally articulated words, and Human Operator lets AI move part of a person's body to practise a skill [#156, #192, #734].
- Autonomous vehicles crossed a regulatory threshold. San Francisco allowed Waymo and Cruise robotaxis to run 24/7 with no driver inside, conditional on preventing incidents [#206].
- Developmental psychology can inform robot design. A Tokyo lab uses Piaget's assimilation, accommodation, genetic epistemology and structuralism to model how a robot's internal functions should cohere and adapt; see *Piaget-inspired robotics* [#566, #567, #568, #569].
- Fiction anticipates the neural interface. The Peripheral imagines a screenless, controller-less VR headset with a neural interface that connects two real worlds [#118].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Generative vs discriminative models (as applied to protein design)
Purpose: the channel explains why a DALL-E-type model could be used to create proteins. Two kinds of models are distinguished [#172]:
1. Generative model (генеративная/порождающая модель) — a model that can generate new objects from the data it has received. DALL-E 2 is the example: it creates realistic images from user prompts written in natural language [#172].
2. Discriminative model (дискриминативная) — a model that only tells different objects apart; the channel's example is distinguishing cats from dogs (вроде кошечек от собачек, *like kitties from doggies*) [#172].
The researchers kept the generative architecture but swapped the task: instead of producing images from text prompts, the adapted model predicts amino-acid sequences of proteins that would achieve specified structural targets [#172].

#### Generative protein design (MIT, MIT-IBM Watson AI Lab, Tufts)
What it is: machine-learning algorithms, developed by researchers at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, the MIT-IBM Watson AI Lab and Tufts University, that generate proteins with a predefined structure [#172].
How it works, in the order the source describes:
1. Start from a model architecturally similar to the one used in systems like DALL-E 2 (a generative model) [#172].
2. Replace the input/output: the goal is no longer an image from a natural-language prompt but a structural goal for a protein [#172].
3. The model predicts amino-acid sequences that would reach those structural goals — including proteins that do not exist in nature [#172].
Why it matters (the channel's heading «Чем это классно?», *Why is this cool?*): it would allow materials with desired mechanical properties such as stiffness or elasticity to be created; such protein materials could potentially replace materials made from oil or ceramics while leaving a much smaller carbon footprint [#172].
Outside view: Philip LeDuc, professor of mechanical engineering at Carnegie Mellon University, who watched the work from outside, said: «Эта конкретная работа увлекательна, потому что она в основном исследует создание белков, которые не существуют, и то, какими будут их характеристики с точки зрения механики» (This particular work is fascinating because it mostly explores the creation of proteins that do not exist and what their characteristics will be from the point of view of mechanics) [#172].
Author's extension: HGUM writes that she is thinking about how these technologies could be applied to create genome-correction proteins in the area of transgenerational epigenetic inheritance (TGI), and ends with «Exciting!». This is the author's speculation, not a result of the study [#172].

#### AI-guided antibiotic discovery (abaucin)
Problem: Acinetobacter baumannii is a pathogen described as untreatable, causing pneumonia, meningitis and other serious infections; the MIT News headline adds that it lurks in many hospital settings and is drug-resistant [#182, img #182].
Pipeline as reported:
1. Screen — a neural network analysed 7000 drug compounds for their potential to suppress the growth of A. baumannii [#182].
2. Shortlist — the algorithm identified 240 antibacterial molecules [#182].
3. Test in the lab — those 240 molecules were tested experimentally [#182].
4. Result — scientists obtained a new compound, abaucin (абауцин), which kills the target bacterium selectively (точечно, *pinpoint*) and does not affect other bacteria [#182].
5. Outcome claimed — the forwarded post says abaucin treats drug-resistant infections «у мышей и людей» (in mice and humans); the attached MIT headline only says the compound could combat drug-resistant infections. See Caveats [#182, img #182].
The forwarding channel's verdict: «Поиск антибиотиков ещё никогда не был таким быстрым» (The search for antibiotics has never been this fast), followed by «наблюдаем дальше» (we keep watching) [#182].

#### Piaget-inspired robotics (Intelligent Systems and Informatics Lab, University of Tokyo)
Context: at her first scientific conference, organised in Serbia by the Jean Piaget Society for the Study of Knowledge and Development, the author's favourite talk came from the Intelligent Systems and Informatics Lab of the University of Tokyo. The speaker explained how, in robotics, they apply Piaget's ideas of assimilation and accommodation, genetic epistemology, and structuralism (the author notes in passing that Piaget later turned from structuralism toward functionalism). The author promised to explain all of this later in simple and detailed form; the post itself gives the slides as the overview [#566]. The four slides form a progression:

1. Piaget as a dynamic, biological, mathematical Kant (slide 3). The speaker's framing: «Piaget is a Dinamic, Biological, Mathematical Kant» [sic], rendered by the author in Russian as «Пиаже — это Кант от биологии, математики и динамических систем» (Piaget is the Kant of biology, mathematics and dynamical systems). The slide quotes two Piaget texts. From *Genetic Epistemology* (1971): in sensory-motor intelligence there is already a certain logic of inclusion, a logic of orderings and a logic of correspondence, which Piaget maintains are the foundations of logical-mathematical structures; they are not yet operations but are the beginnings of what will become operations, and they already contain the beginnings of two essential characteristics of operations — a form of conservation and a form of reversibility (highlighted on the slide). From *Structuralism* (1968): even at this stage the child's behaviour may be called intelligent, but this intelligence is entirely sensori-motor, does not involve representation, and is essentially tied to action and coordinations of action (highlighted); here one meets the advent of logic, though limited in two essential respects. The highlighted passages put the emphasis on logic and intelligence beginning in action and its coordination, before representation; (!) 2. Assimilation: schema as mathematical (slide 12). The slide shows Piaget's book *Adaptation and Intelligence: Organic Selection and Phenocopy* and a passage stating that empirical abstraction never operates by itself: to derive information from an object, even information that can only come from that object, an assimilatory apparatus is indispensable. «This assimilatory apparatus is of a mathematical» nature: it involves relating one or more classes (or schemes of action at the sensory-motor level, which are already a kind of practical concept), correspondences, functions, identities, differences or equivalences. 3. Structuralism: irreducible wholeness (slide 15). The slide «Structuralism: Irreducible Wholeness» shows a two-level model from the paper *Mental causation in a physical world: A self-causation model of downward causation* by Yoshiyuki Ohmura and Yasuo Kuniyoshi. Components:
   - *External causes x* enter the system [#568].
   - *Macro level: neural network modules* — two supervenient functions f1 and f2 [#568].
   - *Axiom, macro-level equation* — f1·f2 = f2·f1 (applying the two functions in either order should give the same result) [#568].
   - *Supervenient macro-level feedback error* — for all x, the squared difference |f1·f2(x) − f2·f1(x)|² [#568].
   - *Micro level: neurons, synapses* — the macro modules supervene on physical neurons and synapses [#568].
   - *Control* — the macro-level feedback error is sent down to the micro level, which adjusts synaptic weights to reduce the feedback error [#568].
   As drawn, the error defined at the whole-system (macro) level is fed back to change the parts (synaptic weights) — the downward causation of the paper's title — and the whole box is labelled Observation at macro level; the slide title presents this as structuralism's irreducible wholeness [#568, img #568].

### Detailed insights

#### Wearables that read the face and silent speech
- Dolami smart VR mask. Framed as yet another replacement for Zuckerberg's legless and faceless digital avatars. The Japanese startup Dolami developed a smart mask that reads the user's facial expressions and transmits them to a VR avatar, using sensors built into the inside of the mask. Reported specs: expression transfer accuracy above 95% and latency of only 200 milliseconds; planned to go on sale at the end of 2023 for 90 dollars. The forwarding channel's claim: in VR your facial expressions will now appear exactly on your digital avatar [#156].
- MIT AlterEgo. MIT researchers presented AlterEgo, a device for communicating by thought: it reads neural signals associated with the internal articulation of words and accurately recognises what the person wants to say. Patients with multiple sclerosis and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis can use it without voice or movement anywhere — from counting purchases in a shop to choosing TV channels. Verdict of the forwarding channel: «Совершенно футуристическая штука!» (A completely futuristic thing!) [#192].

#### AI that moves the body: Human Operator and the HARD MODE hackathon
- The hackathon. MIT launched the HARD MODE hackathon, a marathon for creating smart objects — devices able to perceive the environment around them, learn, adapt and interact with people. Organisers were the Design Intelligence Lab of the architecture faculty and the AHA group of the MIT Media Lab (the author jokes about whether Morten Harket sits on the board). The task was to go beyond the usual understanding of AI: instead of yet another chatbot or app, build real devices that can be held, worn, installed in a space and made part of everyday life. 200 specialists — engineers, designers and researchers — had 48 hours to create tangible, functional prototypes offering a new view of how people communicate and think, learn and work, develop and play [#734].
- The winner, Human Operator. It lets a person hand control of part of their body to AI for a short time, and during that time mechanically practise a skill the person cannot yet perform for one reason or another. The author's reaction: «Жутко (и) интересно!» (*Creepy (and) interesting!*) [#734].
- Caveat on the demo. In the project video, everything after 0:28 shows only hypothetical goals the engineers would like to reach, not achieved results [#734].
- Author's opinion on use. «На мой взгляд, эта технология, безусловно, была бы полезна в реабилитации.» (In my view, this technology would certainly be useful in rehabilitation.) She closes by asking readers what they would do with such capabilities [#734].

#### Robotaxis
Waymo and Cruise received permission to operate driverless robotaxis (no driver inside) around the clock, which the forwarding channel calls a turning point for the self-driving industry. The decision came after a six-hour hearing and applies so far only to San Francisco. Before, autonomous taxis ran only at limited times and in specific locations; now they may drive across the whole city 24/7. Condition: the companies must ensure incidents are not repeated, otherwise the authorities will cap the number of driverless cars in the city. The attached Washington Post headline (Trisha Thadani and Jeremy B. Merrill, 10 August 2023) states that California opened the floodgates for self-driving cars after a state regulatory board ruling and calls it a pivotal moment for the industry. The forwarding channel jokingly predicts more protests in which people put traffic cones on robotaxi hoods [#206, img #206].

#### Observations from the Piaget conference
The author reports that the conference had many scientists from Italy, France and Japan and, unexpectedly for her, Turkey (almost a third of the works); the programme consisted of talks, posters and discussions, with each theme made up of many 15-minute talks. She found the charm of the Tokyo talk not only in the depth of the approach but in how sweetly and naively the speaker put the Kant comparison [#566].

#### Fiction: The Peripheral
The channel recommends the series The Peripheral, based on William Gibson's novel. The quoted Wiki synopsis: Flynne Fisher lives in provincial America, works in a 3D-print shop and plays VR games. At her brother Burton's request she agrees to test a new virtual-reality headset with a neural interface, with no screen and no controllers. The device transports her to a futuristic London at the end of the 21st century. Gradually Flynne realises the events are happening not in a game or simulation but in reality, and she must find out who connected the two worlds and why. The post is tagged as transhumanism and something to watch [#118].
