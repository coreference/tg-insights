## Emotion circuits: fear extinction, anger and aggression

### What this covers

Two strands of brain research as the channel presents them. The first and larger strand is the amygdala: not only as the brain's fear centre but as the place where fear is *unlearned*, through a second, competing neuron population whose extinction memory overrides the fear memory — a process driven by dopamine arriving from the ventral tegmental area, which makes extinction a form of reward learning [#599, #600, #602, #603]. The second strand is anger and aggression: a systematic review and meta-analysis supporting a limbic-hyperactivity model in aggression-prone individuals [#150], and a quoted evolutionary argument about why slow and hot tempers each pay off in different conditions [#264].

The author states why the amygdala findings matter: the MIT work "расширило это представление открытием фундаментальной механики, как именно мы перестаём бояться вещей" (expanded this picture with the discovery of the fundamental mechanics of how exactly we *stop* being afraid of things), and she flags this as what she sees as important to note for psychotherapy and for pharmacology [#599].

### Core ideas

- The amygdala is the brain's main fear centre — in humans, mice and other vertebrates — and the relationship between fear and amygdala size/activity runs both ways. The more a person is exposed to fear, the larger the amygdala becomes and the more active it is; conversely, the more active the amygdala is for any reason at all, the more actively the person will fear a wide variety of things [#599].
- Being afraid and ceasing to be afraid are two separate programmes, not one dial. The 2020 Tonegawa-lab work showed they are two distinct programmes in the brain that compete with each other within the amygdala, and strengthening one weakens the other [#600].
- Both of them are learned. Fear is learned from experience of danger, and the end of fear is learned from experience of safety — each written into memory by its own genetically distinct neuron population [#600].
- Fear extinction is reward learning, not suppression. Dopamine from the VTA activates reward-linked amygdala neurons, and the disappearance of fear follows; as Zhang puts it, unlearning fear "похоже, заключается не в простом его подавлении" (does not appear to consist in simply suppressing it) but is a learning process that works through reward mechanisms [#602].
- The same neurons encode reward feelings, which may explain the bodily relief after danger passes. Because the Ppp1r1b population doubles as a reward-coding population, it may be why we feel literally physically good — relieved, relaxed or joyful — when we realise an expected danger turned out smaller than feared or did not happen at all [#602].
- This circuit is a key node, not the whole story. The researchers note that extinction of fear as a complex phenomenon happens across the whole brain and not in this single circuit alone, because our life impressions involve a whole series of associations; but this circuit appears to be the key node for the signal that lets fear fade [#603].
- Aggression goes with limbic hyperactivity and altered temporal/occipital activity. A systematic review and meta-analysis confirms the limbic-hyperactivity model in individuals with a history of aggression, and shows that at the moment anger or aggression is expressed, activity levels in the temporal and occipital regions are altered [#150].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Two competing amygdala programmes: fear memory (aBLA / Rspo2) vs extinction memory (pBLA / Ppp1r1b)

Purpose: to explain how the same structure can both install a fear and dismantle it. The model comes from the Tonegawa lab's 2020 result — "«бояться» и «перестать бояться» — это две отдельные программы в мозге" ("to fear" and "to stop fearing" are two separate programmes in the brain) — which compete inside the amygdala, so that strengthening one weakens the other [#600].

1. Learning that a place is dangerous → fear memory. When a mouse learns that a place is "dangerous" — because it receives a small electric shock to the paw there — neurons in the *anterior* part of the basolateral amygdala (aBLA) express the gene Rspo2 and write (encode) the fear memory [#600].
2. Learning that the place is no longer dangerous → extinction memory. When the mouse subsequently learns that the place is no longer dangerous — because it expects the shock and the shock does not recur — neurons in the *posterior* part of the basolateral amygdala (pBLA) express the gene Ppp1r1b and encode a new memory of fear extinction, which overcomes the original fear [#600].
3. The competition. Because the two programmes compete within the amygdala, extinction is not an erasure of the fear memory but a second, stronger memory laid over it; the balance between the two populations determines whether the animal freezes or relaxes [#600].

The new study, led by Xiangyu Zhang and Caitlin Flick, goes beyond this division of labour to reveal the precise mechanism of how we unlearn fear [#600].

#### The VTA dopamine routing map: which part of the VTA talks to which amygdala population

Purpose: to connect the brain's "surprise detector" to the two fear programmes. The amygdala receives signals from various brain regions; one of them is the ventral tegmental area (VTA), the region involved in reactions to surprises and unexpected events, which instructs the brain to remember those moments — and does so in a special way, with dopamine [#602].

The scientists asked whether this "surprise detector" is connected to the amygdala, and the pattern turned out to be quite clear-cut — two separate addressed channels [#602]:

1. Rspo2 genes take part in the processes of, loosely speaking, "пора бояться" ("time to be afraid"): the Rspo2 cells are the amygdala target for dopaminergic neurons of the anterior, left and right parts of the VTA [#602].
2. Ppp1r1b genes carry out the programmes "перестаём бояться, обошлось" (we stop being afraid, it turned out fine): these cells are the target for "shots" from the central and posterior parts of the VTA [#602].

Dopamine's wider role is part of the model's reasoning: we most often know dopamine as the "цацу удовольствия" (the trinket/darling of pleasure) — capricious, indiscriminately drawn to anything, such as three hours of TikTok, and sometimes showing up only when it suits it. But in fact dopamine is critically important and works reliably in very many bodily processes — from learning and memorising to the control of attention and motivation (hence ADHD), and even so that we can simply move of our own will (hence Parkinson's disease) [#602].

#### Proving dopamine is causal, not a bystander: the two experimental blocks

Purpose: to rule out the possibility that dopamine merely "happened to be passing by" or was activated in parallel with extinction rather than driving it; the final parts of the experiment settled this [#602, #603]. The tool is optogenetics — a technology that lets researchers precisely activate or quiet neurons using light [#603].

Block 1 — controlling the signals coming out of the VTA [#603]:
1. Suppressing dopaminergic signals (D-signals) from the VTA to the pBLA prevented the disappearance of fear.
2. Activating those same VTA→pBLA signals accelerated fear extinction.
3. Activating D-signals from the VTA to the aBLA reinstated fear — even without any new electric shocks.

Block 2 — controlling the dopamine receptors inside the amygdala, specifically the D1 dopamine receptors on amygdala neurons [#603]:
4. In Ppp1r1b cells, suppressing the receptors impeded extinction; conversely, increased expression of these receptors disrupted the *recall* of fear and promoted extinction.
5. In Rspo2 cells, suppressing the receptors reduced freezing behaviour.

Together the manipulations show the circuit is bidirectional and sufficient as well as necessary: the extinction branch can be blocked or sped up at will, and the fear branch can be switched back on by dopamine alone [#603].

### Detailed insights

#### Why relief feels physically good

The author highlights the point she finds most interesting: because the Ppp1r1b neurons that encode the extinction memory are the same neurons that encode feelings of reward, this may explain why it feels literally physically good — relieved, relaxed or joyful — when we realise that an expected danger turned out to be smaller than expected or did not happen at all [#602]. She offers four everyday illustrations of that sensation [#602]:
- the feeling you have after the peak scary moments, if you like horror films or rollercoasters;
- when an unexpected meeting with your manager turns out to be a promotion;
- when you partied before an exam, your head is splitting, and the professor has just announced a postponement — with the aside "(потому что тусил вместе с вами)" (because he was partying with you);
- all of these are presented as the same mechanism: "Это оно!" (That's it!) [#602].

#### Anger and aggression in the brain

The channel forwards a write-up (from Психиатрия & Нейронауки, translated by E. A. Sizikova, under the title "НЕЙРОБИОЛОГИЯ ГНЕВА И АГРЕССИИ: РОЛЬ ЛИМБИЧЕСКОЙ ГИПЕРАКТИВНОСТИ" — "The neurobiology of anger and aggression: the role of limbic hyperactivity") of a systematic review and meta-analysis by Maja Nikolic et al. in *Progress in Neuro-Psychopharmacology & Biological Psychiatry*. Its results confirm the model of limbic hyperactivity in individuals with a history of aggression, and additionally show that at the moment anger or aggression is expressed, the level of activity in the temporal and occipital regions is altered [#150, img #150].

#### The evolutionary trade-off of temper

The author quotes Patricia Churchland on why neither temperament is simply better: a slow-to-anger disposition may serve animals better in times of peace and plenty, whereas a hot-tempered disposition may be more beneficial in times of scarcity or war [#264]. She adds a personal, self-deprecating application of the criterion to herself — "That's how I know I'm a creature of peace 🕊️" — i.e. her own slowness to anger as evidence of living in peace and plenty [#264].

## Brain maintenance: waste clearance, mental fatigue and Alzheimer's links

### What this covers

Three strands of neuroscience news the channel author translated and commented on: (1) a 2022 study linking the tiredness after a long day of demanding thinking to glutamate build-up in the lateral prefrontal cortex and to a shift toward short-term choices [#169, #170]; (2) two February 2024 Nature studies showing that synchronized neuronal activity — delta waves in deep sleep, and 40 Hz gamma rhythms driven by light and sound — pushes cerebrospinal fluid through brain tissue and clears waste, including amyloid [#314, #315]; (3) a mouse study showing that the common fungus Candida albicans can enter the brain and itself generate amyloid-beta-like peptides [#246, #247]. According to the source, waste accumulation in the working brain is a main cause of many neurological disorders, and mental fatigue is a physiological, measurable state rather than something that is "only in your head" [#314, #169].

### Core ideas

- Mental fatigue has a metabolic signature. More than six hours of hard mental work raised glutamate in the lateral prefrontal cortex, and the same people became more likely to choose small immediate rewards; the authors suggest rest may let the brain restore proper regulation of glutamate. This is so far a correlation, not a proven cause [#169].
- Brain cleaning is driven by neurons firing together. During deep sleep, synchronized neuronal activity creates ionic waves that move fluid and solutes through the parenchyma; silencing neurons slows clearance and activating them speeds it up [#314].
- 40 Hz stimulation pumps fluid via arteries. Light-and-sound stimulation at 40 Hz made arteries constrict and dilate more strongly, pushing solutes through the brain and out to the cervical lymph nodes, through VIP-releasing interneurons [#315].
- A fungus can make Alzheimer's-type peptides. Candida albicans breaks the blood-brain barrier with its Sap proteases and uses the same enzymes to cut amyloid precursor protein into Aβ-like peptides — a source of these peptides outside the brain's own proteases [#246, #247].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Wiehler et al. cognitive-fatigue experiment
Purpose: test whether the consequences of cognitive fatigue are tied to metabolic changes in the brain; the team of cognitive neuroscientist Antonius Wiehler (Paris Brain Institute) suspected they were [#169].
1. Participants and groups. 40 participants were recruited; 24 got a hard task and the remaining 16 a similar but easier task [#169].
2. The hard task. An example given: watch letters appear on a computer screen every 1.6 seconds and record when a letter matches the one shown three letters earlier (a 3-back task). The easy group did an analogous, lighter version [#169].
3. Duration. Both groups worked for just over six hours, with two ten-minute breaks [#169].
4. Measurement. While participants worked, the researchers used magnetic resonance spectroscopy to measure glutamate in the lateral prefrontal cortex — the seat of cognitive control, the system people rely on to suppress impulses and to pick long-term gains over tempting short-term rewards such as an unhealthy snack. Wiehler's illustration: «Если вас ужалило насекомое, вам хочется почесаться» (If an insect stings you, you want to scratch); «Если вы остановите этот рефлекс, это будет когнитивный контроль» (If you stop that reflex, that is cognitive control) [#169].
5. Choice test. Participants chose between an immediate cash reward and a larger reward received several months later, at the start and at the end of the day [#169].
6. Result. The hard-task group accumulated more glutamate in this region by the end of the day than the easy-task group, and they were more likely to choose the smaller short-term reward than at the start of the day; at the end of the workday they were also more likely than the easy-task group to pick quick, easily obtained, lower-value rewards over larger rewards that require a longer wait or more effort [#169].
7. Interpretation by the authors. Too much glutamate can disrupt brain function, and a period of rest may let the brain restore proper regulation of the molecule [#169].
8. Next step. Wiehler hopes to use the system to learn how people recover from mental exhaustion: «Было бы здорово узнать больше о том, как восстанавливается уровень глутамата» (It would be great to learn more about how glutamate levels recover) [#169]. The continuation frames the open questions as «Полезен ли сон? Какой продолжительности должны быть перерывы, чтобы иметь положительный эффект?» (Does sleep help? How long should breaks be to have a positive effect?) [#170].

#### Kipnis study design: does neuronal synchrony drive brain clearance? (Study 1)
Purpose: find evidence for the hinted correlation between neuronal excitation and glymphatic function [#314].
1. Premise. Neural networks synchronize their signalling (action potentials). This creates rhythmic, self-propagating, high-amplitude ionic waves in the interstitial fluid between brain tissues — the same waves that can be measured from the skull surface with EEG [#314].
2. Tools. Jonathan Kipnis's team (Washington University in St. Louis) used chemogenetic and optogenetic tools that control a subset of neurons in mice with millisecond precision, so they could activate or silence neurons in the hippocampus at will [#314].
3. Observation in sleep. In sleeping mice, ionic waves moved through the parenchyma in sync with delta-wave electrical rhythms (1–4 Hz) seen in deep sleep (stage N3) [#314].
4. Silencing. When neuronal activity was dampened, the waves predictably faded and dissolved substances moved through the brain very slowly [#314].
5. Activating. Raising neuronal activity had the opposite effect: stronger ionic waves and faster passage of solutes [#314].
6. Conclusion. According to the researchers, this specific synchronous neuronal activity and the ionic waves it generates during sleep are the main driving force of the cleaning process. They joked that neurons that fire together "shower" together, and suggest the results open ways to improve brain clearance by using neuronal dynamics [#314].

#### Tsai study design: 40 Hz stimulation and the VIP link (Study 2)
Purpose: extend the evidence that neuronal activity can move cerebrospinal fluid; led by Li-Huei Tsai at MIT, published in Nature [#315].
1. Model. Six-month-old mice of the 5XFAD genetic line, bred to model Alzheimer's disease [#315].
2. Tracer. Fluorescently labelled ovalbumin was injected into their cerebrospinal fluid [#315].
3. Stimulation. One hour of sound and light at 40 Hz, compared with 8 Hz, 80 Hz and no stimulation [#315].
4. Inflow result. After 40 Hz stimulation, four times as much ovalbumin reached the cortex as in the 8 Hz, 80 Hz or unstimulated mice [#315].
5. Outflow result. Cerebrospinal fluid outflow also responded to 40 Hz, clearing brain waste 50% faster than in control mice [#315].
6. Proposed mechanism. Stronger dilation and constriction of brain blood vessels: the fast gamma rhythm (40 Hz) was linked to stronger arterial pulsation, which pushes solutes through the brain and out to the cervical lymph nodes. The effect was explained by interneurons releasing vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP), which regulates blood flow [#315].
7. Causal test. When the VIP interneurons were silenced, 40 Hz stimulation no longer affected the arterial pulse or amyloid removal — pointing to VIP as the link between neurons and arteries [#315].
8. Authors' claim. «Наши результаты устанавливают новые механизмы, которые задействуют глимфатическую систему для удаления амилоида из мозга» (Our results establish new mechanisms that engage the glymphatic system to remove amyloid from the brain) [#315].

#### Candida albicans route into and out of the brain
Purpose: explain, from Baylor College of Medicine mouse work, how this common fungus enters the brain, how the brain clears it, and how it produces Alzheimer's-relevant peptides [#246].
1. Entry. C. albicans produces enzymes called secreted aspartic proteases (Sap) that break down the blood-brain barrier, giving the fungus access to the brain, where it causes damage (first author Yifan Wu) [#246].

### Detailed insights

#### Why the fatigue study matters and what it cannot yet show
- Cognitive-fatigue research may become key to understanding how workers respond to and recover from high-stakes mental work such as air traffic control, where even a brief loss of concentration can cost lives [#170].
- Health psychologist Martin Hagger (UC Merced) notes that earlier studies showed mental effort affects physiological measures such as heart-rate variability and blood flow, but those effects tend to be subtle: «Это не похоже на то, когда вы тренируете скелетные мышцы» (It is not like when you train skeletal muscles) — «Но это ощутимо» (But it is tangible) [#169].
- Hagger hopes other researchers will adopt the new measurement approach: «Средства для обнаружения этого до сих пор были недостаточно чувствительными» (The means of detecting this have so far not been sensitive enough), so the study paves the way for future work on cognitive fatigue [#170].
- Carmen Sandi (EPFL Lausanne) says the study matters for linking cognitive fatigue to neurometabolism, but establishing a causal link between exhaustion and metabolic change will need further research, possibly in non-human animals where glutamate levels can be experimentally altered. Such work could reveal the molecular mechanisms that make glutamate accumulate during hard mental work and how this affects brain activity — «Это сложная часть» (That is the hard part) [#169, #170].

#### Background to brain clearance
- Waste accumulation in the working brain is a main cause of many neurological disorders, yet until recently knowledge of how cleaning happens was fragmentary [#314].
- Earlier work established that sleep — long and seemingly useless — lets the brain «расхламиться» (declutter) for good work the next day [#314].
- Later it became known that the brain's «метаболический мусор» (metabolic garbage) accumulated during the day is washed out by increased cerebrospinal-fluid flow through the parenchyma. What powers that flow was the open question; earlier work only hinted at a correlation between neuronal excitation and glymphatic function [#314].
- 40 Hz rhythms are usually typical of wakefulness, though some studies observed them in sleep (Bergel et al., 2018; Laurino et al., 2019). Nedergaard and Hablitz suggest different brain regions may have different stimulation frequencies for moving cerebrospinal fluid and clearing waste [#315].
