## Living under authoritarian regimes: compliance, denunciation and resistance

### What this covers

This topic collects the channel's posts (own and forwarded, 2022–2023) on the social psychology of life under repressive or dysfunctional states: what motivates denunciations, why people comply with mobilisation against their own convictions, how to think clearly about political events, how contradictory laws trap citizens, why magical belief fills the gap left by failing institutions, and how humour and ridicule act as resistance. The source frames these as recurring human mechanisms — the "low-level" (низовой) side of resistance and of collaboration — illustrated with the Third Reich, the USSR, contemporary Russia, Mongolian nomads and the US Ku Klux Klan [#12, #33, #77, #79, #108, #141, #167].

### Core ideas

- Denunciation is a form of "payback" to the state. In exchange for whatever guarantees a regime offers, citizens give something back; denunciation can be one such form, driven by profit, fear, or love of control (see *Three motives for denunciation*) [#33].
- Ask who concretely benefits. Political events should be judged by whether they make specific people's lives better, not by abstractions like nation or empire — the less abstraction, the more accurate the analysis (see *Who-benefits analysis*) [#77].
- Seemingly irrational compliance has concrete cognitive causes. Conscripts who oppose the war yet obey summonses act on identifiable mechanisms and distortions of thinking [#79].
- Repressive law can produce a double bind. When every possible answer is criminalised, the citizen is in Bateson's double bind — a situation the forwarded post calls "slightly schizophrenic" [#108].
- Witchcraft belief substitutes for failing institutions. Where courts and police don't work, fear of curses regulates behaviour; witchcraft belief is more common in failed states and pessimist-conformist cultures [#141, #140].
- Ridicule is resistance. Whispered jokes were one of the most widespread forms of grassroots resistance in Nazi Germany, and changing the emotional colouring of something feared (making it childish and public) can radically change attitudes to it [#12, #167].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Three motives for denunciation
Context / mechanism. The channel author argues that any government is searching for legitimacy: for people to support it, it must — however poorly — give some guarantees: some safety, some rights and freedoms (the author adds an ironic "(ха-ха)" ("ha-ha")), some self-realisation for citizens. In return people agree to perform certain actions: social ones, labour ones, and sometimes demonstrations of loyalty. The author notes this could lead into a discussion of the social contract and Hobbes, but deliberately does not go into it. The surprising claim is that one form of this "giving back" can be denunciations [#33].

The three kinds of denunciation, as listed by the author [#33]:
1. With additional profit (the "apartment" denunciation). In the USSR of the 1920s–30s this kind was known as "квартирный" ("apartment-related"), because the vacated place of a neighbour in a communal flat (коммуналка) — and not only there — could be immediately and "joyfully" occupied by the denouncer. The motive is material gain from the victim's removal [#33].
2. For one's own safety. Based on the principle "человек человеку волк" ("man is a wolf to man"): "если я не донесу сегодня, завтра донесут на меня" ("if I don't denounce today, tomorrow they'll denounce me"). The motive is pre-emptive self-protection in an atmosphere of mutual suspicion [#33].
3. Out of love for grassroots control. People denounce because they enjoy bottom-up policing of others. The author's own opinion: "на мой взгляд, вариант самый тёмный по перспективе для прав человека" ("in my view, the darkest option in terms of prospects for human rights") [#33].

The author recommends anthropologist Alexandra Arkhipova's conversation on this with Vlad Gorin of Meduza as a vivid discussion of the question "how do we come to this?" (see *Tools and resources*) [#33].

#### Who-benefits analysis ("Минута аналитики для самых маленьких")
Purpose. A forwarded post (from the channel Mykhology), ironically titled "Минута аналитики для самых маленьких" ("A minute of analytics for the very little ones"), gives a simple method for thinking about any big political event [#77].

Steps.
1. Ask the one key question first. Every time a big political event happens, ask first of all: "кто выигрывает от произошедшего?" ("who wins from what happened?") [#77].
2. Test it on yourself. Is it you? Does the event make your life happier? Richer? Better fed? Longer? [#77]
3. Widen to concrete neighbours. Does it make life better for your neighbours on the stair landing? For the residents of your town? [#77]
4. Use a concrete "average person" as a test case. "For convenience" take Glafira Pafnutyevna from Borovsk (a deliberately ordinary-sounding provincial woman): did her life get better or worse from what happened? [#77]
5. Drop the abstractions. "Не думайте о нации, религии и отечестве. Это условные общности. Нет никакой нации и никакого отечества, есть только люди, которые их составляют." ("Don't think about nation, religion and fatherland. These are notional communities. There is no nation and no fatherland, there are only the people who compose them.") [#77]
6. Identify the narrow group that actually gains. This applies to absolutely anything. Historical examples given:
   - When Caesar crosses the Rubicon, this benefits neither "Glafiria Pafnutida from Pompeii" nor the Senate and People of the Roman Republic, but personally G. J. Caesar and his friends [#77].
   - When Nicholas I enters the Crimean War, this is done neither to protect the hereditary honorary citizens of Borovsk nor even the needs of the Russian Empire. "У Российской империи, как у Римской, нет нужд - империя это абстрация [sic]" ("The Russian Empire, like the Roman one, has no needs — an empire is an abstraction"). It is done to protect the interests and tastes of a narrow group of the empire's elites [#77].

Rule of thumb. "Чем меньше абстракции в вашем анализе, тем точнее он будет." ("The less abstraction in your analysis, the more accurate it will be.") [#77]

#### Double bind applied to repressive law
Definition. A forwarded post from (Не)занимательная антропология explains that psychology (and anthropology) has a dilemma called double bind ("двойная связка"): a situation where a person receives two mutually contradictory signals from a figure who is an authority for them. The person doesn't know how to act, and a strong inner conflict results [#108].

Origin. The theory was devised by logician, mathematician and philosopher Gregory Bateson, who was actually studying how schizophrenia arises. He believed schizophrenia could be provoked by constantly receiving double binds from an authority figure, for example a mother [#108].

Applied example (the meme). The post says the situation is indeed "слегка шизофренична" ("slightly schizophrenic"), as proven by a "joke" image [#108]. The image (November 2022) asks "ПОДДЕРЖИВАЕШЬ СДАЧУ ХЕРСОНА?" ("Do you support the surrender of Kherson?"), with two arrows:
- ДА (Yes) → Criminal Code of the Russian Federation, Article 280.1 — public calls for actions aimed at violating the territorial integrity of the Russian Federation (Публичные призывы к осуществлению действий, направленных на нарушение территориальной целостности Российской Федерации) [#108]
- НЕТ (No) → Criminal Code of the Russian Federation, Article 280.3 — public actions aimed at discrediting the use of the Armed Forces of the Russian Federation (Публичные действия, направленные на дискредитацию использования Вооруженных Сил Российской Федерации) in order to protect the interests of the Russian Federation and its citizens, maintain international peace and security, or for state bodies of the Russian Federation to exercise their powers for these purposes [#108].

The lesson: the state (the authority figure) sends contradictory signals so that either answer to the question is punishable — the structure of a double bind [#108, img #108].

#### Witchcraft as an extrajudicial regulating institution
Case 1 — the Mongolian kharaalchi. The author of the forwarded post (from (Не)занимательная антропология) once wrote an article about Mongolian sorcerers called хараалчи (kharaalchi) [#141]:
1. Who they are said to be. They are feared terribly in Mongolia. They don't curse just anyone — they punish mainly for cattle theft, a major crime among nomads [#141].
2. How the curse supposedly works. They have a forked black tongue. The first time, they curse the thief and he gets the shakes and paralysis of the limbs. If he does not return what he stole, the kharaalchi curses again and he dies [#141].
3. The key point: they don't exist. "Самое главное в этих милых людях - то, что их не существует." ("The most important thing about these nice people is that they don't exist.") No one claims to be a kharaalchi — shamans yes, fortune-tellers yes, kharaalchi no [#141].
4. Function. It is an imagined force which, because of great fear of retribution, acts as an extrajudicial punishing institution. People won't steal cattle simply because they are scared; everyone believes cattle owners can curse the thief (and his family). "Вера в хараалчи лучше револьвера шерифа регулирует отношения в степи." ("Belief in the kharaalchi regulates relations in the steppe better than a sheriff's revolver.") [#141]

Case 2 — Boris Gershman's global study (confirmation). The author says this observation found unexpected confirmation: people from N+1 pointed her to a paper by economist Boris Gershman on what the spread of witchcraft in the world correlates with [#141]:
1. Data. Gershman built a database from opinion surveys about witchcraft around the world. The main survey question: do you believe an act of witchcraft can lead to bad consequences for you and your loved ones? [#141]
2. Variables. Various economic and socio-demographic variables were then brought in, to understand what the spread of modern witchcraft is linked to [#141].
3. Finding on education. Level of education, for example, is not the main or principal dependency [#141].
4. Mechanism. Belief in witchcraft creates a dense network for interpreting right and wrong behaviour — you are afraid to act wrongly because you'll be cursed. So when ordinary social institutions ("суд, администрация и т.д." — court, administration and the like) start working badly, witchcraft becomes the force that regulates relations: you won't go and steal a cow because you fear either the police or the sorcerer who will curse you [#141].
5. Conclusion. Witchcraft will be more present in states that are a "failure state" — where laws and courts don't work, and there are bribes, torture, wars, mercenaries [#141].
6. Cultural correlate (the author was "especially delighted" by this). Witchcraft belief is more characteristic of a culture of pessimist-conformists — the people who like to say "ну, вы же все понимаете!" ("well, you all understand!"). That is, they don't go against the system but justify their own inaction (conformism) by expecting the listener to recall the community's unspoken rules and agree with them [#141].

The channel author introduced this forwarded post herself as an interesting story about how witchcraft is connected with the collapse of social and legal institutions [#140].

#### Ridicule as resistance ("Смех против масок" — "Laughter against masks")
Principle. The channel author presents the story as a "прекрасная история" ("wonderful story") about how, by changing the emotional colouring of an event, you can radically change attitudes towards it and influence its further course [#167].

The story (from the book Freakonomics), step by step [#167]:
1. The problem. Kennedy wanted to destroy the Ku Klux Klan — a powerful secret organisation, a male brotherhood that kept many people in fear — but could not take it to court. So he decided to ridicule it.
2. The trigger idea. He once noticed a group of teenagers playing spies and exchanging secret passwords; the game reminded him of the Klan. He thought of sharing the Klan's passwords and other secrets with teenagers across the country. The reasoning, as the story puts it: what could disarm a secret society better than painting its activities in tones of infantility and making its main secrets public?
3. The channel. Kennedy began supplying his information about the Klan to the producers of a children's Superman radio show. They wrote scripts for broadcasts running four weeks, with the plot of Superman freeing the country from the Klan.
4. The effect. At the first Klan meeting after the broadcasts began, the whole Klavern (secret council) was in turmoil. The Grand Dragon (chair of the cell) tried to run the meeting as usual but was quickly silenced by rank-and-file members' shouting. One member complained that he came home from work to find his son and friends playing: some with big towels tied round their necks like capes, others with pillowcases on their heads, the caped children chasing the pillowcase-wearers. They called it a new game, Superman vs. the Klan — "Ловля банды!" ("Catching the gang!"). "Они знали все наши секреты, пароли и все тому подобное. Я никогда в жизни не чувствовал себя так нелепо!" ("They knew all our secrets, passwords and everything. I've never felt so ridiculous in my life!") — and he feared what would happen if they ever found his robe with the Klan sign.

The lesson: a fear-based organisation that cannot be beaten in court can be undermined by making it childish, public and laughable, so that its own members feel ridiculous [#167].

### Detailed insights

#### Whispered jokes in the Third Reich
One of the most widespread forms of "grassroots" (низовой) Resistance in the Third Reich was jokes. They were called "флюстервитце" (Flüsterwitze) — "шутки, рассказанные шепотом" ("jokes told in a whisper") — because telling them could easily land you "туда, куда не хотелось" ("where you didn't want to be") [#12].

The author notes a caveat: a significant part of these jokes cannot be translated into Russian without losing meaning, because German humour is largely built on puns, ambiguities and wordplay that are lost in translation. The remaining ones are still worth remembering [#12]. The ten jokes the post gives, with the point each makes:
1. The robber and the police. A Berliner walking at night hears from a dark gateway: "Stop, or I'll shoot!" He stops and raises his hands. "Hand over your wallet!" — "Phew, you shouldn't scare people like that! I thought you were from the police." Point: the police are more frightening than a street robber [#12].
2. The charity poster. A German sees a state charity poster: "Никто не должен голодать, никто не должен мерзнуть!" ("Nobody must go hungry, nobody must be cold!") — "Что, и это нам тоже официально запретили?!" ("What, has that been officially banned for us too?!") Point: a regime of bans, where every "must not" reads as another prohibition [#12].
3. The Swiss navy ministry. A Swiss shows a German around Bern: "This is our naval ministry." — "Why do you need a naval ministry? You don't even have a sea!" — "Well, you have a ministry of justice!" Point: justice does not exist in Nazi Germany [#12].
4. Göring's chauffeur and the pig. Göring's limousine kills a pig in a village; Göring tells the chauffeur to find the owner and pay compensation. The chauffeur comes back late, drunk and laden with gifts: "I went up to the peasants and said: I'm Göring's chauffeur, and I killed this pig. And then they started pouring me drinks!" Point: the peasants heard "this swine" as Göring and celebrated [#12].
5. The bomb in the bunker. Question: a bomb hits the bunker where Hitler, Göring and Goebbels are sitting. Who will be saved? Answer: Germany [#12].
6. Hess and Churchill. Hess flies to England and meets Churchill. "So you are that madman," says Churchill. "No, no, I'm his deputy," Hess answers. Point: the madman is Hitler [#12].
7. Hess in prison. "What are you in for?" — "On 5 May I said Hess had gone mad. And you?" — "On 15 May I said Hess was completely sane." Point: the official line changes, and you can be jailed for either statement [#12].
8. The radio tower. Göring and Hitler stand on a radio tower looking over Berlin. The Führer says: "I'd like to give the Berliners some joy." — "Adolf, I beg you, don't jump!" [#12]
9. The rabbits at the Belgian border. A crowd of rabbits arrives at the Belgian border: "We're fleeing Germany because giraffes have been outlawed!" — "But you're not giraffes!" says the surprised guard. "Мы-то знаем. А попробуйте объяснить это гестапо!" ("We know that. But try explaining it to the Gestapo!") Point: persecution is arbitrary, and innocence is no protection [#12].
10. The fence inscription. Two men in prison. "What are you in for?" — "I wrote on a fence: Göring is a fool." — "How much did you get?" — "Five years, six months and two weeks." — "Why such a strange term?" — "Two weeks for damaging the fence, six months for insulting a party leader, and five years for disclosing a state secret…" [#12]

#### Why conscripts comply with mobilisation
A forwarded post (from the channel Безвольные каменщики, September 2022) addresses the astonishment many feel since the start of mobilisation at the behaviour of men of conscription age who do not support the invasion of Ukraine yet obediently accept summonses or even go to the military enlistment offices themselves — "to go and kill and die". Observers keep asking: "Как это возможно, ведь у них же семьи, дети? Почему они не сопротивляются?" ("How is this possible, they have families, children? Why don't they resist?") [#79].

The post's answer: there is nothing surprising here — such seemingly absolutely irrational actions rest on quite concrete mechanisms of how our thinking works, and on its distortions (cognitive biases). The post promises a video discussing the main features of brain functioning that make us commit illogical acts [#79].

#### The word "armamentarium"
In a post written while ill (the author describes "this human (me)" watching "this machine (also me)" struggle with illness for the 10th day), the channel author shares a Latin word she found "very cool and not overused" for her synaesthetic taste, beautiful in its "ornately woven fundamentality": "Armamentarium" (pronounced Arma-mentArium). Literally from Latin "маленький арсенал" ("small arsenal"): a set of tools denoting the materials, devices and methods available, usually to a doctor, for doing the work. It is also used to mean a place where weapons are stored. The author suggests one might say that one's armamentarium contains CBT, Yudkowsky's chains, Neuralink, the Go language, or, say, a manual on modern Gothic [#140].

## Small actions and social change: beating helplessness

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel author's material on why individual and small-scale action is worth doing even when a person feels like "a grain of sand in an indifferent cosmos". It covers the nirvana fallacy as the belief that blocks action, five historical cases in which a single or local action grew into systemic change, the three factors that made those processes succeed, eight "mechanics of change" to keep in mind when one's contribution feels meaningless, and an earlier post arguing that helping others is itself a remedy for helplessness and depression. The author frames it as relevant to changes toward inclusivity and to coping with a period of global catastrophes [#554, #555, #556, #11].

### Core ideas

- Helplessness is a belief that stalls change. After five years of global catastrophes many people feel it is pointless to do anything — "the big system can't be beaten" — and conclude it is better to save energy and stay silent until it passes. The author says this belief can stall change work at every level and names it the nirvana fallacy [#554].
- Big change follows different laws than the fallacy assumes. Historical facts show that large changes — social initiatives protecting minorities or acts of resistance — grew out of individual or local actions that were strategically amplified [#554, #555].
- Success rested on visibility, pressure and systematic action (see *Three success factors of historical change*) [#555].
- There are concrete mechanics by which small contributions count, from the ripple effect to micro-legacies, community, leverage points, turning pain into purpose, rewriting the narrative, and finding meaning in the struggle itself (see *Eight mechanics of change*) [#556].
- Helping others is an antidote to helplessness. Helping fights powerlessness and depression, invests in one's own meaning of life, and increases support and faith in the good, which makes self-care, stability and planning easier [#11].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### The nirvana fallacy (the belief that blocks action)
The author defines the nirvana fallacy as the situation "когда решение или действие отвергается, если оно не идеально и не решает проблему целиком" (when a solution or action is rejected if it is not ideal and does not solve the problem entirely). The lived form of it is the feeling of the senselessness of doing anything: "большую систему не победишь, зачем пытаться? Лучше экономить силы и молчать в тряпочку, пока это не кончится" (you can't beat the big system, why try? Better to save your strength and keep quiet until this is over). The author's context: over the last 5 years society has faced a series of global catastrophes before which an individual seems like "маленькой песчинкой в безразличном космосе" (a small grain of sand in an indifferent cosmos). The danger, per the author, is that the belief can halt work on changes "на самых разных уровнях" (at the most diverse levels) — including the changes toward inclusivity that prompted the post. The author closes with a Buddhist pun: "мечты о нирване не избавят от дуккха" (*dreams of nirvana will not rid you of dukkha*, i.e. waiting for a perfect solution does not relieve suffering). The author points readers to Russian-language examples of the fallacy on the channel @mental_radar (post 410) [#554].

The author's counter-argument is historical: real facts show that big changes "работают по иным законам" (work by different laws). He presents "only an excerpt" from his earlier work, deliberately, "Дабы не звучать излишне революционно" (so as not to sound overly revolutionary), enough to demonstrate the importance of even small steps — that excerpt is the five cases and the eight mechanics below [#554].

#### Three success factors of historical change
Purpose: explains what turned single actions in the five historical cases (see *Historical cases*) into systemic change; the author asks "Что обеспечивало успешность этих процессов?" (What ensured the success of these processes?) and answers with three factors [#555]:

1. Visibility (Видимость). Each action drew attention to systemic injustice, provoking conversations and gathering support. Example from the cases: Carson's book created public resonance; Gandhi's march became a national and international symbol [#555].
2. Pressure (Давление). Sustained movements made the status quo untenable, often through financial, social or political tension. Example: the Montgomery bus boycott put financial pressure on the bus system; international sanctions and divestment pressured apartheid South Africa [#555].
3. Systematic action (Систематические действия). Consistent action led to legal or political changes because authorities were forced to respond. Example: Browder v. Gayle, the Representation of the People Act, negotiations with Indian leaders [#555].

The author's conclusion: "индивидуальные или локальные действия, если их стратегически усилить, могут привести к глубоким системным изменениям" (individual or local actions, if strategically amplified, can lead to deep systemic changes) [#555].

#### Eight mechanics of change to remember when a contribution feels pointless
Purpose: Part 2 of the series, titled "Что важно помнить, когда отдельный вклад кажется бессмысленным?" (What is important to remember when an individual contribution seems meaningless?). Derived by the author from the historical examples, these are components that contribute not only to changing a situation but also to raising people's quality of life — "и даже тех, кто не успеет застать все результаты своего труда" (even those who will not live to see all the results of their work) [#556].

1. Ripple effect (Эффект расходящихся кругов). Small purposeful actions create ripples that extend far beyond what you can see. Over time the cumulative effect of many people doing similar things can lead to meaningful, systemic change. Therefore voting, supporting grassroots organisations and even showing kindness in personal interactions all play a role [#556].
2. The power of legacy and passing the baton (Сила наследия и передача эстафетной палочки). History is full of people who fought for justice, equality and truth even when the odds seemed impossible. Most did not live to see the fruits of their labour, but "длинная дуга прогресса отражает их усилия" (the long arc of progress reflects their efforts). By contributing to that arc, people can align their actions with something larger than themselves. When no immediate solution seems possible, the author says it is important to remember to equip future generations — through education, stories or concrete steps you contribute to a foundation on which others can keep building change [#556].
3. Micro-legacies (Микронаследия). On a smaller scale it makes sense to create micro-legacies — things that directly improve the lives of those around you. The listed forms: teaching skills, sharing resources, mentoring, and even advocating for those who feel alone. These can leave a long-term positive impact [#556].
4. Finding strength in community (Обретение силы в сообществе). Connecting with like-minded people can provide both tangible influence and emotional resilience. Whether through activism, mutual aid or smaller local efforts such as suitable public spaces, cooperation amplifies strength and helps soften feelings of isolation or helplessness [#556].
5. Knowing useful leverage points (Знание полезных точек воздействия). Targeting points of influence in systems — from supply chains to institutions and policy — and acting on them as a group can create exponential change. Hence even simply supporting and joining groups that advocate for specific changes can be key to having tangible impact [#556].
6. Turning pain into purpose (Превращение боли в цель). Instead of suppressing feelings of helplessness or anger, use them as fuel for action. Many movements and breakthroughs began because someone said: "Это невыносимо, и я отказываюсь это принимать" (This is unbearable, and I refuse to accept it). Use frustration to feed art, activism, innovation; "Самовыражение преобразует энергию в действие" (Self-expression transforms energy into action) [#556].
7. Rewriting the narrative (Переписывание повествования). Acknowledge injustice while refusing to let it define the future. A mindset that has room for resistance and hope creates a different context for one's actions — one where despair does not have the last word. The author's signature line: "Оптимизм не наивен — это акт бунта в мрачные времена" (Optimism is not naive — it is an act of rebellion in dark times) [#556].
8. Meaning is in the struggle (Смысл — в борьбе). Continuing the previous idea, the author cites Camus, who argued that the very act of resisting the absurd in anything has meaning. Even if your personal chances of success are small, there is dignity and purpose in the struggle itself; the struggle becomes its own form of rebellion against hopelessness. The author adds: "Важно помнить и говорить, когда какое-то положение дел не нормально" (It is important to remember and to say when some state of affairs is not normal) [#556].

#### What helping gives the helper (three effects)
In an early post (March 2022) the author lists, as a three-line anaphora "Помогая, мы…" (By helping, we…), what helping others does for the helper [#11]:

1. It fights helplessness and depression — "Помогая, мы боремся с бессилием и депрессией" (By helping, we fight powerlessness and depression) [#11].
2. It invests in one's own meaning of life — "Помогая, мы вкладываемся в свой смысл жизни" (By helping, we invest in our own meaning of life) [#11].
3. It increases the amount of support, giving and faith in the good and the living — for oneself and for others. The mechanism the author proposes: the more of this there is in us, the easier it is to reach states of readiness for self-care, contact and stability, planning, forecasting and development [#11].

### Detailed insights

#### Historical cases: single actions that led to big results
Part 1 of the series, "Следы в истории: как отдельные действия приводили к большим результатам" (Traces in history: how individual actions led to big results), gives five cases, each structured as an action and its results [#555]:

1. Environmental movement. Action: Rachel Carson's book *Silent Spring* (1962) exposed the dangers of pesticides such as DDT for the environment and health. Results: the book caused public resonance and drew national attention to environmental problems; public awareness led to policy changes, including a nationwide ban on DDT in agriculture in 1972; in 1970 the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) was created, establishing a regulatory framework for environmental protection. Lesson: one book made a hidden harm visible and shifted national policy [#555].
2. Civil rights movement (USA). Action: Rosa Parks's refusal to give up her seat on a segregated bus in Montgomery, Alabama, in 1955. Results: her arrest mobilised the African American community, leading to the Montgomery bus boycott and a mass protest lasting more than 1 year; the boycott put significant financial pressure on the bus system and exposed systemic racism; ultimately, in Browder v. Gayle (1956), the Supreme Court ruled bus segregation unconstitutional, banning segregation on public transport at the federal level. Lesson: one refusal became a sustained, economically costly collective action that forced a legal ruling [#555].
3. Indian independence movement. The author adds the aside "кстати о бизнесе" (speaking of business). Action: Gandhi's Salt March in 1930 — a 240-mile walk to the sea protesting the British monopoly on salt production. Results: the march drew national and international attention and became a symbol of Indian disobedience to British rule; it spurred broad participation in civil disobedience, including boycotts of British goods; British authorities were forced to negotiate with Indian leaders, leading to greater political autonomy and eventually independence in 1947 [#555].
4. British suffragette movement. Action: Emmeline Pankhurst and the Women's Social and Political Union (WSPU) launched militant campaigns — protests, hunger strikes and civil disobedience — to demand votes for women. Results: the visibility and persistence of their efforts kept the issue in public consciousness and pressured Parliament to act; during World War I women took on traditionally male roles, further proving their capabilities; in 1918 the Representation of the People Act gave the vote to women over 30 who met a property qualification, and full suffrage was won in 1928 [#555].
5. Anti-apartheid movement (South Africa). Action: internal resistance — strikes and protests led by Nelson Mandela and other leaders the source does not name ("и др.") — combined with international solidarity campaigns, including boycotts and sanctions. Results: protests and strikes made governing South Africa under apartheid increasingly difficult; international economic sanctions and divestment created economic pressure; in 1990 Mandela was released from prison and negotiations to dismantle apartheid began; by 1994 apartheid ended and Mandela became South Africa's first democratically elected president [#555].

The author shares it with the words "Немедленно множу добро" (Immediately multiplying good), i.e. sharing the material is itself framed as an act of spreading help [#11].
