## Group polarization, conformity and social norms

### Core ideas

- Groups push opinions toward the poles. Contrary to the early expectation that a group would settle near its members' average, discussion in a group makes people's answers more radical (the risky shift, later generalized as group polarization), in both decisions and expressed attitudes [#28].
- Arguing with an opponent radicalizes. The more we conflict with a person of a different opinion, the more radical our views become after the dispute [#28].
- Fierce defence of a position rests on threat and status. After extending four polarization hypotheses, the author concludes that the drivers are fear for identity, fear of social isolation and the desire to take and keep a higher place in the hierarchy — threat, thirst for status, or both [#55].
- Perceived majority size bends stated opinion. Kuran's preference falsification model: the larger people believe the majority is, the more strongly they choose its opinion [#57].
- The famous conformity experiments were products of their era. Conformity was a value under McCarthyism and became something condemned in the 1960s; closer analysis shows far less blanket conformity than the popular account, and one dissenter is enough to break it [#52, #91, img #97].
- Traditions are a stability tool whose rigidity has to be balanced. At either extreme (rigid or chaotic) a system adapts poorly; the author favours a "middle way" of stable but revisable roles and rules [#86].
- News spreads when it is novel yet confirms what we already "know". Novelty captures attention; confirmation of folk knowledge rewards us with dopamine; even disgust-driven retelling spreads the meme [#369].

- Social movements have moved the boundaries of acceptable masculinity. Feminist, queer and transgender movements and cancel culture have started changing what is acceptable within habitual masculinity, and the author says this has its upsides [#615].
- Men face a double bind. Guys find it extremely hard to know how to act so as not to end up «между молотом и наковальней» ("between the hammer and the anvil") — between «токсичной маскулинностью» ("toxic masculinity") and «бабством» ("being womanish") [#615].
- Old supports lost, new ones not built. The lecture's diagnosis (really about late-20th-century trends): the man is still expected to succeed, while the emancipated woman now values emotional openness and shared childcare as much as or more than his earnings [#615].
- If masculinity is a social construct, its building blocks are open to question. The author asks which "кубики" ("building blocks") the 2025 palette of masculinity contains, and whether we have grown up enough to stop tying the non-biological part of ourselves to our sex [#615].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Group polarization: history of the concept
1. Starting assumption (1920s–1930s). G. Allport and other researchers of the 1920s–1930s assumed that individuals make more extreme decisions than groups. This created the expectation that people together would express a position close to the average riskiness of the group's members [#28].
2. The risky shift (1961). J. Stoner, a student at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, noticed the so-called «сдвиг риска» (risky shift): after discussing decisions in a group, people gave more radical answers to the same questions they had just filled in on their own. Notably, the difference persisted even when the group and individual decisions were made in reverse order [#28].
3. Generalization. Although Stoner's work was unfinished, it triggered a surge of research, and by the end of the 1960s it became clear that the risky shift is one of the effects of a shift toward the poles of positions within a group. S. Moscovici and M. Zavalloni later named this «групповой поляризацией» (group polarization), and experimental data confirming the regularity have since accumulated [#28].
4. Scope. Group polarization shows up not only in decision-making but also in the attitudes people express toward phenomena — attitude or belief polarization [#28].
5. Current explanations. The mechanism is now explained through two social theories: social comparison (motivation for recognition and approval) and social identity (motivation for belonging and for self-expression as a member of a particular group). There are also ongoing discussions of how being in a particular information bubble and dividing people into «своих» and «чужих» ("us" and "them") affect opinion shift. A further finding the author highlights: the more we conflict with a person of a different opinion, the more radical we become in our views when leaving such a dispute. The keywords the author attaches: polarization, groupthink and anchoring [#28].

#### Four hypotheses of group polarization, extended to explain fierce defence of positions
Purpose: the author found that a webinar on polarization did not answer her real question — why people hold a position is not the same as why they defend it with fury in a «споре» (dispute). One would expect that when the other side adds its data, people would move toward a middle opinion — «но нет» (but no). She therefore takes the existing theoretical approaches to polarization (the post links the Wikipedia section on major theoretical approaches) and, under the heading «Достраиваем» ("we build it out"), extends each one into an explanation of why people strengthen and defend their previous positions [#52].

1. Hypothesis 1 — prestige of the radical opinion. Short meaning: «быть несогласным — круто» ("being in disagreement is cool"). Extension:
   - If a society rewards disagreement, that position is more likely to lead to higher places in the hierarchy.
   - Drawing on Sapolsky and testosterone research, one can suppose polarization would level out if a society rewarded conformity instead. According to the author, this is exactly what experiments of the 1950s showed, when McCarthy was in power and the ability to fit into the dominant social paradigm was the highest value. By the late 1950s–early 1960s personal freedom and independence came to the fore, and what had been a virtue appeared in a quite different light — in Zimbardo's Stanford prison experiment and Milgram's obedience-to-authority experiment.
   - Signature line: «За что боролись, про то и поляризация» (roughly "what people fought for is what polarization is about" — i.e. polarization follows whatever the society rewards) [#52].
2. Hypothesis 2 — social identity theory. Short meaning: «я — часть группы «своих», не хочу относиться к «чужим»» ("I am part of the group of 'us', I don't want to belong to 'them'"). Extension: if we identify with a certain group, changing our opinion on our own on a question that seems important for belonging to it may be hard. Possibly because we perceive the change through fear of social isolation and of our identity being blurred; therefore the more dependent we are on this, the harder we resist, in order to preserve our integrity [#52].
3. Hypothesis 3 — social comparison theory, or the drive for leadership. Short meaning: «я — не просто член, но и лидер группы мнения» ("I am not just a member but also a leader of the opinion group"). The theory says people first compare their own ideas with those of other group members, then observe and evaluate what the group values and prefers; then, to gain recognition, they take a position similar to everyone else's but slightly more extreme, presenting themselves as the group's "leaders". Extension: (a) again hierarchy, the top of it — as in hypothesis 1; (b) the stronger the pressure to move a person off a position, the stronger the resistance must be to keep it [#52].
4. Hypothesis 4 — persuasive arguments / informational influence within the group. Short meaning: the more information there is in favour of the position of the group one feels one belongs to, the less interesting the alternatives. In essence it describes information bubbles and confirmation bias:
   - The more closed my circle of communication and information sources, the fewer other viewpoints I hear.
   - The more I hear about something and the more plausible a belief seems, the more I consider other opinions mistaken or based on incomplete information («вот если бы они знали ВСЮ правду…» — "if only they knew ALL the truth…"). When I hear something matching what I know, I strengthen my position; when I hear the opposite or something different, I decide that people don't know something and therefore draw wrong conclusions. And then it becomes all the more interesting to strengthen my views.
   - The author's objection: this theory does not explain confrontation, which leads to her extension (the next framework) [#52].

#### Three qualities that make an information bubble attractive
The author's extension of hypothesis 4: an information bubble is not all the posts a social network feeds us. It is the posts created by those who, as it seems to us:
1. "Close" («близкий») — understands us: is somehow like us, has had similar experience or has gone through the same difficulties.
2. "Competent" («компетентный») — knows how the current situation works: what is right and wrong in it, who is to blame.
3. "Leader" («лидер») — in addition, understands what needs to be done for us to feel better [#52].

Consequence, in the author's opinion: if these three parts are missing, we will look for other bubbles — in the opposition, in the underground, anywhere — until we find one that has them. Or until we give up and prefer not to read any of it, outside all bubbles (though some small bubbles will surely remain, even at the level of kitchen conversations with neighbours) [#55].

Where confrontation comes from, in the author's view: from the wish for a clear picture of the world without contradictions, and for a course of action that is understandable, sufficiently definite and predictable [#55].

#### Three root drivers of fierce position defence
The author's summary («Итого») of the whole analysis — at the base, it seems, lie:
1. Fear for identity («страх за идентичность») — from the social identity hypothesis: changing a belonging-relevant opinion threatens to blur who we are.
2. Fear of social isolation («страх социальной изоляции») — losing the group of "us".
3. The desire to take and hold a higher place in the hierarchy («желание занять и удержать место повыше в иерархии») — from the prestige and leadership hypotheses.

Thus the main mechanics of a more stormy defence of one's opinion in an argument seem to come from a sense of threat or a thirst for status, and sometimes both. The author presents this as a hypothesis («похоже», "it seems") and invites constructive criticism [#55].

Afterword the author attaches, from the Zhuangzi (chapter IV, "Among people"): to have outstanding abilities and impeccable honesty but not see what lies in another's soul, not to understand the human heart and to preach goodness, justice and noble deeds before a hard-hearted ruler means showing off one's beauty by exposing another's ugliness — «А тому, кто доставляет неудовольствие другим, люди, конечно же, тоже будут стараться навредить» ("And people will of course also try to harm the one who displeases others") [#55].

#### Tradition on the flexibility-rigidity spectrum of a system
Purpose: to judge whether tradition matters for society by viewing it as part of the system of human relationships ("one puzzle piece from the systems approach") [#86].

1. Definition. In Thomas Green's encyclopedia of folklore (referenced by Wikipedia), a tradition is a belief or behaviour with special meaning that came from the past and is passed from generation to generation within a group of people. Ozhegov's dictionary says something similar but covers not only customs but also ways of thinking, worldviews, ways of acting and preferences in taste (the author jokes: probably so as not to argue about them at least within one's community). In other words, tradition is «призма взглядов и набор ритуалов для поддержания установленного порядка группой людей» ("a prism of views and a set of rituals for maintaining an established order by a group of people") — something stable and conservative, predictable and understandable [#86].
2. Range of forms. A tradition can be light and cheerful, like exchanging gifts at New Year, or a gruelling ordeal, like the initiation rite of the Gbaya people. Either way, these properties can give adherents a very powerful sense of community and safety, and "perhaps there is no better time to lean on traditions than a time of change and war" [#86].
3. Systems view. Relationships — with the outside world and inside communities — undergo regular changes, big and small, everyday and sudden. The reaction depends on how flexible or rigid the system's boundaries are. This is a spectrum: from striving to resist and maximally preserve the existing order, to changing one's structure and ways of interacting almost without hesitation ("if it must be, it must be"). For more, the author points to David Olson's circumplex model [#86].
4. Four domains where flexibility/rigidity shows (tradition can set reference points for all of them):
   - how ideas are proposed and decisions made — "leadership";
   - how processes are observed — "control";
   - how rules are followed — "discipline";
   - how responsibility is distributed — "roles in the system" [#86].
5. Extremes reduce adaptation. The closer a system gets to an extreme of the spectrum — the chaotic or the rigid pole — the harder it is for the system and its members to interact with each other and with the world, and the lower the ability to adapt [#86].
6. Exception: acute crisis. Extreme values can be adaptive only in states of short-term powerful crisis: when it is important either to hold the boundaries with all one's strength under pressure of a factor unfavourable to the system, or when any means are good for survival and it does not matter who does what when — «война всё спишет» ("war will write everything off") and «победителей не судят» ("winners are not judged") [#86].
7. Peacetime costs of rigidity. In peacetime, rigid unquestionable traditions will rather shackle communities, hindering their development and the expression of members' personal abilities — for example, a tradition by which an unchanging leader stays in power until death and does not correct course despite changes in "the climate situation and the wishes of the pack" [#86].
8. Costs of no tradition. Equally, a complete absence of traditions can bring chaos into people's minds and lead to loss of life meanings, orientation, understanding of oneself and one's place in the world [#86].
9. The "middle way". Hence the author sees it as important to keep a balance between stability and change, helped by the position of the «срединного пути» ("middle way"): roles and rules are stable, but the possibility to revise them is open, common tasks can be discussed together, and the minority's opinion is taken into account [#86].

#### Two-step recipe of a spreading meme
Question: why do some news items provoke a strong response so that we retell them, while others are quickly forgotten or ignored? The author's answer (an article of hers from 15.05.2021, republished; based on Alexandra Arkhipova's lecture "Memes in social anthropology"): spreading news has two properties — (1) it creates an impression of novelty, but at the same time (2) it meets expectations. The paradox is captured by the saying «Никогда не было и вот опять» ("It never happened before, and here it is again") [#369].

Mechanism behind the two properties:
- Folk psychology as fertile ground. Such news lands on our folk psychology — intuitively understood everyday knowledge obtained from parables and myths, rituals and omens, grandparents and other sources of folk wisdom. These contain extracts of ideas and "valuable instructions" that long helped us survive and were evolutionarily useful. Such extracts are called memes; the word was introduced by Richard Dawkins [#369].
- Why confirmation rewards. What helps survival is fixed at the level of biochemistry: when we make predictions about something (which happens regularly when interacting with the world), dopamine is released; if the prediction turns out right, this works as a reward and dopamine release increases. A right prediction is confirmation that our knowledge of the world is correct — which is why confirmation of folk knowledge works so well [#369].
- Why novelty matters. Why not savour the same familiar idea? Because of boredom. We notice the new because it is unfamiliar, and unfamiliar means we must work out whether it is useful or dangerous; this grabs attention. Examples: bushes nearby suddenly rustle — a tiger? Or you are running through the jungle and feel a burning in your back — a poisonous liana? If you don't pay attention, you can die quickly. There is even a separate neural network for this, the salience network, «что-то вроде «сети заметности, выпуклости»» ("something like a 'network of noticeability, prominence'") [#369].

The recipe:
1. Step 1 — grab attention with novelty, at least some novelty.
2. Step 2 — give something intuitively understandable and close to the person's experience — their everyday knowledge, folk psychology — and create a pleasant feeling that they understand how the world works, i.e. that they are clever, well adapted and will live longer [#369].

The insidious twist: even if the news seemed unpleasant or stupid, we will probably still tell it to someone else — just with the message «фу, какая гадость» ("ugh, how disgusting"). That other person will decide something of their own and may take it seriously. «Результат один — мем успешно размножается» ("The result is the same — the meme successfully reproduces") [#369].

#### Two trends of late-20th-century masculinity (old supports lost, new ones not built)
Purpose: the lecture's explanation of why masculinity "nowadays is who-knows-what". The historian described it, in the author's words, as «фиг знает, что вообще такое» ("who the heck knows what it is at all"). The author notes that in fact the lecture was mostly about trends of the end of the 20th century, summed up as «старые опоры утрачены, новые — не выстроены» ("the old supports are lost, the new ones are not built"). The two trends pull in different directions [#615]:

1. The man must still be successful. On the one hand the requirement of success has not gone away. Under instability and the neoliberal economic order meeting it is getting harder and harder, because competition is high. So the old demand stays while the conditions for fulfilling it worsen [#615].
2. The emancipated woman needs something else. On the other hand the woman has emancipated, and it is no longer necessary for her that the man earns a lot. What now matters as much (if not more) is that:
   - he shows feelings — the stated reason: «ведь она — женщина, а не посудомойка» ("after all she is a woman, not a dishwasher");
   - he helps take care of the children, if and when the couple are ready to have them — the stated reason: both partners now develop different facets of their personalities, not only «дети-кухня-церковь» ("children-kitchen-church") [#615].

The resulting squeeze: the traditional demand (succeed, earn) persists and is harder to meet, while a new set of demands (emotional expressiveness, shared care) is added, and no stable new model tells a man how to combine them [#615].

### Detailed insights

#### Conformity vs conformism
The author defines conformity («конформность») as changing one's attitudes and behaviour to fit the majority's position under its pressure, real or expected. In her observation this mechanism largely helps maintain and transmit information about how it is "right" to think and act — which is why looking at traditions broadly, as a system of views, inevitably runs into conformity. She separates it from conformism («конформизм»): an adaptation in which a person does not even question the offered viewpoint and passively accepts the existing order of things [#91].

#### The Asch, Milgram and Zimbardo experiments reread
The three famous 1950s–1960s experiments, as the author describes them:
- Solomon Asch — the "vision test": confederates declared lines of completely different lengths to be the same, and one real participant had to give his opinion. (!) UNCLEAR: the source says «67 подставных людей» ("67 planted people"); the figure is reproduced as written.
- Stanley Milgram: some participants had to apply increasingly strong electric shocks to others on the instruction of a person in a white coat.
- Philip Zimbardo — the prison game: students were divided into prisoners and guards and told to do «всё, что положено» ("everything that's required") [#91].

The popular account: these experiments presented — and at the same time condemned — almost absolute human conformity and obedience to authority, which, especially in Zimbardo's view, fall on the fertile soil of a supposedly innate cruelty. Behind the scenes the experimenters' conviction of that very mindless conformism seemed to show through [#91].

Historical reframing (the author's reading): Asch's similar studies, a decade earlier, rested on a quite different ethical tradition. The first studies of conformity were done mainly in the US in the 1950s, when McCarthyism (the time of Senator Joseph McCarthy) was at its peak and the ability to blend in, be one of "us" and adapt to the system was among the most important values. In the 1960s, apparently, the value of "fitting in" gave way to the value of "freedom", and the same experiments came to be viewed increasingly negatively; the value of conformity was replaced by condemnation. Hence it is not surprising that the humanist and hippie movements of the time directed their energy to highlighting the bright and independent in people, stressing that by creating conditions for supportive development we would get a virtuous, independent, self-actualized individual [#91]. (The same era-dependence argument appears in hypothesis 1 of the polarization framework [#52].)

What closer study of the results and later experiments showed, according to the author:
1. In Asch's work, the 37% rate of agreeing was made up of only 25% consistent "caving" participants, the same share (25%) who strictly disagreed with the majority, and 50% who agreed only from time to time.
2. In Milgram's work, a certain number of people saw through the make-believe nature of the experiment and therefore were not afraid to raise the voltage.
3. In later re-checks, 20 or more years on, conformity rates kept getting lower.
4. Adding just one person who disagreed with the majority was enough for other dissenting voices to appear — even if their opinion differed in another respect, and even if the first dissenter was later removed from the sample [#91].

The accompanying image card states the single-dissenter finding: "A SINGLE DEFECTOR WAS ENOUGH TO COMPLETELY WIPE OUT THE CONFORMITY EFFECT. THEY DIDN'T EVEN HAVE TO AGREE WITH THE SUBJECT, THEY JUST HAD TO HAVE A DIFFERENT OPINION FROM THE MAJORITY" [#97].

The author's concluding thought ("мысле-выводы"): if a person is the result of the interaction of genetics and environment, the question arises — what world do we want to create around us, which values do we want to support and multiply, and with which traditions should we surround ourselves accordingly? [#91]

#### Social norms in practice: unwritten rules of Serbian hospitality
The author shares the unwritten rules a guest in a Serbian home is expected to follow ("true Serbs" are expected to):
1. Bring a gift (a bottle of wine or, say, sweets), and when the host formally refuses, be sure to perform the ritual back-and-forth (the author calls it «потанцевать», "to dance"): «Да бросьте, это пустяки» (Ma kakvi, nije to ništa — "Oh come on, it's nothing"); the author privately labelled this the «правилом макакви» ("makakvi rule").
2. Be ready to eat without limit — otherwise you hear «Ты что не ешь ничего?!» ("Why aren't you eating anything?!") and «Не можешь есть? Ну ешь без хлеба, ладно» ("Can't eat? Fine, eat without bread then").
3. Offer help in the kitchen, even if you are a man — take out the rubbish; most likely you won't be allowed to do anything, but you will be given a credit of trust.
4. Argue about politics heatedly and knowingly — «Ваше политическое кредо?» ("Your political creed?") is always relevant here; if you don't feel able to withstand this stormy but usually very short — "I'd say hot-tempered" — argument, use the previous rule (go help in the kitchen).
5. Most importantly, be ready to answer awkward questions — "Yes, it's a genre. Yes, here too." Came alone? — Do you have a boyfriend? Came with a boyfriend? — When's the engagement? Have a child? — When's the second? Have everything? — Not so fast: «Па шта чекаш?/НУ, ЧЕГО ЖДЁШЬ?» ("So what are you waiting for?") [#423].

## Cooperation, game theory and the origins of morality

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's posts on cooperation as a game-theoretic problem and on where morality, empathy and kindness come from. The game-theory material runs from the classic prisoner's dilemma and the cap-exchange game, through Nash equilibrium and Axelrod's tournaments (tit-for-tat and its variants), to a worked debunking of the viral red/blue button pseudo-dilemma. The morality material covers Frans de Waal's work on the evolutionary origins of empathy and cooperation, a Pinker and Goldstein discussion of moral progress, a comic about trust and who gets saddled with responsibility for helping, and a guest post on how a culture without everyday shouting feels. The author frames the game-theory questions as ones that look ethical (forgive or retaliate? cooperate long-term or grab a big win now?) but are studied by mathematics [#343].

### Core ideas

- Individual vs group rationality. In a prisoner's dilemma each player, not knowing the partner's move, is better off defecting, yet mutual defection is worse for both than mutual cooperation. A group of self-interested members can end up worse off than a group acting for the common good [#330]. Details in *Prisoner's dilemma* below.
- Cooperation strategies are a mathematical question. Game theory studies which cooperation strategies are optimal in which situations; repeated play favours simple reciprocal strategies such as tit-for-tat, but no strategy is best against every opponent [#343, #345].
- The calculations apply only to non-cooperative games. The author calls this the most important nuance: the results hold only where participants cannot form coalitions or coordinate decisions [#344].
- Not every life game is zero-sum. In chess or poker one player's win is the other's loss, but in human society goods can be multiplied without taking from another player [#343].
- Beware pseudo-dilemmas. Some viral dilemmas have a dominant option and work only by pressing on emotions; the defence is to pause and check the payoffs [#450].
- Morality has evolutionary roots. Through de Waal, the author holds that empathy, fairness, cooperation and altruism have origins shared with other primates, which counters claims that humans stand as a moral beacon above the animal kingdom [#274].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Prisoner's dilemma (story one: Tanya and Cinque)
Purpose: the textbook model of the conflict between individual and joint interest. The channel's source is the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy [#330].

Setup: Tanya and Cinque were arrested for robbing the Hibernia savings bank and placed in separate isolation cells. Each cares much more about personal freedom than about the accomplice's welfare. A clever prosecutor offers each the same deal: you may confess or stay silent, and leave a note with the jailer before the prosecutor returns the next morning if you want to confess [#330].

The four outcomes the prosecutor lays out:
1. You confess, your accomplice stays silent: all charges against you are dropped, and your testimony is used to make sure the accomplice gets a serious sentence [#330].
2. Your accomplice confesses, you stay silent: the accomplice goes free and you serve the sentence [#330].
3. Both confess: both receive a sentence, but the prosecutor will make sure each gets early parole [#330].
4. Both stay silent: the prosecutor has to settle for token sentences on a firearms-possession charge [#330].

Why it is a dilemma: «не зная действий напарника, каждому в отдельности лучше признаться, чем хранить молчание» (not knowing the partner's actions, each of them individually is better off confessing than keeping silent). But if both confess, each is worse off than if both had stayed silent [#330].

Common reading: the puzzle is widely taken to illustrate the conflict between individual and group rational decision-making. A group whose members pursue personal interests may end up worse off than one whose members act for the common good, sometimes with a smaller payoff in the moment but with greater guarantees of payoffs in the future. Most of the modern literature focuses on identifying the conditions under which players could or should make the cooperative move [#330].

The author's later restatement of the payoff structure (used to contrast it with the button poll): each person gains most by cheating the other, but if everyone does so everyone gets a result that is not terrible, only minimally acceptable; if both cooperate they grab a big win, but neither knows whether the other will cooperate, no prior agreement is possible, and if one turns out more naive, the other gets the maximum payoff [#450].

#### Exchange game (story two: Bill and Rose's caps)
Purpose: the same structure stripped of crime, as an everyday trade [#330].
1. Setup: Bill has a blue cap but would prefer a red one; Rose has a red cap and would prefer a blue one. Both would be happy to have two caps, but one is better than none [#330].
2. Choice: each can keep their own cap or hand it to the other [#330].
3. Individual logic: because the other person's moral qualities are unknown, giving means risking ending up with nothing, so keeping your own looks better [#330].
4. Joint logic: both are better off swapping than keeping, which raises the core problem of how to know whether the other will hand theirs over [#330].

Situations like these, where participants for whatever reason cannot form coalitions and coordinate decisions with each other, are called in game theory некооперативные игры (non-cooperative games) [#330].

#### Nash equilibrium (the channel's account)
The author tells it as a turn in economic thought: Adam Smith held that competition on an every-man-for-himself basis is the key to survival in the economic jungle, and in 1950 the discoveries of John Nash (Nobel laureate in economics and the prototype of the hero of the film A Beautiful Mind) overturned these previously dominant ideas. In the author's words, Nash showed that if everyone does the best for themselves while also doing better for others, everyone ends up winning more. Hence the Nash equilibrium: «в какой-то момент никто не сможет выигрывать больше, если подход изменит только кто-то один, а не многие» (at some point no one can win more if only one person, rather than many, changes their approach) [#343].

#### Tit-for-tat protocol (око за око)
Origin: in 1980 Robert Axelrod and Anatol Rapoport set out to study the consequences of different types of cooperation and ran Axelrod's tournament. Tit-for-tat became popular because it let a player win the most with a cooperative partner while losing the least to a cheat [#343].
1. Open with cooperation: «вы начинаете с «кооперации» и дальше копируете действия другого» (you start with cooperation and then copy the other's actions) [#343].
2. Mirror cooperation: cooperate as long as the other cooperates [#343].
3. Mirror defection: refuse after the other's refusal [#343].
4. Return to cooperation: go back to cooperating as soon as, on the previous move, the other again said yes [#343].

The Stanford text's account: tit-for-tat cooperates on the first move and then does whatever the opponent did on the previous move. Against always-defect it therefore defects after the first move; against always-cooperate it always cooperates; against itself it always cooperates. It gains all the benefits of cooperation with a friendly opponent and protects itself by defecting against a defector [#345].

Flaw: «нет права на ошибку» (no right to make a mistake). After one refusal, players risk never returning to cooperation if neither steps away from tit-for-tat [#343]. Also, tit-for-tat assumes the opponent is trying to maximise its score; paired with a senseless strategy such as RANDOM it «опускается до уровня противника» (sinks to the opponent's level), which is why it cannot be called the best strategy [#345].

Where the results were applied, per the author: from explaining reciprocal altruism in animals to inter-state military strategy, and even resources like BitTorrent, where people exchange files trusting that each will share their own [#343]. The arms race example: the author, citing the Veritasium video, says that the 1950-1968 escalation of nuclear arsenals between the USA and USSR was reduced by voluntarily disarming bombs one per year; each side checked whether the other kept the agreement and took another step toward trust the next year [#343]. The author's view: tit-for-tat still holds first place, including in international politics, while decisions not to retaliate in response to a threat signal are taken by people on the ground at their own risk; the footnote refers to Stanislav Petrov, the Soviet officer who prevented a potential nuclear war after a false alarm of the missile-attack warning system about a US strike on 26 September 1983 [#343].

#### Tit-for-two-tats (око за два ока)
Context: in the author's circle of applied rationalists they played a game similar to the prisoner's dilemma and tried to use a different strategy, which the author renders as «око за два ока» (*an eye for two eyes*, tit-for-two-tats) [#343].
- Rule: when someone refuses to cooperate with you or cheats, allow for error or chance and respond with symmetric damage only after the second consecutive harm [#343]. The Stanford text calls it a forgiving strategy that defects only if the opponent defected twice in a row [#345].
- Why: it repairs tit-for-tat's no-room-for-error flaw [#343].
- Risk the author raises: what if there are many dishonest players and your other cheek gets exploited more often than you would be prepared to allow? [#343]
- Historical twist: in Derek Muller's interview with one of the tournament authors, it emerged that Rapoport bet on tit-for-tat only at Axelrod's request. In Axelrod's view, Rapoport, as a researcher of peaceful conflict resolution, was by nature more forgiving and resistant to provocation, and tit-for-two-tats could have won the first tournament, before chaotic dishonest strategies became more numerous in the second [#343].

#### Axelrod tournament strategy catalogue (Stanford text, machine-translated screenshot)
Context: in 1980 Robert Axelrod, professor of political science at the University of Michigan, held a tournament of strategies for the prisoner's dilemma. He invited well-known game theorists to submit strategies run by computers; the programs played repeatedly against each other and against themselves; each strategy decided whether to cooperate or defect based on the previous moves of itself and its opponent [#345].
1. Always defect: defects on every move. The text says «Это то, что отстаивает теория игр.» (This is what game theory advocates.) It is the safest strategy because it cannot be exploited, but it misses the chance of larger payoffs from cooperating with an opponent willing to cooperate [#345].
2. Always cooperate: does very well against itself, but brings poor results if the opponent decides to defect [#345].
3. Random: «Случайно: стратегия работает в 50% случаев.» (Random: the strategy works in 50% of cases.) [#345]
4. Tit for tat (the winner): described in the protocol above [#345].
5. Tit for two tats: the forgiving variation described above [#345].
6. Two tits for tat: punishes every defection with two defections of its own; the machine translation renders it «ДВА СИСКА ЗА ТО», which the author jokes about separately: «Кому «два сиска за то»?» (Who wants some two tits for tat?) [#345, img #345].

The pre-set strategies (always defect, always cooperate, random) are fixed in advance and so cannot use knowledge of the opponent's previous moves to work out its strategy [#345].

Conclusion of the text: «на самом деле не существует «лучшей» стратегии решения дилеммы заключенного» (in fact there is no best strategy for the prisoner's dilemma). Each strategy does best when matched against the worst one; to win, a player must work out the opponent's strategy and then choose the strategy best suited to the situation [#345].

#### Real dilemma vs pseudo-dilemma test (the red and blue buttons)
The poll: the year before the post it took over Twitter (started by lisatomic5, with a second wave by annoyinghippy) and Russian Afisha wrote an article about it. The conditions: which button would you press if (a) blue guarantees survival for everyone if more than 50% of people choose it, and (b) red gives everyone a chance to survive if the other button gets less than half the votes? No talking is allowed and you cannot learn others' decisions [#450].

The author's method of analysis:
1. Check whether the options balance each other. In a genuine prisoner's dilemma they do: cheating pays most individually, but universal cheating gives everyone only a minimally acceptable result; mutual cooperation pays big, but you cannot know or arrange the other's move, and the more naive player loses to the other [#450].
2. Look for a scenario where the supposedly altruistic option is better. «В «кнопках» же никакой дилеммы нет» (In the buttons there is no dilemma at all): there is no scenario in which choosing blue is more advantageous than red [#450].
3. Walk through each type of player. If a person does not want to die, pressing red is enough. If a person does want to die, they simply will not press red, and it is the blue-pressers who take that choice away from them [#450].
4. Name the pressure. «Эта псевдо-дилемма работает только за счёт давления на эмоции» (This pseudo-dilemma works only through pressure on the emotions), with arguments like «А ВДРУГ ваша бабушка не нажмёт красную?» (What IF your grandma doesn't press red?) or the accusation that you are such a terrible person that you will not try to save everyone, and in the altruistic way at that, as if self-sacrifice were alien to you [#450].
5. Find the actual best joint move. «лучший шанс всех спасти — это просто нажать красную кнопку каждому» (the best chance of saving everyone is simply for everyone to press red), and it works without any need to coordinate [#450].

Takeaway: «Остерегайтесь подделок» (Beware of fakes), and do not rush to conclusions: take a pause and ask whether someone is trying to stop you weighing the pros and cons simply by pressing your sensitive button [#450].

#### Factors that shape cooperation strategy in real life
From the second part of the game-theory series, the author names what will strongly influence strategy choice in reality [#344]:
1. Speed toward prosperity: how quickly, within the long-term perspective, participants want to reach prosperity; the author adds that ideas of fairness may surprise you here [#344].
2. Repeated contact: whether participants will be forced to cross paths after the decisions are made [#344].
3. Scope limit: the most important nuance in the author's opinion is that all of these calculations work only for non-cooperative games, where coalitions and coordination are impossible [#344].

People do not shout at each other or talk rudely in ordinary communication. In a year there she never once heard parents shout at children, owners at dogs, or teachers at pupils [#462].
- Contrast: only there did she realise how rude everyone at home is; she half-fears she and her partner will be arrested for domestic violence when they are merely discussing who has overworked more at home [#462].
- Workplace norm: a speaker describing the local film industry said «Здесь совсем нельзя орать на людей» (Here you absolutely cannot yell at people), about work on a film set, and the phrase stuck with her: «Нельзя. Орать. На людей.» (You cannot. Yell. At people.) [#462]
- Everyday kindness: people constantly compliment your coat, hair, nails, bag; leave a tip and you are told you are «муй амабле» (muy amable, very kind) and praised [#462].
- Counterfactual question: she wonders who she would have become had she not been shouted at and had she sometimes been told as a child that she did well or had something good in her; she guesses people raised that way would be braver, brighter, more talented, calmer, kinder and merrier [#462].
- Framing without blame: she writes without reproach to her parents and surroundings: they did their best, it simply happened that most of us grew up where any adult could yell at you and that was normal. Her closing: «Какие мы молодцы.» (How well we have done.), meaning that we coped even so [#462].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- (!) CONTRADICTION: the author's account says Nash showed that doing the best for yourself while doing better for others makes everyone win more [#343], while the Stanford text says always defect is what game theory advocates [#345]. The source does not reconcile the two.
- (!) UNCLEAR: in #343 the 1980 tournament is credited to Axelrod and Rapoport together, while the Stanford text names Axelrod as organiser who invited theorists to submit strategies, and the interview story implies Rapoport was a submitter [#343, img #345].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the Random strategy is described only as working in 50% of cases, without saying what working means [#345].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: evolutionarily stable strategies and cooperative games are announced but not explained here [#344].
- The arms-race reduction by disarming one bomb per year is the author's paraphrase of the Veritasium video, not independently documented in the source [#343].
- The author's own hedge on tit-for-two-tats: it may be exploited when dishonest players are many [#343].
- The Argentina post is one person's year of observation, not a study [#462].
