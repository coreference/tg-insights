## The double empathy problem: mutual misunderstanding between autistic and neurotypical people

### What this covers

A four-part article series by the channel author, "Аутизм и проблема двойной эмпатии" ("Autism and the double empathy problem"), plus an afterword. It contrasts the long-dominant "mind-blindness" account of autism with Damian Milton's double empathy theory, walks through the experiments on how autistic (РАС) and neurotypical (НТ) people understand each other and form first impressions, and closes with how both sides can build a "two-way road". The author describes the series as her original article, compiled over a month from many sources with her own translation of the theory [#420]. According to the source this matters because the findings "shake the usual foundation" and remove from autistic people the sole responsibility for unsuccessful social contact [#420].

### Core ideas

- Misunderstanding is mutual, not an autistic deficit. Milton's double empathy theory holds that communication breakdowns between neurotypical and autistic people are a two-sided problem of mutual understanding; difficulty in understanding and empathising was found mainly between members of different groups, not within them [#420]. Full detail in *Mind-blindness model vs double empathy problem*.
- Difference itself makes understanding harder. The more different we feel from someone, the harder it is to find common language, form an accurate picture of them and, above all, to be motivated to do so [#425].
- Neurotypical people have blind spots too. Studies show neurotypical people misread autistic people's emotions, expressions and experience; autistic pairs communicate as effectively as neurotypical ones, and quality drops only in mixed groups [#425, #426]. Full detail in *Six research findings*.
- Snap judgements are a major contributor. Neurotypical people form negative first impressions of autistic people within seconds, driven by non-verbal self-presentation, and these impressions are very stable [#432]. Full detail in *Sasson et al. 2017 three-experiment design*.
- The gap can be bridged from both sides through refusing hasty conclusions, developing executive functions, reinterpreting behaviour that only looks like disinterest, and spreading knowledge [#443]. Full detail in *Two-way road*.
- The deeper issue is untested worldviews. In the author's view the double empathy problem highlights a more general failure to test the epistemic status of our beliefs, which closes us to other people's perspective; openness and empathy can be learned starting with kindness to oneself [#444].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Mind-blindness model vs double empathy problem
The older model (mind-blindness). In the mid-1980s the prominent researcher Simon Baron-Cohen claimed that people on the spectrum have "умственной слепотой" ("mind-blindness"), i.e. that what we now know as theory of mind is fundamentally impaired in them. Theory of mind is defined as: "Теория разума — это способность представлять убеждения, эмоции, желания и намерения других людей, а также понимать и предсказывать их поведение." (Theory of mind is the ability to represent other people's beliefs, emotions, desires and intentions, and also to understand and predict their behaviour.) [#420]

Its reach. According to the author, the mind-blindness concept is still taught in many psychology textbooks and is supported by more than 75% of the top scientific articles on theory of mind and autism in Google Scholar. It also guides work in sociology, philosophy, economics, anthropology, robotics and other fields. Through this lens Baron-Cohen and his followers, right up to recent years, saw and explained the "impairments" of autistic people — in empathy, in verbal and non-verbal communication and in social interaction generally — even though the very concept of "autism", its components and its diagnostic criteria have changed enormously over the past 40 years [#420].

The challenge. From the 1990s autistic activists such as Jim Sinclair argued that their ways of communicating mesh poorly with neurotypical ones. In 2012 the autistic researcher Damian Milton presented the essay "Об онтологическом статусе аутизма: проблема двойной эмпатии" ("On the ontological status of autism: the double empathy problem"). In it he proposed that "сбои в общении между НТ- и РАС-людьми — это двусторонняя проблема, с трудностями взаимо_понимания" (breakdowns in communication between NT and autistic people are a two-sided problem, with difficulties of mutual understanding). Milton is described as a British sociologist and social psychologist with academic qualifications in sociology, psychology, philosophy and education, who teaches at the University of Kent, consults for the UK National Autistic Society and advocates for the rights of autistic people, being autistic himself [#420].

What followed. The essay became a turning point for research on autistic communication. Studies in the following years showed that most autistic people fit into groups without problems, communicate effectively, show social reciprocity and empathise sensitively and accurately — with other autistic people. It turned out that understanding and empathising were hard only for members of different groups, on and off the spectrum [#420].

Why it matters. These results remove from autistic people the sole responsibility for failed social contact. They also echo neurodiversity principles: autistic people generally have a different approach to working with information at every stage — gathering, processing, storing and exchanging. Milton stresses that "теория соответствует аутистической феноменологии, полученной из инсайдерских источников" (the theory corresponds to autistic phenomenology obtained from insider sources), i.e. it reflects the personal experience and perception of people on the spectrum [#420].

Status of the evidence. Scientific support is growing but not yet robust, and as of 2024 the leading classifications, ICD and DSM, still follow the old reference points in their criteria. M. Lerner, associate professor of psychology, psychiatry and pediatrics at Stony Brook University in New York, is quoted: "Не все исследователи настроены на это новое направление" (Not all researchers are on board with this new direction) and "Проблема двойной эмпатии с эмпирической точки зрения является более молодой теорией" (From an empirical point of view, the double empathy problem is a younger theory) [#420].

#### Why difference produces mutual misunderstanding (the mechanism)
The author explains the mechanism as follows. Autism affects how its bearers perceive and interact with the world. She lists the questions on which autistic and non-autistic experience differs noticeably, to a greater or lesser degree [#425]:
1. What attention focuses on, and how.
2. What is interesting and what is a waste of effort.
3. How intense something is for the senses.
4. What is important (including what is important to show outwardly) and what is secondary.

The answers to these questions, and the experience accumulated through them, differ between people on and off the spectrum. That difference makes it harder to understand each other and to predict each other at the level of intentions, feelings, experiences and motivations behind actions. Because of this, recent research has shifted from the usual practice of observing autistic people in social situations to studying how autistic people communicate with each other and how neurotypical people perceive them [#425].

#### Six research findings on mutual misunderstanding
The author numbers the findings of this newer research [#425, #426]:

1. Neurotypical "blind spots". Neurotypical people also have "слепые зоны" ("blind spots") regarding autistic people — in recognising their emotional state and their lived experience (studies from 2015–2016). In one study where participants conveyed their lived experience and emotions through animation, neurotypical people had trouble decoding autistic people's states; in another, they had trouble accurately interpreting autistic people's facial expressions [#425].
2. "Broken telephone" information transfer (2020). A study of information passing, modelled on the game "Испорченный телефон" ("broken telephone"), showed that autistic people communicate with one another as effectively as neurotypical people do with one another, and that the quality of transmission and understanding falls only in mixed groups [#425].
3. Rapport in pairs — Crompton and colleagues (2020). Catherine Crompton and colleagues set out to stress-test the double empathy problem: if the issue is the difference between neuro-groups, contact within a group should be better. Part 1: participants in three groups (NT, autistic, mixed) rated their personal impressions of talking in pairs. Part 2: outside observers from matching groups rated the quality of the first participants' contact from video recordings. Results: self-reported mutual understanding was highest in NT pairs; in autistic pairs it was somewhat lower but still high and clearly higher than in mixed pairs. From the outside, contact quality in mixed pairs also looked the weakest, while autistic pairs gave the impression of more understanding than NT pairs. The author adds a playful question: to what extent is neurotypical people's feeling of being understood linked to their urge to look for hidden meaning between the lines, unlike autistic people — "a topic for further research" [#425].
4. Autistic conversation patterns — Heasman and a colleague (2019). Observing 30 autistic people communicating during cooperative video games, B. Heasman and a colleague noted distinctive features. "Typical" ping-pong exchange, with regular alternation of turns, happened only when players needed to coordinate actions in the game. The rest of the time, according to the authors, periods of contact and disengagement alternated fairly sharply: active discussion gave way to long silence, or one person dominated the conversation — and participants experienced this as natural and easy. Autistic people also proceeded from a "презумпции общности" ("presumption of commonality"): if nothing contradicted it, they established rapport with each other immediately. Conversely, when they ran into misunderstanding, responses could become rough (the authors recorded "потенциально деструктивные для общения высказывания" — *utterances potentially destructive to communication* — a judgement the channel author notes was made by authors who, as far as she knows, are neurotypical), and people tried to readjust. Lesson: autistic-to-autistic communication has its own pattern that would likely feel unfamiliar to neurotypical people [#426].
5. Five-minute unstructured conversations (2019). Strangers held a free, unstructured 5-minute conversation and then shared impressions of their partner and rated communication quality. Pairs were homogeneous (22 neurodivergent pairs and 23 neurotypical pairs) or mixed (25 pairs). Participants in all groups rated partners who turned out to be off the spectrum as warmer and more socially skilled. Yet autistic participants preferred interacting with other autistic adults and reported telling them more about themselves than they told neurotypical partners. Neurotypical participants likewise reported wanting to talk more with people of their own group [#426].
6. Autistic people's own reports of comfort. Autistic people report feeling more comfortable with others on the spectrum: (a) in interviews, 12 participants said they observe more ease and understanding within the spectrum; (b) a study of 12 adolescents (6 on the spectrum and 6 not) showed that they prefer to communicate with a group of their own neurotype and gender when it comes not to requests but to sharing thoughts and experience [#426].

Author's conclusion from the six findings. It appears that autistic people not only have no problem with empathy (as known from a large 2019 meta-analysis discussed in the channel's earlier "myth 2" post), but show especially pronounced sympathy and understanding towards similar people. This mirrors how neurotypical people respond with empathy to people like themselves, but less, and far from always, to those who differ — something earlier research on empathy for in-group vs out-group members had already shown [#426].

#### Sasson et al. 2017 three-experiment design on first impressions of autistic people
Part 3 of the series is titled "Спектр и предубеждение" ("Spectrum and prejudice"). Its thesis: quick emotional assessments that neurotypical people form during superficial contact with autistic people appear to contribute noticeably to the difficulty of contact [#432].

Headline finding. A large 2017 study (Nature Scientific Reports link) showed that people off the spectrum consistently form a negative first impression of autistic people's character traits — even though neuro-status was not discussed in the study at all. The impression forms within a few seconds and is extremely stable: it does not change as interaction continues, persists through repeated interactions interleaved with non-autistic people, and appears in both child and adult age groups, even when neurotypical raters did not know who had a diagnosis. In control groups of neurotypical people this did not happen. The article described three independent experiments with real-world social interaction [#432]:

1. Experiment 1. 214 people rated autistic people as less socially competent, less likeable and more submissive, and expressed less desire to talk and hang out with them. Ratings of intelligence and trustworthiness, and willingness to sit next to them or live nearby, were on par with neurotypical participants. The cause turned out to be the style of outward social presentation, including in static pictures: the effect appeared with audio and visual information, but not when raters read transcripts of speech [#432].
2. Experiment 2. 12 autistic and 16 neurotypical people of student age were recorded talking with an experimenter on an everyday topic ("Видел(а) ли ты какие-нибудь интересные фильмы за последнее время?" — *Have you seen any interesting films lately?*). From randomly chosen first-person photos (from the experimenter's viewpoint), 37 participants used a slider between extremes to rate how socially awkward the person was, how approachable, and how open they themselves would be to friendship. Neurotypical raters judged autistic people as less approachable and more awkward than controls and indicated a lower likelihood of friendship with members of the autistic group [#432].
3. Experiment 3. Participants were autistic school-age children — an age when close attention is often paid to developing social skills. Nonetheless they received low ratings from both adults and peers [#432].

Combined conclusion. The results of the three independent studies were strikingly consistent despite different samples and methods. Together they are evidence that autistic people's social self-presentation, especially non-verbal — facial expression, body posture and prosody (the pattern of pronouncing words: strength, pitch, breathiness; the source closes this list with "и др.") — is perceived less favourably and goes together with neurotypical people's unwillingness to continue social interaction [#432].

Limitation and next step. Showing images, even repeatedly, is not equivalent to deeper acquaintance, so the study authors proposed testing in future how impressions change over prolonged interaction [#432].

#### Rose vs cactus model of neurotypical and autistic friendship
According to some self-reports (the "Learn From Autistics" piece on autism and friendship), it is precisely deeper acquaintance and getting to know each other better that helps neurotypical and autistic people find common ground and warm to each other. This happens despite a difference in friendship styles described in an "Articulate Autistic" article: neurotypical friendship is more like "на розу, которой нужен регулярный уход и внимание" (a rose that needs regular care and attention), while autistic friendship is more like "на кактус, который доволен подпиткой время от времени и сохраняет свой цвет независимо от условий внешней среды" (a cactus that is content with occasional feeding and keeps its colour regardless of the external environment) [#432].

Why it matters. In experiment 2, autistic participants reported a much stronger feeling of loneliness than the control group. This agrees with earlier results showing autistic people did not differ from neurotypical peers in their desire for relationships, and suggests that their need for social experience and closeness is deeply unmet [#432].

#### Two-way road: how to bridge the gap
The final part is titled "Как создать дорогу с двусторонним движением" ("How to build a two-way road") [#443].

Starting diagnosis. Contact between people on and off the spectrum appears to be exactly the case where "непонятное сочетается с непредсказуемым, это бывает дискомфортным" (the incomprehensible combines with the unpredictable, and that can be uncomfortable). With any neural architecture, even once we are sure that people who differ from us pose no harm, sometimes only a special interest — research-driven, trauma-driven or other — makes us voluntarily go deeper into such contact [#443].

Components of bridging, as the source lays them out:
1. Empathy beyond first impressions. Fortunately this is not the limit: as human beings we are capable of empathy even towards those who seem unlike us at first impression, by refusing hasty conclusions [#443].
2. Developing executive functions. The same executive functions that help us make weighed decisions and function in other areas of life support this. The author notes the irony that the need to develop executive functions is something neurotypical and autistic people share, only in different contexts [#443].
3. An evolutionary framing. Empathy is presumed to have evolved as a separate superstructure whose main function is to foster cooperation and the protection of the weak and suffering; the author suggests we may now be at the stage of evolution where it is time to take the next step [#443].
4. Changing training programmes. Researchers are already acting: changes are being made to the training curricula of the National Autistic Society, to the ATLASS training run by Studio3, and to the Synergy programme developed by AT-Autism. Researchers have also set the task of further evaluating these programmes from the perspectives of both autistic people and practitioners [#443].
5. Distinguishing real disinterest from apparent disinterest. Jaswal and Akhtar (2018) emphasise the difference between social disinterest and what merely looks like it to neurotypical people. Example 1: autistic people say that avoiding eye contact helps them avoid overload from part of the incoming information and concentrate during a conversation. Example 2: pointing in children has two functions, "императивную («дай») и декларативную («смотри»)" (*imperative ("give") and declarative ("look")*). It is commonly believed that autistic children do not use the gesture because they are not interested in drawing others' attention to their discoveries. However, in autistic children the gesture is absent altogether — even in the "give" sense — so it is simply not an active part of their "vocabulary", rather than a sign of disinterest [#443].
6. Learning and telling others more. On our side, regardless of which side of the spectrum each of us is on, we can learn and talk more about all of this [#443].

Outcome the author expects. Equally two-way efforts to narrow the gap in perceiving the world have a very good chance of making the "трудности перевода" (lost in translation / difficulties of translation) merely a "вопросом времени… которое мы уделяем друг другу" (matter of time… that we give to each other) [#443].

#### Testing the epistemic status of one's worldview and learning empathy through self-kindness
In the afterword the author argues the following chain [#444]:
1. Interaction is by definition two-sided. Social interaction involves more than one person, so communication difficulties can arise not only from one person's behaviour but also from the perception, judgements and social decisions made by the people on the other side.
2. The general problem behind double empathy. In the author's own view, the double empathy problem highlights a more general problem: we stop stress-testing the epistemic status of our worldview — what we believe and, crucially, why: on what basis and by what we are guided. This is what closes us to new information and to the possibility of seeing a situation through another person's eyes and truly understanding them.
3. Why the test feels frightening. Such testing can be scary, but only at first — while there is little experience of colliding with real and diverse life and while inner supports are weak, which can make it seem that "другой» значит «опасный" ("other" means "dangerous").
4. The cost of not testing. "Можно быть уверенным в одном и том же от младенчества до последних дней и так и не понять, что, на самом деле, мы никогда не уходили далеко от дома и не знаем, как устроен мир за его пределами." (One can be sure of the same thing from infancy to one's last days and never understand that, in fact, we never went far from home and do not know how the world beyond it works.)
5. The practice. Hence the importance of talking with living people instead of with matrices created long ago in one's head, switching on openness to many-sided experience and readiness to empathise. This can be learned, starting with kindness to oneself — the author links Kristin Neff's book on self-compassion here (see *Tools and resources mentioned*).

### Detailed insights

#### Value of the theory despite its weaknesses
The author acknowledges many open questions about the theory of double — "perhaps even two-way" — empathy: different studies include slightly different aspects in it, and the number of studies on the topic is very small. Even so, the theory has already prompted questions that let us move "в сторону от слепой стигматизации — той, где есть безупречные и неправильные" (away from blind stigmatisation — the kind where there are the flawless and the wrong), which she sees as infinitely valuable [#443].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The author herself states that scientific support for the theory is growing but not yet robust, that ICD and DSM criteria (as of 2024) still follow the old model, that different studies include slightly different aspects in the theory, and that there are only a handful of studies [#420, #443].
- In the Crompton study, self-reported understanding was highest in NT pairs, while outside observers saw more understanding in autistic pairs than in NT pairs; the author offers only a tongue-in-cheek hypothesis (NT people's search for hidden meaning between the lines) and labels it a topic for further research [#425].
- In the 2019 conversation study, all groups rated non-autistic partners as warmer and more socially skilled, even though autistic participants preferred and disclosed more to autistic partners [#426].
- The characterisation of rough responses in autistic conversations as "potentially destructive" is flagged by the author as the view of authors who, to her knowledge, are neurotypical [#426].
- The first-impressions study used images and short recordings; its authors note this is not equivalent to deeper acquaintance and call for testing over prolonged interaction [#432].
- The evolutionary account of empathy is marked by the author as presumptive ("Предположительно", *presumably*) [#443].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the 2020 rapport study lead is spelled "Кэтрин Комптон" in #425 and "Кэтрин Кромптон" in #432; the linked Frontiers paper and the podcast picture ("Dr Catherine") suggest the same person [#425, #432].

## Autism and employment: strengths, barriers and workplace inclusion

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's material on autistic people at work. It covers a three-part guest series by Maria Shut ("Профессиональный спектр", "Professional spectrum") on career-choice and teamwork myths and on the conditions and accommodations that help. It also covers the channel author's four-part series, written after a lecture, on the UK Buckland Review of Autism Employment: its statistics, the benefits of hiring autistic people, the barriers it found and its recommendations. A recommended article on autistic and neurotypical colleagues and a post on research into remote work complete the topic. The material is explicitly about people at support level 1 (a note in Maria's series begins "*Описанное ниже касается людей*" (what is described below concerns people) and specifies support level 1 of ASD) [#379, #544].

Why it matters according to the source: stereotypes held by employers limit where autistic people end up working. Autistic people work much less often than others and earn less, even though they want to work. When they are well placed they can be much more productive than their colleagues. The author argues that the picture is often painted as more hopeless than it is: "*там, где и так бывает ~~хреново~~ непросто, не нужно накидывать лишней безнадёги*" (where things are already hard, there is no need to pile on extra hopelessness) [#544, #545].

### Core ideas

- Career-choice stereotypes are self-reinforcing. Employers offer autistic people technical roles, which keeps them in technical fields. In reality, many choose helping professions when they follow their interests. See *The stereotype loop* [#379].
- "Autistic people are not team players or leaders" is a myth. Neurodivergent people can strengthen teams through original thinking and honesty, and there are autistic leaders [#383].
- Conditions matter more than the field. Job satisfaction, adaptation and professional growth depend less on the field of work than on its conditions. See *Four workplace conditions* and *Six overload-protection accommodations* [#386].
- Work itself can be developmental. Many autistic people use work to build social skills and lower anxiety, and to "become truly themselves" in fields that interest them [#386].
- Typical autistic cognitive traits are workplace advantages. The author names successive perception with strong detail-noticing, and monotropism/hyperfixation. The employer's task is to protect these traits through a comfortable sensory and communication environment [#544].
- There is a large, costly employment gap. The Buckland Review documents low employment, lower pay and poor graduate outcomes, alongside large productivity gains for well-placed autistic employees [#545].
- The barriers are systemic. They cover career preparation, hiring and the workplace. The burden of identifying and arguing for accommodations falls on the worker, which drives non-disclosure and masking [#549].
- Fixes should be multi-agency, gradual and universally beneficial. "Comfort is not exclusive": accommodations designed to help everyone also let autistic people choose whether to disclose [#550].
- Remote and flexible work can improve well-being and productivity. Research the author cites from Poland and Turkey supports this [#675].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### The stereotype loop of technical job offers
Purpose: explains why autistic people are seen as fit only for technical work. The stereotype says autistic people are tech specialists, scientists, nuclear physicists, mathematicians or IT workers. In other words, "*путь человека на спектре — работа по чётким алгоритмам, с цифрами и данными, без контактов с людьми*" (the path of a person on the spectrum is work by clear algorithms, with numbers and data, without contact with people). According to Maria Shut, employers themselves contribute "no small part" to this stereotype. Diana Mirakaj-Finnerty, former head of Specialisterne (a company focused on employing neurodivergent people), observes that people on the spectrum are often offered analytics or engineering positions. The resulting "vicious circle" ("*замкнутый круг*"):
1. Employers rely on the stereotype and offer people with ASD technical/IT vacancies.
2. Autistic people end up in that field, and much less often in others.
3. The idea that their work options are narrow gets stronger, which feeds back into step 1.

Counter-evidence: a 2021 study of 92 people on the spectrum, cited by Maria, found that when following their own interests, 39% of autistic students choose helping professions: psychologist, teacher, kindergarten teacher/educator, social worker. A Reddit autism-thread comment shows how mismatched standard career tests can be: "*Результаты моих карьерных тестов всегда были одинаковы: мне советовали стать фокусником, комиком или клоуном*" (My career-test results were always the same: I was advised to become a magician, a comedian or a clown) [#379].

#### Four workplace conditions that help autistic professionals (2014 study)
Purpose: what to build into a job so an autistic specialist adapts and thrives. Maria cites a 2014 study of 130 people with high-functioning autism. It found that job satisfaction, workplace adaptation and successful professional development depend "not so much on the field of work as on its conditions". The suitable conditions are:
1. Measurable and visible results ("*Измеримость и видимость результата*"). When this condition is met, the specialist can say "*Я выполнил(а) задачу и выполнил(а) её хорошо*" (I completed the task and completed it well). They can also hear this from their manager and/or team. Why: it reinforces motivation and enjoyment of the work [#386].
2. Flexible work format ("*Гибкость формата работы*"). For people on the spectrum it is often very valuable when *what* they create matters more than *how* they create it. Being able to choose a suitable path and tools for a task gives room for manoeuvre and variation. This adds both to motivation and to productivity. Example: if an autistic specialist is more productive in the early hours, it may be better for the company not to force them to sit out the standard hours of the staff schedule [#386].
3. Transparent communication ("*Прозрачную коммуникацию*"). Autistic people find it hard to deal with communication full of "politics" and internal intrigue, subtext and unwritten conditions about what may or may not be said, to whom and in which circumstances. Open dialogue and clear instructions shared by the whole team "will always be valued". Case: an IT architect and project manager says the team knows the diagnosis and, "of course, they are all IT nerds", so it is easier inside the team. The trouble is with managing external factors. When he says he needs something from another team by date X and they answer "OK", that does not mean they will actually do it, so he has to keep following up: "*Ненавижу это, ведь мы договорились!*" (I hate that, because we agreed!). His solution: he now delegates this part to a team member. Lesson: implicit, non-literal agreements are a specific pain point, and delegating that interface is one workable adaptation [#386].
4. Protection from overload ("*Подстраховку от перегрузок*"). Spelled out as the six accommodations in the next framework [#386].

#### Six overload-protection accommodations named by autistic employees
Purpose: concrete, mostly low-cost options that autistic employees themselves report as helping. According to employees on the spectrum, in a study Maria links, these options improve the process and the result of work and add to well-being at work:
1. Adjusting the lighting (relevant because bright light can be a sensory load).
2. Bringing needed toys to the workplace.
3. Wearing headphones.
4. Breaking large tasks into small ones.
5. Working from home. Two-thirds (⅔) of respondents choose this.
6. Taking part in corporate gatherings *voluntarily* (shared lunches and outings). The emphasis on "voluntarily" is in the original.
[#386]

#### Five benefits of hiring autistic people (Buckland Review)
Purpose: the business case, which the author considers valid regardless of country. As the author relays it from the Buckland Review, researchers identified these specific advantages:
1. Mental skills and cognitive strengths. Autistic people statistically often excel at: (a) recognising regularities and patterns; (b) sustained attention to detail; (c) analytical thinking; (d) reasoning by the laws of logic. These are needed in many fields linked to data analysis, software testing, design/engineering ("проектирование") and finance.
2. Values towards the workplace and team. Autistic employees tend to show high dedication and loyalty when hired into a suitable position that takes their characteristics into account.
3. Benefits for the team. Inclusive hiring methods lead to: (a) better problem-solving in the team; (b) more empathy and adaptability in the workplace; (c) better team culture when neurodiverse perspectives are included.
4. Talent pool. Many autistic people want to work and have above-average qualifications, especially in STEM fields. Employers who ignore this group miss a high-potential source of talent.
5. Innovation and perspectives. A neurodiverse view brings unique approaches to problem-solving. It often uncovers problems or solutions that neurotypical teams overlook.
The studies cited in the review, including one from Harvard Business Review, show that autistic employees with the right support, in roles that match their strengths, achieve 45–140% higher productivity than their neurotypical colleagues [#545].

#### Barriers map across career preparation, hiring and the workplace (Buckland Review)
Purpose: where along the path autistic people get stuck, as the author relays the review's findings ("What did the British find?").
A. Preparing for future work
1. From an early age, people with ASD have less time and fewer connections than their peers for finding first work experience.
2. Career advisers are often not well enough prepared to support them.

B. Hiring
3. They have to work through generic job descriptions and ambiguous interview questions.
4. They often face recruiters' emphasis on social rather than professional skills.
5. Because of points 3 and 4, they have much more negative experiences of interviews, group tasks and psychometric tests.

C. In the workplace
6. Many do not get the support they need, not even simple accommodations that would let them do their role in demanding sensory and social environments.
7. Employers and managers often report poor knowledge of autism and feel under-prepared to identify and put such accommodations in place.
8. As a result, access to accommodations still varies a lot, and the responsibility for identifying them and arguing for them usually lies with the worker.

D. Additionally
9. Many autistic adults do not know their legal rights regarding requests for working conditions.
10. About a third felt they could not discuss their needs for such conditions at all. Of those who did request changes, more than a quarter were refused. However, of those whose requests were granted, only about 10% reported poor implementation.

E. Consequences
11. In practice only about 35% of autistic employees are fully open about their autism, and 1 in 10 does not disclose it at work.
12. Of those who do disclose, they do so after starting the job, out of fear of discrimination during hiring.
13. Many feel they must hide their autistic traits to succeed.
[#549]

#### Three transferable approaches of the Buckland Review
Purpose: Sir Buckland proposed 19 points to improve employment outcomes for autistic people, aimed at employers, government departments, educational institutions, foundations and support services. The author judges that the specific initiatives are tied to UK realities and not worth discussing in detail. However, she says these approaches are worth adopting when implementing such initiatives in any country:
1. Changes are carried out by a multidisciplinary task force. The review calls for joint efforts of all the bodies listed above. Being systemic and covering many sides is important.
2. Changing everything everywhere at once will not work. So changes are proposed in waves, starting with individual improvements. Research is lacking on some aspects, so the initiators plan to evaluate the effects and adjust the programme "as the ripples spread" ("*по мере «расхождения кругов»*").
3. "Comfort is not exclusive" ("*Комфорт не инклюзивен*"). The proposed changes are designed to also benefit people with other neural profiles, including those with particular sensory features and even neurotypical fatigue. That way things improve for more people at once, not only the target group. Such a design also leaves the person with particular needs free to choose "*«выходить ли из шкафа» и когда*" ("whether to come out of the closet" and when), i.e. whether and when to disclose.
[#550]

#### Five directions of the Buckland initiatives
The initiatives cover five directions:
1. Proposals for raising awareness and reducing stigma. This also includes strategies for unlocking the "very super-productivity" of employees with ASD.
2. Ways to prepare autistic people effectively for starting a career and for returning after a break.
3. Correcting recruitment practices to account for the specifics of autistic candidates.
4. Ways for employers to support autistic people on their staff.
5. Strategies to encourage autistic employees' development and career growth.
[#550]

#### Seven example initiatives of the Buckland Review
The author gives these as examples of concrete initiatives:
1. Encouraging employers to register in the Autistica Neurodiversity Employers Index. This widens the pool of opportunities for job seekers and also gives employers access to recommendations on inclusive hiring practices.
2. Developing the Access to Work scheme. Among other things, employees can get a grant to pay for communication support at interviews, as well as help with managing mental health and with practical work matters.
3. Developing training packages for management and career advisers on supporting autistic employees. This includes developing an online support service: accessible platforms with best practices for recruiting and supporting employees with ASD.
4. Creating design guidelines for autistic people, so that workplaces can be adapted easily and flexibly.
5. Working with software suppliers to develop systems that meet the needs of autistic people.
6. Developing the Disability Confident programme to inform employers not only about the limitations but also about the capabilities of people on the spectrum. The author adds: "*без младенцев с водой и пушки по воробьям*" (without throwing the baby out with the bathwater or using a cannon on sparrows), meaning in a balanced, proportionate way.
7. Supporting self-employment. This means providing resources and guidance to autistic people who are self-employed or work in social entrepreneurship, which by design is also funded from external sources.
After publication, the review gathered first responses during the summer from employers and companies, autistic people, autism charities, special support groups and other stakeholders. The author's stance: "*Посмотрим, что получится*" (Let's see what comes of it) [#550].

### Detailed insights

#### Teamwork and leadership myths
- Myth 1: an autistic person is not a team player. Employers may consider hiring an autistic person risky. They assume the person will not take full part in meetings or keep up team spirit outside the office, and that this will affect work processes. Counter-view: Ludmila Praslova, professor of graduate industrial-organisational psychology programmes at Vanguard (Southern California), believes neurodivergent people are more likely to bring original thinking and honesty. In her view they help the team think in a more detailed, objective and creative way, which strengthens competitive advantage [#383].
- Myth 2: autistic people can follow instructions well but cannot lead a team. Maria says this myth is fed by beliefs that autism deprives people of empathy, of openness in communication and of effective communication skills. Counter-examples Maria gives: Elon Musk, "founder, CEO and chief engineer" of SpaceX and other companies, and Valerie Paradiz. Paradiz is a member of the national council of Autism Speaks and director of the consulting company Valerie Paradiz, LLC. Her company develops curricula, provides technical-support services and does strategic planning for educational institutions, corporations and agencies that support people on the spectrum [#383].
- The "lone genius" image. Maria opens the series with the stereotype of the autistic professional as a lone genius or mad professor: dishevelled hair, piles of papers on and under the desk, lips whispering formulas, "breakfast" at 17:00 between a stack of books and a laptop, wilting flowers, a dog learning to fill its own bowl. Alan Turing, Michael Burry, Elon Musk and Sheldon Cooper are named as the typical images. She calls this "just one of the stereotypes" that she and Rita found [#379].

#### Adaptivity: the cost and the opportunity of adapting to work
- Adapting takes effort even in a good job. Even after finding a suitable place for professional self-realisation (matching individual interests, giving moral satisfaction and income, without discrimination), a person with ASD has to make an effort to adapt. Changes to familiar routine, new people, tasks and routes, and the need to learn the rules can all raise anxiety on their own. On top of this come individual features: some find sensory load hard (bright light or the hum of voices), others find phone calls hard [#386].
- Many see work as a chance to grow. Many autistic specialists and people on the spectrum see work as an opportunity to develop skills useful for work and life in general. Maria lists: spontaneous communication, understanding the other person, lower anxiety about uncertainty, emotional self-regulation, learning new fields and practising new skills. First-person examples she quotes:
  - A hospital secretary: a very social job with many phone calls and meetings. "*это хорошая практика; это помогло мне избавиться от беспокойства по поводу телефона*" (it's good practice; it helped me get rid of my anxiety about the phone).
  - A hairdresser who deals with people all day: at first it was "terrible", he could not talk to people and everyone thought he was strange and that the job was not for him. "*После 7 лет такой рутины я супер общительный*" (After 7 years of this routine I'm super sociable). He is friends with everyone and gets on great with people.
  [#386]
- Work helps people "become truly themselves". Employment helps autistic people "*стать по-настоящему собой*" (become truly themselves) by developing in fields that genuinely interest them. Example, a social-media marketer: "*Нестандартное аутичное мышление полезно и для маркетинговых трюков, и для идей контента!*" (Non-standard autistic thinking is useful both for marketing tricks and for content ideas!) [#386].

#### The lecture debate: "Why does neurotypical society need this?"
- The author attended a lecture at a club (the second meeting there on this subject, so basics had already been covered). It included a mini-lecture with statistics on how much and how people on the spectrum can work and what they need to adapt at work. What the author found most important was the discussion: "*Зачем оно надо [нейротипичному/НТ] обществу?*" (Why does [neurotypical/NT] society need it?). Participants included people from education, caring citizens and various neurodivergent people. The discussion was mostly about personal experience [#544].
- The lecturer's position (attributed). Few people on the spectrum can hold a job, few have well-developed advantages over neurotypical people, and special conditions should be created "*хотя бы если мы просто не хотим истерик на рабочем месте*" (at least if we simply don't want hysterics/tantrums at the workplace). The author interprets "hysterics" as "breakdowns" ("срывов"), without negative connotation, because the lecturer is an advocate for people on the spectrum. She notes she does not consider herself entitled to distort someone else's words [#544].
- The author's counter-position. She found that picture both incomplete and emotionally depressing. Even taking only some of the pronounced thinking and focus traits she most often meets in people with ASD, she argues these already give a huge advantage at work:
  1. Successive perception ("*сукцессивность*", sequential perception) together with a strong ability to notice details.
  2. Immersion in a task for several hours without distraction, and digging into an interesting topic for weeks ("*монотропизм и гиперфиксация*", monotropism and hyperfixation).
  The task in work processes can then be to maintain a comfortable environment where nothing gets in the way of these traits, above all in terms of sensory conditions and communication formats. She stays open to correction: "*Я готова признавать ошибки там, где недостаточно знаю*" (I'm ready to admit mistakes where I don't know enough). That is why she went to test all the opinions by gathering more data, which led to the Buckland series [#544].

#### Maximal relevance and colleague strategies
The author recommends an article by Manya Borzenko, an autism researcher who is herself autistic and whose comments are valued by various media (from "Labirint" to "Reminder"). The article offers practical, research-based strategies for productive cooperation between autistic and neurotypical people, including why companies need this and the advantages of this neural organisation. The author credits the article with the term "*максимальная релевантность*" (maximal relevance). She had not met the term before and finds that it names the drive to speak clearly, briefly and to the point [#552].


#### Remote work and the post-pandemic workplace
- The 5/2 office was not a point of balance for many. According to the author, the pandemic showed this. Having lived the alternatives, a noticeable share of people did not want to go back. She lists those alternatives: a workplace cosy for oneself, clothes comfortable for the body regardless of weather, transport or a strict dress code, the chance to rest and take a walk during the day, and choosing whom to meet in person and whom to deal with without a camera, only in chat [#675].
- What many people need now. Later experience confirmed this, including restructured work formats after COVID and the emigration of Russian residents in 2022–2025. For many, a particular combination matters, different from what was usual before the pandemic: live contact with colleagues, involvement in the company's fate, and freedom to organise the conditions of their own work [#675].
- Modern work as a mosaic. The author concludes "*современная работа — это новая мозаика*" (modern work is a new mosaic). It would be good to understand how to fit the pieces that are within one's own control into the overall picture [#675].

### Evidence and numbers

#### Buckland Review: key data (UK)
As the author relays it [#545]:
- As of 2021, about 1 million people in Britain are on the spectrum (about 1 in 70). In 2022–23, about 680,000 reported ASD as their main or secondary long-term condition.
- Only about 30% of working-age autistic people are employed, despite wanting to work. The comparison is 50% of disabled people and 80% of non-disabled people. Autistic people's pay is on average a third lower than that of the latter.
- Autistic students are twice as likely as non-disabled peers to be unemployed 15 months after graduating, and only 36% find permanent work in that period. They are more often overqualified for their jobs, more often get contracts that require no work experience, and are least likely to get a permanent position.
- In a number of roles, autistic employees showed much larger productivity gains than their neurotypical colleagues: 45–145%.
- Raising autistic employment could bring substantial economic benefits. For example, doubling the employment rate by 2030 is projected to bring £0.9–1.5 billion in social benefits per year.
- Background on the review (from the post's footnote): a comprehensive report led by Sir Robert Buckland and supported by Autistica, a large British charity that funds and campaigns for research into autism and related conditions. The author considers the reasons for hiring autistic people valid regardless of country and useful for building processes more smoothly, "*не наступая даже на чужие грабли*" (without stepping even on other people's rakes, i.e. learning from others' mistakes).

#### Other studies cited
- 2021 study of 92 people on the spectrum: 39% of autistic students choose helping professions when following their interests (details in *The stereotype loop*) [#379].
- 2014 study of 130 people with high-functioning autism: conditions matter more than the field (details in *Four workplace conditions*) [#386].
- Poland, 2021–2022, long-term study at one company. About 500 employees, roughly half on-site and half remote. According to regular surveys, the remote workers had better relationships with colleagues and better psychological well-being in terms of: (a) emotional load from management; (b) managing emotions ("which they did not need to hide"); (c) role conflicts and trust in colleagues. Over time they also had higher psychological well-being, especially regarding psychological strain, depression and burnout [#675].
- Turkey, 2019–2023, a large call centre moved 3,500 employees to fully remote work. Results:
  - productivity (calls per hour) rose by 10%, because work calls were shorter without office background noise;
  - customer satisfaction and audit ratings rose;
  - those onboarded in person (meeting people and adapting to the job on site) and then moved to remote kept higher long-term productivity and stayed in the role longer;
  - side benefits: a higher share of women, including married women, and of residents of rural areas and small towns;
  - the share of university graduates rose by 14%, because the company could reach groups with traditionally low labour-force participation.
  [#675]

### Tools and resources mentioned

- Specialisterne: a company focused on employing neurodivergent people. It is mentioned through its ex-head's observation about the technical roles autistic people are offered [#379].
- Buckland Review of Autism Employment: report and recommendations: published on the UK government website (gov.uk). The author treats it as the main data source for the series and judges that its reasons for hiring and its approaches are useful in any country [#545, #550].
- Autistica: the UK charity that supported the review; it also runs the Autistica Neurodiversity Employers Index, which gives employers recommendations on inclusive hiring [#545, #550].
- Access to Work (UK): a scheme with grants for communication support at interviews and help with mental health and practical work matters [#550].
- Disability Confident (UK): a campaign/programme for informing employers about both the limitations and the capabilities of people on the spectrum [#550].
- Harvard Business Review, "Neurodiversity as a competitive advantage" (2017): cited within the review as evidence for the 45–140% productivity figure [#545].
- Fast Company article by Ludmila Praslova ("neurodivergent people make great leaders, not just employees"): the source of her view on neurodivergent people in teams [#383].
- Manya Borzenko's article in Reminder on recognising and working with autistic colleagues: recommended by the author as practical, research-based strategies for autistic–neurotypical collaboration [#552].
- Lena Movshina's group (mixed post): the author recommends a group by her colleague Lena Movshina, who ran a survey to help people with autism and ADHD at work and writes about education and careers "when everything is complicated". The group's stated content: trying out and implementing things that make work finally "work" (for oneself and for the system), spending time with people with similar values and difficulties in a setting where neuroinclusion is the norm, and discussing the world of work based on texts about current work trends. Its topics include portfolio careers and invisible labour: glue work, on which the whole project rests, and the "second shift" at home for women. The author states she is not paid for the recommendation; schedule and pricing details are omitted as logistics [#675].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- Scope limit. Maria's series is explicitly about support level 1: "*Ваш опыт и мнение могут отличаться*" (Your experience and opinion may differ). The lecture recap is also about level 1 [#379, #544].
- (!) CONTRADICTION: Buckland Review publication date. Part 2 says it was published on the UK government site on 28 February 2024. Part 4 says "*Обзор был опубликовал 2 апреля 2023 года (World Autism Awareness Day)*" (The review was published on 2 April 2023, World Autism Awareness Day) [#545, #550].
- (!) CONTRADICTION: productivity range. The same post gives 45–145% in the key data and 45–140% when citing the studies including Harvard Business Review [#545].
- (!) UNCLEAR: Buckland's description. The footnote calls Sir Robert Buckland "*члена парламента Канзаса*" (member of parliament of Kansas). This is reproduced as stated [#545].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the 19 recommendations. The author says Buckland proposed 19 points and announces "19 recommendations — for employers and for the government". She deliberately does not list all 19 because they are tied to UK realities. Only the three approaches, the five directions and seven example initiatives are given [#545, #550].
- Disagreement on prognosis. The lecturer held that few autistic people can keep a job or have real advantages. The channel author disagrees, based on her own experience and the Buckland data, while saying she is ready to admit mistakes where she knows too little [#544].
- Hedged evidence. The leadership counter-examples (Elon Musk, Valerie Paradiz) are anecdotal. Praslova's view is presented as her opinion ("*считает*", believes) [#383].
- Remote-work research is not autism-specific. The Polish and Turkish studies concern general employee populations. The author connects them to the difficulties of autistic people at work but does not present autism-specific remote-work data. The Turkish data also suggest an in-person onboarding phase benefits long-term outcomes [#675].
- Open question posed to readers. The author asks what could substantially improve quality of life "*в сфере работы на каждом её этапе*" (in the sphere of work at each of its stages), for a close one with ASD or for oneself [#550].
