# Scientific thinking, philosophy and reading lists

## What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's posts on how to think about science itself: the author's personal criteria for which sciences are "good" (they keep updating, they integrate with neighbouring fields, they stay scientific and open), an open-science ecosystem diagram she endorses, her answer to whether philosophy is a joke or a useful discipline, and a forwarded physicist's analysis of the failed LK-99 superconductor claim as a lesson in reproducibility. It also covers reading: the ten books that formed the author's worldview, books on economics and on humanity's future that she sought as a dialectical counterweight to optimism, and free portals for finding leading scientists and reading research. According to the source, this matters because the way we build our values depends partly on education and on the information we take from the wider world, so the quality, openness and self-correction of the sciences — and our own willingness to test our worldview — directly shape how we see the world [msg #109, #494].

## Core ideas

- **A good science is defined by approach more than subject.** The author judges the sciences "of the bright present and beautiful future" by whether they are updatable (Bayesian refreshing of concepts on new data) and integrated (open to neighbouring fields), while keeping the basic criteria of scientificity. Full detail under *Criteria of a "good" science* [msg #109].
- **Openness produces better science.** An open-science ecosystem, given the right framework and ecosystem conditions, yields collaborative outputs and outcomes including higher quality of science and democratised knowledge — see *Open Science co-creation ecosystem model* [msg #109, #110].
- **Philosophy is not a science but a separate branch of knowing the world**, whose main "why" is offering ways to think about life, the universe and everything; in the author's opinion it still owns the initial creation of worldview concepts and morality/ethics, and is for now "the cradle of knowledge and sandbox of modus operandi" [msg #121].
- **Extraordinary claims fall when they do not replicate.** LK-99 was claimed as a room-temperature superconductor, but groups that synthesised it from the original recipe saw no superconductivity; a result that exists only in one "magic sample" is questionable as real science [msg #208].
- **Books can build a worldview**, and the author's formative ten span genetics, evolution, scepticism, neurology, cosmology, the self, religion, science fiction, meaning without gods and personality theory [msg #116].
- **Read dialectically and update.** Optimistic narratives (Ridley's *Rational Optimist*) should be balanced by works showing how things can go wrong (Reinert on economics, Ord on existential risk); authors who revise their own estimates deserve extra credit; and one should separate a concept from the person promoting it [msg #494].
- **Research is findable and readable for free** via research.com (rankings of works and scientists), Edge (conversations with leading thinkers) and JSTOR (100 free articles a month) [msg #40, #58, #334].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Three sources from which we learn what we value
Purpose: the author's starting point for why the quality of the sciences matters. We understand what is valuable to us by collecting information from different sources [msg #109]:
1. **Samples from our close circle** — the models and examples of the people near us [msg #109].
2. **Personal experience** — specific events and the decisions we made in them [msg #109].
3. **Broadcasts from the distant environment and the outside world** — and education belongs to this third source [msg #109].

She notes that Russian-speaking Europe, now scattered around the world, still receives its education in a predominantly secular rather than spiritual format ("Хвала богам" — "Thank the gods"), and defines that secularity: "Светскость в этой сфере предполагает опору на научные данные и отделяет их, в первую очередь, от религиозной этики." (Secularity in this sphere means relying on scientific data and separating it, first of all, from religious ethics.) Because education rests on the sciences, she asks whether all sciences are currently good — useful and sufficient — and whether they can be accepted unconditionally or whether it is time for "reinforcement" [msg #109].

### Criteria of a "good" science (updatable, integrated, basic scientificity)
Purpose: a personal-opinion checklist (tagged by the author as personal opinion) for judging which sciences help people and improve the world versus which are relics of the past "that even postmodernism cannot ironically outplay". What matters to her is not so much what a science studies as its approach [msg #109]:
1. **Updatable (обновляемые).** "обновляемые — те науки, что продолжают байесиански освежать свои концепты под влиянием новых данных" (updatable — those sciences that keep refreshing their concepts in a Bayesian way under the influence of new data). The point is that a science's concepts are not fixed dogma but beliefs revised as evidence arrives [msg #109].
2. **Integrated (проинтегрированные).** "проинтегрированные — ведь по-настоящему обновляться, на мой взгляд, они смогут только путём открытости и взаимопроникновения с ближними и дальними своими соседями, особенно социальных наук с естественными" (integrated — because, in my view, they can truly update only through openness and interpenetration with their near and distant neighbours, especially the social sciences with the natural ones). The reasoning links criterion 2 to criterion 1: integration is the mechanism that makes genuine updating possible [msg #109].
3. **Basic scientificity preserved.** "Ну и базовые критерии «научности» per se, конечно, должны быть сохранены, чтобы оставаться, знаете… науками" (And the basic criteria of "scientificity" per se, of course, must be preserved — to remain, you know… sciences). Openness and integration must not dissolve the discipline's scientific standards [msg #109]. (!) INCOMPLETE: the source does not spell out what these basic criteria of scientificity are.

### Four key branches of academic philosophy
According to the author, the key directions of academic philosophy today are [msg #121]:
1. **Metaphysics** — questions of the existence of the human being and of reality [msg #121].
2. **Epistemology** — about whether phenomena in the world are knowable, and about belief in particular ideas [msg #121].
3. **Ethics** — how one should live, what good and evil are [msg #121].
4. **Logic** — how to draw conclusions from true premises [msg #121].

She finds it most interesting to track the differences in these areas by comparing the continental and analytic philosophical traditions. To a modern eye, she adds, there seem to be two main lines of thought: something resembling the natural sciences and something resembling the social sciences [msg #121].

### What remains in philosophy's domain (two areas)
Purpose: the author's answer (explicitly "debatable" and her opinion) to "if the sciences are already formed and are slowly but surely investigating all necessary questions, why do we need philosophy — should we not be thorough materialists and eliminate it?" She names two things that still belong to philosophy [msg #121]:
1. **The initial creation of concepts and beliefs that form the basis of a worldview**, in places where we have only fragmentary ideas. She calls this "the very 'proto'" — i.e. the proto-scientific role Churchland describes [msg #121].
2. **Morality and ethics** — "the juiciest part". Its open questions: what should guide decisions — maximising benefit or minimising harm; for a specific person or for society; judging by consequences or by some deep nature of the act itself; and overall "стоит ли человеку быть птичкой, созданной для счастья, или только махать крыльями, чтобы не крякнуться сразу?" (should a person be a bird created for happiness, or only flap their wings so as not to crash right away?) [msg #121].

Why ethics stays human for now: perhaps in time it will be fully calculated by super-powerful machines and placed into a convenient table with assigned weights, where one sets the parameters of desired results over a given time span. But for now this is done by people for people and is debatable at every step; she cites as a vivid illustration the recent story of automating court decisions and the colossal differences between machine and human judgements in sentencing [msg #121].

### Red flags of a non-replicable breakthrough (LK-99 / magic sample)
Purpose: the checks the forwarded author (of the physics channel Соня и наука (Sonya and Science)) applied to the LK-99 claim, usable for judging any sensational result [msg #208]:
1. **Is it only a preprint?** A preprint is a work posted openly before journal publication; it is not moderated and "may with some probability be full of obscurantism". LK-99 was announced in a preprint [msg #208].
2. **Is the team size plausible for the claim?** The three Korean authors described in detail what each did. For such experimental work she expects a team of 5–6, because the range of tasks is very wide: growing (synthesising) the material, setting up the experiment, interpretation. It is "very hard to believe" that three people made a breakthrough of the claimed scale. She notes the authors had another preprint with a different author list (hinting there are more people involved, though she declines to speculate) [msg #208].
3. **What is the history?** The authors had long held a superconductivity-related patent, the "99" in the name refers to 1999, the year LK-99 was first synthesised, and they had published in a Korean journal earlier in 2023; the resonance only arose at the end of July, when groups worldwide "started dragging the hype around" and testing it [msg #208].
4. **Does it replicate from the recipe?** The questions are mostly about methods: was the synthesis done correctly, is it reproducible, did they lie. The claimed physics was seen only by the original Koreans; six more groups worldwide got a negative result, and groups that synthesised LK-99 from the original preprint's recipe did not observe superconductivity. FIAN (the Lebedev Physical Institute) researchers' short answer: no — "Материал проявляет свойства изолятора и не левитирует." (The material shows the properties of an insulator and does not levitate.) [msg #208]
5. **Is it a "magic sample"?** Her term for a pattern: "у тебя есть ОДИН ЕДИНСТВЕННЫЙ образец, его головокружительная физика и публикации о ней" (you have ONE SINGLE sample, its dizzying physics and publications about it). As soon as you replace it with an identical sample, the physics dissipates and all the tasty effects stop being observed. "Насколько это можно считать тру наукой с таким уровнем воспроизводимости, думайте сами." (How far this can be considered true science with such a level of reproducibility — judge for yourselves.) [msg #208]

Besides serious checks, a wave of memes and conspiracy theories rolled through the scientific community on whether LK-99 was a prank or a ChatGPT product [msg #208].

### Dialectical reading (read both poles and the grey zone)
Purpose: the author's approach to choosing books on how the world is going, from #494 [msg #494]:
1. **Notice one-sided framing.** Reading the annotation of Ridley's *The Rational Optimist* (life keeps getting better, medicines more accessible, technology more progressive; not everything is smooth, but we move forward and are resilient to various failures), she realised it lacked dialectics of the kind "А что, блин, если нет, вот так вот раз — и нет?" (What the hell if not — just like that, and no?) — and pointedly notes the book is from 2010 [msg #494].
2. **See both poles and the grey zone, systemically.** "Ведь важно видеть оба полюса и серую зону между ними." (After all, it is important to see both poles and the grey zone between them.) — and to understand systemically how the shift towards one or the other happens [msg #494].
3. **Look for counter-authors field by field.** In economics she found Erik Reinert; then she looked for similar authors among socio-anthropologists and found Toby Ord's *The Precipice* (details under *Books on economics and humanity's future*) [msg #494].
4. **Prefer recent, self-updating work.** She values that *The Precipice* (March 2020) is closer to the reality we have faced and that Ord revisited the book's data and updated his risk estimates in January 2024: "Такой подход в моих глазах выглядит оперативным и готовым к обновлению своей позиции, что, я считаю, дорогого стоит." (In my eyes such an approach looks prompt and ready to update its position, which, I believe, is worth a lot.) [msg #494]
5. **Separate the concept from the person.** Despite reservations about the author's movement, she reads the work because "стоит разделять концепцию и персону" (one should separate the concept and the person): good things can become a weapon in bad hands, and vice versa [msg #494].

## Detailed insights

### What philosophy is, and Patricia Churchland's challenge
The question "Is philosophy some kind of joke or a useful scientific discipline?" was asked in Agmentek's writing challenge, and the author takes it up as a continuation of her post on useful and not-so-useful sciences, calling philosophy their "damned mother" [msg #121].

Her definition: "Философия как таковая — не наука, это отдельная ветвь познания мира." (Philosophy as such is not a science; it is a separate branch of knowing the world.) It has its own tools for gathering and processing information, which differ by school — Socratics, sceptics, materialists, Stoics, phenomenalists, voluntarists, with the list left open-ended in the source ("и пр.") [msg #121].

Its main occupation is the search for what Douglas Adams packaged into the number 42 — answers to questions about life, the universe and everything. "Она предлагает разные варианты того, как про это подумать, как посмотреть и какие гипотезы и выводы представить." (It offers different options for how to think about it, how to look at it, and what hypotheses and conclusions to put forward.) That, she says, is probably its main "why" [msg #121].

Patricia Churchland, described as a neurophilosopher of our time (the cover image of the Edge post shows her), agrees with the "something resembling science" part: she sees philosophy as a "прото-науку" (proto-science) that poses hard questions, primarily of a practical nature. As an eliminative materialist she further argues that intuitive and "folk" concepts such as "free will" or "consciousness" should be revised in a reductionist key as scientists (here neurobiologists) uncover more confirmed data on how the phenomenon (here the brain and thinking) is built and works. Put simply, "необходимо устранить все сущности, что рассогласуются с научными данными" (it is necessary to eliminate all entities that are inconsistent with scientific data) [msg #121, #58].

The author's conclusion: at this historical moment "философия видится мне колыбелью познания и песочницей" (philosophy appears to me as the cradle of knowledge and the sandbox) of modus operandi — both for beginning thinkers and for those who have travelled a long path of scientific knowledge and now want to assemble what they found into a coherent worldview concept. She closes by asking readers whether modern people need philosophy and, if so, in what form and why [msg #121].

### The LK-99 case: background on superconductivity
The forwarded post opens by saying the world of modern physics was shaken by something stronger than the Barbie fever, and covers "the discovery (and closing)" of LK-99 [msg #208].

**The claim.** A couple of weeks before the post, three Koreans posted a preprint claiming to have discovered a substance that is superconducting at temperatures below 120 °C — i.e. a room-temperature superconductor ("this is not a drill") [msg #208].

**What superconductivity is.** A phenomenon in which a substance has zero electrical resistance and expels magnetic field from itself — hence the effect of a superconductor levitating over a magnet, the so-called "Mohammed's coffin". It was first discovered in mercury at -270 °C (the temperature of liquid helium) a little over a century ago; later research aimed at creating a high-temperature superconductor. Superconductors working at liquid-nitrogen temperature (-196 °C) are already called high-temperature, because nitrogen is cheap and easy to obtain. For a popular explanation she suggests an episode of the Russian cartoon Smeshariki (Смешарики) on the topic; a picture in her previous post was taken from it and shows Bardeen, Cooper and Schrieffer running to collect their Nobel Prize [msg #208].

**Why high-temperature superconductivity matters.** If current flows through a sample without resistance, there are no losses; the main applications relate to the electric power industry, magnetic-levitation trains, the quantum computer "and everything else". A room-temperature superconductor therefore was, and remains, a "blue dream" on the level of cold fusion. She also points readers to a post by "Katerina" (another channel) which she says lays out the facts [msg #208].

### The ten childhood books that shaped the author
Context: the author defines childhood as something pervaded by carefreeness, freedom of discovery and calm, warm support, without anxieties for oneself and loved ones — and says "Моё «детство» началось, когда мне было 19." (My "childhood" began when I was 19.) For a long time she had been a support and mentor, friend and mother to others and a reflection of them, always knowing which face to create for the other person ("your needs are more important than mine"), while her own wants and values were an ellipsis; her personality was hidden under a thick layer of amalgam, with only endurance and will "like Demi Moore's G.I. Jane", apart from rare moments of solitude in creativity and sport. Only far from her former surroundings, washing off the tension with silence and calm support, could she look inward and gradually feel out what in the outer world resonated with her inner one. The following years brought discoveries that opened her world "from the size of a needle's eye to an almost infinite universe", including these books, which laid the foundation of her professional development and reflected and completed how she sees the world — in many ways to this day [msg #116]:
1. **Genome — Matt Ridley.** Inspiring popular science about the structure of human DNA; it captured her so much that in her first years of searching for a profession she wanted to become a geneticist [msg #116].
2. **The Red Queen: Sex and the Evolution of Human Nature — Matt Ridley** (Russian title «Секс и эволюция человеческой природы»; the post gives the English title as "The red queen theory: sex and evolution of human being"). How does the struggle between viruses, mutations and adaptation processes go on inside a human, and what role does reproduction play in all of it? [msg #116]
3. **The God Delusion — Richard Dawkins.** A book with well-raised questions for beginning sceptics (not necessarily atheists): "Но только начинающих!" (But only for beginners!) [msg #116]
4. **The Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat — Oliver Sacks** (she read it in English). What illusions a brain damaged in different areas can create, and how to live with that in ordinary life [msg #116].
5. **The Large, the Small and the Human Mind — Roger Penrose, Stephen Hawking et al.** Hard to describe in two words; she says it "очень помогает масштабировать представления о мире" (helps a lot to scale up one's ideas about the world) [msg #116].
6. **The Ego Tunnel — Thomas Metzinger.** A close acquaintance with the very brain program we are used to calling "ourselves", with illustrations of how changeable it actually is [msg #116].
7. **Religion Explained — Pascal Boyer.** A deep study of religion in the lives of different peoples — from its kinds and functions in different societies to how all of it is connected with the features of our brain's structure; spoiler: there are big and very big differences from what might come to mind at first [msg #116].
8. **The Blindsight / Echopraxia duology — Peter Watts.** Science fiction about intelligent superorganisms and the change in a person's inner picture of the world in the process. She agrees with Russian Wikipedia that Blindsight explores themes of personality, consciousness, free will, artificial intelligence, game theory, neurobiology and human evolution [msg #116].
9. **The Age of Nothing (Эпоха пустоты) — Peter Watson** (she warns not to confuse him with Peter Watts). Non-fiction about how people, including great ones, construct life meanings when every god has died for them [msg #116].
10. **Personality Theories — Larry Hjelle and Daniel Ziegler.** In her view "фундаментальнейший труд, который внёс порядок во всю массу подходов к исследованию личности" (a most fundamental work that brought order into the whole mass of approaches to studying personality). Her incomplete list of what it covers: whether there is free will or everything can be calculated; what is greater in a person — "nature" or "nurture", the inner impulse to act or reaction to the external, the constant or the variable; how and why different psychological schools view this; and how all of it can be measured [msg #116].

### Books on economics and humanity's future
The post's occasion was a personal one (spending a remaining account balance), and it led her to her wish-list library [msg #494]:
- **The Rational Optimist — Matt Ridley (2010).** Her "beloved" Ridley's optimistic book, even by title: life gets better, medicines more accessible, technologies more progressive; not everything is smooth, but we move forward and are resilient to failures. She found it lacking dialectics (see *Dialectical reading*) [msg #494].
- **How Rich Countries Got Rich and Why Poor Countries Stay Poor — Erik Reinert** (not to be confused with the "useless pop" *Rich Dad Poor Dad*). Found a couple of years earlier; still her favourite for how clearly he points to economists' "sclerosis" and the needless acceptance on faith of the maximalist utopia of free-trade joys. Her summary of his argument: it has been shown several times over recent centuries that free trade starts to work only after a long period of a country getting on its feet through outside injections of funds (sponsorship, investment); otherwise it exhausts "newcomers" in futile attempts to play on equal terms with those who already have established production, lowered costs and can trade without fearing for their last pants. Reading economic history, as Reinert does with readers, shows how, why and where economics at some point came to dominate as a theory detached from life. She thinks this was not malice but partial knowledge, wishful thinking and a thirst for ease, reinforced by the dominant discourses of the late 19th–early 20th centuries ("opportunities should be equal for all" and the like), which affect how information is filtered — so "нужно иметь и кругозор, и нехилую смелость, чтобы проверять на прочность мировоззрения" (one needs both breadth of outlook and considerable courage to stress-test worldviews) [msg #494].
- **The Precipice: Existential Risk and the Future of Humanity — Toby Ord** (published March 2020). About which risks humanity should pay attention to given the achievements and accumulated problems of the 20th–21st centuries: from climate change to man-made pathogens and artificial intelligence. In January 2024 Ord published a review of the book's data with updated risk estimates. Her caveat: she plans to stay on guard because Effective Altruism, of which Ord is a supporter, is utilitarian — the end may justify the means, and the ethics of acts is defined by their usefulness. She still considers it important to read because, first, it is unknown whether the book touches ethical aspects at all or is simply a good historical-analytical review, and second, concept and person should be separated. She asks readers who have read it to share impressions [msg #494].
