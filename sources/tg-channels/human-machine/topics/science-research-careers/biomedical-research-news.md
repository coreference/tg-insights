# Biomedical research news: aging, epigenetics and medicine

## What this covers

A set of short science digests the channel posted between 2022 and 2023, some written by the channel author and some forwarded from other science channels (Безвольные каменщики, Блуждающий нерв). They cover sex differences in strength gains, mitochondrial transplants, transgenerational epigenetic inheritance (a mouse model and the aspartame study), cryopreservation of organs, parabiosis and aging, a manifesto for a digital twin of the worm nervous system, entropy as the reason life on Earth needs the Sun, the public-health lessons of COVID and a breakthrough in treating multidrug-resistant tuberculosis [msg #39, #146, #174, #178, #186, #187, #201, #210, #238, #255].

The posts matter, in the authors' framing, because they show how one generation's environment and habits can shape the next generation's biology [msg #174, #238], how close certain life-extension and preservation technologies are to becoming practical [msg #186, #201], how neuroscience may change through in-silico modelling [msg #210], and how good medicine can still fail when communication and policy fail [msg #178, #255].

## Core ideas

- **Same training, same strength gains.** Men carry more upper-body muscle on average because of testosterone, but with identical training and nutrition the absolute strength gain is statistically the same in both sexes; differences lie in accumulated averages, not in trainability [msg #39].
- **The environment of parents can be inherited.** Parental overweight, poor diet or stress can affect children and possibly grandchildren through epigenetic marks passed in eggs and sperm; new mouse models with targeted methylation make the mechanism studyable [msg #174]. Aspartame consumption in mice impaired spatial memory and the effect appeared in the offspring of exposed males [msg #238].
- **Young blood extends old lives, mechanism unknown.** Joining old mice to young ones for three months lengthened the old mice's lives by 6–9%, yet nobody knows why young blood rejuvenates [msg #201].
- **Successful cryopreservation strengthens trust in long-term freezing.** Frozen rat kidneys worked after transplant, which could fix donor-organ logistics and makes cryopreservation more credible [msg #186].
- **Model a whole simple nervous system before mice.** A manifesto argues for building a working digital twin of C. elegans' nervous system rather than a connectome [msg #210].
- **COVID was handled badly despite medical success.** The forwarded author argues that distrust, poor communication and impulsive policy cost lives, and that humanity would handle the next pandemic just as poorly [msg #178].
- **MDR-TB treatment became shorter and far cheaper.** The endTB regimen cuts treatment from 18 to 9 months and from 5000 to 300 dollars [msg #255].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Reasoning for changing COVID vaccination policy
The forwarded post explains why people were first forced to vaccinate en masse and later appeared to be left alone — something authorities failed to explain, so people could not tell whether they needed another booster [msg #178].
1. **Early pandemic: vaccinate everyone, urgently.** The strains were "злее" (*"more vicious"*) and there was no collective immunity, so herd immunity had to be created quickly by vaccinating everyone [msg #178].
2. **Later phase: regular universal vaccination is no longer obligatory.** Strains now cause severe illness less often, and roughly 100% of everyone older than three years has immunity, so it is no longer necessary to vaccinate every single person regularly [msg #178].
The author's lesson: there is nothing strange in this change of policy; the failure was in not communicating the reasoning [msg #178].

### Catalogue of failures in the COVID response
According to the forwarded author, despite huge pharmacological and medical success (effective, safe vaccines and reliable treatment protocols were developed within a year), the three years of COVID showed [msg #178]:
1. "пещерное антивакцинаторство" (*"cave-man anti-vaccinationism"*) and distrust of medicine in general;
2. illogical and impulsive government policy;
3. poor informing of the public;
4. excessive tightening of restrictions — or, the opposite, refusal of any responsibility for citizens' deaths;
5. too slow a reaction at the start;
6. and at the same time too long a refusal to lift measures once everything was over [msg #178].

### Vision of future neuroscience (digital-twin manifesto)
The forwarded author reads in the manifesto an image of science its authors foresee and bring closer: neuroscience will be fed by two sources [msg #210]:
1. **Observation tools** with grown capabilities that can see the activity and organization of living networks in the finest detail;
2. **Machine learning resources** that make it possible to extract correlations from petabytes of data, build models and experiment *in-silico* [msg #210].
The practical recommendation of the authors is to postpone mouse experiments for now and derive the principles of neural computation from a simpler model organism, C. elegans [msg #210].

## Detailed insights

### Sex differences in strength and training (study by Paulo Gentil and colleagues)
The post, titled with a wordplay "Пчёлки работают попой, а пчелы рубят с плеча" (*roughly "female bees work with their bottoms, male bees chop from the shoulder"*), asks whether men and women differ in strength and physical capacity and what role testosterone and progesterone play [msg #39].

A study by Paulo Gentil and colleagues on upper-body strength showed two things [msg #39]:
- On the one hand, in the absence of physical training, testosterone lets men accumulate more muscle mass in the shoulders than women (as a statistical norm) [msg #39].
- On the other hand, with identical training and unchanged nutrition, physical (Newtonian) **strength** increases equally in both — the difference is within tenths, so small that it is recognized as statistically insignificant [msg #39].

Further points from the post: in women, strength gains occur predominantly when progesterone rises, which happens in the second phase of the menstrual cycle; and, according to another study mentioned in the accompanying video, women gain strength more easily in the lower body, whereas men gain it in the upper body [msg #39]. The author's reading of the article: what actually differs between the sexes is the average absolute muscularity and shoulder width accumulated by a certain age, not the response to training [msg #39].

The author closes with open questions rather than answers: what would happen — and surely does happen — if women had higher testosterone during puberty; at what point a female MMA fighter becomes able to pin an untrained man; and, jokingly, whether the study will lead to a new sport, "арм-тай-рестлингу (или по нашему — бёдрорукоборью)" (*"arm-thigh-wrestling (or in our terms, thigh-arm-wrestling)"*), combining the upper-body strength of men with the lower-body strength of women. "Так много вопросов, так мало ответов." (*"So many questions, so few answers."*) [msg #39]

### Mitochondrial transplants for rare genetic disease
A one-line news item: children with rare genome mutations were treated with mitochondrial transplants from their mothers. A Russian translation of the news (on N+1) was suggested by a reader [msg #146].

### Transgenerational epigenetic inheritance: a new mouse model (Takahashi et al., Cell)
Parental overweight, a diet low in nutrients, or stress can endanger the health of children and possibly grandchildren through the inheritance of "эпигенетических модификаций" (*"epigenetic modifications"*) [msg #174].

- **What epigenetic modifications are.** Biochemical changes to DNA or to the associated histone proteins — for example adding methyl or acetyl groups — that influence gene expression, cell type and many other things without changing the DNA sequence [msg #174].
- **How they arise and travel.** They can appear in response to environmental signals and be passed to subsequent generations in eggs and sperm [msg #174].
- **The open debate.** It is currently disputed how epigenetic marks such as methyl groups on DNA can be transmitted from parents to offspring [msg #174].
- **The model.** In a paper in Cell, Takahashi et al. presented a mouse model in which methylation is induced at targeted DNA sites, and showed that the approach can be used to address pressing mechanistic questions about transgenerational epigenetic inheritance (TGI). Their targeted methylation lets researchers probe specifically the genomic features that may be linked to TGI [msg #174].
- **Next steps named by the article.** Determining how the molecular landscape (epigenetic modifications, three-dimensional chromatin organization, with the list left open by the source: "и т. д.") and the underlying DNA sequence together contribute to protecting edited genes from the reprogramming machinery [msg #174].
- **Further use.** The approach can also be applied to study the heritability of epigenetic marks on disease-linked genes [msg #174].
- **Why it matters.** Identifying the mechanisms that connect the accumulation of parental epigenetic marks with the development and health of offspring could strongly affect our understanding of hereditary diseases [msg #174].

### Aspartame and spatial memory in mice (and their offspring)
Headline claim: the sugar substitute aspartame contributes to deficits in spatial memory and learning [msg #238].
- **Exposure.** Lab mice drank water with aspartame for 16 weeks, in an amount described as 8 ounces a day (roughly equal to 237 ml of soda) [msg #238].
- **Effect.** The experimental mice performed significantly worse in working memory than mice that drank plain water; during learning they were worse at holding and handling new spatial information [msg #238].
- **Inheritance.** The effect was seen not only in the exposed mice but also in the offspring of the males of the experimental group [msg #238].
- **Interpretation by the author.** This part of diet seems to belong to environmental factors whose influence is passed on "генетически" (*"genetically"*) to 1–2 generations — another example of how the behavior and habits of one generation affect the brain organization of the next [msg #238].
- **Dose context.** The dose used was 7–15% of the amount currently classified as safe by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [msg #238].
The author ends half-jokingly that she will drink water and eat one piece of normal milk chocolate — with sugar [msg #238].

### Parabiosis extends the life of old mice
A forwarded post (from Блуждающий нерв): a group of scientists extended the life of old mice by joining their blood vessels with those of young mice. The pairs were "stitched" together for three months and then separated; the old animals lived 6–9% longer, which is equivalent to six years for a human [msg #201].
- The authors of the Nature Aging paper write that the old mice's bodies became younger as judged by molecular markers [msg #201].
- What surprises the forwarded author: parabiosis has been studied for a long time (this group is far from the first), yet it is still not clear what in young blood rejuvenates the old organism — and the authors of the new study do not know either [msg #201].
- The team came from Duke, UCLA and Harvard and included Vadim Gladyshev, Sergei Dmitriev, Anastasia Shindyapina and Alexander Tyshkovskiy; the forwarded author notes with curiosity that he did not know the Belozersky Institute of Physico-Chemical Biology at Moscow State University (НИИ ФХБ МГУ) was connected with such projects [msg #201].

### Cryopreserved kidneys transplanted in rats
Researchers at the University of Minnesota successfully restarted previously frozen organs (kidneys) in rats after transplantation [msg #186]. The channel author draws two implications:
1. **Locally — donor-organ logistics.** One of the hardest parts of transplantation is delivering the organ in time; mistakes are costly both financially and in human lives. Freezing could help solve this [msg #186].
2. **Globally — long-term cryopreservation.** It is an example of a successful technological solution for the idea of long-term cryopreservation. The author notes that life extension through freezing currently raises "вопросики" (*"little questions"*, i.e. doubts) at the present stage of technology, so successful practices like this help one count on the reliability of such procedures in the future [msg #186].
She closes with a joke about future wedding vows: "В богатстве и бедности, до заморозки и после…" (*"For richer, for poorer, before freezing and after…"*) [msg #186].

### Entropy: how Earth uses the Sun's energy to sustain life
The author frames Earth and the Sun as some of the largest "machines" known to us and recommends a video that explains, in simple language and from an interesting, non-pop angle, the concept of entropy: how Earth uses the Sun's energy to create and maintain life, and why life on Earth would be impossible without the Sun. The video is in English; the author suggests switching on Russian auto-subtitles [msg #187].

### A digital twin of the C. elegans nervous system
A fresh manifesto by a group of authors including Ed Boyden and George Church, "To reverse engineer an entire nervous system" (arXiv, 2023), calls for building a working model of the nervous system of a worm — not a connectome, which "не дал понимания" (*"did not give understanding"*), but all the interactions of the cells; in effect, a "цифрового двойника" (*"digital twin"*) of the nervous system [msg #210].
- The authors think mouse experiments should be postponed for now and the principles of neural computation derived from a simpler model [msg #210].
- Their stated promise: reverse engineering the causal interactions of C. elegans' nervous system would establish in-silico simulations as a way to accelerate neuroscience and teach how to run experiments that reveal how circuits causally work [msg #210]. For the broader picture of science the manifesto implies, see *Vision of future neuroscience* above.

### COVID: the end of the emergency and what it showed
Forwarded from the channel Безвольные каменщики. WHO cancelled the global health emergency status for COVID. Practically, the author says, this changes little, because most countries had long since cancelled restrictions and support measures such as free testing; but as a symbolic declaration it was a "must" [msg #178].

- **Death toll.** Officially 6.9 million died of COVID, but WHO head Ghebreyesus voiced a consensus figure of 20 million. The roughly threefold gap is explained by massive under-reporting of deaths: estimated 12-fold in Egypt, 8-fold in Iran and 13-fold in Belarus [msg #178].
- **The paradox.** Instead of showing people the power of modern medicine, COVID sharpened distrust of it and strengthened anti-vaccinationists, partly because authorities failed to explain what was happening and why (see *Reasoning for changing COVID vaccination policy* and *Catalogue of failures in the COVID response*) [msg #178].
- **Retreat of state control.** Part of the reason for the "отката к средневеквым [sic] представлениям о медицине" (*"rollback to medieval notions of medicine"*) is that states — including authoritarian ones such as Russia — abandoned strict control in healthcare [msg #178].
- **Ideology over evidence.** In Russia, Poland and some US states the attitude to vaccination is "we won't force anyone", while the attitude to abortion moves rapidly to the opposite pole, "ban everything". The author concludes that instead of implementing the results of years of research, health policy is increasingly defined by primitive ideas about what is "богопротивности" (*"ungodliness"*); in that light, banning abortions and refusing vaccination turn out to be logical and mutually consistent [msg #178].
- **Outlook.** The author welcomes the end of the acute phase and the transition to an endemic state, and that no new pathogen has appeared yet — but predicts that with a new one humanity will behave just as incompetently, and nobody guarantees it would take 20 million lives rather than, say, 220 million [msg #178].

### Multidrug-resistant tuberculosis: the endTB breakthrough
- **Definition.** Multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (ТБ-МЛУ, MDR-TB) is a disease that ignores the strongest drugs in this field, isoniazid and rifampicin [msg #255].
- **The result.** Independent clinical trials of a new approach from the EndTB project (the post glosses the name as "Stop tuberculosis") succeeded; the presentation was met with a standing ovation [msg #255].
- **What changed.** Treatment of this form of tuberculosis now takes 9 months instead of 18 and costs 300 dollars instead of 5000 [msg #255].
- **Advocacy.** Брайан Грин (as named in the post) explains in a video how these results could change the lives of those who get access, and insists that WHO must recommend these protocols everywhere as the gold standard and increase their availability — without deferring to Johnson & Johnson and other pharmaceutical companies — otherwise organizations such as Nerdfighteria will push for it with tougher measures [msg #255].

## Evidence and numbers

- Gentil et al.: strength gains with identical training differ between men and women only within tenths — statistically insignificant [msg #39].
- Parabiosis: 3 months joined, old mice lived 6–9% longer, equivalent to ~6 human years [msg #201].
- Aspartame: 16 weeks, 8 ounces/day (~237 ml of soda); dose = 7–15% of the FDA safe level; effect seen in offspring of exposed males, inferred to pass 1–2 generations [msg #238].
- COVID: 6.9 million official deaths vs 20 million consensus (WHO head); under-reporting ×12 Egypt, ×8 Iran, ×13 Belarus; ~100% of people over 3 years old have immunity; vaccines and protocols developed within one year [msg #178].
- MDR-TB (endTB): 9 vs 18 months, 300 vs 5000 dollars [msg #255].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The aspartame and parabiosis findings are mouse studies; the parabiosis "six human years" is a stated equivalence, not a human result [msg #201, #238].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the aspartame post says the effect is passed "генетически" (*"genetically"*) to 1–2 generations, while the topic it illustrates (inheritance of environmental effects) is elsewhere described as epigenetic, i.e. without changing the DNA sequence [msg #174, #238]. Also, "8 ounces a day" is the human-equivalent framing given in the post; the actual mouse dose is not stated separately [msg #238].
- The mechanism by which young blood rejuvenates old organisms is explicitly unknown, including to the study's authors [msg #201].
- How epigenetic marks pass from parents to offspring is described as an ongoing debate [msg #174].
- The channel author hedges that life extension by freezing still raises doubts; the rat kidney result only increases confidence in future reliability [msg #186].
- The strength post mentions progesterone-linked gains and lower-body advantage in women from "another study" shown in the video, without details of that study [msg #39].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the MDR-TB post names the video author as "Брайан Грин" and links him to the Nerdfighteria community; the post gives no further identification [msg #255].
- The COVID retrospective is an opinion piece by the forwarded author (Безвольные каменщики), including political judgments about Russia, Poland and US states [msg #178].
- The mitochondrial-transplant item is a single line without details of the mutations, number of children or outcomes [msg #146].
