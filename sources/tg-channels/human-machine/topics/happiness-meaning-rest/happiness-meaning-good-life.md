# Happiness, meaning and the good life

## What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's posts on what makes a life good: why wishes and acquisitions so often fail to deliver happiness (the hedonic treadmill, consumer culture, and four features of how the mind predicts and judges), Robert Nozick's experience machine as a test of whether pleasant inner experience is all that matters, the newer idea of a psychologically rich life as a third axis next to happiness and meaning, meaning in life as an inner support against stress (with Viktor Frankl's three routes to meaning), and personal values - what they are and how the author found her own. It also contains the author's story of discovering that popular happiness claims from a reputable university's lecture did not match the original studies [msg #145, #151, #139, #664, #130, #101, #134].

According to the author, it matters because happiness is subjective and nobody teaches it: there is no school, institute or social structure that teaches from childhood how to reach that state and still less how to sustain it, so people fill the gap with "ideal" second-hand information and build castles in the air [msg #145, #151].

## Core ideas

- **Happiness can only be self-reported.** It cannot be measured from outside - neither by the number of goods a person has nor by facial signs - only by asking the person (self-report) [msg #145].
- **What we chase often does not make us happy.** Today, as at least eighty years ago, the things we use to try to make ourselves happy often fail to do so; consumer capitalism pushes people onto a hedonic treadmill where satisfaction vanishes the moment a goal is reached (see *Four drivers of the consumer hedonic treadmill*) [msg #145].
- **Our predictions about happiness are systematically wrong.** Long-running studies on large samples show that almost none of the classic expected sources of happiness work the way we expect, because of four features of the mind (see *Four features of the mind that block happiness*) [msg #151].
- **Good inner experience may not be everything.** Nozick's experience machine asks whether anything matters besides how life feels from the inside (see *Nozick's experience machine*) [msg #139].
- **The good life has (at least) three axes.** Besides hedonic happiness and eudaimonic meaning, a third independent factor - psychological richness - shows up in studies (see *Three axes of a good life*) [msg #664].
- **Meaning is a foundation and an inner support.** Meaning gives inner support and helps withstand stressors; it is tightly tied to values (see *Frankl's three ways to discover meaning* and *Meaning in life*) [msg #130].
- **Values stay ours whatever path brought us to them.** Once cleaned of the husk of duty, judgment and imposed guilt, they can give a new life [msg #101, #102].
- **Check the primary source.** Even material from a large international university can misstate research; the author checks the references and drops claims that do not hold [msg #134].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Five properties of individual values
Purpose: a compact working definition of what a personal value is, given by the author "for clarity in context" before telling how she found her own values (she had analysed values versus needs at length in a separate post on another channel) [msg #101].

Individual values are personal beliefs that:
1. **Go beyond specific situations** - a value is not tied to one occasion but carries across contexts [msg #101].
2. **Define goals and motivate action** - values are what we set goals by and what moves us to act [msg #101].
3. **Serve as reference points for evaluations** - we judge things, people and events against them [msg #101].
4. **Signal through emotions how fulfilled they are** - emotions tell us the degree to which a value is being met [msg #101].
5. **Are ordered by importance** - and, the author adds as her own impression, this ordering seems to persist over considerable periods of life; she suspects for years: «упорядочены по важности — которая, как мне кажется, сохраняется на протяжении значительных периодов времени жизни; подозреваю, что лет» (ordered by importance - which, it seems to me, is preserved over significant periods of life; I suspect for years) [msg #101].

### Frankl's three ways to discover meaning
Purpose: routes by which a person can uncover meaning, according to Viktor Frankl, who, as the author puts it, worked deeply on questions of meaning in situations of life and death [msg #130].
1. **By creating something or doing a deed** - meaning through action and creation [msg #130].
2. **By experiencing something or encountering someone** - meaning through experience and through meeting another person [msg #130].
3. **Through our acceptance of the inevitability of suffering** - together with the idea that everything can be taken from a person except one thing: «последней из человеческих свобод — выбирать свое отношение при любом стечении обстоятельств» (the last of the human freedoms - to choose one's attitude in any set of circumstances) [msg #130].

### Value-oriented strategy and daily tactic
Purpose: the author's personal model of turning meaning into everyday life, offered as her own example [msg #130].
1. **Name the key meaning (strategy).** Her key meaning at the moment is to expand knowledge and move humanity to a new level of development - on one side by studying and creating new theories and tools; on the other by supporting other people and supplying them with existing tools so they can understand themselves better and accumulate the resources needed to steer themselves along the path of the most interesting and strongest contribution to their own growth, and through that to the world. She calls this «ориентированной на ценности стратегией» (a value-oriented strategy) [msg #130].
2. **Give the strategy a fitting tactic.** In her view any strategy needs a suitable tactic. Hers: every day she must have something to research, create and invent, the means to do it and a reason for it («каждый день мне было что, чем и ради чего исследовать, творить и изобретать» - every day I have something, something to do it with, and a reason to research, create and invent) [msg #130].
3. **Transfer the tactic when the main field is blocked.** When she cannot do this in "neuro-things" (нейроштуки), she carries it over to everyday life, nutrition, physical activity, beauty, health and a million things people around her ask for - and sometimes do not ask for. Her humorous summary, in bold: «И перестать оптимизировать, изобретать!» (you can't just simply take and stop optimizing, inventing!) [msg #130].

### Four features of the mind that block happiness
Purpose: the author's explanation, based on studies she promised to discuss in detail later, of why expected sources of happiness disappoint. Each feature can bring "a sea of benefit" but, misapplied, brings pain and suffering [msg #151].
1. **Our strongest intuitions turn out to be completely wrong.** If (a) we are strongly convinced of something - for example because everyone says so, or because someone defended the position especially emotionally - or (b) we once packed something into a convenient algorithm (of actions, thoughts, sensations, emotional experiences), we begin to err badly in estimates and predictions, especially about how we will feel emotionally during certain events. This shapes expectations and choices so that we are often displeased by the very fact of the error: in thought it sounds like "we expected more and got less", or "it should have been bad, it did not happen, and we wait for when it will". Either way we are not grateful to the world for such "deception" [msg #151].
2. **The mind does not think in absolutes - it judges by relative reference points.** In general this is useful: it was bad and became good, which is joyful; this item looks more harmonious on me than another. Trouble starts when we compare our parameters (from attractiveness to competence) not with our own earlier ones but with other people's. It is especially hard when the comparison group is either an "ideal" that does not exist in nature (including a local, made-up one) or people not at our current level of development but from the "top league" of a field important to us - who most likely spent a decade getting there, while we see only the result, which looks easily and quickly achieved [msg #151].
3. **The mind tends to get used to things and events.** Humans, like many living organisms, are about adapting to the environment. We vitally need to learn over time to perceive things as "normal" - this keeps us from getting stuck in states of excessive emotional charge. However, some of our deep values may require that we remember or feel certain things sharply; for that one can and should do special things to refresh the experience. When we do not, life can become too bland [msg #151].
4. **We expect ourselves to be less resilient to bad things than we are.** This partly overlaps with the previous points: on average we expect more disappointment and helplessness from things and events than actually occurs, and we feel weaker and less stable than we could [msg #151].

### Four drivers of the consumer hedonic treadmill
Purpose: the author's account of how extreme forms of capitalism, in which "almost the whole European and American part of society" splashes together, push people onto a treadmill of «как_бы_наслаждения жизнью» (sort-of enjoying life) [msg #145].
1. **Fast wear of affordable things** - cheap items quickly wear out ("ugh, already looks awful") [msg #145].
2. **Fast moral obsolescence of things** - items go out of fashion quickly ("ugh, already unfashionable") [msg #145].
3. **The drive to sole ownership of as many things as possible** - "I can afford it, and nobody touches what is MINE" [msg #145].
4. **Constant comparison** - of us with each other and with ideals that do not exist in nature, and as a consequence intense competition across many spheres of life, from career to personal life and appearance [msg #145].

Why it works this way, per the author: at the heart of capitalism is profit maximization, which needs a society able and motivated to buy regularly - a consumer society. The easiest way to create it is through a morality: «Счастлив лишь только тот, кто на вершине всего» (Only the one at the top of everything is happy). She notes a current shift away from mass production toward attention to different customer categories, tied to care and inclusiveness, but says the tail of former "Fordism" still drags the attributes above along [msg #145].

The treadmill itself - known literally as the hedonic treadmill - is where, chasing a phantom carrot, we seem to approach happiness, but where satisfaction, after a flicker, disappears exactly at the moment the goal is reached. Therefore the more we chase it, the more we are disappointed [msg #145].

### Six things we wrongly believe will bring happiness
Purpose: the list of classic expectations that, per the author, long-running studies with large samples show almost never work for happiness the way we expect at the start [msg #151].
1. Cool things [msg #151].
2. Constant salary increases ("the more, the better") [msg #151].
3. True love, a wedding [msg #151].
4. Perfect appearance (a conventionally beautiful face and a thin body) [msg #151].
5. Cool "grades", external recognition [msg #151].
6. Good genes and/or a super-correct environment [msg #151].

Where these beliefs come from: as we grow we learn, and some things can be tried on ourselves immediately (that a stove is hot), while others are unavailable until a certain moment (sex, student life, the first salary) or require many tries (discovering that sushi, or cheese with wine, is tasty after all). Until we experience something ourselves, we collect "ideal" information - how it works for others, what parents think, what mass media or classmates say - and this, it seems, is where we build especially airy castles about happiness. The author's summary: «мы думаем, что всё так просто, и да, всё просто, но совсем не так» (we think it's all so simple, and yes, it is simple, but not at all like that) [msg #151].

### Nozick's experience machine (thought experiment)
Purpose: to test whether a life of only positive experiences is what we really want - a challenge to hedonism, where one strives for maximum joy and pleasure and minimum hardship and sadness ("to the hundredth power" if you preach hedonism) [msg #139].

The setup, quoted from Nozick (Russian translation of Anarchy, State and Utopia): «Предположим, что существовала бы машина по производству личного опыта, которая могла бы обеспечить вам любой опыт по вашему желанию» (Suppose there were an experience machine that would give you any experience you desired). Neuroscientists could stimulate your brain so you would feel you are writing a great novel, making a friend, or reading an interesting book, while all the time you float in a tank with electrodes attached to your brain. You would program in advance all the events that should happen to you and plug in for life; while plugged in you would not know you are in the tank - you would think it is all really happening [msg #139].

The questions the author draws out:
1. Would we want to be placed into a virtual machine whose experience we cannot distinguish from reality, if it generates only positive impressions? [msg #139]
2. Would we want to live there if it contained no suffering, hard experiences or pain? [msg #139]
3. The core question: is there anything else «что… имеет для нас значение — кроме того, как мы ощущаем нашу жизнь изнутри» (that matters to us - besides how we feel our life from the inside)? [msg #139]

Illustration the author offers: the hero of DiCaprio in *Inception* spent a whole virtual life in a dream with his wife, in a world the two of them created only for themselves, exactly as they wanted to see it [msg #139].

The post closed with a reader poll (yes, of course - why need anything else; no, that is not enough for me; ambiguous - explain in comments) and invited readers to say whether their opinion changed after watching a TED-Ed animation of Nozick's own view. ### Three axes of a good life - happiness, meaning, psychological richness
Purpose: a model from an article in *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* (described in a post by the Telegram channel Черномырдин нашей психологии (roughly: the Chernomyrdin of our psychology), forwarded by the author) for thinking about what a good life is [msg #664].
1. **Happiness / subjective well-being (hedonic approach).** Traditionally, discussions of the good life contrast a hedonic approach, which puts happiness and subjective well-being first, with a eudaimonic one. Happiness and subjective well-being are often linked to stability, safety, conservatism and religiosity. Surprising finding flagged as «Шок-контент» (shock content): people living in less ethnically diverse communities tend to feel happier. Education changes people's personality but does not make them happier [msg #664].
2. **Meaningfulness (eudaimonic approach).** Meaning partly correlates with happiness, but the two are often singled out as relatively independent stable factors of quality of life. Meaningfulness is linked to persistence in one's work and habitual routines; politically right-leaning, more conformist, authoritarian personalities more often consider their lives more meaningful. The forwarding author recalls a post showing that having children lowers the feeling of happiness but raises the feeling of meaningfulness [msg #664].
3. **Psychological richness (the third axis).** The authors put into this concept curiosity about life and openness to new experiences that change one's views. Having developed special questionnaires to measure psychological richness, they show it often appears as a third axis independent of well-being and meaning across different studies. It is about instability, novelty and a life full of events; it is linked to living in different places and to reading, and to perceiving the world as multifactorial, interconnected and complex [msg #664].

Big Five link: happiness and meaningfulness often correlate with four Big Five factors - extraversion, neuroticism, conscientiousness and agreeableness - but almost never with openness to experience; psychological richness correlates first of all precisely with openness [msg #664].

Overall conclusion of the forwarded post: the classic quality-of-life metrics (happiness and meaning) are tied to a conservative way of life, while psychological richness is the opposite [msg #664].

How to use it: the forwarded post invites readers to think about how they are doing on each of the three scales and which seems most important to them. The illustration of what people might say after living a good life by each definition: hedonists - *"It was fun!"*; existentialists - *"I made a difference!"*; the psychologically rich - *"What a journey!"* [msg #664].

### Source-checking happiness claims
Purpose: the author's own practice, shown in a post about finding errors in lecture material on happiness [msg #134].
1. **Take material from reliable sources** - she tries to; this piece came from a large international university [msg #134].
2. **Always read the primary source behind the reference** («я всегда проверяю ссылки» - I always check the links/references). In the first case the original said something quite different: the lecturer's thesis was mentioned only in intermediate data, and the authors then clarified that things work otherwise and differently in certain categories of people [msg #134].
3. **Discard claims that do not hold** - she decided to throw the thesis out of her arguments as untenable, and planned a separate post on that study [msg #134].
4. **Check the next claim too** - the next thesis turned out to be an overgeneralization from a sample of 50-year-olds, so it also cannot support a statement about how things work in people in general [msg #134].
5. **Report the error** - she planned to write to the university about it [msg #134].

Lesson: two of the happiness theses from a reputable institution's lecture (or its speechwriter's text) turned out to be errors; authority of the source is not a substitute for checking the original study, sample and qualifications [msg #134].

## Detailed insights

### Happiness is subjective - and that is the problem
Happiness is a very subjective thing. It cannot be measured by observing from outside - not by the number of goods, nor by "Ekman" wrinkles around the eyes; one can only ask the person or have them give a self-report. «В этой субъективности — вся соль на рану человечества» (In this subjectivity lies all the salt in humanity's wound): no school, institute or social structure teaches from childhood how to reach happiness or keep it. The main riddle: happiness is a personal experience, yet today (as at least eighty years ago) the things we use to make ourselves happy often fail to [msg #145].

The author's framing question: how is it that some people speak of their happiness with minimal needs met, while others sadly drink themselves to death while buried in gold - what is the secret of the first and the curse of the second? Is there anything that really makes us happier? Her answer: «Ответ: да.» (Answer: yes.) She positions the series as filling in knowledge about our happiness for which we had no "good enough" mentors [msg #145].

### Meaning in life and the signs of its absence
Signs that we are not looking forward to tomorrow: we can sleep 10 hours in a row; notice that we have rarely become hungry; binge series and play until late at night. The author shares that recently she walked herself to zero to tire herself out and fall asleep sooner. «Такое случается, когда мы не предвкушаем завтрашний день» (This happens when we don't look forward to the next day) [msg #130].

Everything we do - having breakfast, going to work, walking a beloved dog - we do for some purpose («зачем-то»); it matters to us that what we do has MEANING. In the author's view, "Why?" is one of the main questions we ask ourselves throughout life [msg #130].

Definition: in this context meaning is why we engage in something, what we get or expect to get from it; it is what the situation is "about" for us, what place it occupies in our system of ideas about ourselves and the world, and why. Examples: a cook's meaning may be «мы кормим людей — мы даём людям пищу, важнейшую вещь для выживания» (we feed people - we give people food, the most important thing for survival); a pensioner may save a little money from the pension in order to be able to help the grandchildren [msg #130].

Function: having meaning gives us an inner support and helps us resist stressors and tension - we understand what we are doing something for, or where we are going. If the words feel elevated, the author says that is right: meanings lie at the depth of our actions; they are «их фундамент и путеводная звезда» (their foundation and guiding star). Meaning is firmly tied to our values [msg #130].

### Values: how the author found hers
The author describes values as something worth multiplying in the world "by any route that works". Two values her parents planted, openly and indirectly, grew most vividly: the value of the life of living beings (the medical "do no harm"), and the value of equal rights and equality of people regardless of faith, race, sex, orientation and social class [msg #101].

Other values she collected "bit by bit", sometimes with trembling wounded fingers. Each is told as a story with its lesson [msg #101]:
- **Gratitude: cooperation, self-worth and contribution.** When you live in a world where everything is impossible, attempts are useless and you should expect only the worst, you have nothing with which to receive sudden help and gifted solutions - they seem to come from another world, for other people. Only arguments for keeping the status quo are at hand. "Thank you" sticks in the throats of those you look up to, and over time you build your own lump - so as not even to ask. Tired of hopelessness, you learn to do the impossible and be all-powerful for those nearby, and "with the change" for yourself; then "thank you" is unnecessary because you do everything yourself. The trap: you begin offering sudden help and gifting solutions - and in your old environment you are now the person from another world. Sometimes a miracle happens when expectations are exceeded: a surprised "thank you" escapes on an exhale, and you feel how you wrecked your health climbing this Everest and just want some "home". You do not want such a life for others; you have seen their efforts all along, so you start clearing that lump with this strong hot word at every possible step. For a long time you cannot make out "thank you" in others' mouths, but hearing it more often, you gradually accept that people were not mistaken and that it really is for you and about you [msg #101].
- **Calm development.** «Выживать — ценно, жить — бесценно» (Surviving is valuable, living is priceless). Only by ceasing to defend oneself can one open oneself; only by ceasing to hide can one explore; only by ceasing to squander oneself can one accumulate and multiply; only in silence can one hear the voice that before could only reflect others, and now let it echo across boundless spaces [msg #101].
- **Letting go, forgiveness and farewell.** The author admits she still sometimes cannot stop clinging, looking for solutions to the last and beyond, fixing what is broken and picking at wounds. She wishes she could turn to rituals of transformation more often and be able to grieve, and says she is gradually learning this transformation, like an alchemist creating something new from ashes [msg #101].
- **Moments of love and closeness.** She was not ready to feel death nearby so early and be unable to ask, say or do anything to keep a person from slipping through her fingers, leaving only love with nowhere to go. Since then she never waits: when she believes something is real and hers, she lives it to all possible depths. «Теперь это основа моей полноты жизни» (Now this is the basis of the fullness of my life) [msg #101].

Her conclusion: whatever path brings us to our values, some will remain ours despite everything. Sometimes this is a path of pain, despair and resistance; but having cleaned values of «шелухи долга, осуждения и навязанной вины» (the husk of duty, judgment and imposed guilt), we can give a new life - to ourselves with them [msg #102].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The author's own errata: two happiness theses from a large international university's lecture turned out wrong - one misrepresented a study's intermediate data, the other overgeneralized from 50-year-olds. The author does not name which theses they were in this post (!) INCOMPLETE [msg #134].
- The claim that the order of value importance persists for years is explicitly the author's impression and suspicion, not a finding [msg #101].
- The "four features of the mind" and the "six beliefs" are attributed to studies the author promises to discuss in detail later; the studies themselves are not named here (!) INCOMPLETE [msg #151].
- The account of capitalism and consumer society is the author's own interpretation; she notes a current shift toward care and inclusiveness that only partly softens it [msg #145].
- The three-axes post is a forwarded view from another channel describing someone else's article; its correlations (with ethnic homogeneity, religiosity, right-wing politics, authoritarianism) are correlations, and the forwarding author reacts with a grimace emoji to the conclusion that happiness and meaning go with a conservative lifestyle [msg #664].
