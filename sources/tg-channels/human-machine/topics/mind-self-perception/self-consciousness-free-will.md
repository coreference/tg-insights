# The self, consciousness and free will

## What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's posts on what the "I" is, whether we have free will, and how consciousness and the sense of self are measured in the lab. It has two strands: the author's own position that the "I" is a model the brain builds and that wording which separates "us" from "our neurons" is a mistake, and news write-ups of research on consciousness under anesthesia and on the brain rhythm behind body ownership. According to the source, this matters because the "I", though "only" a symbol, is still a key tool for planning and cooperating. Better measures of shifts in consciousness are also described as the key to treating insomnia and to bringing people back from coma. The body-ownership findings may help explain schizophrenia, where the sense of self is disrupted [msg #13, #14, #173, #690].

## Core ideas

- **The "I" is a model the brain makes of itself.** The self is a social-modelling routine that the organism turns on itself. It is a convenient symbol and not a thing in the head (see *Five-part definition of the "I"*) [msg #13, #14].
- **Illusory but useful.** At this stage of evolution the "I" is still an important and sometimes necessary tool, so the author says we should make the most of it (see *The "I" as a tool*) [msg #14].
- **No little decider in the head.** The author objects to IIT authors writing that "we, not our neurons" cause our actions. In the author's view this is leftover Cartesian-theatre thinking [msg #50].
- **Sapolsky: no free will, and that makes the world more humane.** In Sapolsky's view, giving up free will does not lead to anarchy or meaninglessness [msg #248].
- **Measuring consciousness without disturbing it.** A breath-squeeze task removes the sound stimuli that distort anesthesia studies, and it detects loss of responsiveness earlier [msg #173].
- **Alpha frequency sets the timing resolution of the bodily self.** A faster parietal alpha rhythm means sharper separation of "my body" from "not my body" [msg #690].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Five-part definition of the "I" (the self as a brain model)
The post opens with a joke-warning that it will be "hard, but good" and suggests forwarding it to Saved Messages. It then builds a definition of the "I" in five layers, each starting with «Я» — [msg #13]:
1. **The first marker of separateness.** "I" is the first word a human being says at about three years old. With it the child marks itself as something separate from the world and from other beings [msg #13].
2. **The narrator of inner experience.** The "I" is what the stream of thoughts in the head is described through, along with bodily sensations, labels for emotions, impulses and decisions to act [msg #13].
3. **The anchor of memory and prediction.** Through the "I", experience is stored in the memory of a specific individual. This lets the individual forecast how successful its activity will be [msg #13].
4. **Social modelling turned inward.** "«Я» — это результат социального моделирования организма, только применяемый к самому себе" (*the "I" is the result of the organism's social modelling, only applied to itself*). The same machinery we use to model other beings is pointed at ourselves [msg #13].
5. **The keeper of continuity (Ship of Theseus).** The "I" keeps up the subjective impression that a person's "Ship of Theseus" stays unchanged, even when all its parts have gradually been replaced [msg #13].

The post is illustrated with a picture: a painting signed "Magritte" of a man in a bowler hat and dark coat whose face is hidden by a green apple. It contains no text [img #13].

The conclusion comes in part 2: "«Я» — это всего лишь удобный символ" (*the "I" is merely a convenient symbol*). In the current evolutionary branch of Homo sapiens, a "describer program" in the brain uses this symbol for two jobs. It reflects the dynamics of the organism's different systems, and it observes the forecasts of neighbouring programs. According to the author, the second becomes possible through special training of so-called "mindfulness" and "self-awareness" [msg #14].

### Breath-squeeze method for tracking loss and recovery of responsiveness under anesthesia
**Purpose.** The method assesses when a person loses and regains consciousness under sedation without external stimuli from the experimenters. The source says it was first described in 2014 by sleep researchers and was used by the MIT/MGH team [msg #173].
1. **Instruct once, then stay silent.** Before the anesthetic was given, 14 volunteers were told to squeeze a special force sensor on every in-breath and release it on every out-breath. After this the scientists gave no more external stimulation [msg #173].
2. **Sedate and watch the squeezing.** The sedative (dexmedetomidine) was then given. When a subject stopped the "inhale-squeeze" pattern, they were counted as having lost responsiveness. When they started it again after the dose was lowered, they were counted as having recovered it [msg #173].
3. **Record the brain throughout.** The whole time, 64 scalp electrodes recorded brain rhythms. These showed the standard pattern of dexmedetomidine's effects, then the reverse as people woke. For example, as people lost responsiveness, the power of the occipital "alpha" rhythm (~10 Hz) fell and the power of slower "delta waves" rose [msg #173].

**Why it was needed.** The usual design has scientists use voice commands to find the moment a sedated volunteer stops responding, which means the anesthetic has taken effect. Guay argues that this sound can itself keep people awake longer and wake them earlier than usual. Guay also works on a second study, which tests whether closed-loop acoustic stimulation (CLAS) strengthens dexmedetomidine's effects. That study also needed a way to assess consciousness without sounds that skew the results [msg #173].

**Results.** (1) Under these conditions, the auditory-stimulation artifacts that had disrupted these EEG patterns in an earlier sound-based study disappeared. (2) Judging by drug concentration in the brain, the breath-squeeze method flagged loss of responsiveness at a lower amount of anesthetic than the sound method. The source reads this as greater sensitivity to change [msg #173].

### Rubber hand illusion protocol
The source calls this a classic method for studying how sensations are perceived. The Karolinska study used it [msg #690].
1. One of the person's real hands is hidden from view. A realistic rubber hand is placed next to it, within the person's field of view [msg #690].
2. For some time the experimenter does the same thing to both hands at once, the hidden real one and the rubber one, for example stroking them [msg #690].
3. The person sees the rubber hand being touched while feeling the touch in their own hand. After a while, in many people these signals synchronise. When the artificial hand is touched they start to feel it as if in their own hand, and they may even flinch if the rubber copy is struck [msg #690].
4. Control: if the stimulation is asynchronous, the feeling soon fades, partly or completely [msg #690].

### Four-method design of the Karolinska body-ownership study
The study had 106 participants and asked how the brain combines visual and tactile signals into one whole sensation. It combined four methods [msg #690]:
- **Behavioural experiment.** This was the rubber hand illusion, above [msg #690].
- **EEG recording of the brain's electrical activity.** This found that the frequency of alpha waves in the parietal cortex, where information about bodily sensations is processed, predicted how accurately participants told touches to their own hand from touches to the rubber one [msg #690].
- **Brain stimulation.** To test whether alpha frequency itself drives the effect, the researchers used non-invasive electrical stimulation to slightly speed up or slow down participants' alpha waves. This clearly changed the sense of body ownership. It also changed whether visual and tactile stimuli were perceived as simultaneous or asynchronous [msg #690].
- **Computational modelling.** The models showed that alpha frequency affects how precisely the brain estimates the timing of sensory signals. So these brain waves regulate the temporal precision of perception, and through that they help form the bodily "I" [msg #690].

## Detailed insights

### The "I" as a tool, not just an illusion
After calling the "I" "merely a convenient symbol", the author adds that it is currently a very important and sometimes necessary tool. It helps a person to:
- understand their own capabilities;
- set goals for themselves;
- build a program of action;
- pass important information to other people;
- cooperate with others to expand the ability to adapt and develop.

The author's stance is pragmatic: «Если в данный исторический период мы устроены так, давайте брать от этого возможный максимум» (*If in this historical period we are built this way, let's get the most we can out of it*). The post says the practice in the next post aims to strengthen exactly this "somewhat unique" ability of our species [msg #14].

### Critique of "we, not our neurons" wording in the IIT free-will paper
The authors of integrated information theory, Tononi, Koch and colleagues, published a paper detailing how fundamental free will follows directly from their theory. Its title is "Only what exists can cause: An intrinsic view of free will". The post quotes their claim in Russian translation: «…у нас есть истинные альтернативы, мы принимаем истинные решения, и мы — а не наши нейроны или атомы — являемся истинной причиной наших волевых действий и несем истинную ответственность за них» (*…we have true alternatives, we make true decisions, and we — not our neurons or atoms — are the true cause of our willed actions and bear true responsibility for them*) [msg #50].

The author objects to the split itself: why do «мы» (*we*) and «нейроны» (*neurons*) keep being separated? As a more grown-up alternative (the source says «по-взрослому»), the author offers this example wording: «Та часть наших мозговых программ, которая высчитывает, какое из решений наберёт наибольшее число аргументов за» (*That part of our brain programs which calculates which of the decisions will gather the largest number of arguments in favour*). The author calls this the minimum ("Ну хотя бы" — *at least that*). In other words, the decider should be described as a computing sub-process of the brain, not as a separate subject [msg #50].

The author's diagnosis is that the wording comes from the "Cartesian theatre" approach, a little decider-person in the head who supposedly *is* "us", and that such wording is still surprisingly hard to kill. The author asks readers why this is so and says they need peace on this subject. The author also mocks the title. It is translated as "Only what exists can compel", and the author asks who "compelled" the authors to think up such a title, since the paper argues for free will. The post signs off with «можно утащить моего человечка со сцены» (*you can drag my little man off the stage*), a joke that turns the Cartesian-theatre image back on the author [msg #50].

### Sapolsky's "Determined": life without free will
The post frames the topic with everyday questions: Could I have acted otherwise? Am I to blame for having been in that state then? Did I really have a choice if I didn't see it at the time? According to the post, Sapolsky lays the free-will question out in terms of anthropology and ethics. The channel shares its own translation of the book's annotation, which makes these claims [msg #248]:
- The book synthesises what we know about how the mind works. It covers the tight link between "reason" and "emotions" and between stimulus and response, both in the moment and across a whole life [msg #248].
- Sapolsky goes through all the main arguments for free will and dismisses them, taking readers through quantum physics and the wildest shores of philosophy [msg #248].
- The history of medicine, and of brain science in particular, is in large part a history of realising that fewer and fewer things are anyone's "fault". For example, for centuries seizures were thought to be a sign of demonic possession [msg #248].
- Sapolsky admits it is very hard to give up our eagerness to judge others and ourselves. He applies his idea of understanding life without free will to some of the most important questions of human behaviour: punishment, morality and living together peacefully [msg #248].
- He concludes that living with the knowledge that there is no free will will be extremely hard day to day. It will not lead to anarchy, meaninglessness or existential malaise. On the contrary, it will make the world much more humane [msg #248].

### Why studying consciousness is hard: the observer problem
Consciousness research runs into a general scientific problem: it is hard to study a system without affecting it. The MIT/MGH approach offers a way to assess consciousness without external stimulation, and it also shows that this way can be more accurate (see *Breath-squeeze method*). Co-author Emery N. Brown says the approach removes a major confound tied to the usual external stimulus. The team wants to use the technique to study other anesthetics. Guay notes that understanding the move from consciousness to unconsciousness could help treat insomnia better. Clarity about how waking happens could raise the chances of bringing people back from coma. Better ways to assess transitions of consciousness are key to both efforts [msg #173].

The channel's own note explains CLAS: clicks are played into headphones during sleep. This is believed to boost the slow electrical brain waves linked to sleep, and so to improve sleep quality and memory consolidation during that period (note signed HGUM/M. K.) [msg #173].

### Alpha frequency and the boundary between self and world
The post opens with everyday examples of body ownership. What tells you that your body is really yours? That the gloves fit your hands, and that it was a passer-by who slipped, not you? Like many things we do easily every day, telling self from everything else is actually a complex brain process. The Karolinska study shows that we can tell our own body from the outside world thanks to a particular rhythmic brain activity in a specific part of the brain [msg #690].

The main finding compared two groups:
- **Higher alpha frequency.** These people were more sensitive to time differences between touches to self and to not-self. They noticed smaller differences than the other group, as if their brain worked at a higher temporal resolution [msg #690].
- **Lower alpha frequency.** These people had a wider "temporal binding window". Asynchronous touches were perceived more as simultaneous, and this loss of timing precision weakened the distinction between the body and the surrounding world [msg #690].

Implications stated by the researchers: lead author Mariano D'Angelo says the results may give new understanding of disorders such as schizophrenia, in which the sense of one's own "I" is disrupted. Co-author H. Henrik Ehrsson says they help explain how the brain combines signals from the body into a whole sense of self, and could help develop better prostheses as well as virtual reality that feels more real [msg #690].

## Evidence and numbers

- MIT/MGH anesthesia study (British Journal of Anaesthesia, lead author Christian Guay, co-author Emery N. Brown): 14 volunteers, dexmedetomidine, 64 scalp EEG electrodes; occipital alpha ~10 Hz falling and delta rising at loss of responsiveness; loss of responsiveness detected at lower brain drug concentration than with the sound method. The source is the MIT news article by David Orenstein (Picower Institute), 21 April 2023. Other co-authors listed: Darren Hight, Gaurang Gupta, Mohammad Mehdi Kafashan, Anhthi Luong, Michael Avidan, Ben Julian Palanca [msg #173].
- Karolinska study: 106 participants. Published as "Parietal alpha frequency shapes own-body perception by modulating the temporal integration of bodily signals" by Mariano D'Angelo, Renzo C. Lanfranco, Marie Chancel and H. Henrik Ehrsson in Nature Communications, online 12 January 2026, doi: 10.1038/s41467-025-67657-w. The news article is from Karolinska Institutet [msg #690].
- Emery N. Brown leads a new MIT/MGH initiative, the Brain Arousal State Control Innovation Center (BASCIC). Its aim is to bring anesthesiology and the neuroscience of brain arousal systems together so they inform and improve each other and drive clinical innovation [msg #173].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The "I"-as-symbol view is the channel author's own synthesis, written in programming metaphors ("describer program", "neighbouring programs"). It is not presented as a single study's finding. The author backs it with the reading list (Hood, Metzinger, two papers) [msg #13, #14].
- (!) CONTRADICTION (between sources): Tononi, Koch et al. argue that IIT implies fundamental free will and true responsibility. Sapolsky argues that all the main arguments for free will fail. The author's critique of the IIT paper is about its wording (the "we" vs "neurons" split). The author does not argue the free-will question in full [msg #50, #248].
- The author openly asks readers why Cartesian-theatre wording persists and gives no answer. The question stays open in the source [msg #50].
- The anesthesia findings are reported through an MIT news article. The claim that sound prolongs wakefulness and causes early waking is Guay's view ("считает К. Гуэй"). The sensitivity claim rests on 14 volunteers [msg #173].
- The Karolinska implications for schizophrenia, prostheses and virtual reality are stated as possibilities ("могут дать", "может способствовать"), not as demonstrated results [msg #690].
