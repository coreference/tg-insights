# Music and the brain

## What this covers

This topic collects what the channel taught about why music moves people biologically: the author's notes from the Coursera course "Music as biology" (music as an outgrowth of speech intonation; how major/minor, rhythm and specific intervals carry mood), a long two-part review of the 2025 neural resonance theory paper by Eleonora E. Harding and co-authors (brain waves physically synchronizing with music, consonance/dissonance as frequency ratios, rhythm vs melody as different frequency bands, emotion as movement across a landscape of stability), Bobby McFerrin's live demonstration of universal note prediction, and the sonification of stars ("music of space"). [msg #157, #683, #684, #685, #720, #25]

The source's reason this matters: it answers why music produces a whole "sea of feelings" — not only joy but longing, awe, bright sadness, goosebumps and anger — and why people understand these emotional nuances despite cultural differences; the author's conclusion is that music is an ordered structure that lets people feel connected across eras and cultures, "literally on the same wave". [msg #683, #684]

## Core ideas

- **Music is built on speech intonation.** The author's course notes hold that music is inseparable from emotion, derives from the intonations of the human voice, and that the most common modes survived historically because they best match speech. [msg #157]
- **Mood has two axes in music.** Valence (+/-) is carried by major vs minor; energy by rhythm, speed and the sharpness of transitions between notes. [msg #157]
- **The old consensus was prediction-reward; the new view is physical resonance.** For years science explained musical pleasure as the reward of correctly predicting the next sounds; Harding et al. (2025) propose that perception does not depend on prediction or on a cultural "dictionary" — brain oscillations physically synchronize with the oscillations in the structure of music. [msg #683]
- **Consonance and dissonance follow frequency ratios.** Simple ratios feel stable, complex ones tense, the classical major/minor triads slightly shaky. [msg #684, #685]
- **Rhythm and melody are different frequency bands** processed in different brain systems (brainstem at 50–4000 Hz for melody; auditory and motor structures at ~1–8 Hz for rhythm). [msg #684]
- **Emotion arises from moving across gradients of stability** (valleys = attractors, ridges = dissonance), and this is said to be universal for all cultures within our species; culture only shapes preferences via Hebbian learning. [msg #684]
- **People share an innate sense of harmony**, as Bobby McFerrin showed by having an audience predict pentatonic notes from direction alone. [msg #720]
- **Stars can be heard**: their radiation can be converted into sound for research or for aesthetic pleasure. [msg #25]

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Music emotion code (from the "Music as biology" notes)
Purpose: explains how musical structure transmits mood, according to the author's notes from the Coursera course "Music as biology", which concerns the biological preconditions behind music becoming established in the human species. The author presents these as her "заметок-размышлений" (notes-reflections) and invited comments from musicians and other interested readers. [msg #157]

1. **Origin in the voice.** Music is inseparable from emotion. It was created on the basis of the intonations of the human voice, and the modes (лады) most frequently found in music became established historically because they correspond most fully to speech. [msg #157]
2. **Valence via mode.** The basic shades of mood — valence/charge (+/-) — are conveyed through major or minor. [msg #157]
3. **Energy via rhythm and articulation.** The degree of energy is conveyed through rhythm, speed (tempo) and the sharpness of transitions between notes. [msg #157]
4. **The most expressive intervals.** Thirds, sixths and sevenths are the brightest carriers of emotional mood: the minor versions are used more often in minor, the major versions in major. [msg #157]
5. **Folk vs classical nuance.** There are small differences between folk music (НМ, народная музыка) and classical music (КМ, классическая музыка): the minor sixth conveys a sad mood more often in classical music, the minor seventh — more often in folk music. [msg #157]
6. **Hypothesis about composers' brains.** The author writes that people who compose music "probably" must have a close connection between the limbic system and the temporal areas (the temple), especially in the right hemisphere, where the zones involved in the intonational side of speech are located. This is hedged as a probability, not a finding. [msg #157]

### Neural resonance theory (Harding et al., 2025)
Purpose: a new explanation of music perception proposed in May 2025 by Eleonora E. Harding and co-authors (paper at https://www.nature.com/articles/s41583-025-00915-4). The author calls the section "Дирижёр нейронного оркестра" (Conductor of the neural orchestra). [msg #683]

- **The problem it addresses.** The prior consensus: we enjoy music thanks to a feeling of reward when we correctly predict the sounds following those already heard. But that does not explain why we feel not only joy but also longing, uplift, awe, bright sadness, goosebumps and anger, nor how we "understand" emotional nuances despite cultural differences. [msg #683]
- **The claim.** Our perception of music does not depend on predictions and does not even require a musical cultural "dictionary". Synchronization between the oscillations in the structure of music and the electrical activity of the brain happens physically. [msg #683]
- **The theory.** Neural resonance theory (ТНР) is a concept of dynamic coordination of rhythms — of the brain, the body and external stimuli. According to the approach, this coordination underlies our attention, perception and actions. [msg #683]
- **Source limitation.** Because the full text is copyrighted, the author shares only its most striking ideas, plus a selection of melodies and rhythms for readers to feel the ideas (that selection was posted as links in a comment and is not part of the source text). [msg #683]

The components of the theory, as the author lays them out, follow as separate frameworks below (neural entrainment, the two mechanisms, the ratio classes, the frequency bands, the stability landscape). [msg #684]

### Neural entrainment — the brain synchronizes its own waves with musical ones
1. **How brain waves arise.** Neurons transmit chemical and electrical signals, causing excitation or inhibition of neighbouring neurons. This produces waves of electrical activity, part of which oscillate in sync and part out of sync. [msg #684]
2. **What frequency means.** Waves have a frequency — the number of rises and falls per second (Hz). From their pattern one can judge alertness, fatigue and cognitive load, as well as disturbances in neurological and mental illnesses (measured with EEG and MEG). [msg #684]
3. **Entrainment.** Waves in the auditory and related networks synchronize with the rhythm and tempo of music (neural entrainment): their phase and frequency reflect the structure of the sound. [msg #684]

### Two synchronization mechanisms: how we hear notes and feel tension
1. **Phase locking ("temporal coding")** — happens in the hearing system; it is what lets us perceive a sound signal as a specific note. [msg #684]
2. **Mode locking** — a physics concept: oscillating systems synchronize their frequencies and phases. Through this we register a sound as harmonious (consonance) or as somehow wrong, unstable (dissonance). [msg #684]

### Three stability classes of intervals by frequency ratio
How a sound is felt depends on the ratio of the frequencies involved: [msg #684]
1. **Stable (consonance)** — when the frequency ratio is simple: unison (one note, 1:1), octave (2:1), perfect fourth (4:3) and perfect fifth (3:2). "здесь живёт консонанс" (here lives consonance). [msg #684]
2. **Tense (dissonance)** — when the ratio is complex: all sevenths (the source gives 28:27 followed by "и др." without listing the rest), seconds (10:9 and 9:8), and tritones — "аккорды дьявола" (the devil's chords), 1.414:1, which split the octave exactly in half. [msg #684]
3. **Slightly shaky** — the triads of classical major (major third 5:4) and minor (minor third 6:5), and the pair of sixths (8:5, 5:3). [msg #684]

The accompanying images give the reference numbers. The first [img #684] shows one octave of the C major scale with frequencies in Hz: C 130.8, D 146.8, E 164.8, F 174.6, G 196.0, A 220.0, B 246.9, C 261.6, labelled "musical intervals", with Root, 3rd, 5th and Octave marked (C, E, G and the upper C — the major triad plus octave). [img #684, #685]

### Two frequency bands of hearing: melody vs rhythm
Rhythm and melody feel different to us "не просто так" (not by chance): they are different frequency ranges. [msg #684]
1. **Melody (notes, timbre, harmony)** — continuing the mechanisms above, part of the waves are heard as melody; here the brainstem synchronizes phases with high-frequency sounds (50–4000 Hz). [msg #684]
2. **Rhythm or pulse** — when waves are low-frequency (delta/theta, ~1–8 Hz), processed in auditory and motor structures of the brain. This is exactly where we get the bodily sense of rhythmic movement and the desire to move in response. [msg #684]
3. **The in-between zone** — when frequencies lie somewhere between these ranges, we hear a buzzing of an unclear note and unclear rhythm. [msg #684]

### Stability landscape of music: attraction and attractors
The author calls attraction the key to emotional impression ("привлекательность (а точнее, «аттракция»)" — attractiveness, or more precisely "attraction"). Attraction is the process by which a dynamic system tends towards certain states in which it gains stability; the point of attraction is the attractor. [msg #684]

Imagining music perception as a landscape: [msg #684]
1. **Unison (the tonic) and major triads** — very stable, like deep valleys with a strong attractor at the bottom. [msg #684]
2. **Minor triads** — still valleys, but shallower, moderately stable. [msg #684]
3. **Dissonances** — unstable, like ridges. [msg #684]

Our expectation, tension and emotional resolution arise as we move between these gradients of stability. This, per the source, is how music becomes emotionally expressive — and it is universal for all cultures within our species. [msg #684]

### Two purposes of space-music sonification
Every star emits radiation in different ranges, and this radiation can be converted into sound. "Music of space" can be created for two purposes: [msg #25]
1. **Research** — to collect information and look for patterns. [msg #25]
2. **Aesthetic pleasure.** [msg #25]

## Detailed insights

### Is music a cultural phenomenon at all?
The author's answer, drawing on the neural resonance review: culture shapes our preferences as a palette of rhythms and tonalities through our "сонастройки" (attunement) within a process of (Hebbian) learning. Styles differ, but our emotional reactions to basic structural patterns remain shared because the underlying neurodynamics are the same. Her conclusion: music is a "delightfully ordered structure" that can serve as a tool against the entropy of the world, and even without special knowledge it lets us feel connected with each other — erasing the boundaries of eras and cultures and becoming literally "on the same wave". [msg #684]

### Why noise cannot be hummed
The author asked herself where white, pink and brown noise fit. Noise is not one frequency but almost all frequencies at once, irregular and differently shaped (by "colour"). That is why we cannot hum noise and why it falls into neither the category of rhythm nor even "roughness". Even if we hear "s-s", "sh-kh" or a "whistle", the sound would have to be filtered around a single frequency (for example 440 Hz) to become a pitch. [msg #684]

### Even rhythms
The author recommends "starting with something sweet" before the long read: a video by Alexander Pushnoy which, in her words, very nicely shows one of the key ideas — why we like even rhythms. The video itself has no transcription in the source. [msg #683]

### Universal note prediction: Bobby McFerrin's demonstration
About 17 years before the post, at the World Science Festival, musician Bobby McFerrin demonstrated in real time that people have an understanding of musical harmony independent of cultural experience. Through a live performance with the audience he showed that people can predict which note will come next in a melody if they are only shown the direction — "Сила пентатоник!" (The power of pentatonics!). The author links this to the "recent discovery of a universal biological basis" of musical perception (the neural resonance post). [msg #720]

### Personal illustration of an early musical drive
The author recalls a childhood exchange: "Mommy, I'll go play." — "Play what?" — "The keys…" — offered as a light P.S. to her notes on music biology. [msg #157]
