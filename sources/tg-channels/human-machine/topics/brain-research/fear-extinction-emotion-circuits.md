# Emotion circuits: fear extinction, anger and aggression

## What this covers

Two strands of brain research as the channel presents them. The first and larger strand is the amygdala: not only as the brain's fear centre but as the place where fear is *unlearned*, through a second, competing neuron population whose extinction memory overrides the fear memory — a process driven by dopamine arriving from the ventral tegmental area, which makes extinction a form of reward learning [msg #599, #600, #602, #603]. The second strand is anger and aggression: a systematic review and meta-analysis supporting a limbic-hyperactivity model in aggression-prone individuals [msg #150], and a quoted evolutionary argument about why slow and hot tempers each pay off in different conditions [msg #264].

The author states why the amygdala findings matter: the MIT work "расширило это представление открытием фундаментальной механики, как именно мы **перестаём** бояться вещей" (expanded this picture with the discovery of the fundamental mechanics of how exactly we *stop* being afraid of things), and she flags this as what she sees as important to note for psychotherapy and for pharmacology [msg #599].

## Core ideas

- **The amygdala is the brain's main fear centre — in humans, mice and other vertebrates — and the relationship between fear and amygdala size/activity runs both ways.** The more a person is exposed to fear, the larger the amygdala becomes and the more active it is; conversely, the more active the amygdala is for any reason at all, the more actively the person will fear a wide variety of things [msg #599].
- **Being afraid and ceasing to be afraid are two separate programmes, not one dial.** The 2020 Tonegawa-lab work showed they are two distinct programmes in the brain that compete with each other within the amygdala, and strengthening one weakens the other [msg #600].
- **Both of them are learned.** Fear is learned from experience of danger, and the end of fear is learned from experience of safety — each written into memory by its own genetically distinct neuron population [msg #600].
- **Fear extinction is reward learning, not suppression.** Dopamine from the VTA activates reward-linked amygdala neurons, and the disappearance of fear follows; as Zhang puts it, unlearning fear "похоже, заключается не в простом его подавлении" (does not appear to consist in simply suppressing it) but is a learning process that works through reward mechanisms [msg #602].
- **The same neurons encode reward feelings, which may explain the bodily relief after danger passes.** Because the Ppp1r1b population doubles as a reward-coding population, it may be why we feel literally physically good — relieved, relaxed or joyful — when we realise an expected danger turned out smaller than feared or did not happen at all [msg #602].
- **This circuit is a key node, not the whole story.** The researchers note that extinction of fear as a complex phenomenon happens across the whole brain and not in this single circuit alone, because our life impressions involve a whole series of associations; but this circuit appears to be the key node for the signal that lets fear fade [msg #603].
- **Aggression goes with limbic hyperactivity and altered temporal/occipital activity.** A systematic review and meta-analysis confirms the limbic-hyperactivity model in individuals with a history of aggression, and shows that at the moment anger or aggression is expressed, activity levels in the temporal and occipital regions are altered [msg #150].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Two competing amygdala programmes: fear memory (aBLA / Rspo2) vs extinction memory (pBLA / Ppp1r1b)

Purpose: to explain how the same structure can both install a fear and dismantle it. The model comes from the Tonegawa lab's 2020 result — "«бояться» и «перестать бояться» — это две отдельные программы в мозге" ("to fear" and "to stop fearing" are two separate programmes in the brain) — which compete inside the amygdala, so that strengthening one weakens the other [msg #600].

1. **Learning that a place is dangerous → fear memory.** When a mouse learns that a place is "dangerous" — because it receives a small electric shock to the paw there — neurons in the *anterior* part of the basolateral amygdala (aBLA) express the gene **Rspo2** and write (encode) the **fear memory** [msg #600].
2. **Learning that the place is no longer dangerous → extinction memory.** When the mouse subsequently learns that the place is no longer dangerous — because it expects the shock and the shock does not recur — neurons in the *posterior* part of the basolateral amygdala (pBLA) express the gene **Ppp1r1b** and encode a **new memory of fear extinction**, which **overcomes the original fear** [msg #600].
3. **The competition.** Because the two programmes compete within the amygdala, extinction is not an erasure of the fear memory but a second, stronger memory laid over it; the balance between the two populations determines whether the animal freezes or relaxes [msg #600].

The new study, led by Xiangyu Zhang and Caitlin Flick, goes beyond this division of labour to reveal the precise mechanism of how we unlearn fear [msg #600].

### The VTA dopamine routing map: which part of the VTA talks to which amygdala population

Purpose: to connect the brain's "surprise detector" to the two fear programmes. The amygdala receives signals from various brain regions; one of them is the **ventral tegmental area (VTA)**, the region involved in reactions to surprises and unexpected events, which instructs the brain to remember those moments — and does so in a special way, with dopamine [msg #602].

The scientists asked whether this "surprise detector" is connected to the amygdala, and the pattern turned out to be quite clear-cut — two separate addressed channels [msg #602]:

1. **Rspo2 genes** take part in the processes of, loosely speaking, "пора бояться" ("time to be afraid"): the Rspo2 cells are the amygdala target for dopaminergic neurons of the **anterior, left and right** parts of the VTA [msg #602].
2. **Ppp1r1b genes** carry out the programmes "перестаём бояться, обошлось" (we stop being afraid, it turned out fine): these cells are the target for "shots" from the **central and posterior** parts of the VTA [msg #602].

Dopamine's wider role is part of the model's reasoning: we most often know dopamine as the "цацу удовольствия" (the trinket/darling of pleasure) — capricious, indiscriminately drawn to anything, such as three hours of TikTok, and sometimes showing up only when it suits it. But in fact dopamine is critically important and works reliably in very many bodily processes — from learning and memorising to the control of attention and motivation (hence ADHD), and even so that we can simply move of our own will (hence Parkinson's disease) [msg #602].

### Proving dopamine is causal, not a bystander: the two experimental blocks

Purpose: to rule out the possibility that dopamine merely "happened to be passing by" or was activated in parallel with extinction rather than driving it; the final parts of the experiment settled this [msg #602, #603]. The tool is **optogenetics** — a technology that lets researchers precisely activate or quiet neurons using light [msg #603].

**Block 1 — controlling the signals coming out of the VTA** [msg #603]:
1. **Suppressing** dopaminergic signals (D-signals) from the VTA to the **pBLA** prevented the disappearance of fear.
2. **Activating** those same VTA→pBLA signals accelerated fear extinction.
3. **Activating** D-signals from the VTA to the **aBLA** reinstated fear — even without any new electric shocks.

**Block 2 — controlling the dopamine receptors inside the amygdala**, specifically the **D1** dopamine receptors on amygdala neurons [msg #603]:
4. In **Ppp1r1b** cells, suppressing the receptors impeded extinction; conversely, increased expression of these receptors disrupted the *recall* of fear and promoted extinction.
5. In **Rspo2** cells, suppressing the receptors reduced freezing behaviour.

Together the manipulations show the circuit is bidirectional and sufficient as well as necessary: the extinction branch can be blocked or sped up at will, and the fear branch can be switched back on by dopamine alone [msg #603].

## Detailed insights

### Why relief feels physically good

The author highlights the point she finds most interesting: because the Ppp1r1b neurons that encode the extinction memory are the same neurons that encode feelings of reward, this may explain why it feels literally physically good — relieved, relaxed or joyful — when we realise that an expected danger turned out to be smaller than expected or did not happen at all [msg #602]. She offers four everyday illustrations of that sensation [msg #602]:
- the feeling you have **after** the peak scary moments, if you like horror films or rollercoasters;
- when an unexpected meeting with your manager turns out to be a promotion;
- when you partied before an exam, your head is splitting, and the professor has just announced a postponement — with the aside "(потому что тусил вместе с вами)" (because he was partying with you);
- all of these are presented as the same mechanism: "Это оно!" (That's it!) [msg #602].

### Anger and aggression in the brain

The channel forwards a write-up (from Психиатрия & Нейронауки, translated by E. A. Sizikova, under the title "НЕЙРОБИОЛОГИЯ ГНЕВА И АГРЕССИИ: РОЛЬ ЛИМБИЧЕСКОЙ ГИПЕРАКТИВНОСТИ" — "The neurobiology of anger and aggression: the role of limbic hyperactivity") of a systematic review and meta-analysis by Maja Nikolic et al. in *Progress in Neuro-Psychopharmacology & Biological Psychiatry*. Its results confirm the model of **limbic hyperactivity** in individuals with a history of aggression, and additionally show that **at the moment anger or aggression is expressed**, the level of activity in the **temporal and occipital** regions is altered [msg #150, img #150].

### The evolutionary trade-off of temper

The author quotes Patricia Churchland on why neither temperament is simply better: a slow-to-anger disposition may serve animals better in times of peace and plenty, whereas a hot-tempered disposition may be more beneficial in times of scarcity or war [msg #264]. She adds a personal, self-deprecating application of the criterion to herself — "That's how I know I'm a creature of peace 🕊️" — i.e. her own slowness to anger as evidence of living in peace and plenty [msg #264].

## Evidence and numbers

- **MIT study, dated 25 April by the channel** ("MIT* от 25 апреля" — MIT, of 25 April), from the Picower Institute for Learning and Memory; it expanded the picture of the amygdala as a fear centre by discovering the fundamental mechanics of how we stop being afraid [msg #599]. The author notes MIT is "моего любимого Массачусетского технологического института" (my beloved Massachusetts Institute of Technology) [msg #599].
- **Tonegawa lab, 2020:** showed fear and the end of fear are two separate, competing programmes, and identified the aBLA/Rspo2 fear-memory and pBLA/Ppp1r1b extinction-memory populations in mice, using a paw-shock paradigm and the subsequent non-recurrence of the expected shock [msg #600].
- **The new study** (Zhang and Flick, Tonegawa lab) mapped VTA subregion → amygdala population connectivity and used optogenetics plus D1-receptor manipulation to establish dopamine's causal role; see *Proving dopamine is causal* above [msg #600, #602, #603].
- **Nikolic et al. systematic review and meta-analysis** in *Progress in Neuro-Psychopharmacology & Biological Psychiatry*: limbic hyperactivity confirmed in people with a history of aggression; temporal and occipital activity altered during expression of anger/aggression. The source gives no effect sizes or sample counts [msg #150].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- **Scope limit stated by the researchers themselves:** fear extinction as a complex phenomenon takes place across the whole brain, not in this single circuit, because our impressions of life involve a whole series of associations; the circuit is nonetheless described as "по-видимому — ключевой узел" (apparently the key node) for the signal that makes fear fade — note the hedge "apparently" [msg #603].
- **Hedged causal language:** Zhang's claim is hedged ("похоже" / appears), and the author's own reward-explanation of the relief feeling is offered as a possibility ("это может объяснять" — this may explain), not an established fact [msg #602].
- **Species scope:** the mechanistic findings are from mice (paw-shock paradigm, optogenetics); the amygdala-as-fear-centre claim is asserted for humans, mice and other vertebrates, but the source does not report human tests of the dopamine mechanism [msg #599, #600, #603].
- **Potential conflict-of-interest context the author discloses:** Zhang is currently a senior research scientist at Orbimed, a healthcare investment company; the author notes this in a footnote without commenting on it [msg #602].
- **Applications left open:** the author says the mechanism is important to note for psychotherapy and pharmacology [msg #599] but does not specify any intervention; she instead asks readers how it could be used in real life, what could help to live through frightening moments more easily, and how to become braver [msg #603].
