# Small actions and social change: beating helplessness

## What this covers

This topic gathers the channel author's material on why individual and small-scale action is worth doing even when a person feels like "a grain of sand in an indifferent cosmos". It covers the nirvana fallacy as the belief that blocks action, five historical cases in which a single or local action grew into systemic change, the three factors that made those processes succeed, eight "mechanics of change" to keep in mind when one's contribution feels meaningless, and an earlier post arguing that helping others is itself a remedy for helplessness and depression. The author frames it as relevant to changes toward inclusivity and to coping with a period of global catastrophes [msg #554, #555, #556, #11].

## Core ideas

- **Helplessness is a belief that stalls change.** After five years of global catastrophes many people feel it is pointless to do anything — "the big system can't be beaten" — and conclude it is better to save energy and stay silent until it passes. The author says this belief can stall change work at every level and names it the nirvana fallacy [msg #554].
- **Big change follows different laws than the fallacy assumes.** Historical facts show that large changes — social initiatives protecting minorities or acts of resistance — grew out of individual or local actions that were strategically amplified [msg #554, #555].
- **Success rested on visibility, pressure and systematic action** (see *Three success factors of historical change*) [msg #555].
- **There are concrete mechanics by which small contributions count**, from the ripple effect to micro-legacies, community, leverage points, turning pain into purpose, rewriting the narrative, and finding meaning in the struggle itself (see *Eight mechanics of change*) [msg #556].
- **Helping others is an antidote to helplessness.** Helping fights powerlessness and depression, invests in one's own meaning of life, and increases support and faith in the good, which makes self-care, stability and planning easier [msg #11].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### The nirvana fallacy (the belief that blocks action)
The author defines the **nirvana fallacy** as the situation "когда решение или действие отвергается, если оно не идеально и не решает проблему целиком" (*when a solution or action is rejected if it is not ideal and does not solve the problem entirely*). The lived form of it is the feeling of the senselessness of doing anything: "большую систему не победишь, зачем пытаться? Лучше экономить силы и молчать в тряпочку, пока это не кончится" (*you can't beat the big system, why try? Better to save your strength and keep quiet until this is over*). The author's context: over the last 5 years society has faced a series of global catastrophes before which an individual seems like "маленькой песчинкой в безразличном космосе" (*a small grain of sand in an indifferent cosmos*). The danger, per the author, is that the belief can halt work on changes "на самых разных уровнях" (*at the most diverse levels*) — including the changes toward inclusivity that prompted the post. The author closes with a Buddhist pun: "мечты о нирване не избавят от дуккха" (*dreams of nirvana will not rid you of dukkha*, i.e. waiting for a perfect solution does not relieve suffering). The author points readers to Russian-language examples of the fallacy on the channel @mental_radar (post 410) [msg #554].

The author's counter-argument is historical: real facts show that big changes "работают по иным законам" (*work by different laws*). He presents "only an excerpt" from his earlier work, deliberately, "Дабы не звучать излишне революционно" (*so as not to sound overly revolutionary*), enough to demonstrate the importance of even small steps — that excerpt is the five cases and the eight mechanics below [msg #554].

### Three success factors of historical change
Purpose: explains what turned single actions in the five historical cases (see *Historical cases*) into systemic change; the author asks "Что обеспечивало успешность этих процессов?" (*What ensured the success of these processes?*) and answers with three factors [msg #555]:

1. **Visibility (Видимость).** Each action drew attention to systemic injustice, provoking conversations and gathering support. Example from the cases: Carson's book created public resonance; Gandhi's march became a national and international symbol [msg #555].
2. **Pressure (Давление).** Sustained movements made the status quo untenable, often through financial, social or political tension. Example: the Montgomery bus boycott put financial pressure on the bus system; international sanctions and divestment pressured apartheid South Africa [msg #555].
3. **Systematic action (Систематические действия).** Consistent action led to legal or political changes because authorities were forced to respond. Example: Browder v. Gayle, the Representation of the People Act, negotiations with Indian leaders [msg #555].

The author's conclusion: "индивидуальные или локальные действия, если их стратегически усилить, могут привести к глубоким системным изменениям" (*individual or local actions, if strategically amplified, can lead to deep systemic changes*) [msg #555].

### Eight mechanics of change to remember when a contribution feels pointless
Purpose: Part 2 of the series, titled "Что важно помнить, когда отдельный вклад кажется бессмысленным?" (*What is important to remember when an individual contribution seems meaningless?*). Derived by the author from the historical examples, these are components that contribute not only to changing a situation but also to raising people's quality of life — "и даже тех, кто не успеет застать все результаты своего труда" (*even those who will not live to see all the results of their work*) [msg #556].

1. **Ripple effect (Эффект расходящихся кругов).** Small purposeful actions create ripples that extend far beyond what you can see. Over time the cumulative effect of many people doing similar things can lead to meaningful, systemic change. Therefore voting, supporting grassroots organisations and even showing kindness in personal interactions all play a role [msg #556].
2. **The power of legacy and passing the baton (Сила наследия и передача эстафетной палочки).** History is full of people who fought for justice, equality and truth even when the odds seemed impossible. Most did not live to see the fruits of their labour, but "длинная дуга прогресса отражает их усилия" (*the long arc of progress reflects their efforts*). By contributing to that arc, people can align their actions with something larger than themselves. When no immediate solution seems possible, the author says it is important to remember to equip future generations — through education, stories or concrete steps you contribute to a foundation on which others can keep building change [msg #556].
3. **Micro-legacies (Микронаследия).** On a smaller scale it makes sense to create micro-legacies — things that directly improve the lives of those around you. The listed forms: teaching skills, sharing resources, mentoring, and even advocating for those who feel alone. These can leave a long-term positive impact [msg #556].
4. **Finding strength in community (Обретение силы в сообществе).** Connecting with like-minded people can provide both tangible influence and emotional resilience. Whether through activism, mutual aid or smaller local efforts such as suitable public spaces, cooperation amplifies strength and helps soften feelings of isolation or helplessness [msg #556].
5. **Knowing useful leverage points (Знание полезных точек воздействия).** Targeting points of influence in systems — from supply chains to institutions and policy — and acting on them as a group can create exponential change. Hence even simply supporting and joining groups that advocate for specific changes can be key to having tangible impact [msg #556].
6. **Turning pain into purpose (Превращение боли в цель).** Instead of suppressing feelings of helplessness or anger, use them as fuel for action. Many movements and breakthroughs began because someone said: "Это невыносимо, и я отказываюсь это принимать" (*This is unbearable, and I refuse to accept it*). Use frustration to feed art, activism, innovation; "Самовыражение преобразует энергию в действие" (*Self-expression transforms energy into action*) [msg #556].
7. **Rewriting the narrative (Переписывание повествования).** Acknowledge injustice while refusing to let it define the future. A mindset that has room for resistance and hope creates a different context for one's actions — one where despair does not have the last word. The author's signature line: "Оптимизм не наивен — это акт бунта в мрачные времена" (*Optimism is not naive — it is an act of rebellion in dark times*) [msg #556].
8. **Meaning is in the struggle (Смысл — в борьбе).** Continuing the previous idea, the author cites Camus, who argued that the very act of resisting the absurd in anything has meaning. Even if your personal chances of success are small, there is dignity and purpose in the struggle itself; the struggle becomes its own form of rebellion against hopelessness. The author adds: "Важно помнить и говорить, когда какое-то положение дел не нормально" (*It is important to remember and to say when some state of affairs is not normal*) [msg #556].

### What helping gives the helper (three effects)
In an early post (March 2022) the author lists, as a three-line anaphora "Помогая, мы…" (*By helping, we…*), what helping others does for the helper [msg #11]:

1. **It fights helplessness and depression** — "Помогая, мы боремся с бессилием и депрессией" (*By helping, we fight powerlessness and depression*) [msg #11].
2. **It invests in one's own meaning of life** — "Помогая, мы вкладываемся в свой смысл жизни" (*By helping, we invest in our own meaning of life*) [msg #11].
3. **It increases the amount of support, giving and faith in the good and the living — for oneself and for others.** The mechanism the author proposes: the more of this there is in us, the easier it is to reach states of readiness for self-care, contact and stability, planning, forecasting and development [msg #11].

## Detailed insights

### Historical cases: single actions that led to big results
Part 1 of the series, "Следы в истории: как отдельные действия приводили к большим результатам" (*Traces in history: how individual actions led to big results*), gives five cases, each structured as an action and its results [msg #555]:

1. **Environmental movement.** Action: Rachel Carson's book *Silent Spring* (1962) exposed the dangers of pesticides such as DDT for the environment and health. Results: the book caused public resonance and drew national attention to environmental problems; public awareness led to policy changes, including a nationwide ban on DDT in agriculture in 1972; in 1970 the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) was created, establishing a regulatory framework for environmental protection. Lesson: one book made a hidden harm visible and shifted national policy [msg #555].
2. **Civil rights movement (USA).** Action: Rosa Parks's refusal to give up her seat on a segregated bus in Montgomery, Alabama, in 1955. Results: her arrest mobilised the African American community, leading to the Montgomery bus boycott and a mass protest lasting more than 1 year; the boycott put significant financial pressure on the bus system and exposed systemic racism; ultimately, in Browder v. Gayle (1956), the Supreme Court ruled bus segregation unconstitutional, banning segregation on public transport at the federal level. Lesson: one refusal became a sustained, economically costly collective action that forced a legal ruling [msg #555].
3. **Indian independence movement.** The author adds the aside "кстати о бизнесе" (*speaking of business*). Action: Gandhi's Salt March in 1930 — a 240-mile walk to the sea protesting the British monopoly on salt production. Results: the march drew national and international attention and became a symbol of Indian disobedience to British rule; it spurred broad participation in civil disobedience, including boycotts of British goods; British authorities were forced to negotiate with Indian leaders, leading to greater political autonomy and eventually independence in 1947 [msg #555].
4. **British suffragette movement.** Action: Emmeline Pankhurst and the Women's Social and Political Union (WSPU) launched militant campaigns — protests, hunger strikes and civil disobedience — to demand votes for women. Results: the visibility and persistence of their efforts kept the issue in public consciousness and pressured Parliament to act; during World War I women took on traditionally male roles, further proving their capabilities; in 1918 the Representation of the People Act gave the vote to women over 30 who met a property qualification, and full suffrage was won in 1928 [msg #555].
5. **Anti-apartheid movement (South Africa).** Action: internal resistance — strikes and protests led by Nelson Mandela and other leaders the source does not name ("и др.") — combined with international solidarity campaigns, including boycotts and sanctions. Results: protests and strikes made governing South Africa under apartheid increasingly difficult; international economic sanctions and divestment created economic pressure; in 1990 Mandela was released from prison and negotiations to dismantle apartheid began; by 1994 apartheid ended and Mandela became South Africa's first democratically elected president [msg #555].

### Helping in practice: the Takie Dela interview (2022)
The author's March 2022 post on helping was attached to an article by the Russian outlet «Такие дела» (*Takie Dela*): a large interview with Russian citizens who were supporting those having a hard time, regardless of their country of origin. According to the author, the article describes opportunities for different spheres of life and social groups — ranging from OVD-Info (ОВД-инфо) to obtaining grants in foreign laboratories. The author shares it with the words "Немедленно множу добро" (*Immediately multiplying good*), i.e. sharing the material is itself framed as an act of spreading help [msg #11].
