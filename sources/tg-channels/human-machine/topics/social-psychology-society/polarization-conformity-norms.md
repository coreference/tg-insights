# Group polarization, conformity and social norms

## What this covers

This topic gathers the author's series on group polarization (parts 1 and 2 plus a follow-up "outside the hypotheses"), her posts on traditions and conformity (including a rereading of the Asch, Milgram and Zimbardo experiments and the single-dissenter effect), a post on why news, rumors and memes spread, and a light case study of unwritten Serbian hospitality norms. The author's driving question is why people in an argument do not converge on a middle opinion when the other side brings new data, but instead dig in and defend their positions "ever more fiercely" — and, more broadly, what kind of social environment and traditions we want to build, given that people are shaped by the interaction of genetics and environment [msg #28, #52, #91].

This part adds one new post (#615) with its photo album (#607–#614 and the image in #615): the author's report on a lecture about the "crisis of masculinity" and her own open question list about what masculinity can mean in 2025. It **extends** the topic's existing material on social norms (so far: conformity, traditions as system rigidity, and the unwritten norms of Serbian hospitality) with gender norms as a case of a social construct whose boundaries are currently being renegotiated. The source frames it as mattering because men find it "extremely hard" to understand what to do so as not to be caught between two condemned poles [msg #615].

Relation to the existing knowledge file: the existing file treats traditions as a stability tool (old order passed between generations) and conformity as adjusting to the majority's expected norm. The new post describes a situation where the old norm of manhood has lost its force while a new one has not formed («старые опоры утрачены, новые — не выстроены»). The source does not itself link this post to the earlier tradition or conformity posts; the connection is only by subject. Nothing in the new post contradicts the existing file [msg #615].

## Core ideas

- **Groups push opinions toward the poles.** Contrary to the early expectation that a group would settle near its members' average, discussion in a group makes people's answers more radical (the risky shift, later generalized as group polarization), in both decisions and expressed attitudes [msg #28].
- **Arguing with an opponent radicalizes.** The more we conflict with a person of a different opinion, the more radical our views become after the dispute [msg #28].
- **Fierce defence of a position rests on threat and status.** After extending four polarization hypotheses, the author concludes that the drivers are fear for identity, fear of social isolation and the desire to take and keep a higher place in the hierarchy — threat, thirst for status, or both [msg #55].
- **Perceived majority size bends stated opinion.** Kuran's preference falsification model: the larger people believe the majority is, the more strongly they choose its opinion [msg #57].
- **The famous conformity experiments were products of their era.** Conformity was a value under McCarthyism and became something condemned in the 1960s; closer analysis shows far less blanket conformity than the popular account, and one dissenter is enough to break it [msg #52, #91, img #97].
- **Traditions are a stability tool whose rigidity has to be balanced.** At either extreme (rigid or chaotic) a system adapts poorly; the author favours a "middle way" of stable but revisable roles and rules [msg #86].
- **News spreads when it is novel yet confirms what we already "know".** Novelty captures attention; confirmation of folk knowledge rewards us with dopamine; even disgust-driven retelling spreads the meme [msg #369].

- **Social movements have moved the boundaries of acceptable masculinity.** Feminist, queer and transgender movements and cancel culture have started changing what is acceptable within habitual masculinity, and the author says this has its upsides [msg #615].
- **Men face a double bind.** Guys find it extremely hard to know how to act so as not to end up «между молотом и наковальней» ("between the hammer and the anvil") — between «токсичной маскулинностью» ("toxic masculinity") and «бабством» ("being womanish") [msg #615].
- **Old supports lost, new ones not built.** The lecture's diagnosis (really about late-20th-century trends): the man is still expected to succeed, while the emancipated woman now values emotional openness and shared childcare as much as or more than his earnings [msg #615].
- **If masculinity is a social construct, its building blocks are open to question.** The author asks which "кубики" ("building blocks") the 2025 palette of masculinity contains, and whether we have grown up enough to stop tying the non-biological part of ourselves to our sex [msg #615].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Group polarization: history of the concept
1. **Starting assumption (1920s–1930s).** G. Allport and other researchers of the 1920s–1930s assumed that individuals make more extreme decisions than groups. This created the expectation that people together would express a position close to the average riskiness of the group's members [msg #28].
2. **The risky shift (1961).** J. Stoner, a student at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, noticed the so-called «сдвиг риска» (risky shift): after discussing decisions in a group, people gave more radical answers to the same questions they had just filled in on their own. Notably, the difference persisted even when the group and individual decisions were made in reverse order [msg #28].
3. **Generalization.** Although Stoner's work was unfinished, it triggered a surge of research, and by the end of the 1960s it became clear that the risky shift is one of the effects of a shift toward the poles of positions within a group. S. Moscovici and M. Zavalloni later named this «групповой поляризацией» (group polarization), and experimental data confirming the regularity have since accumulated [msg #28].
4. **Scope.** Group polarization shows up not only in decision-making but also in the attitudes people express toward phenomena — attitude or belief polarization [msg #28].
5. **Current explanations.** The mechanism is now explained through two social theories: social comparison (motivation for recognition and approval) and social identity (motivation for belonging and for self-expression as a member of a particular group). There are also ongoing discussions of how being in a particular information bubble and dividing people into «своих» and «чужих» ("us" and "them") affect opinion shift. A further finding the author highlights: the more we conflict with a person of a different opinion, the more radical we become in our views when leaving such a dispute. The keywords the author attaches: polarization, groupthink and anchoring [msg #28].

### Four hypotheses of group polarization, extended to explain fierce defence of positions
Purpose: the author found that a webinar on polarization did not answer her real question — why people hold a position is not the same as why they defend it with fury in a «споре» (dispute). One would expect that when the other side adds its data, people would move toward a middle opinion — «но нет» (but no). She therefore takes the existing theoretical approaches to polarization (the post links the Wikipedia section on major theoretical approaches) and, under the heading «Достраиваем» ("we build it out"), extends each one into an explanation of why people strengthen and defend their previous positions [msg #52].

1. **Hypothesis 1 — prestige of the radical opinion.** Short meaning: «быть несогласным — круто» ("being in disagreement is cool"). Extension:
   - If a society rewards disagreement, that position is more likely to lead to higher places in the hierarchy.
   - Drawing on Sapolsky and testosterone research, one can suppose polarization would level out if a society rewarded conformity instead. According to the author, this is exactly what experiments of the 1950s showed, when McCarthy was in power and the ability to fit into the dominant social paradigm was the highest value. By the late 1950s–early 1960s personal freedom and independence came to the fore, and what had been a virtue appeared in a quite different light — in Zimbardo's Stanford prison experiment and Milgram's obedience-to-authority experiment.
   - Signature line: «За что боролись, про то и поляризация» (roughly "what people fought for is what polarization is about" — i.e. polarization follows whatever the society rewards) [msg #52].
2. **Hypothesis 2 — social identity theory.** Short meaning: «я — часть группы «своих», не хочу относиться к «чужим»» ("I am part of the group of 'us', I don't want to belong to 'them'"). Extension: if we identify with a certain group, changing our opinion on our own on a question that seems important for belonging to it may be hard. Possibly because we perceive the change through fear of social isolation and of our identity being blurred; therefore the more dependent we are on this, the harder we resist, in order to preserve our integrity [msg #52].
3. **Hypothesis 3 — social comparison theory, or the drive for leadership.** Short meaning: «я — не просто член, но и лидер группы мнения» ("I am not just a member but also a leader of the opinion group"). The theory says people first compare their own ideas with those of other group members, then observe and evaluate what the group values and prefers; then, to gain recognition, they take a position similar to everyone else's but slightly more extreme, presenting themselves as the group's "leaders". Extension: (a) again hierarchy, the top of it — as in hypothesis 1; (b) the stronger the pressure to move a person off a position, the stronger the resistance must be to keep it [msg #52].
4. **Hypothesis 4 — persuasive arguments / informational influence within the group.** Short meaning: the more information there is in favour of the position of the group one feels one belongs to, the less interesting the alternatives. In essence it describes information bubbles and confirmation bias:
   - The more closed my circle of communication and information sources, the fewer other viewpoints I hear.
   - The more I hear about something and the more plausible a belief seems, the more I consider other opinions mistaken or based on incomplete information («вот если бы они знали ВСЮ правду…» — "if only they knew ALL the truth…"). When I hear something matching what I know, I strengthen my position; when I hear the opposite or something different, I decide that people don't know something and therefore draw wrong conclusions. And then it becomes all the more interesting to strengthen my views.
   - The author's objection: this theory does not explain confrontation, which leads to her extension (the next framework) [msg #52].

### Three qualities that make an information bubble attractive
The author's extension of hypothesis 4: an information bubble is not all the posts a social network feeds us. It is the posts created by those who, as it seems to us:
1. **"Close" («близкий»)** — understands us: is somehow like us, has had similar experience or has gone through the same difficulties.
2. **"Competent" («компетентный»)** — knows how the current situation works: what is right and wrong in it, who is to blame.
3. **"Leader" («лидер»)** — in addition, understands what needs to be done for us to feel better [msg #52].

Consequence, in the author's opinion: if these three parts are missing, we will look for other bubbles — in the opposition, in the underground, anywhere — until we find one that has them. Or until we give up and prefer not to read any of it, outside all bubbles (though some small bubbles will surely remain, even at the level of kitchen conversations with neighbours) [msg #55].

Where confrontation comes from, in the author's view: from the wish for a clear picture of the world without contradictions, and for a course of action that is understandable, sufficiently definite and predictable [msg #55].

### Three root drivers of fierce position defence
The author's summary («Итого») of the whole analysis — at the base, it seems, lie:
1. **Fear for identity** («страх за идентичность») — from the social identity hypothesis: changing a belonging-relevant opinion threatens to blur who we are.
2. **Fear of social isolation** («страх социальной изоляции») — losing the group of "us".
3. **The desire to take and hold a higher place in the hierarchy** («желание занять и удержать место повыше в иерархии») — from the prestige and leadership hypotheses.

Thus the main mechanics of a more stormy defence of one's opinion in an argument seem to come from a sense of threat or a thirst for status, and sometimes both. The author presents this as a hypothesis («похоже», "it seems") and invites constructive criticism [msg #55].

Afterword the author attaches, from the Zhuangzi (chapter IV, "Among people"): to have outstanding abilities and impeccable honesty but not see what lies in another's soul, not to understand the human heart and to preach goodness, justice and noble deeds before a hard-hearted ruler means showing off one's beauty by exposing another's ugliness — «А тому, кто доставляет неудовольствие другим, люди, конечно же, тоже будут стараться навредить» ("And people will of course also try to harm the one who displeases others") [msg #55].

### Preference falsification model (Timur Kuran)
Context: a follow-up "outside the hypotheses" to the polarization post. The author found it in an article about a recent Levada poll on a sensitive subject («сами знаете, о чём», "you know what about"): Philipp Chapkovsky found out how many Russians may actually support the authorities' actions when a list experiment is run and people's fear for their safety is lowered; the author especially liked the comparison with a direct poll and calls it a separate topic [msg #57].

The article mentions Timur Kuran's book «Частная правда, публичная ложь» (Private Truths, Public Lies), which describes the preference falsification model: people prefer to choose the majority opinion more strongly the larger they believe that majority to be [msg #57].

The author's question and reading: does that make the key to changing an interlocutor's view the "old-and-not-at-all-kind" argumentum ad populum, the appeal to the majority — «большинство не может ошибаться, ты один такой дурак» ("the majority can't be wrong, you're the only fool")? She hears in it social isolation mixed with a threat to status — the same drivers as in her summary above [msg #57].

### Tradition on the flexibility-rigidity spectrum of a system
Purpose: to judge whether tradition matters for society by viewing it as part of the system of human relationships ("one puzzle piece from the systems approach") [msg #86].

1. **Definition.** In Thomas Green's encyclopedia of folklore (referenced by Wikipedia), a tradition is a belief or behaviour with special meaning that came from the past and is passed from generation to generation within a group of people. Ozhegov's dictionary says something similar but covers not only customs but also ways of thinking, worldviews, ways of acting and preferences in taste (the author jokes: probably so as not to argue about them at least within one's community). In other words, tradition is «призма взглядов и набор ритуалов для поддержания установленного порядка группой людей» ("a prism of views and a set of rituals for maintaining an established order by a group of people") — something stable and conservative, predictable and understandable [msg #86].
2. **Range of forms.** A tradition can be light and cheerful, like exchanging gifts at New Year, or a gruelling ordeal, like the initiation rite of the Gbaya people. Either way, these properties can give adherents a very powerful sense of community and safety, and "perhaps there is no better time to lean on traditions than a time of change and war" [msg #86].
3. **Systems view.** Relationships — with the outside world and inside communities — undergo regular changes, big and small, everyday and sudden. The reaction depends on how flexible or rigid the system's boundaries are. This is a spectrum: from striving to resist and maximally preserve the existing order, to changing one's structure and ways of interacting almost without hesitation ("if it must be, it must be"). For more, the author points to David Olson's circumplex model [msg #86].
4. **Four domains where flexibility/rigidity shows** (tradition can set reference points for all of them):
   - how ideas are proposed and decisions made — "leadership";
   - how processes are observed — "control";
   - how rules are followed — "discipline";
   - how responsibility is distributed — "roles in the system" [msg #86].
5. **Extremes reduce adaptation.** The closer a system gets to an extreme of the spectrum — the chaotic or the rigid pole — the harder it is for the system and its members to interact with each other and with the world, and the lower the ability to adapt [msg #86].
6. **Exception: acute crisis.** Extreme values can be adaptive only in states of short-term powerful crisis: when it is important either to hold the boundaries with all one's strength under pressure of a factor unfavourable to the system, or when any means are good for survival and it does not matter who does what when — «война всё спишет» ("war will write everything off") and «победителей не судят» ("winners are not judged") [msg #86].
7. **Peacetime costs of rigidity.** In peacetime, rigid unquestionable traditions will rather shackle communities, hindering their development and the expression of members' personal abilities — for example, a tradition by which an unchanging leader stays in power until death and does not correct course despite changes in "the climate situation and the wishes of the pack" [msg #86].
8. **Costs of no tradition.** Equally, a complete absence of traditions can bring chaos into people's minds and lead to loss of life meanings, orientation, understanding of oneself and one's place in the world [msg #86].
9. **The "middle way".** Hence the author sees it as important to keep a balance between stability and change, helped by the position of the «срединного пути» ("middle way"): roles and rules are stable, but the possibility to revise them is open, common tasks can be discussed together, and the minority's opinion is taken into account [msg #86].

### Two-step recipe of a spreading meme
Question: why do some news items provoke a strong response so that we retell them, while others are quickly forgotten or ignored? The author's answer (an article of hers from 15.05.2021, republished; based on Alexandra Arkhipova's lecture "Memes in social anthropology"): spreading news has two properties — (1) it creates an impression of novelty, but at the same time (2) it meets expectations. The paradox is captured by the saying «Никогда не было и вот опять» ("It never happened before, and here it is again") [msg #369].

Mechanism behind the two properties:
- **Folk psychology as fertile ground.** Such news lands on our folk psychology — intuitively understood everyday knowledge obtained from parables and myths, rituals and omens, grandparents and other sources of folk wisdom. These contain extracts of ideas and "valuable instructions" that long helped us survive and were evolutionarily useful. Such extracts are called memes; the word was introduced by Richard Dawkins [msg #369].
- **Why confirmation rewards.** What helps survival is fixed at the level of biochemistry: when we make predictions about something (which happens regularly when interacting with the world), dopamine is released; if the prediction turns out right, this works as a reward and dopamine release increases. A right prediction is confirmation that our knowledge of the world is correct — which is why confirmation of folk knowledge works so well [msg #369].
- **Why novelty matters.** Why not savour the same familiar idea? Because of boredom. We notice the new because it is unfamiliar, and unfamiliar means we must work out whether it is useful or dangerous; this grabs attention. Examples: bushes nearby suddenly rustle — a tiger? Or you are running through the jungle and feel a burning in your back — a poisonous liana? If you don't pay attention, you can die quickly. There is even a separate neural network for this, the salience network, «что-то вроде «сети заметности, выпуклости»» ("something like a 'network of noticeability, prominence'") [msg #369].

The recipe:
1. **Step 1 — grab attention with novelty**, at least some novelty.
2. **Step 2 — give something intuitively understandable and close to the person's experience** — their everyday knowledge, folk psychology — and create a pleasant feeling that they understand how the world works, i.e. that they are clever, well adapted and will live longer [msg #369].

The insidious twist: even if the news seemed unpleasant or stupid, we will probably still tell it to someone else — just with the message «фу, какая гадость» ("ugh, how disgusting"). That other person will decide something of their own and may take it seriously. «Результат один — мем успешно размножается» ("The result is the same — the meme successfully reproduces") [msg #369].

### Two trends of late-20th-century masculinity (old supports lost, new ones not built)
Purpose: the lecture's explanation of why masculinity "nowadays is who-knows-what". The historian described it, in the author's words, as «фиг знает, что вообще такое» ("who the heck knows what it is at all"). The author notes that in fact the lecture was mostly about trends of the end of the 20th century, summed up as «старые опоры утрачены, новые — не выстроены» ("the old supports are lost, the new ones are not built"). The two trends pull in different directions [msg #615]:

1. **The man must still be successful.** On the one hand the requirement of success has not gone away. Under instability and the neoliberal economic order meeting it is getting harder and harder, because competition is high. So the old demand stays while the conditions for fulfilling it worsen [msg #615].
2. **The emancipated woman needs something else.** On the other hand the woman has emancipated, and it is no longer necessary for her that the man earns a lot. What now matters as much (if not more) is that:
   - **he shows feelings** — the stated reason: «ведь она — женщина, а не посудомойка» ("after all she is a woman, not a dishwasher");
   - **he helps take care of the children**, if and when the couple are ready to have them — the stated reason: both partners now develop different facets of their personalities, not only «дети-кухня-церковь» ("children-kitchen-church") [msg #615].

The resulting squeeze: the traditional demand (succeed, earn) persists and is harder to meet, while a new set of demands (emotional expressiveness, shared care) is added, and no stable new model tells a man how to combine them [msg #615].

### The author's "palette of masculinity" question list for 2025
Purpose: what the lecture left unanswered for the author — and, she assumes, for many readers — in the 21st century: «Какова «палитра маскулинности» в 2025-м?» ("What is the 'palette of masculinity' in 2025?") and «Если мужественность — это общественный конструкт, то какие у нас есть кубики?» ("If masculinity is a social construct, what building blocks do we have?"). She marks each question ✔️/❌ (acceptable or not) and leaves it open [msg #615].

How OK is it for a man to:
1. **Want to stay home with the children and not be the main breadwinner** — a direct reversal of trend 1 above [msg #615].
2. **Say directly when it hurts, is hard, is miserable or he needs support** — instead of a «каменного лица» ("stone face"); the author adds the editorial aside «и инфаркта в 50, прим. ред.» ("and a heart attack at 50, editor's note"), implying a health cost of the stone-faced norm [msg #615].

## Detailed insights

### Conformity vs conformism
The author defines conformity («конформность») as changing one's attitudes and behaviour to fit the majority's position under its pressure, real or expected. In her observation this mechanism largely helps maintain and transmit information about how it is "right" to think and act — which is why looking at traditions broadly, as a system of views, inevitably runs into conformity. She separates it from conformism («конформизм»): an adaptation in which a person does not even question the offered viewpoint and passively accepts the existing order of things [msg #91].

### The Asch, Milgram and Zimbardo experiments reread
The three famous 1950s–1960s experiments, as the author describes them:
- **Solomon Asch — the "vision test"**: confederates declared lines of completely different lengths to be the same, and one real participant had to give his opinion. (!) UNCLEAR: the source says «67 подставных людей» ("67 planted people"); the figure is reproduced as written.
- **Stanley Milgram**: some participants had to apply increasingly strong electric shocks to others on the instruction of a person in a white coat.
- **Philip Zimbardo — the prison game**: students were divided into prisoners and guards and told to do «всё, что положено» ("everything that's required") [msg #91].

The popular account: these experiments presented — and at the same time condemned — almost absolute human conformity and obedience to authority, which, especially in Zimbardo's view, fall on the fertile soil of a supposedly innate cruelty. Behind the scenes the experimenters' conviction of that very mindless conformism seemed to show through [msg #91].

Historical reframing (the author's reading): Asch's similar studies, a decade earlier, rested on a quite different ethical tradition. The first studies of conformity were done mainly in the US in the 1950s, when McCarthyism (the time of Senator Joseph McCarthy) was at its peak and the ability to blend in, be one of "us" and adapt to the system was among the most important values. In the 1960s, apparently, the value of "fitting in" gave way to the value of "freedom", and the same experiments came to be viewed increasingly negatively; the **value** of conformity was replaced by **condemnation**. Hence it is not surprising that the humanist and hippie movements of the time directed their energy to highlighting the bright and independent in people, stressing that by creating conditions for supportive development we would get a virtuous, independent, self-actualized individual [msg #91]. (The same era-dependence argument appears in hypothesis 1 of the polarization framework [msg #52].)

What closer study of the results and later experiments showed, according to the author:
1. In Asch's work, the 37% rate of agreeing was made up of only 25% consistent "caving" participants, the same share (25%) who strictly disagreed with the majority, and 50% who agreed only from time to time.
2. In Milgram's work, a certain number of people saw through the make-believe nature of the experiment and therefore were not afraid to raise the voltage.
3. In later re-checks, 20 or more years on, conformity rates kept getting lower.
4. Adding just one person who disagreed with the majority was enough for other dissenting voices to appear — even if their opinion differed in another respect, and even if the first dissenter was later removed from the sample [msg #91].

The accompanying image card states the single-dissenter finding: "A SINGLE DEFECTOR WAS ENOUGH TO COMPLETELY WIPE OUT THE CONFORMITY EFFECT. THEY DIDN'T EVEN HAVE TO AGREE WITH THE SUBJECT, THEY JUST HAD TO HAVE A DIFFERENT OPINION FROM THE MAJORITY" [img #97].

The author's concluding thought ("мысле-выводы"): if a person is the result of the interaction of genetics and environment, the question arises — what world do we want to create around us, which values do we want to support and multiply, and with which traditions should we surround ourselves accordingly? [msg #91]

### Social norms in practice: unwritten rules of Serbian hospitality
The author shares the unwritten rules a guest in a Serbian home is expected to follow ("true Serbs" are expected to):
1. **Bring a gift** (a bottle of wine or, say, sweets), and when the host formally refuses, be sure to perform the ritual back-and-forth (the author calls it «потанцевать», "to dance"): «Да бросьте, это пустяки» (Ma kakvi, nije to ništa — "Oh come on, it's nothing"); the author privately labelled this the «правилом макакви» ("makakvi rule").
2. **Be ready to eat without limit** — otherwise you hear «Ты что не ешь ничего?!» ("Why aren't you eating anything?!") and «Не можешь есть? Ну ешь без хлеба, ладно» ("Can't eat? Fine, eat without bread then").
3. **Offer help in the kitchen, even if you are a man** — take out the rubbish; most likely you won't be allowed to do anything, but you will be given a credit of trust.
4. **Argue about politics heatedly and knowingly** — «Ваше политическое кредо?» ("Your political creed?") is always relevant here; if you don't feel able to withstand this stormy but usually very short — "I'd say hot-tempered" — argument, use the previous rule (go help in the kitchen).
5. **Most importantly, be ready to answer awkward questions** — "Yes, it's a genre. Yes, here too." Came alone? — Do you have a boyfriend? Came with a boyfriend? — When's the engagement? Have a child? — When's the second? Have everything? — Not so fast: «Па шта чекаш?/НУ, ЧЕГО ЖДЁШЬ?» ("So what are you waiting for?") [msg #423].
