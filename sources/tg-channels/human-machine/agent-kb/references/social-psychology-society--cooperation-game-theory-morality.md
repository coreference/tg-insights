# Cooperation, game theory and the origins of morality

## What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's posts on cooperation as a game-theoretic problem and on where morality, empathy and kindness come from. The game-theory material runs from the classic prisoner's dilemma and the cap-exchange game, through Nash equilibrium and Axelrod's tournaments (tit-for-tat and its variants), to a worked debunking of the viral red/blue button pseudo-dilemma. The morality material covers Frans de Waal's work on the evolutionary origins of empathy and cooperation, a Pinker and Goldstein discussion of moral progress, a comic about trust and who gets saddled with responsibility for helping, and a guest post on how a culture without everyday shouting feels. The author frames the game-theory questions as ones that look ethical (forgive or retaliate? cooperate long-term or grab a big win now?) but are studied by mathematics [msg #343].

## Core ideas

- **Individual vs group rationality.** In a prisoner's dilemma each player, not knowing the partner's move, is better off defecting, yet mutual defection is worse for both than mutual cooperation. A group of self-interested members can end up worse off than a group acting for the common good [msg #330]. Details in *Prisoner's dilemma* below.
- **Cooperation strategies are a mathematical question.** Game theory studies which cooperation strategies are optimal in which situations; repeated play favours simple reciprocal strategies such as tit-for-tat, but no strategy is best against every opponent [msg #343, #345].
- **The calculations apply only to non-cooperative games.** The author calls this the most important nuance: the results hold only where participants cannot form coalitions or coordinate decisions [msg #344].
- **Not every life game is zero-sum.** In chess or poker one player's win is the other's loss, but in human society goods can be multiplied without taking from another player [msg #343].
- **Beware pseudo-dilemmas.** Some viral dilemmas have a dominant option and work only by pressing on emotions; the defence is to pause and check the payoffs [msg #450].
- **Morality has evolutionary roots.** Through de Waal, the author holds that empathy, fairness, cooperation and altruism have origins shared with other primates, which counters claims that humans stand as a moral beacon above the animal kingdom [msg #274].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Prisoner's dilemma (story one: Tanya and Cinque)
Purpose: the textbook model of the conflict between individual and joint interest. The channel's source is the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy [msg #330].

Setup: Tanya and Cinque were arrested for robbing the Hibernia savings bank and placed in separate isolation cells. Each cares much more about personal freedom than about the accomplice's welfare. A clever prosecutor offers each the same deal: you may confess or stay silent, and leave a note with the jailer before the prosecutor returns the next morning if you want to confess [msg #330].

The four outcomes the prosecutor lays out:
1. **You confess, your accomplice stays silent:** all charges against you are dropped, and your testimony is used to make sure the accomplice gets a serious sentence [msg #330].
2. **Your accomplice confesses, you stay silent:** the accomplice goes free and you serve the sentence [msg #330].
3. **Both confess:** both receive a sentence, but the prosecutor will make sure each gets early parole [msg #330].
4. **Both stay silent:** the prosecutor has to settle for token sentences on a firearms-possession charge [msg #330].

Why it is a dilemma: «не зная действий напарника, каждому в отдельности лучше признаться, чем хранить молчание» (*not knowing the partner's actions, each of them individually is better off confessing than keeping silent*). But if both confess, each is worse off than if both had stayed silent [msg #330].

Common reading: the puzzle is widely taken to illustrate the conflict between individual and group rational decision-making. A group whose members pursue personal interests may end up worse off than one whose members act for the common good, sometimes with a smaller payoff in the moment but with greater guarantees of payoffs in the future. Most of the modern literature focuses on identifying the conditions under which players could or should make the cooperative move [msg #330].

The author's later restatement of the payoff structure (used to contrast it with the button poll): each person gains most by cheating the other, but if everyone does so everyone gets a result that is not terrible, only minimally acceptable; if both cooperate they grab a big win, but neither knows whether the other will cooperate, no prior agreement is possible, and if one turns out more naive, the other gets the maximum payoff [msg #450].

### Exchange game (story two: Bill and Rose's caps)
Purpose: the same structure stripped of crime, as an everyday trade [msg #330].
1. **Setup:** Bill has a blue cap but would prefer a red one; Rose has a red cap and would prefer a blue one. Both would be happy to have two caps, but one is better than none [msg #330].
2. **Choice:** each can keep their own cap or hand it to the other [msg #330].
3. **Individual logic:** because the other person's moral qualities are unknown, giving means risking ending up with nothing, so keeping your own looks better [msg #330].
4. **Joint logic:** both are better off swapping than keeping, which raises the core problem of how to know whether the other will hand theirs over [msg #330].

Situations like these, where participants for whatever reason cannot form coalitions and coordinate decisions with each other, are called in game theory **некооперативные игры** (*non-cooperative games*) [msg #330].

### Nash equilibrium (the channel's account)
The author tells it as a turn in economic thought: Adam Smith held that competition on an every-man-for-himself basis is the key to survival in the economic jungle, and in 1950 the discoveries of John Nash (Nobel laureate in economics and the prototype of the hero of the film A Beautiful Mind) overturned these previously dominant ideas. In the author's words, Nash showed that if everyone does the best for themselves while also doing better for others, everyone ends up winning more. Hence the Nash equilibrium: «в какой-то момент никто не сможет выигрывать больше, если подход изменит только кто-то один, а не многие» (*at some point no one can win more if only one person, rather than many, changes their approach*) [msg #343].

### Tit-for-tat protocol (око за око)
Origin: in 1980 Robert Axelrod and Anatol Rapoport set out to study the consequences of different types of cooperation and ran Axelrod's tournament. Tit-for-tat became popular because it let a player win the most with a cooperative partner while losing the least to a cheat [msg #343].
1. **Open with cooperation:** «вы начинаете с «кооперации» и дальше копируете действия другого» (*you start with cooperation and then copy the other's actions*) [msg #343].
2. **Mirror cooperation:** cooperate as long as the other cooperates [msg #343].
3. **Mirror defection:** refuse after the other's refusal [msg #343].
4. **Return to cooperation:** go back to cooperating as soon as, on the previous move, the other again said yes [msg #343].

The Stanford text's account: tit-for-tat cooperates on the first move and then does whatever the opponent did on the previous move. Against always-defect it therefore defects after the first move; against always-cooperate it always cooperates; against itself it always cooperates. It gains all the benefits of cooperation with a friendly opponent and protects itself by defecting against a defector [img #345].

Flaw: «нет права на ошибку» (*no right to make a mistake*). After one refusal, players risk never returning to cooperation if neither steps away from tit-for-tat [msg #343]. Also, tit-for-tat assumes the opponent is trying to maximise its score; paired with a senseless strategy such as RANDOM it «опускается до уровня противника» (*sinks to the opponent's level*), which is why it cannot be called the best strategy [img #345].

Where the results were applied, per the author: from explaining reciprocal altruism in animals to inter-state military strategy, and even resources like BitTorrent, where people exchange files trusting that each will share their own [msg #343]. The arms race example: the author, citing the Veritasium video, says that the 1950-1968 escalation of nuclear arsenals between the USA and USSR was reduced by voluntarily disarming bombs one per year; each side checked whether the other kept the agreement and took another step toward trust the next year [msg #343]. The author's view: tit-for-tat still holds first place, including in international politics, while decisions not to retaliate in response to a threat signal are taken by people on the ground at their own risk; the footnote refers to Stanislav Petrov, the Soviet officer who prevented a potential nuclear war after a false alarm of the missile-attack warning system about a US strike on 26 September 1983 [msg #343].

### Tit-for-two-tats (око за два ока)
Context: in the author's circle of applied rationalists they played a game similar to the prisoner's dilemma and tried to use a different strategy, which the author renders as «око за два ока» (*an eye for two eyes*, tit-for-two-tats) [msg #343].
- **Rule:** when someone refuses to cooperate with you or cheats, allow for error or chance and respond with symmetric damage only after the second consecutive harm [msg #343]. The Stanford text calls it a forgiving strategy that defects only if the opponent defected twice in a row [img #345].
- **Why:** it repairs tit-for-tat's no-room-for-error flaw [msg #343].
- **Risk the author raises:** what if there are many dishonest players and your other cheek gets exploited more often than you would be prepared to allow? [msg #343]
- **Historical twist:** in Derek Muller's interview with one of the tournament authors, it emerged that Rapoport bet on tit-for-tat only at Axelrod's request. In Axelrod's view, Rapoport, as a researcher of peaceful conflict resolution, was by nature more forgiving and resistant to provocation, and tit-for-two-tats could have won the first tournament, before chaotic dishonest strategies became more numerous in the second [msg #343].

### Axelrod tournament strategy catalogue (Stanford text, machine-translated screenshot)
Context: in 1980 Robert Axelrod, professor of political science at the University of Michigan, held a tournament of strategies for the prisoner's dilemma. He invited well-known game theorists to submit strategies run by computers; the programs played repeatedly against each other and against themselves; each strategy decided whether to cooperate or defect based on the previous moves of itself and its opponent [img #345].
1. **Always defect:** defects on every move. The text says «Это то, что отстаивает теория игр.» (*This is what game theory advocates.*) It is the safest strategy because it cannot be exploited, but it misses the chance of larger payoffs from cooperating with an opponent willing to cooperate [img #345].
2. **Always cooperate:** does very well against itself, but brings poor results if the opponent decides to defect [img #345].
3. **Random:** «Случайно: стратегия работает в 50% случаев.» (*Random: the strategy works in 50% of cases.*) [img #345]
4. **Tit for tat (the winner):** described in the protocol above [img #345].
5. **Tit for two tats:** the forgiving variation described above [img #345].
6. **Two tits for tat:** punishes every defection with two defections of its own; the machine translation renders it «ДВА СИСКА ЗА ТО», which the author jokes about separately: «Кому «два сиска за то»?» (*Who wants some two tits for tat?*) [msg #345, img #345].

The pre-set strategies (always defect, always cooperate, random) are fixed in advance and so cannot use knowledge of the opponent's previous moves to work out its strategy [img #345].

Conclusion of the text: «на самом деле не существует «лучшей» стратегии решения дилеммы заключенного» (*in fact there is no best strategy for the prisoner's dilemma*). Each strategy does best when matched against the worst one; to win, a player must work out the opponent's strategy and then choose the strategy best suited to the situation [img #345].

### Real dilemma vs pseudo-dilemma test (the red and blue buttons)
The poll: the year before the post it took over Twitter (started by lisatomic5, with a second wave by annoyinghippy) and Russian Afisha wrote an article about it. The conditions: which button would you press if (a) blue guarantees survival for everyone if more than 50% of people choose it, and (b) red gives everyone a chance to survive if the other button gets less than half the votes? No talking is allowed and you cannot learn others' decisions [msg #450].

The author's method of analysis:
1. **Check whether the options balance each other.** In a genuine prisoner's dilemma they do: cheating pays most individually, but universal cheating gives everyone only a minimally acceptable result; mutual cooperation pays big, but you cannot know or arrange the other's move, and the more naive player loses to the other [msg #450].
2. **Look for a scenario where the supposedly altruistic option is better.** «В «кнопках» же никакой дилеммы нет» (*In the buttons there is no dilemma at all*): there is no scenario in which choosing blue is more advantageous than red [msg #450].
3. **Walk through each type of player.** If a person does not want to die, pressing red is enough. If a person does want to die, they simply will not press red, and it is the blue-pressers who take that choice away from them [msg #450].
4. **Name the pressure.** «Эта псевдо-дилемма работает только за счёт давления на эмоции» (*This pseudo-dilemma works only through pressure on the emotions*), with arguments like «А ВДРУГ ваша бабушка не нажмёт красную?» (*What IF your grandma doesn't press red?*) or the accusation that you are such a terrible person that you will not try to save everyone, and in the altruistic way at that, as if self-sacrifice were alien to you [msg #450].
5. **Find the actual best joint move.** «лучший шанс всех спасти — это просто нажать красную кнопку каждому» (*the best chance of saving everyone is simply for everyone to press red*), and it works without any need to coordinate [msg #450].

Takeaway: «Остерегайтесь подделок» (*Beware of fakes*), and do not rush to conclusions: take a pause and ask whether someone is trying to stop you weighing the pros and cons simply by pressing your sensitive button [msg #450].

### Factors that shape cooperation strategy in real life
From the second part of the game-theory series, the author names what will strongly influence strategy choice in reality [msg #344]:
1. **Speed toward prosperity:** how quickly, within the long-term perspective, participants want to reach prosperity; the author adds that ideas of fairness may surprise you here [msg #344].
2. **Repeated contact:** whether participants will be forced to cross paths after the decisions are made [msg #344].
3. **Scope limit:** the most important nuance in the author's opinion is that all of these calculations work only for non-cooperative games, where coalitions and coordination are impossible [msg #344].

## Detailed insights

### Zero-sum vs positive-sum
The author asks: «Всегда ли победа одного означает поражение другого?» (*Does one person's victory always mean another's defeat?*) In chess or poker, yes; these are zero-sum games. But human society has a privilege: «мы можем приумножать благо без отъёма у другого «игрока», а черпая ресурсы «в банке»» (*we can multiply good without taking it from another player, drawing resources from the bank instead*) [msg #343].

### Frans de Waal and the evolutionary origins of morality
Frans de Waal was a famous primatologist who worked at Emory University from 1991 and died of stomach cancer on 14 March 2024 at 75 [msg #274]. For the author he is one of their most beloved authors:
- His book translated as «Истоки морали» (*The Origins of Morality*) is, in the author's words, «одно из главных моих противоядий» (*one of my main antidotes*) whenever someone wants to argue that humans are meant to be a moral beacon above the animal kingdom [msg #274].
- He was one of the first to talk not only about aggressive chimpanzees, to which humans were then compared (with the whole territorial-patriarchal agenda that came with it), but also about the «миролюбивых и сексуальных бонобо» (*peace-loving and sexual bonobos*), who turned out to be just as close to our species [msg #274].
- He wrote «о происхождении эмпатии, чувстве справедливости, сотрудничестве, решении конфликтов и альтруизме» (*about the origins of empathy, the sense of fairness, cooperation, conflict resolution and altruism*), which the author calls the most important things [msg #274].
- His final work, about gender differences, came out in Russian in 2022; the author was listening to it at the time and shared an excerpt published by N+1. The author recalls his audio about sexual arousal in primates bursting at full volume from their headphones in an airport in the Emirates. The post's photo shows de Waal as a young man holding a baby chimpanzee [msg #274].
- The author's call: remember him and introduce his views to those who did not get to them in his lifetime [msg #274].

### Trust and gendered responsibility for helping (comic)

### Everyday kindness and aggression: a guest view from Argentina
A forwarded post by Евгения Дашкова (Evgenia Dashkova) asks what we would be like if people were kind to us [msg #462]:
- **Observation:** despite a million problems, Argentina differs strikingly from Russia in having a very low level of everyday, interpersonal aggression. People do not shout at each other or talk rudely in ordinary communication. In a year there she never once heard parents shout at children, owners at dogs, or teachers at pupils [msg #462].
- **Contrast:** only there did she realise how rude everyone at home is; she half-fears she and her partner will be arrested for domestic violence when they are merely discussing who has overworked more at home [msg #462].
- **Workplace norm:** a speaker describing the local film industry said «Здесь совсем нельзя орать на людей» (*Here you absolutely cannot yell at people*), about work on a film set, and the phrase stuck with her: «Нельзя. Орать. На людей.» (*You cannot. Yell. At people.*) [msg #462]
- **Everyday kindness:** people constantly compliment your coat, hair, nails, bag; leave a tip and you are told you are «муй амабле» (*muy amable, very kind*) and praised [msg #462].
- **Counterfactual question:** she wonders who she would have become had she not been shouted at and had she sometimes been told as a child that she did well or had something good in her; she guesses people raised that way would be braver, brighter, more talented, calmer, kinder and merrier [msg #462].
- **Framing without blame:** she writes without reproach to her parents and surroundings: they did their best, it simply happened that most of us grew up where any adult could yell at you and that was normal. Her closing: «Какие мы молодцы.» (*How well we have done.*), meaning that we coped even so [msg #462].
