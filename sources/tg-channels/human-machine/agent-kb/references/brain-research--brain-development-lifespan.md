# How the brain changes across the lifespan

## What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel taught about how the brain's structure and wiring change over a whole human life and over human evolution: the 2025 Cambridge study that found four structural turning points (about 9, 32, 66 and 83 years) dividing life into five brain "eras"; the author's clarification that the adult "plateau" does not stop cognitive growth because different skills peak at different ages; a 2023 study linking slowed adolescent synaptic pruning to a brain pattern shared by many mental disorders; a study showing that musicians' brains age more slowly in sensorimotor regions; and a gene (TKTL1) that may explain why the human brain became denser than the Neanderthal one [msg #64, #175, #176, #181, #678, #679, #680, #682].

The channel author frames it as practically important for mental-health professionals: classical age-stage textbooks (Piaget, Erikson, Leontiev) increasingly fail to match real 21st-century people aged 20, 40 or 60, and the new neurobiological data appear to confirm and extend what practitioners observe in people of our time [msg #678].

## Core ideas

- **The brain's architecture changes in five eras, not continuously.** A Cambridge study of 3,802 people aged 0–90 found four turning points — about 9, 32, 66 and 83 — where the trajectory of the brain's network organisation shifts (full detail in *Five eras of the human brain*) [msg #678, #679, #680].
- **Brain "adolescence" lasts until about 32.** Judged by neural architecture alone, the adolescent pattern of brain change ends only in the early thirties, and age 32 is the largest single shift of the whole lifespan [msg #679].
- **The adult "plateau" is not a ceiling.** The plateau the Cambridge authors mention is a very general inverted-U concept; finer studies show that different skills and traits peak at different ages, some as late as 60 (see *Asynchronous peaks of cognitive skills*) [msg #682].
- **Many adolescent mental disorders share one brain pattern.** A study the channel cites found a common connectivity pattern — the "neuropsychopathological factor" — across eating and behavioural disorders, depression, anxiety and ADHD, linked to a gene variant that slows synaptic pruning (see *Neuropsychopathological factor*) [msg #176].
- **Musical practice slows brain aging.** Musicians keep sensorimotor cortex function better and, even when old, distinguish speech in noise better than non-musicians [msg #181].
- **Human brain evolution may hinge on cell density, not skull size.** A TKTL1 mutation that triggers a burst of neuron growth may be what separated our species from Neanderthals, whose skulls were about the same size [msg #64].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Five eras of the human brain (Cambridge, 2025)
**What it is and where it comes from.** The channel presents it under the heading «Пять возрастов человеческого мозга» (*Five ages of the human brain*) as a discovery by University of Cambridge researchers published on 25 November 2025 [msg #678]. Researchers from Cambridge's MRC Cognition and Brain Sciences Unit compared the brains of 3,802 people aged 0 to 90 using diffusion MRI, which maps neural connections by tracking the movement of water molecules in brain tissue. They identified four main turning points in brain architecture — around 9, 32, 66 and 83 years — that divide a human life into five structural "eras" [msg #679]. The study's diagram labels the eras: 0–9 Childhood, 9–32 Adolescence, 32–66 Adulthood, 66–83 Early Ageing, 83 onwards Late Ageing [img #678].

**Why it matters (the authors' reasoning).** Lead author Alexa Mousley explains that although neural structure is known to be critical for development, there was no overall picture of how and why it changes across life: «Мы знаем, что нейронная структура мозга критически важна для нашего развития, но у нас нет общей картины, как и почему она меняется на протяжении жизни» (*We know that the brain's neural structure is critically important for our development, but we have no overall picture of how and why it changes across life*). The eras, she says, give context for understanding at which life stages the brain may be most efficient or most vulnerable, and why some people's brains develop differently at key moments — whether learning difficulties in childhood or dementia in later years [msg #679].

**The five eras, in order:**

1. **Childhood: infancy to about 9.** From infancy to age 9, connections across the whole brain are rebuilt according to one and the same principle, which has three components [msg #680]:
   - «консолидируются сети» (*networks consolidate*): the enormous number of synapses is gradually cut back to the most active ones [msg #680];
   - grey and white matter grow rapidly in volume, so that cortical thickness — the distance between the outer grey matter and the inner white matter — reaches its peak [msg #680];
   - cortical folding (the characteristic ridges on the outer surface of the brain) stabilises [msg #680].
   **Turning point at 9:** by this first turning point the brain undergoes a leap in cognitive abilities, and at the same time the risk of mental disorders rises [msg #680].

2. **Adolescence: about 9 to about 32.** The channel highlights it with the headline «Оказалось, «юность» начинается в 9 лет и продолжается до ~32» (*It turns out "youth" starts at 9 and lasts until ~32*). In this second era white matter keeps growing in volume, so the organisation of the brain's communication networks becomes ever more refined, which is confirmed by water diffusion during scanning. The efficiency of connections between brain regions rises and networks become more refined, giving faster communication between distant regions. This "youthful" brain structure persists far beyond the teenage years or the early twenties. The researchers' explanation: puberty gives a clear starting point, but the end of adolescence is much harder to define scientifically; «Основываясь исключительно на нейронной архитектуре, мы обнаружили, что подростковые изменения в структуре мозга заканчиваются примерно в начале тридцатилетней декады» (*Based purely on neural architecture, we found that adolescent changes in brain structure end around the early thirties*) [msg #679].
   **Turning point at 32 — the strongest restructuring of the whole lifespan.** As the brain moves into adulthood, the largest shift in neural architecture is observed. At this point the most efficient connections within individual regions are built along with fast communication across the whole brain, and cognitive abilities rise alongside. Mousley: «…примерно к 32 годам мы наблюдаем наиболее направленные изменения в нейронных связях и наибольший общий сдвиг траектории по сравнению со всеми другими поворотными моментами» (*…by around 32 we see the most directional changes in neural connections and the largest overall shift in trajectory compared with all other turning points*) [msg #679].

3. **Adulthood: 32 to 66.** At 32 the longest era — maturity — begins. Brain architecture stabilises, with no major turning points for about thirty years. The scientists note that this corresponds to a "plateau" in intelligence and personality reported in other studies. Even so, the era includes the start of slow structural changes: the brain begins to move toward more segregated (separate) networks instead of global ones [msg #680]. For what the "plateau" does and does not mean, see *Inverted U-shaped model* and *Asynchronous peaks* below [msg #682].

4. **Early ageing: 66 to 83.** The shift at 66 is a subtler, more gradual process than the changes of youth, but it still marks a noticeable reorganisation with two components [msg #680]:
   - connectivity gradually declines as white matter slowly degenerates [msg #680];
   - the brain reroutes information in a compensatory mode [msg #680].
   Mousley links the age to health risks: «Это возраст, когда люди сталкиваются с повышенным риском различных заболеваний, которые могут поражать мозг, таких как гипертония» (*This is the age when people face an increased risk of various conditions that can affect the brain, such as hypertension*) [msg #680].

5. **Late ageing: 83 onwards.** Even at 83+ the changes are structured rather than chaotic, and the brain does not "switch off" uniformly. Instead it becomes more locally focused, relying on isolated regional neural networks rather than global network communication. Senior author Professor Duncan Astle, professor of neuroinformatics at Cambridge, summarised: «Оглядываясь назад, многие из нас чувствуют, что наша жизнь состояла из разных этапов. Оказывается, мозг также проходит через эти этапы» (*Looking back, many of us feel that our lives consisted of different stages. It turns out the brain also goes through these stages*) [msg #680].

**The channel author's reading.** The author finds the results on the end of adolescence and early adulthood the most interesting for changing one's starting point of view, and says the study appears to confirm and extend practitioners' observations that real people of the 21st century at 20, 40 and even 60 no longer match people "from the textbook" [msg #678]. The author invited readers to share whether they had noticed this in their own life and practice [msg #678].

### Inverted U-shaped model of brain development
**Context.** A reader asked whether the "intelligence plateau" mentioned in the Cambridge study means cognitive power can no longer be built up after it begins. The channel author's answer: «нет, всё совсем не так печально» (*no, it's not at all that sad*) — «32 года — это не приговор» (*32 is not a sentence [verdict]*) [msg #682].

**What the plateau actually refers to.** The author followed the citation next to the "plateau" phrase in the Cambridge paper and found it points to a very general idea from a 2013 article: the concept of «перевернутом U-образном» (*inverted U-shaped*) brain development. The model has three phases [msg #682]:
1. first, many rearrangements and the creation of connections [msg #682];
2. then a plateau [msg #682];
3. then again many rearrangements, this time with a reduction of connections [msg #682].

The author's verdict: the study's authors "stirred up intrigue" with the plateau, dropping it in casually «как весеннее пальто» (*like a spring coat*) — it describes only a broad shape of whole-brain development, not a limit on any particular ability [msg #682].

### Asynchronous peaks of cognitive skills
After the 2013 inverted-U concept, many finer studies showed that different skills and traits follow asynchronous trajectories — they do not all peak and decline together. The author's examples, from MIT research (2015) [msg #682]:
- the skill of recognising other people's emotions peaks between 40 and 50 [msg #682];
- vocabulary peaks only around 60 [msg #682].
The practical lesson the author draws: reaching the structural adult plateau around 32 does not mean the end of cognitive growth, because several abilities keep improving for decades afterward [msg #682]. The author said a more detailed article on this was in preparation [msg #682].

### Neuropsychopathological factor (NP factor) — a shared brain pattern behind adolescent mental disorders
**Source.** The channel reported (headline: «Обнаружен общий рисунок мозговой активности у молодых людей с ментальными расстройствами» — *A common pattern of brain activity found in young people with mental disorders*) a study published in Nature Medicine, with a plain-English summary from the University of Cambridge [msg #175, #176].

**The datasets (projects named in the study)** [msg #175]:
- **IMAGEN** — a European project on how biological, psychological and environmental factors contribute to adolescent brain development and mental health; based at the Institute of Psychiatry, Psychology and Neuroscience (IoPPN), Professor Sylvane Desrivières [msg #175].
- **ESTRA** — one of the focus areas within IMAGEN; studies eating disorders in young people [msg #175].
- **ABCD** — the largest long-term study of brain development and child health in the United States [msg #175].
- **Stratify** — a European research project identifying the neural networks underlying reinforcement-related disorders: major depression, alcohol-use disorders, schizophrenia and eating disorders [msg #175].

**Findings, step by step:**
1. **A shared pattern.** Analysis of 1,750 adolescents aged 14 from IMAGEN showed that people with eating and other behavioural disorders, depression, anxiety and ADHD have a particular pattern of interaction between brain regions. The scientists named it the «нейропсихопатологическим фактором» (*neuropsychopathological factor*, NP factor). Regardless of which disorder from that list a person had, the pattern was consistently observed, especially in the frontal lobes [msg #176].
2. **A genetic link to pruning.** Looking at genetics, the researchers found in participants a specific variant of the gene IGSF11, which plays an important role in synaptic pruning — the removal of unused and redundant neural connections. Pruning is necessary for efficient brain function: fast information transmission and no overload. The IGSF11 variant slows pruning and contributes to delayed development of the prefrontal cortex [msg #176].
3. **Tracking over development.** Once identified in adolescents, the NP factor could be followed across several later developmental periods [msg #176].
4. **Prediction across ages and samples.** The factor consistently predicted symptoms of ADHD, conduct disorder, anxiety and depression in population data not only of adolescents (IMAGEN, age 14) but also of pre-adolescents (ABCD, ages 10–11) and young adults (IMAGEN, age 19), and also in a clinical sample of young adults (Stratify, age 23) [msg #176].

**Significance.** According to the post, the results allow a reproducible, shared neural basis for symptoms of multiple mental disorders to be unified with multidimensional behavioural, neuroimaging and genetic data. This may help develop new therapeutic interventions for co-occurring (comorbid) mental illnesses [msg #176].

## Detailed insights

### Music and the aging brain
The channel reposted (forwarded from the channel Блуждающий нерв, Vagus Nerve) a note on the 28 April 2023 cover of *Science Advances*, titled «Музыка омолаживает» (*Music rejuvenates*). The cover shows an elderly musician moving against the current of the river of age; fish representing brain age grow gradually younger as they swim upstream. The underlying study, Zhang et al., showed that musicians better preserve the function of sensorimotor areas of the cortex, and even in old age they distinguish speech against background noise well — unlike people who never practised music [msg #181].

### Human brain evolution: TKTL1 and brain density
Researchers at the Max Planck Institute found that a mutation in the human TKTL1 gene leads to a burst of neuron growth. Anneline Pinson and Wieland Huttner checked that ancient versions of the gene, as further experiments in mice showed, do not cause such growth — neither of neurons nor of progenitor cells [msg #64].

Why this matters for evolution: existing archaeological analysis of fossils indicated that our species and Neanderthals had roughly the same skull size, yet the complexity of their thinking differed dramatically. The new results suggest that it was precisely the densification of the brain — through a sharp increase in the number of brain cells — that separated our species from Neanderthals and from non-human primates. The channel presents this as a suggestion the results allow, not a settled conclusion [msg #64].

## Evidence and numbers

- Cambridge brain-eras study: 3,802 people, ages 0–90, diffusion MRI; turning points at about 9, 32, 66, 83; adult era 32–66 has no major turning point for about 30 years [msg #679, #680].
- NP-factor study: 1,750 adolescents aged 14 (IMAGEN); validated at ages 10–11 (ABCD), 19 (IMAGEN) and 23 (Stratify clinical sample) [msg #176].
- Asynchronous peaks: emotion recognition peaks at 40–50; vocabulary around 60 [msg #682].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The "intelligence plateau" in the Cambridge paper rests on a general 2013 inverted-U concept, not on new measurements of intelligence; the channel author warns against reading it as a cap on cognitive growth [msg #682].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the Cambridge study is described in terms of structural brain wiring (diffusion MRI); the claimed cognitive leaps at 9 and 32 and the rise in mental-disorder risk at 9 are reported in the post without saying which data support them [msg #680].
- The TKTL1 result is framed with a hedge: the findings «позволяют предположить» (*allow one to suggest*) that brain densification separated humans from Neanderthals — a hypothesis, supported by mouse experiments [msg #64].
- The NP-factor results "may help" develop new therapies — a prospective claim, not a demonstrated treatment [msg #176].
- The classical age classifications (Piaget, Erikson, Leontiev) are presented by the channel author as increasingly mismatched with real people — an opinion based on practitioners' observations [msg #678].
