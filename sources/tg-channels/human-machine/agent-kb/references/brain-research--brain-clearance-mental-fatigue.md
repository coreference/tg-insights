# Brain maintenance: waste clearance, mental fatigue and Alzheimer's links

## What this covers

Three strands of neuroscience news the channel author translated and commented on: (1) a 2022 study linking the tiredness after a long day of demanding thinking to glutamate build-up in the lateral prefrontal cortex and to a shift toward short-term choices [msg #169, #170]; (2) two February 2024 Nature studies showing that synchronized neuronal activity — delta waves in deep sleep, and 40 Hz gamma rhythms driven by light and sound — pushes cerebrospinal fluid through brain tissue and clears waste, including amyloid [msg #314, #315]; (3) a mouse study showing that the common fungus Candida albicans can enter the brain and itself generate amyloid-beta-like peptides [msg #246, #247]. According to the source, waste accumulation in the working brain is a main cause of many neurological disorders, and mental fatigue is a physiological, measurable state rather than something that is "only in your head" [msg #314, #169].

## Core ideas

- **Mental fatigue has a metabolic signature.** More than six hours of hard mental work raised glutamate in the lateral prefrontal cortex, and the same people became more likely to choose small immediate rewards; the authors suggest rest may let the brain restore proper regulation of glutamate. This is so far a correlation, not a proven cause [msg #169].
- **Brain cleaning is driven by neurons firing together.** During deep sleep, synchronized neuronal activity creates ionic waves that move fluid and solutes through the parenchyma; silencing neurons slows clearance and activating them speeds it up [msg #314].
- **40 Hz stimulation pumps fluid via arteries.** Light-and-sound stimulation at 40 Hz made arteries constrict and dilate more strongly, pushing solutes through the brain and out to the cervical lymph nodes, through VIP-releasing interneurons [msg #315].
- **A fungus can make Alzheimer's-type peptides.** Candida albicans breaks the blood-brain barrier with its Sap proteases and uses the same enzymes to cut amyloid precursor protein into Aβ-like peptides — a source of these peptides outside the brain's own proteases [msg #246, #247].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Wiehler et al. cognitive-fatigue experiment
Purpose: test whether the consequences of cognitive fatigue are tied to metabolic changes in the brain; the team of cognitive neuroscientist Antonius Wiehler (Paris Brain Institute) suspected they were [msg #169].
1. **Participants and groups.** 40 participants were recruited; 24 got a hard task and the remaining 16 a similar but easier task [msg #169].
2. **The hard task.** An example given: watch letters appear on a computer screen every 1.6 seconds and record when a letter matches the one shown three letters earlier (a 3-back task). The easy group did an analogous, lighter version [msg #169].
3. **Duration.** Both groups worked for just over six hours, with two ten-minute breaks [msg #169].
4. **Measurement.** While participants worked, the researchers used magnetic resonance spectroscopy to measure glutamate in the lateral prefrontal cortex — the seat of cognitive control, the system people rely on to suppress impulses and to pick long-term gains over tempting short-term rewards such as an unhealthy snack. Wiehler's illustration: «Если вас ужалило насекомое, вам хочется почесаться» (*If an insect stings you, you want to scratch*); «Если вы остановите этот рефлекс, это будет когнитивный контроль» (*If you stop that reflex, that is cognitive control*) [msg #169].
5. **Choice test.** Participants chose between an immediate cash reward and a larger reward received several months later, at the start and at the end of the day [msg #169].
6. **Result.** The hard-task group accumulated more glutamate in this region by the end of the day than the easy-task group, and they were more likely to choose the smaller short-term reward than at the start of the day; at the end of the workday they were also more likely than the easy-task group to pick quick, easily obtained, lower-value rewards over larger rewards that require a longer wait or more effort [msg #169].
7. **Interpretation by the authors.** Too much glutamate can disrupt brain function, and a period of rest may let the brain restore proper regulation of the molecule [msg #169].
8. **Next step.** Wiehler hopes to use the system to learn how people recover from mental exhaustion: «Было бы здорово узнать больше о том, как восстанавливается уровень глутамата» (*It would be great to learn more about how glutamate levels recover*) [msg #169]. The continuation frames the open questions as «Полезен ли сон? Какой продолжительности должны быть перерывы, чтобы иметь положительный эффект?» (*Does sleep help? How long should breaks be to have a positive effect?*) [msg #170].

### Kipnis study design: does neuronal synchrony drive brain clearance? (Study 1)
Purpose: find evidence for the hinted correlation between neuronal excitation and glymphatic function [msg #314].
1. **Premise.** Neural networks synchronize their signalling (action potentials). This creates rhythmic, self-propagating, high-amplitude ionic waves in the interstitial fluid between brain tissues — the same waves that can be measured from the skull surface with EEG [msg #314].
2. **Tools.** Jonathan Kipnis's team (Washington University in St. Louis) used chemogenetic and optogenetic tools that control a subset of neurons in mice with millisecond precision, so they could activate or silence neurons in the hippocampus at will [msg #314].
3. **Observation in sleep.** In sleeping mice, ionic waves moved through the parenchyma in sync with delta-wave electrical rhythms (1–4 Hz) seen in deep sleep (stage N3) [msg #314].
4. **Silencing.** When neuronal activity was dampened, the waves predictably faded and dissolved substances moved through the brain very slowly [msg #314].
5. **Activating.** Raising neuronal activity had the opposite effect: stronger ionic waves and faster passage of solutes [msg #314].
6. **Conclusion.** According to the researchers, this specific synchronous neuronal activity and the ionic waves it generates during sleep are the main driving force of the cleaning process. They joked that neurons that fire together "shower" together, and suggest the results open ways to improve brain clearance by using neuronal dynamics [msg #314].

### Tsai study design: 40 Hz stimulation and the VIP link (Study 2)
Purpose: extend the evidence that neuronal activity can move cerebrospinal fluid; led by Li-Huei Tsai at MIT, published in Nature [msg #315].
1. **Model.** Six-month-old mice of the 5XFAD genetic line, bred to model Alzheimer's disease [msg #315].
2. **Tracer.** Fluorescently labelled ovalbumin was injected into their cerebrospinal fluid [msg #315].
3. **Stimulation.** One hour of sound and light at 40 Hz, compared with 8 Hz, 80 Hz and no stimulation [msg #315].
4. **Inflow result.** After 40 Hz stimulation, four times as much ovalbumin reached the cortex as in the 8 Hz, 80 Hz or unstimulated mice [msg #315].
5. **Outflow result.** Cerebrospinal fluid outflow also responded to 40 Hz, clearing brain waste 50% faster than in control mice [msg #315].
6. **Proposed mechanism.** Stronger dilation and constriction of brain blood vessels: the fast gamma rhythm (40 Hz) was linked to stronger arterial pulsation, which pushes solutes through the brain and out to the cervical lymph nodes. The effect was explained by interneurons releasing vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP), which regulates blood flow [msg #315].
7. **Causal test.** When the VIP interneurons were silenced, 40 Hz stimulation no longer affected the arterial pulse or amyloid removal — pointing to VIP as the link between neurons and arteries [msg #315].
8. **Authors' claim.** «Наши результаты устанавливают новые механизмы, которые задействуют глимфатическую систему для удаления амилоида из мозга» (*Our results establish new mechanisms that engage the glymphatic system to remove amyloid from the brain*) [msg #315].

### Candida albicans route into and out of the brain
Purpose: explain, from Baylor College of Medicine mouse work, how this common fungus enters the brain, how the brain clears it, and how it produces Alzheimer's-relevant peptides [msg #246].
1. **Entry.** C. albicans produces enzymes called secreted aspartic proteases (Sap) that break down the blood-brain barrier, giving the fungus access to the brain, where it causes damage (first author Yifan Wu) [msg #246].

## Detailed insights

### Why the fatigue study matters and what it cannot yet show
- Cognitive-fatigue research may become key to understanding how workers respond to and recover from high-stakes mental work such as air traffic control, where even a brief loss of concentration can cost lives [msg #170].
- Health psychologist Martin Hagger (UC Merced) notes that earlier studies showed mental effort affects physiological measures such as heart-rate variability and blood flow, but those effects tend to be subtle: «Это не похоже на то, когда вы тренируете скелетные мышцы» (*It is not like when you train skeletal muscles*) — «Но это ощутимо» (*But it is tangible*) [msg #169].
- Hagger hopes other researchers will adopt the new measurement approach: «Средства для обнаружения этого до сих пор были недостаточно чувствительными» (*The means of detecting this have so far not been sensitive enough*), so the study paves the way for future work on cognitive fatigue [msg #170].
- Carmen Sandi (EPFL Lausanne) says the study matters for linking cognitive fatigue to neurometabolism, but establishing a causal link between exhaustion and metabolic change will need further research, possibly in non-human animals where glutamate levels can be experimentally altered. Such work could reveal the molecular mechanisms that make glutamate accumulate during hard mental work and how this affects brain activity — «Это сложная часть» (*That is the hard part*) [msg #169, #170].

### Background to brain clearance
- Waste accumulation in the working brain is a main cause of many neurological disorders, yet until recently knowledge of how cleaning happens was fragmentary [msg #314].
- Earlier work established that sleep — long and seemingly useless — lets the brain «расхламиться» (*declutter*) for good work the next day [msg #314].
- Later it became known that the brain's «метаболический мусор» (*metabolic garbage*) accumulated during the day is washed out by increased cerebrospinal-fluid flow through the parenchyma. What powers that flow was the open question; earlier work only hinted at a correlation between neuronal excitation and glymphatic function [msg #314].
- 40 Hz rhythms are usually typical of wakefulness, though some studies observed them in sleep (Bergel et al., 2018; Laurino et al., 2019). Nedergaard and Hablitz suggest different brain regions may have different stimulation frequencies for moving cerebrospinal fluid and clearing waste [msg #315].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The glutamate finding is correlational; causality is unproven (Sandi) [msg #169].
- Open questions the source poses but does not answer: does sleep help recovery, and how long must breaks be to have an effect [msg #170].
- Nedergaard and Hablitz note the Kipnis approach is ahead of what can be imaged: interstitial fluid and ion flow cannot yet be visualized in real time [msg #314].
- Both clearance studies are in mice [msg #314, #315].
- (!) UNCLEAR: Sandi's name is transliterated «Санди» in #169 and «Сэнди» in #170 [msg #169, #170].
