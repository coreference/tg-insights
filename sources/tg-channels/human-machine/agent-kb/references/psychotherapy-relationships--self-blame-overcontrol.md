# Self-blame, overcontrol and the inner critic

## What this covers

This topic gathers the channel author's posts on the inner mechanics of self-judgement: how hindsight bias arises from the brain's prediction and error-correction programs and turns into self-gnawing; why the popular consolation "I did the best I could" rings false; what a life built on control, planning and restraint costs (a pattern the author recognised in herself and found addressed by RO DBT); how children raised "for others" develop a false self; how her own habit of overcomplicating everything gave way to valuing "good enough"; a small self-experiment on replacing a habit; and the drivers of creators' urge to start the next project right after a release. The author frames these as "bugs and features" of the brain: programs that are useful in principle but become harmful when they misfire, are too frequent or are out of place, and she repeatedly argues that recognising the misfire removes the grounds for self-blame and points toward support and action in the present [msg #20, #22, #89, #195, #473].

## Core ideas

- **Hindsight is a bug, not a verdict.** The feeling that "I could have known then what I know now and would surely have acted better" is a malfunction of the error-correction system; the author says not to believe it, and therefore not to blame oneself for it [msg #20, #22].
- **We cannot use what we do not have.** In the moment we take the decision available to us; sometimes we simply lack inner resources and information, and that drops out of focus when we judge ourselves afterward [msg #22].
- **Most everyday mistakes are compensable.** Acting differently next time helps more than suffering over what did not happen while doing nothing in the present [msg #23].
- **"Best" is the wrong frame.** Both condemning ("those who erred must correct themselves") and praising ("you did your best") are extremes; with chronically limited resources and shifting priorities, some valued part of us almost always loses, and it needs grieving and care tomorrow rather than proof of optimality [msg #195].
- **Overcontrol has a shadow side.** Cultivating restraint, long-term striving, solo self-reliance and detailed systems makes it hard to live with spontaneity, makes us emotionally invisible and lonely, and is driven by radical non-acceptance of chaos and stagnation rather than by perfectionism or superiority [msg #473].
- **The "good daughter" is shaped by subtle calibration.** Being "easy to raise" often means learning to suppress one's needs; the result is a false self that secures attachment but loses access to one's own identity [msg #543].
- **Good enough can beat best.** Under real uncertainty and limited resources, satisficing can outperform maximizing designed for non-existent conditions, and simpler models can be more elegant [msg #89].
- **A new habit needs the old cue to become dull.** A pleasant joke placed at the old location rewards the old route and entrenches it [msg #497].
- **Post-release restlessness has different engines.** Anxiety about wasting time, impatience of the "Artist" to realise the whole vision, and hunger for novelty [msg #717].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Hindsight bias as a failure of the prediction and error-correction cycle
**Definition and recognition.** The author defines hindsight bias («ошибка/эффект послезнания», English hindsight bias) as the tendency to perceive events and facts that became clear only now as obvious, even though in reality we did not have enough information to predict them precisely, and perhaps nobody did. Its folk name is «крепость заднего ума» (roughly "the strength of the mind that comes afterwards"). It is recognisable by exclamations after an uncertain outcome resolves: "I told you so!", "I knew it", or regret like "Damn, I should have done it that way after all, how could I be so slow!". The post's subtitle, "I told me so", plays on this [msg #20, #22].

**What it does to us.** The most common outcome, says the author, is "gnawing at oneself" («погрызть себя»): "But I knew!", "Tsk, I should have done it like this then, I could have". It is hardest when the act is already done and we condemn ourselves for not having taken a better decision, as if we chose some important things and ignored or trampled others where we did not have to. Most often we then:
1. feel shame, guilt, deep sadness up to grief, and as a consequence anger at ourselves and/or at the circumstances;
2. may be convinced that the damage is colossal and the consequences irreparable (catastrophizing).

What drops out of focus is that in life we sometimes simply lack the inner resources (strength, focus) and information to know solutions that exist somewhere; in the moment we take the decision available to us, because by definition we cannot use what we do not have [msg #22].

**Bug or feature? The two brain programs involved.** Asked whether this is a bug or a feature, the author answers "yes", as with many brain programs: it depends on how you "cook" it. In her view two programs play the main role:
1. **The predictive program**, which engages attention and memory subprograms to analyse data from experience.
2. **The error-correction system** ("damn it", she adds): whatever did not match expectations must be fixed [msg #22].

**How it should work normally** (the author's model of a healthy internal monologue):
1. Before the outcome: "I know this, and I've had it happen like that. And I'll also ask over here. Aha, so here such-and-such may happen, with such-and-such probability. What else may happen, I don't know."
2. After the outcome: "Oh, so that's how it turned out! Well, well. Next time I can take this into account too."
The healthy cycle explicitly keeps an "unknown" category before the event and treats the outcome as new data for next time [msg #22].

**How it can work instead** (the distorted mode): "What a nobody I am! EVERYONE knew, and I'm being slow! If only I'd thought a bit longer! If only I'd looked into that thing I've known about for exactly five minutes! HOW COULD I MAKE SUCH A MISTAKE?!" [msg #22]

**Where the error lies.** The author suggests that in problem-solving we run a command cycle — "What do I already know?" and "Find new info" — switching between the thoughts "What is known and what is unknown? How do I find out what is unknown? What is known now?". When this sequence fails, we come to believe that the new information was already ours and we just somehow did not pay attention. A second feature makes it worse: our tendency to find justifications for our beliefs. We may analyse experience so as to pick out of memory precisely those crumbs of hints that seemingly pointed to the outcome that happened — «Чтоб побольнее, ну вот так» ("to make it hurt more, like that") [msg #22].

**What to do instead.** Most events in everyday situations can be compensated later: try differently, obtain the missing information, express regret, offer something in place of what was missed. Attempts to act differently in the future bring more benefit than suffering over what did not happen combined with no action to change the present. Only by trying and finding out will we both predict better and get rid of fear and powerlessness — "you only need to let yourself be supported in this" [msg #23].

### Three reasons we keep repeating a "not best" action
**When to use.** The author separates hindsight bias from a different case: "OK, but what if I REALLY knew and still did worse for myself? Like eating at night — is that hindsight bias too?" If we keep making "not best" choices over and over, the likely difficulties lie in one of three areas:
1. **Motivation** — it is actually unclear why one should stop and do something else instead.
2. **Experience** — it is unclear what to replace the behaviour with, since it helps at least somehow to meet these needs; alternative strategies are lacking.
3. **Tools for handling emotions** — there are not enough strategies for stopping before grabbing the habitual action, and not enough strategies for relieving the hardening tension that blocks the brain's functions of measured planning.

### Critique of the "I did my best" frame
**The problem.** Sometimes we do things that are not the best manifestations of ourselves and feel sad, hurt, ashamed and remorseful. As supporters of nonviolent communication and a humanistic approach, people then try to show, or even prove, that the person (oneself or another) did the best they could. Yet this often provokes distrust, rejection and a vague sense that something in the logic is off. The author rejects the explanation that we are merely used to punishing ourselves and need time to learn self-compassion — "Я вижу иначе" ("I see it differently") [msg #195].

**The argument, step by step:**
1. **The question about "best" is already a step toward delusion.** Formerly the idea was "those who erred must correct themselves"; now the pendulum has swung to "great job". "We either execute or extol" — the author wants shades and balance. Trying to convince oneself or another that we did "the best" adds fuel to these extremes, which in her view are not about real life [msg #195].
2. **Resources are chronically short.** In real life, as research in behavioural economics and human decision-making has shown (as the author puts it), we regularly lack strength, time, money, attention, information and other things. So most often we simply do not have the luxury of acting "in the best way" [msg #195].
3. **Priorities shift.** The importance of the same things changes for us across contexts and depending on those resources [msg #195].
4. **Every choice leaves parts behind.** Most often we choose in favour of only part of what we value (or several parts if we have more resources at that moment). Almost always some parts are sad and hurt by the decision, and after them other parts feel ashamed and remorseful: "I could have but didn't", "I could have but chose otherwise". "Yes, exactly so" — the author accepts this as true rather than refuting it [msg #195].
5. **Even the half-measure fails.** Phrases like «сделал(а) лучшее из доступного» ("did the best of what was available") work poorly and do not convince, because different things were available. We cannot prove we did everything right or super-optimally, because "right" means everything we wanted worked out; that requires a lot that we often do not have, and even that is sometimes not enough [msg #195].
6. **A neglected part will answer back.** Even if we shout "Of what you had, you chose the best!", some part will whisper: «Лучшее? А как же я?.. Значит, выбрать меня — не лучшее? Неужели я настолько не важна?» ("The best? And what about me?.. So choosing me is not the best? Am I really that unimportant?") [msg #195].
7. **Labels again.** Dropping labels in favour of the needs and possibilities of a specific person brings more clarity and understanding; so why cling to labels ("best") here, the author asks [msg #195].

**The alternative.** Can we make things get better — have more resources, know and be able to do more, have more strength, manage time more harmoniously for ourselves? The author answers "Yes". As far as we can, today and tomorrow, we can give ourselves more support, attention and care, learn what is needed and ask for help, so that the next decision more likely satisfies more of what is important — "Сегодня и завтра, но не вчера" ("Today and tomorrow, but not yesterday"). In the moment, most often the only choice available supports and pleases some parts of us, and with the other parts we need to grieve: to feel regret and perhaps tomorrow "warm and stroke" what was left in the shade the day before. This, she says, is what we can really do to stay in contact with what we value — do what is available in the specific moment and what makes us somewhat better off in a world of **limited** possibilities [msg #195].

### Overcontrol: four cultivated habits and their four costs (RO DBT)
**The puzzle.** Many remember from childhood that overeating, overspending or over-entertaining is bad and a lack of self-control, whereas proactively solving problems, building clear plans, personal growth and sport lead to heights few reach and deserve praise. The author's ironic summary: "forge discipline while it's hot, and when it cools, add heat, and there will be happiness". Yet for some people it is hard to relax, switch, ask for help and show feelings — especially so that people notice, rather than guess thanks to long acquaintance and knowing where to look. Socialising quickly causes "overheating" even with friendly people; fits of anger and panic arrive when something does not go to plan, whether a late bus or a person who wanted something "at the wrong time". Rest, slowing down and warm contact, which should restore strength, come harder to some of us "than a five-year plan in three years"; the author asks who all the research saying slow life, socialising and enjoying the moment are the real keys to happiness is about [msg #473].

**The model.** The ability to control and plan has a reverse side. If we cultivate:
1. restraining impulses,
2. continuing to work toward a better future regardless of the situation in the moment,
3. managing our resources alone (without involving others), and
4. building strategies and systems thought through to the smallest detail,

then:
1. it will be hard to leave room for life as it is — with its sudden events, other people's wishes and the need for inter-action about all this;
2. we ourselves will be invisible, because in trying to keep face so as not to accidentally evoke the "wrong" feelings in someone, we achieve that no feelings are evoked at all;
3. that held face becomes glass through which signals do not pass to the people around us; and
4. we will always be somewhat in the mode of an "unavailable superhuman" whose very "cockroaches in the head" (quirks) march in formation [msg #473].

**The message we broadcast.** Explicitly or covertly, the message outward is roughly: «Я знаю, как правильно, у меня всё под контролем, мне не нужна помощь, контакта не будет, но вы, если что, иногда обращайтесь» ("I know what is right, I have everything under control, I don't need help, there will be no contact, but you, if anything, do reach out sometimes"), with the author's warning: "traces of judgement possible" [msg #473].

**Consequences.** Such an inner system can leave us always somewhat disappointed — in others, in ourselves and in how chaotically the world is arranged. It is a perpetual-motion machine with an endless crescendo, with no room for the scenery, enjoying what has been achieved, spontaneity or "I just feel like doing it this way right now". When everything is locked up, it becomes sad, cold and lonely; support, understanding and care are lacking, along with everything that drops out of focus, and so one wishes it would somehow happen by itself [msg #473].

**The motive (what it is not).** It may seem to be about not showing weakness, perfectionism or even a sense of superiority, but the author says the motive is entirely different: radical non-acceptance («радикальное непринятие») of chaos and stagnation in all their forms. It may be an escape from past nightmares, or a value that has become a maxim instead of a guide [msg #473].

**The counterweights.** What refills the cup to balance the scales: not correcting (oneself or others), being sad and asking, sometimes being capricious and practising non-action (wu wei), sharing important things without a thought-out plan — with an attitude of «будь что будет, я разберусь на месте» ("come what may, I'll figure it out on the spot") [msg #473].

**Source and illustration.** The author recognised herself in this while trying to understand some of her own traits and found that this is what RO DBT (radically open dialectical behaviour therapy) works with; she says she is now exploring it. The attached four-panel comic [img #473] (signed OWLTURD.COM) shows a relaxed green figure labelled "Type B" telling a rushing red "Type A" with a briefcase and watch: "Hey buddy, slow down!" and "Don't you know that sometimes you need to stop and just smell the flowers?"; Type A then furiously stuffs an armful of flowers into his face and hoists a trophy reading "flower-smelling champion" — overcontrol turning even rest into a competition. The post closes by asking readers whether they feel like flower-smelling champions [msg #473, img #473].

### Seven key ideas of the "good daughter" / false self video
**Context.** The author shares a YouTube video (channel SilentPatterns) "for all who are used to not standing out" («не отсвечивать»): a deep reflection on the emotional and psychological price of growing up in an environment that rewards submission rather than authenticity, especially for women and girls. Being "easy to raise" often means learning to suppress one's own needs, emotions and desires to maintain harmony, avoid conflict and be loved. The video covers the emotional architecture of the "good daughter" — one who never made trouble and never asked for too much, but at some point realised that approval is given at the cost of showing up. Such daughters are formed not by direct violence but by subtle calibration: rewarded for compliance and silence and slowly ground into people who will carry responsibility for the emotional climate of those around them. The author's own view: this holds for everyone raised "for others" with a ban on expressing their own needs — whether the cause is female gender socialisation, parents' narcissistic wish to create a continuation of themselves that they could not be, or anything else [msg #543].

**The key ideas, as the author summarises the video:**
1. **False self** («Ложное Я»). Drawing on Donald Winnicott and Gabor Maté, the video explains how children adapt to their environment by creating a socially acceptable version of themselves — one that secures attachment but erases authenticity [msg #543].
2. **Emotional editing.** Over time such a person learns to filter, dilute or hide true feelings. This adaptation is not about lying; it is a survival strategy in relationships [msg #543].
3. **Invisible trauma.** The impact of emotional neglect or lack of mirroring is described as subtle trauma. The author glosses mirroring as active listening, sensitively telling the other what you observe, empathising with their feelings and various ways of showing that you understand and care. There is no single big event — "just a thousand moments" when self-expression met disapproval, indifference or discomfort [msg #543].
4. **Disconnection from self.** Eventually the person forgets what they actually feel, want and need; they become obedient and agreeable but lose access to their own identity [msg #543].
5. **The hidden cost.** Outwardly successful and calm, the person suffers quietly — through fatigue, anxiety, disconnection and a deep inner emptiness [msg #543].
6. **Survival as simulation.** Life becomes a series of preset responses rather than genuine choices; the person adapts and performs, responding to external demands instead of relying on their own desires [msg #543].
7. **Longing for authenticity.** The video ends on a soft, aching note — acknowledging that what such a person lacks is neither a whim nor drama but something foundational [msg #543].

**Overall message.** It is not about how a person might break under such a burden but about how something in them never got to grow. The call is to let oneself be seen, to return to the self buried under adaptation, and to sit with this quiet truth before trying to fix it [msg #543].

### Habit replacement: the new association must be bright and pleasant, the old one neutral and rarer
**The self-experiment.** The author's household bought a new 30-litre rubbish bin with a pedal and a wooden lid, placed it on the border between the cooking and eating zones, not far from the old small bin under the sink. From habit they kept opening the old door under the sink. She put a cute note with instructions in the old bin, reading "Not here, turn around 180°" [img #497] — expecting the new habit to stick. On the third day of the "new life" the old bin had "smiled at them" for the 30th time [msg #497, img #497].

**The behavioural rule.** Switching off her "exclamation marks" and switching on the behaviour specialist, the author explains: to consolidate a habit or replace an association with a new one (for example, to make you want to return to a certain place), the brain needs the new association to be bright and pleasant **and at the same time** the old one to evoke nothing much and to occur less often — that way the old one gradually fades out [msg #497].

**Why the note backfired.** Each time they opened the old place they enjoyed the joke and only then turned 180° to the new bin, as instructed. So the habit actually being formed was: open the door → laugh at the little "hat" (the old bin) → turn around → be glad of the new "well". Even after a couple of days, when they began stopping at the stage of reaching toward the door, the treacherous "ha-ha" still caught them. The lesson: a reward placed at the old cue strengthens the old route. Her conclusion was to remove the note [msg #497].

### Three drivers of the urge to start the next product right after a release
**The question.** Why, after a product is released, may there be a thirst to take up a new one at once — or more often the next version — even when the result is sincerely pleasing? The author asks what inner process unites scientists, entrepreneurs, artists and engineers in this inspiring but sometimes exhausting state, and proposes several possible causes depending on the nature of the impulse (she frames them as her suppositions):
1. **"No time to lose on the way to the summit."** Cases where a person worries they will not fulfil their purpose, duty and the like (the author's own "и так далее") unless they act continuously; or feels they must "run as fast as they can just to stay in place", like the Red Queen in "Alice in Wonderland". Inspiration may underlie both, but in the author's view it more often coexists with anxiety or is even displaced by it [msg #717].
2. **"The big picture is so attractive there is no strength to wait longer."** The impulse of the Artist to bring the idea to life as quickly as possible. The Artist often vividly pictures the final product at the start and feels that constraints hold them back too much — whether audience or business expectations to ship an MVP (minimum viable product) or a personal limit of inspiration and physical strength per unit of time [msg #717].
3. **Craving for novelty.** All cases where a person is hungry for a cascade of new impressions. It may or may not be accompanied by celebrating each release: from a constant "create → release → celebrate → repeat" cycle to the philosophy "a samurai has no goal, only the path". The author thinks this third variant is often the main driving force for people with ADHD, and probably the second one too [msg #717].
She closes by asking readers what drives them when they want more [msg #717].

## Detailed insights

### Overcomplication as a brain "feature" turned error: from maximizing to satisficing
The author describes her own persistent brain habit: complicating any idea, concept, structure, model, "this very sentence". She traces it to childhood training: before school her godmother taught her draughts and then chess; a cousin added card games; her mother filled the home with countless guests from different countries and social strata, with whom deep conversations were always held about motivation in fundamental questions — principles for choosing partners, sexual orientation, why everyone believes in their own gods (her mother, "to put it mildly, is not afraid of explosive topics"). She calls this "kitchen epistemology mixed with CBT" [msg #89].

By school she could already "read" people's motives and "quickly count" their future moves; doing this with schoolchildren was "easy as pie", because her "neural network" had been trained on teenagers (with their hundred parallel self-images to fit into groups and scheme) and on adults, including well-read and experienced ones who played chess with her [msg #89].

She also believed that normal means thinking in many threads, interestingly, with branches and layers. An inner young Rogerian humanist, without knowing it, insisted: «В остальных случаях что-то помешало развитию, нужно только создать условия» ("In other cases something hindered development; one only needs to create conditions"). If so, it is right to model people as "complex" and the world as an even greater complexity containing them. So she "short-circuited" when someone said "I just did it", "I somehow didn't think", "It's fine by me as it is", "It works somehow — and OK". She could not believe that sometimes things happen because they were simpler, at hand, or one did not want to think — that is, because of the ease of a quickly accessible choice ("there, I did it again", she notes about her own complicated phrasing) [msg #89].

She "breathed" the idea of the "rational man" who weighs all pros and cons and makes the best decisions not only for the present but for the future self; her mind, she says, lived in the 18th–19th centuries and fed on the ideals of Adam Smith and John Stuart Mill (greeting modern utilitarianism and effective altruism) [msg #89].

Only with experience did she build other models of the world and of the person in it — one where information is limited, resources are lacking, sometimes the choice is only between two options, and the environment influences how priorities and values line up in the moment. From outside this may look "simpler". But now she would say that uncertainty in the modern real world is higher than an "ideal" decision model in a spherical vacuum can cover, and there may not be resources to extinguish all unknown risks [msg #89].

Hence she now sees this as the birth of a new complexity — one where simplicity is elegance and, in Herbert Simon's terms, «достаточно хорошее (satisficing) может побеждать наилучшее (maximizing)» ("good enough (satisficing) can beat the best (maximizing)") that was created under some non-existent conditions. It follows that one can keep fewer objects in attention, building models and forecasts with lower cognitive complexity at their base (per George Kelly and James Bieri), and she hopes modern neuroeconomics will help with this [msg #89].

The general frame of the post ("50 shades of grey… matter: when brain features become errors"): many brain habits are useful — at work or in relationships — but become irritating and harmful when too frequent or out of place; she invites readers to name their own features that both please and annoy them [msg #89].

### How the topics connect
The same resource-limited model of decision-making runs through several posts: in hindsight bias we forget we lacked information and inner resources [msg #22]; in the "I did my best" critique, behavioural-economics research on limited resources and shifting priorities is the reason "best" is an illusion [msg #195]; and in the overcomplication post, the move from the "rational man" to bounded, satisficing choice is the author's personal version of the same shift [msg #89]. Overcontrol [msg #473] and the "good daughter" false self [msg #543] both describe adaptive strategies (control, compliance) that cost visibility, contact and access to one's own needs.
