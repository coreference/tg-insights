# Careers in psychology and science: from clinic to research

## What this covers

This topic follows the channel author's own career moves: first, finding paid work as a psychologist on an international online therapy marketplace (Fitcy) while keeping her home-country licence; second, her transition from clinical practitioner to neuroscientist, including job hunting on Academic Transfer, studying a classic guide to surviving in science ("A PhD Is Not Enough!"), and learning to code (MATLAB first, then Python). According to the author, these steps turned out to be more achievable than feared ("нестрашно, несложно и реально"), but the move into research has one hard gate — coding — that a strong theoretical background does not replace [msg #45, #287, #495].

## Core ideas

- **Working abroad online as a psychologist is realistic.** Platforms like Fitcy hire specialists under their own country's licence, need no translated documents and leave taxes to the specialist; the author was hired after a short English interview — see *Online-platform hiring path* [msg #41, #45].
- **Session rates scale with experience.** Beginners' sessions are priced very low, experienced specialists' much higher; client prices are not what the specialist earns — see *Fitcy pricing* [msg #41].
- **Science can pay.** Academic Transfer was the first place where the author saw that being a well-paid scientist is "not an oxymoron" — but job requirements are steep [msg #287].
- **Coding is the main barrier for a clinician moving into neuroscience.** Broad knowledge of neurosciences, physiology, cognitive science and neurophilosophy is not enough; one must code, preferably in Python, because with other languages the number of accessible studies drops dramatically [msg #287, #495].
- **Science survival skills are rarely taught.** Feibelman's book argues that PhD programmes teach technical skills but not how to survive in science, so young scientists need a mentor or a guide — see *Chapter map* [msg #299, #302].
- **Coding can be learned step by step from zero** with hand-holding beginner courses; MATLAB Onramp is recommended for complete beginners — see *Two-pass method* [msg #495].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Online-platform hiring path for a psychologist abroad (Fitcy case)
Purpose: what the process of joining an international online therapy service looked like in the author's experience in 2022, so others can try it ("Кто ещё хотел бы попробовать свои силы, очень советую!" - "Whoever else would like to try their strength, I highly recommend it!") [msg #45].

1. **Research the platform and its rates.** The author studied Fitcy — an intermediary between client and psychologist, a local analogue of the Russian services Alter and Meta (services where people pick a psychotherapist). She first saw "funny" (very low) rates, then discovered those were for sessions with beginners; experienced specialists are priced far higher (see *Fitcy pricing*) [msg #41, #45].
2. **Apply and take the interview.** The interview lasted half an hour, in English, conducted by a woman from India with excellent English. Questions covered how the candidate came into the profession ("как я дошла до жизни такой" - "how I came to live like this"), her experience, and salary. The conversation was pleasant and went into mothers, genetics and the brain [msg #41, #45].
3. **Know your therapeutic approach by name.** Pitfall from the author's own interview: she completely forgot to name the "integrated approach" and spent the time describing it at length instead — "три часа играла в Шекспира вокруг этого" ("for three hours I played Shakespeare around it"), i.e. talking around the term rather than stating it. She frames it humorously ("зато живописно!" - "but picturesque!") [msg #41].
4. **Wait for the answer and do not give up early.** The platform promised an answer within a week, otherwise "афсё, вы не прошли" ("that's it, you didn't pass"). The author had already resigned herself to rejection, but the reply came about a week later than promised — and she was accepted [msg #41, #45].
5. **Paperwork.** The key relief: Fitcy works with a specialist under the licence of **his/her own** country, so no additional retraining is required. No document translations are needed, and taxes can be handled independently however the specialist wishes — the platform does not ask about them [msg #41, #45].
6. **Next step: broaden to other platforms.** The author planned to try TaskHuman next, which accepts not only psychologists but other health and "good life" specialists — home-workout trainers, dietitians, trip planners, dog trainers, with the source itself closing the list with "и пр." (an open-ended Russian abbreviation; no further specialist types are named) [msg #45].

### Chapter map of "A PhD Is Not Enough! A Guide to Survival in Science" (Peter J. Feibelman, revised edition)
Purpose: the author shared the table of contents as a map of the survival skills a young scientist needs, giving her own free Russian rendering ("художественный перевод") meant to sound natural to Russian-speaking readers, with the English original in photographed contents pages [msg #299, #300, #301, #302]. The source gives only the chapter descriptions, not the content of the chapters themselves.

1. **Part 1 — "Do You See Yourself in This Picture?"** A set of nonfiction vignettes illustrating ways young scientists make their lives more unpleasant than necessary or fail entirely to establish themselves in a research career. The author's rendering: how a young scientist with good intentions can pave his own road to hell, and how not to accidentally sabotage oneself ("не подложить себе свинью") [msg #299, #300].
2. **Part 2 — "Advice from a Dinosaur?"** (p. 19) Whether you can expect someone to be an effective mentor who emerged into the scientific marketplace in a world that looked very different — i.e. whether to take a mentor from a past era's science "market" [msg #299, #300].
3. **Part 3 — "Important Choices: A Thesis Adviser, a Postdoctoral Job"** (p. 27) What to consider: a young adviser versus an older one, a superstar versus a journeyman, a small group versus a "factory"; understanding and attending to your own interests as a postdoc — how to choose what is best for **your** interests [msg #299, #301].
4. **Part 4 — "Giving Talks"** (p. 39) Preparing talks that make people want to hire and keep you, and that make the information easy to assimilate — in the author's words, talks that convey the essence [msg #299, #301].
5. **Part 5 — "Writing Papers: Publishing Without Perishing"** (p. 53) Why it is important to write good papers and to write them up in time; how to draw the reader in and draw attention to your results [msg #299, #301].
6. **Part 6 — "From Here to Tenure: Choosing a Career Path"** (p. 69) An unsentimental comparison of the merits of jobs in academia, industry and government laboratories [msg #299, #301].
7. **Part 7 — "Job Interviews"** (p. 91) What will happen on your interview trip and the questions you had better be prepared to answer [msg #299, #301].
8. **Part 8 — "Getting Funded"** (p. 107) What goes into an effective grant proposal, how and when to start writing one [msg #299, #301].
9. **Part 9 — "Establishing a Research Program"** (p. 121) Tuning research efforts to your own capabilities and situation in life; the book's example: why not to start a five-year project when you have a two-year postdoctoral appointment. The author's rendering: make realistic forecasts about your "brainchild" so that neither the contract nor you run out before the project does [msg #299, #301].
10. **Part 10 — "A Survival Checklist"** (p. 135) Necessary safety rules to stay on course and reach the desired destination; the book's tagline, as transcribed: "Do not attempt a takeoff before being sure the" … "Raps are down" (evidently "flaps": do not take off before checking the flaps are down) [msg #299, #302].
11. **Afterthoughts** (p. 141) A behaviorist approach to professional success. The author adds: experience is the best teacher, but only if it is not fatal ("опыт — лучший учитель, только если он не фатальный") [msg #299, #302].

The book's premise, from its preface (transcribed from the photographed page): Feibelman says his scientific career "almost never happened" — he came out of graduate school with a PhD and excellent technical skills but little understanding of how to survive in science, and he was not unusual. Survival skills are rarely part of the graduate curriculum. Many professional scientists believe that "good" students find their way on their own while the rest cannot be helped; this justifies neglect and, "perhaps not incidentally", reduces their workload. He grants there may be some sense to the Darwinian selection implicit in "benign neglect", but on the whole failing to teach science survival wastes a great deal of student talent and time and not infrequently makes a mess of students' lives. Because these skills are rarely taught directly, most young scientists need a mentor — found in graduate school, as a postdoctoral researcher, or perhaps as an assistant professor (the page breaks off mid-sentence) [msg #302].

### Two-pass method for completing MATLAB Onramp
Purpose: how the author, a complete coding beginner, finished her first programming course — "the first of two languages" she needs, since she cannot get hired in a lab and study the brain without coding [msg #495].

1. **Get free access through the Coursera route.** The MATLAB course and platform access are free; you only need to state that you came from the free Computational Neuroscience course on Coursera, which itself recommends this platform [msg #495].
2. **First pass slowly.** She went through the course "улиточкой" ("like a snail") up to the final project, then stopped [msg #495].
3. **Second pass quickly.** She went through the whole course again at a brisk pace to refresh everything before submitting [msg #495].
4. **Submit the final project.** After the refresh, it passed fairly easily — "слегка напряжённенько, но посильно" ("a bit tense, but doable"). Pitfall: the final project has a couple of flaws; in a couple of places she had to guess and read information on the built-in forum [msg #495].
5. **Move on to the next language.** Result: a MathWorks Course Completion Certificate for 100% of the self-paced MATLAB Onramp (28 September 2024). Next "hill": Python [img #495] [msg #495].

## Detailed insights

### Fitcy pricing
Initially the author found Fitcy's rates "смешные" ("funny", i.e. very low); further research showed these were for sessions with beginners. For "seniors — and señoritas, of course" prices are more serious: from 249 AED for 3–5 years of experience and 349 AED for 6+ years, roughly 67–95 US dollars per hour. These are prices for clients; the specialist's pay can (and she expects will) differ — minus the service's cut, plus taxes, "плюс на кофе" ("plus for coffee"). Still, she calls it encouraging [msg #41].

### From clinical practice to neuroscience: what is hard
Academic Transfer is a vacancy site for those building a career in science; it was the first place where the author saw that "быть учёным с деньгами — это не оксюморон" ("being a scientist with money is not an oxymoron"). She started browsing vacancies a year before the post and found requirements "покруче, чем от наших родителей в детстве" ("tougher than our parents' in childhood"). Seriously, the main difficulty is the transition itself — from clinical practitioner to neuroscientist. Her background (medical neurosciences, physiology, cognitive sciences, neurophilosophy from visual perception to theories of consciousness) does not remove the requirement to code. Python is "всему голова" ("the head of everything"): one must be able to code, and better in Python, because with other languages the number of available studies "drops dramatically" [msg #287].

The site's emails push candidates to keep profiles current: the screenshot shows Academic Transfer's "What have you been up to this past half-year? Share it with potential employers" email, asking users to update their profile and CV regularly and prompting three questions [img #287]:
- did you learn a new skill?
- did you publish an article?
- did you find a new job?

### Learning resources for computational neuroscience
The author is taking the Coursera Computational Neuroscience course to understand the practical side of the field and calls it "тоже очень хороший курс" ("also a very good course"). MATLAB Onramp she recommends for complete beginners: every task is explained step by step and the instructions feel like being led by the hand [msg #495].
