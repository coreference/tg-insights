# How perception works: from sensory fragments to meaning

## What this covers

This topic covers how the channel author explains perception: the brain's assembly of separate sensory fragments into a meaningful picture. It also covers why people in the same situation can end up with different pictures. Four posts make up the topic: (1) the visually evoked auditory response (vEAR) and the author's expectation-based explanation of it; (2) perception as "assembly", with the white-or-blue dress as the example; (3) simultaneous versus successive perception from Luria and Gibson, plus how this science turned into the "creative right brain / logical left brain" myth; (4) individual differences in colour perception and an online test of where a person draws the line between blue and green. According to the source, this matters because differences in perceptual "settings" show up everywhere, from optical illusions to a casually dropped phrase that sets off a heated argument (a "holy war") [msg #227].

## Core ideas

- **Perception is assembly, and the assembly differs between people.** We put fragments of sound, smell, colour and shape together into a meaningful picture. Different "settings" and "programs" of assembly give different results, even for people in the same circumstances [msg #227].
- **Expectation shapes perception, possibly down to the body.** The author thinks the most likely explanation of vEAR is that we perceive events through our expectations from past experience. She suggests that expecting a sound could even trigger a real physical effect in the ear (the acoustic reflex) [msg #199].
- **The brain sees in two modes.** It can see the whole at once (simultaneous) or build up details step by step (successive). Classic neuropsychology linked these modes to the right and left hemispheres, and damage studies still support that basic split [msg #366].
- **The "creative right / logical left" brain is a popularised distortion.** It is an appealing dualism that journalists and pop culture took from the real research and oversimplified [msg #366].
- **Sensory hardware differs between people.** Disputes like the dress and Laurel/Yanny show that people can register the same signals differently, depending on the number of different cones and hair cells they have [msg #474].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Simultaneous vs successive perception (Luria / Gibson)
**Purpose:** to explain how we can both recognise what we are looking at in an instant and then examine it piece by piece. The author calls this "zoom-in/zoom-out", seeing either in detail or as a whole. She introduces it as the background to an earlier post on perception in autism spectrum disorder (РАС), where she had used the two terms. She jokingly calls "симультанное" and "сукцессивное" "два матерных слова" (two swear words) because they sound so technical [msg #366].

**The everyday illustration.** When we see a picture we can quickly grasp "a goat". A second later we notice the parts: the cloven hooves, the white fur, the "hellish" slit-pupil eyes "that look straight into your soul". Both happen because the brain can perceive the world in two modes, sequentially and all at once [msg #366].

**The two components:**
1. **Simultaneous perception (симультанное, "общее сразу", the whole at once).** This is the direct, immediate perception of objects and events as a whole, "simultaneous" in English. In Luria's scheme it sits in the **right hemisphere**. When it fails, as after right-hemisphere damage, a person has trouble recognising the overall structure of an image and cannot "see the forest for the trees" ("за деревьями не видя леса") [msg #366].
2. **Successive perception (сукцессивное, "по шагам", step by step).** This is perception built up in steps, through details and through changes that happen over time, "successive" in English. In Luria's scheme it sits in the **left hemisphere**. When it fails, as after left-hemisphere damage, a person misses the small parts and perceives only the general features [msg #366].

**The diagnostic example.** Patients are asked to draw a large letter "M" made of small triangles. People with right-hemisphere damage draw only the separate triangles. People with left-hemisphere damage draw only the overall "M" [msg #366].

**Origin and status of the construct, as the author tells it:**
- In Soviet neuropsychology the split became a fundamental construct with its own terms. Alexander Luria wrote a great deal about it and created many probes (пробы, small diagnostic tasks) to diagnose these functions. He placed the "step-by-step" mode in the left hemisphere and the "holistic" mode in the right [msg #366].
- The author notes that neither Luria's works she found nor her university lectures explain *how* Luria arrived at these concepts. She asks readers to send her the answer if they find it [msg #366].
- At around the same time, the American scientist James Gibson was researching perception. In 1966 he published the book "Чувства как системы восприятия" ("The Senses Considered as Perceptual Systems"), which according to the author was the first to describe these processes. The book distinguished direct perception of objects and events (simultaneous) from perception of changes over time (successive). The author says this distinction reflects Gibson's broader argument: perception is not only the interpretation of sensory data from the sense organs, but also the way those data are organised in real time [msg #366].
- Some of Gibson's work has been criticised in light of new data, but the author says the foundation is still considered correct. Her evidence is the hemisphere-damage pattern and the "M" of triangles described above [msg #366].

### Expectation-removal self-experiment for vEAR
**Purpose:** a test the author made up herself, offered to readers to check whether a phantom sound "heard" from a silent visual comes from expectation [msg #199].
1. **Watch the silent visual that seems to "sound".** In the original post this was a clip of a tower lifting off the ground and coming back down [msg #199].
2. **Remove the expectations.** Strip away your anticipation of what *could* happen during such an event, meaning the sound such an event would normally make [msg #199].
3. **Say the facts out loud, several times.** Describe only what is literally on screen, for example: «Вышка отрывается от земли и опускается обратно» ("The tower lifts off the ground and comes back down") [msg #199].
4. **Check the result.** Ask yourself whether the visual still "sounds", or produces any sensation other than a visual one [msg #199].
**Why it should work, in the author's reasoning:** if vEAR comes from expecting a sound, possibly through the acoustic reflex (see *What vEAR is and how it might work*), then focusing on bare facts should weaken the effect. The author poses this as an open question and does not report results [msg #199].

## Detailed insights

### Perception as assembly of fragments
The author defines perception (восприятие) as how we gather separate pieces of something into an understandable picture. It is the meaning we extract from the flow of sounds, smells, colours, shapes and everything else that hits us, from outside and from inside. The picture we get depends on our assembly "settings" and "programs". Her examples: is the rustling in the bushes the wind or a tiger? Is the waterfall whispering our name? Both are perception at work [msg #227].

The author finds it most interesting when people in the same circumstances assemble the final picture differently. She says this shows up everywhere, from whether a dress is white or blue to how a casually dropped phrase can start a "holivar" (холивар, a heated online dispute) [msg #227].

### What vEAR is and how it might work
The post opens with a reader poll from an earlier post: more than half of the readers said they heard a sound from a silent visual. In reply, the author introduces **vEAR**, the visually evoked auditory response, which she renders in Russian as СОВВ, «слуховой отклик, вызываемый визуально» [msg #199].

**The discovery.** According to the author, researchers at the University of London found it in 2017. People were shown a circle on a screen that flashed in the rhythm of Morse-code signals and were asked to find similar sequences. The people who did this best were those who, while watching, seemed to *hear* the flashes. They made up 22% of the participants [msg #199].

**Explanation 1: synesthesia.** The first hypothesis is that vEAR is a form of synesthesia. The author describes synesthesia as an unusual way the five senses can work, in which stimulating one sense also causes a response in another. Her examples are the number 1 feeling hot, the colour yellow causing a sour taste in the mouth, and the name «Володя» (Volodya) feeling smooth. The last is her own childhood memory [msg #199].

**Explanation 2: anticipation (the author's preferred view).** Some participants described the sensation as anticipation, a kind of readiness to perceive the sound that might go with the image. The author says this is in her view the most likely version. We perceive an event through a whole range of sensations, thoughts and emotions, and our perception is often closely tied to expectations from past experience [msg #199].

**A possible physical mechanism: the acoustic reflex.** The author calls the acoustic reflex well documented. Usually in response to a loud sound, though not always, the stapedius muscle (стременная мышца) in the ear contracts. This reduces the strength of the vibrations passed on to the cochlea (ушная «улитка») so that things get quieter. The contraction also slightly changes the shape of the inner ear, which can make us hear an extra noise. The author then asks whether expecting a sound could trigger the same effect, and answers «Похоже, что да» ("It seems so"). This is offered as her own reasoning, not as a tested finding [msg #199].

### How the hemisphere science became the "creative vs logical brain" myth
The author explains the myth through a general human tendency. There are ideas we pick up easily and with pleasure, and as soon as something close to them appears on the horizon we rejoice: "this is it!" She promised to explain how this works in a repost the next day [msg #366].

Humanity has a very rich history of dualist ideas: soul and body, typically masculine and feminine, logic and ethics. "Rational versus creative" belongs in the same series. So when the idea appeared that the right hemisphere holds whole pictures and the "airy-general", while the left holds separate details and algorithms, it was irresistible. It was especially appealing to people who hunt for the juicy core of the news rather than the devil in the details. The author says the situation fits a well-known comic's phrase, «учёный изнасиловал журналиста» ("the scientist raped the journalist"), which inverts the usual "journalist mangled the science". After that, she says, the simplified version spread to the masses [msg #366].

The lesson is that the real finding concerns *modes of perception*, whole versus parts and simultaneous versus successive. The popular "creative right / logical left" personality typology is an oversimplification of it [msg #366].

### Individual differences in colour and sound perception
The author recalls the heated disputes about whether the dress was blue or white, and whether the voice in the headphones says Laurel or Yanny. In her account, these showed that different people can register such signals differently at the level of the body, depending on the number of their different cones (колбочки, the colour receptors in the eye) and hair cells (волоски, in the ear). Her conclusion: «Тут наша сенсорика по-настоящему экстра-!» ("Here our sensory system is truly extra!") [msg #474].

She then describes a colour test, sent to her by Marco, that measures where a person places the boundary of "blue". She is careful about what it shows: she has not yet looked into how much it is about perception and how much it is about cultural norms for naming colours. Even so, it already gives interesting results. She and Marco turned out to be "beautiful freaks of nature" [msg #474].

**Caveat for sensitive readers:** if some colours can cause you sensory overload, the author advises skipping the test [msg #474].
