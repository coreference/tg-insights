# Understanding autism: myths, stereotypes and the spectrum

## What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's foundational material on what autism spectrum disorder (ASD, Russian РАС) is and is not: the two competing scientific views (deficit vs difference), a two-voice series debunking common myths, a carousel of parenting myths with parents' testimonies, the three DSM-5 levels of support and practical help at each, the argument against "high-functioning / low-functioning" labels, the spectrum drawn as a wheel of traits, the forgotten history of Grunya Sukhareva and Lorna Wing, and how film and TV have depicted autism. [msg #324, #359, #360, #372, #402, #533, #625, #692]

According to the source, this matters because stereotypes and outdated labels make autistic people invisible, push them into exhausting masking and burnout, devalue the efforts of their parents, and lead to useless or harmful "cures" — while the reality is "гораздо сложнее, между строк и в оттенках" (*much more complex, between the lines and in shades*). The author's guiding attitude is relativity: imagining oneself as precisely as possible in another person's place — with their experience, ideas, values and thinking algorithms — makes their choices understandable and allows constructive action instead of blind condemnation or treating them as "bad", "unpredictably dangerous" or "alien". [msg #324, #317, #402, #628]

## Core ideas

- **Autism is a different brain architecture, not an illness.** It is a feature of brain structure and functioning arising during prenatal development, genetically determined and lifelong; research since 2020 increasingly supports the "difference" view over the "deficit" view (see *Two views of ASD*). [msg #359, #534, #625, #627]
- **Autistic people are not empathy-less robots.** Their empathy profile is uneven — in places weaker, in places stronger than in neurotypicals; high empathic accuracy can cause overload and shutdown. [msg #359]
- **Masking is costly.** Autistic people enjoy communication but often have to do it in ways that suit others; imitating expected behaviour drains energy and, when society rewards it, leads to burnout. [msg #360, #402, #534]
- **Support needs, not "functioning".** Old subtypes (including "high-functioning") disappeared from the manuals; DSM-5 uses three levels of needed support, and the spectrum is better pictured as a wheel of traits of differing intensity than as a scale from more to less autistic. [msg #372, #373, #402, #403]
- **People on the spectrum are as different from each other as from everyone else** — in speech, senses, memory, intelligence, movement, gaze, creativity and emotion. [msg #534]
- **Help means adapting the environment and building skills, not "curing".** Medication is used only for co-occurring conditions (depression, anxiety), diets do not affect autism, and unusual behaviour of autistic children reflects anxiety, social disorientation or sensory overload, not bad upbringing. [msg #360, #627, #629]
- **Understanding should come before judgment.** Before giving unsolicited advice to a parent, ask whether you know enough about their life; offering help is better than lecturing. [msg #631]

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Two views of ASD (deficit model vs difference model)
Purpose: to explain why "autism is an illness, a deviation" (Myth No. 1, by Rita) is a myth. The source first states the agreed fact: autism is a feature of the structure and functioning of the brain that arises during intrauterine development. There are then two views of ASD: [msg #359]

1. **(a) Deficit view** — ASD is impaired development with its own deficits (as in DSM-5 and ICD-11), which can be compensated to one degree or another. [msg #359]
2. **(b) Difference view** — ASD is a feature of nervous organisation in which there are by no means always any defects. What differs is the overall pattern: handling of information (from processing to transmission), focus of attention, expression of feelings ("и есть весь спектр!" — *and there is the whole spectrum!*), and other things. In other words it is a cognitive architecture that in a significant number of cases can cope with assigned tasks, just not in the way usually found in the population. [msg #359]

Evidence: the number of studies supporting (b) is growing. In 2020 (C. Crompton et al., 2020) it was found that the quality of information transfer between people with ASD is at the same level as within a neurotypical group, and only in mixed (autistic + neurotypical) groups do the scores drop. Rita stresses that this is not a nod to inclusive political correctness (saying "special abilities" instead of "deviations" — which she also calls an important turn toward a more objective and humane perception of differences) but results of real foreign studies from 2020–2023 that apparently have not yet reached everyone interested; the series promised to cover them in later posts. Rita describes these as her real finds of the previous two weeks, one of which she had not met in any open scientific space. [msg #359]

A note in the series: "autism" and "autism spectrum disorder (ASD)" are currently used as synonyms, including in professional communities. [msg #359]

### Five myths of autism (Rita and Maria series)
A two-part series titled "Мифические существа: кто они, и где обитают" (*Mythical creatures: who they are and where they live*), written "in two voices" by the channels @gazing_upon and @your_real_strory, each myth signed by Rita or Maria. [msg #359, #360]

1. **Myth 1 (Rita): "Аутизм* — это болезнь, отклонение"** (*autism is an illness, a deviation*). Truth: it is a feature of the brain; see *Two views of ASD* above for the full answer and the Crompton 2020 evidence. [msg #359]
2. **Myth 2 (Rita): people on the spectrum cannot sympathise or empathise, which is exactly why they seem like rational robots.** Truth: a 2019 meta-analysis showed that the empathy pattern of people with ASD is in some places less and in other places more pronounced than in neurotypical people. It depends strongly on whether the person is a boy or a girl (the source attributes this to the influence of the environment) and becomes closer to typical with age. Breakdown by empathy component:
   - **Empathic accuracy is initially increased** — the ability to share other people's emotional states and become attuned to them in the charge (+ or −) and intensity of emotion, analogous to emotional contagion and resonance (Decety and Yoder, 2016; Jordan et al., 2016).
   - **Mechanism:** according to some self-reports of autistic people, precisely this sensitivity makes them overheat and drop out of contact — go into a so-called "шатдаун" (*shutdown*). This is one of the factors by which part of the complex, emotionally saturated human world can slip out of attention, and it becomes easier to keep focus either on separate details (shapes, textures, sounds, movements) or on something else entirely — natural phenomena, objects.
   - **Possibly partly for this reason, two components are initially reduced:** *cognitive empathy* — the ability to guess and draw conclusions about other people's beliefs, intentions and feelings (Decety and Yoder, 2016); and *empathic concern / empathic care* — the ability to respond to the emotions of people in pain, including sympathy, compassion and worry (Stocks et al., 2011).
   - The author's emphasis: "Но всё это — изначально" (*but all this is initially*) — i.e. these are starting points, not fixed traits. [msg #359]
3. **Myth 3 (Maria): autistic people are gloomy, taciturn loners.** Truth: autistic people, like everyone else, feel joy from communication, smile, laugh and love chatting about interesting topics. Unfortunately they very often have to do it in a way that does not suit them but is acceptable to their social environment — this leads to masking (see *Masking* under Detailed insights). [msg #360]
4. **Myth 4 (Maria): only technical professions without contact with people suit autistic people.** Truth: take any field and there will be a person with ASD in it; people with autism work as psychologists, artists, social workers, musicians, politicians. Research, according to Maria, shows that what matters for autistic people's success is not the content of the work but the working conditions. The main ones:
   - measurability of results and the possibility to choose independently how exactly to do the work;
   - transparency in business communication;
   - the possibility to arrange the workplace according to one's own needs;
   - employer support instead of discrimination. [msg #360]
5. **Myth 5 (Rita): autism must be treated with pills.** Truth: ASD is a feature that produces "спецэффекты" (*special effects*) in what a person pays attention to, what they remember, what they find interesting and how they transmit information. What helps is "подстелить соломку там, где бывает сложно" (*laying down straw where it gets hard*, i.e. cushioning the difficult spots) and upgrading the "interface": developing ways of thinking and skills that help cope better with life tasks *in the corresponding environment* — from communicating with people to caring for sensory sensitivity or balancing work and rest. Example: some people use a timer to learn over time to check their hunger on time — especially when they badly want to dive into work for 10 hours straight. Medication can be used exactly where everyone else uses it: if there is depression, anxiety disorders "и т. д." (*and so forth*). [msg #360]

### Five myths and facts about autism and parenting (NEN x Anton tut ryadom)
A carousel titled "5 мифов и фактов об аутизме и родительстве" (*5 myths and facts about autism and parenting*), produced by the charity project "Антон тут рядом" (*Anton is right here*) together with НЭН (NEN), described on the cards as "честным изданием о родительстве" (*an honest publication about parenting*). Its premise: although media carry ever more information about developmental differences, stereotypes continue to surround parents of children with ASD everywhere — the opening card quotes the typical remark "Какой он у вас невоспитанный!" (*How ill-mannered your child is!*). The channel reposted only part of the carousel; three myths are transcribed, each with a fact and a parent's testimony. [msg #623, #624, #631]

1. **Myth: "Autism appears because of wrong upbringing."** Fact: autism is a congenital and genetically determined feature of the psyche. In most cases of ASD the first symptoms appear between 1.5 and 2 years of age; they stem not from the parents' behaviour but to features of the development of the child's psyche and nervous system. [msg #625]
   - Natalya, mother of a child with autism: this myth devalues all the parents' efforts; every time she hears such phrases, everything inside her boils. Most people have no idea of the colossal amount of time and energy parents of children with ASD spend to form even the simplest everyday and educational skills in the child — it is hard, long and exhausting, and after such comments one simply loses heart. [msg #626]
2. **Myth: "Autism can be 'cured' with diets."** Fact: an autistic child can be helped through education and social programmes. Diets do not affect autism. Autism cannot be "cured" with medicines or diets: ASD is not an illness but a feature of the psyche that persists throughout life. [msg #627]
   - Igor, father of a child with ASD, names three risks of this myth: (1) **false hopes** — parents spend time, energy and money on useless and sometimes dangerous methods instead of focusing on real help: therapy, education, adaptation; (2) **physical harm** — diets without medical indications can lead to nutrient deficiencies; (3) **stigmatisation** — the idea that diets can "cure" autism implies it is something easily fixed just by changing food, which devalues the real difficulties of autistic people and their loved ones. [msg #628]
3. **Myth: "Autistic children are simply ill-mannered."** Fact: autistic children's behaviour may differ from the usual, but that is a reason for support, not condemnation. A child's unusual behaviour is not a consequence of bad upbringing but a manifestation of autism: strong anxiety, difficulties orienting in social situations, sensory overload. [msg #629]
   - Igor: this is one of the most outdated and harmful myths, yet one still hears comments from random passers-by aimed at parents whose child is having a crisis on the street — or even from teachers: "Он просто плохо воспитан, нужно строже. Ремня ему - и сразу будет как шелковый!" (*He's just badly brought up, you need to be stricter. Give him the belt and he'll be silky-smooth at once!*). [msg #630]

### Three DSM-5 levels of support (with what can help at each level)
Purpose: a current orientation point for finding an approach to loved ones and oneself on the spectrum, replacing the old subtypes. Rita, the author, sets out the levels and the skills and actions that may be useful at each. [msg #372]

Background: the subtypes of autism — early childhood, atypical, high-functioning — disappeared with the change of reference manuals (DSM-IV in 2013, ICD-10 in 2023, the latter footnoted "до недавнего законопроекта" — *until a recent bill*). Lately these subtypes could be met only on forums — for example under the tender name "Аспи" (*Aspie*, a person with Asperger syndrome) — or in the backrooms of psychiatrists who disagree. Instead, other reference points appeared: whether the person has speech or intellectual impairment, catatonia, primary illnesses (to which autism attaches as a consequence) — the source ends the list with "и др.", naming no further items. One of these reference points is the three levels of needed support (DSM-5, p. 52), concerning support useful for a person on the spectrum in: communicating with people; switching between tasks; the repertoire of interests; the amount of needed stimming (twirling things in the hands, finger clicking — the source adds "и др." without further items). The main difference between the levels is the severity of symptoms for the person and the amount of outside input needed for daily functioning. [msg #372]

**Level 1** — mild symptoms that may not particularly affect daily life. There may be some difficulties, but as a rule people can function and find balance in most situations. They can speak in full sentences but may have difficulty with long two-way conversation with neurotypical people. Social anxiety and burnout from prolonged masking may occur here. [msg #372]
- Examples: a child who finds it hard to start a conversation but will join it or a game if peers invite them; an adult with a specific hobby or interest to which they can devote hours every day while still keeping a job and relationships.
- What can help:
  - from time to time, building social skills (group activities, role-play games);
  - periodically improving self-regulation skills for emotions and sensations (breathing practices, physiological regulation);
  - cognitive behavioural therapy to manage anxious thoughts and stereotyped actions — when these bother the person (making plans and schedules to reduce anxiety through predictability);
  - choosing activities where repetitive movements can find a creative and socially adaptive form (for example dancing, workouts, assembling mechanisms, embroidery);
  - opportunities to "take off the mask" in an accepting, safe space (support groups, psychoeducation of loved ones). [msg #372]

**Level 2** — significant difficulties with communication may arise, and stimming is more pronounced. People with ASD-2 find it noticeably harder to mask and fit into society in neurotypical ways (for example to keep up small talk — light conversation on everyday topics or without a particular purpose). It is harder to change focus or switch from one activity to another. [msg #372]
- Examples: a person who paces back and forth during a lesson or meeting and repeats the same thing; a teenager who finds it hard to start and sustain a conversation even with family members and close people, and to understand social signals; an adult who relies heavily on a daily routine and schedules and for whom a clear, set format is important for social contacts.
- What can help:
  - speech therapy — to improve communication skills related to expressiveness of speech;
  - physiotherapy — to cope with motor difficulties and improve gross and fine motor skills;
  - organisations and programmes (including specialised schools) where the environment is structured and the staff are trained in skills important for people with ASD. [msg #372]

**Level 3** — serious difficulties with communication and extremely repetitive behaviour. People with ASD-3 may practically not speak, need help in most daily activities and may have intellectual disability. [msg #373]
- Examples: a child over 3 years old with minimal speech who does not respond to verbal cues and dresses and eats with support; an adult who experiences extremely strong stress when the daily routine or tasks change and uses augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) tools.
- What can help:
  - round-the-clock support programmes, including medical care, education and vocational training;
  - habilitation services — to improve everyday life skills (cooking, cleaning and self-care);
  - assistive technologies (speech-generating devices, specialised software) — to help with communication and the development of everyday life skills. [msg #373]

### General recommendations for all support levels
1. **Individual education plans (ИПО)** — for school-age children they can define specific goals and accommodations to support academic success.
2. **Support for parents and caregivers** — seminars, consultations and respite care.
3. **Regular assessments** — help track progress and adjust the approach as needed.

Caveat from the source: these recommendations must be adapted to the needs and preferences of the specific person, and collaboration between professionals — psychologists, educators, therapists — and parents can help with that. [msg #373]

### Four reasons the "high-/low-functioning" label fails
Rita's argument (June 2024) against the labels ВФ/НФ (*high-/low-functioning*), in four parts. [msg #402]

1. **"Технически неверно" (*technically incorrect* — "the worst kind of incorrect")**. High/low functioning presupposes that ASD is a scale from very much autistic to less autistic. But the autism spectrum is rather a "колесо баланса" (*balance wheel*) of traits with different intensity in different people (see *The autism spectrum as a wheel of traits*). Example: a person with ASD may have phenomenal memory and outstanding musical abilities yet regularly spend hours self-regulating by arranging things all around the house. Is that high-functioning in one thing and low-functioning in another? Perhaps. Do neurotypical people have similar contrasts of abilities? Yes. So do the terms HF/LF add clarity and reflect reality? Hardly. [msg #402]
2. **"Поддерживает невидимость" (*it sustains invisibility*)**. Neurotypical people usually say "ты не похож(а) на аутиста" (*you don't look autistic*) and "ты, должно быть, ВФ" (*you must be high-functioning*) when they don't notice obvious differences from themselves: the autistic person goes to work, manages a budget, makes eye contact, speaks with intonation, responds to feelings — "normal", isn't it? And what is going on inside, who can tell. Even more often NT people say it to support and create a sense of commonality — as a compliment or an attempt to show the person is fine. In practice these goals are not achieved: the autistic person notices differences in deeper things and is more likely to feel a lack of understanding and acceptance, and of being seen as they are. [msg #402]
3. **"Путь к выгоранию" (*a path to burnout*)**. An autistic person hears "you're HF" most often if they actively mask — i.e. against inner impulses and discomfort, strive to maintain an image and a language of interaction understandable to NT people. The more conformity to neurotypicality is approved, the greater the anxiety of losing one's place in the pack if one lets one's guard down. Living in that tension is a path to physical and emotional exhaustion — both from the anxiety itself and from the effort (masking is extremely costly, and the more active, the costlier). Analogy: imagine that from dawn to dusk you are performing on stage in a role that is not close to you, in an uncomfortable costume, without a lunch break, at risk of losing your job for a slip, under the gaze of spectators whose mood is more or less hard to predict — "Примерно так и тут" (*that's roughly how it is here*). [msg #402]
4. **"Давит на гений" (*it pressures the genius*)**. If a person on the spectrum has fluent speech and an IQ above 70, a special interest may cause others to feel awe before a genius and a fierce urge to develop — if not exploit — this seemingly huge potential. This often comes from teachers or superiors: "да, с причудами, но высокофункциональный: смотрите, какие чудеса творит" (*yes, quirky, but high-functioning: look what wonders he works*). But the special interest goes hand in hand with "спецэффекты" (*special effects*): fast overload of senses and emotions; difficulty communicating in understandable language (Rita notes wryly that in academia this sometimes doesn't stop people much); spending significant time on recovery regardless of deadlines; the inability to mute details — in what one sees, hears, thinks; the source ends with "и пр., и пр., и пр. — у каждого своё" (*and so forth — each has their own*), giving no further items. The character is rather balanced: having strengths, they are not without Achilles' heels. If one endlessly demands "bread and circuses" and rewards achievements out of a false and incomplete assessment of the situation, there is a risk that, classically, the actor dies on stage (see the masking point). The potential that is valuable here is the "потенциал покоя" (*potential of calm/rest*) of the person on the spectrum. [msg #402]

Conclusion: "«высокая функциональность» — это не про реальность" (*"high functionality" is not about reality*), and not something that will help an autistic person realise themselves or create correct ideas in society. [msg #402]

### The autism spectrum as a wheel of traits (balance wheel)
Purpose: to replace the image of a linear scale "from more to less autistic". The spectrum is a wheel of traits, each of which can be bright or faint in a given person. Traits named in the text: features of sensory sensitivity; stimming and repetitive actions; speech (hypo- and hyper-talkativeness, unusual vocabulary); adaptation to change; demands for rules; interpretation of social signals. The source marks this list (and the speech sub-list) as open-ended with "и др." without naming more. Co-occurring disorders — depressive, anxiety, obsessive-compulsive (again "и др.") — exert a separate influence. [msg #402]

The illustrating image ("The Autism Spectrum", posted with the caption "Спектр аутизма — это примерно так (вместо шкалы от большего к меньшему)" — *the autism spectrum is roughly like this, instead of a scale from more to less*) is a colour wheel of 12 segments, each with concentric rings of increasing shade toward the rim, showing that each trait can be present at a different intensity. The 12 segments:
1. difficulty reading social cues
2. sensory fixations
3. sensory aversions
4. hyper or hypo verbal
5. difficulty with transitions
6. OCD
7. hyperfixations
8. anxiety
9. repetitive behavior
10. depression
11. echolalia
12. stimming

### Eight dimensions in which people on the spectrum differ
From the channel's World Autism Awareness Day post (2 April): people on the spectrum are as different as people off it — different both from the majority and among themselves. [msg #534]
1. **Speech** — one is an eternal one-actor theatre, another speaks only to the point without "meaningless small talk"; one doesn't know when to join a conversation, another regularly "conducts interviews".
2. **Sensory sensitivity** — one is a delicate masseur, another a borderline BDSM fan; one is a classical musician, another falls asleep to Rammstein.
3. **Memory** — one forgets almost nothing, and their life is "как среди золотых рыбок" (*as if among goldfish*); another "can't reach the data" as soon as the context changes.
4. **General level of intelligence** — one builds a multi-move strategy while others are still grasping what is happening; another gets stuck in details, unable to catch the essence.
5. **Movement** — one regularly claps hands, another is a master of stillness, a third moves with a special graceful rhythm, as if slightly dancing or acting.
6. **Gaze** — one always looks slightly misty-romantic, another seems to talk to emptiness somewhere to the side, a third burns with such a direct gaze that butterflies start fluttering in your stomach.
7. **"Creative streak"** — one creates performances where the rhythm of music is synchronised with sensations, colours, smells and even tastes; another tells everything through touch alone; a third immerses others in a cleansing experience of minimalism bordering on sensory deprivation.
8. **Emotions** — one reads mood from micro-contractions of the face, another won't understand without explanations in the finest detail; one gets sweaty palms from a second of a special glance, another is unsure until someone's underwear lands over their eyes. [msg #534]

### Reverse-perspective exercise ("Why Johnny Doesn't Flap")
Purpose: practise the author's "relativity" idea — seeing typical behaviour through a neurodivergent eye. The channel shared a Russian translation of the 2015 children's book by Clay and Gail Morton, illustrated by Alex Merry, "Why Johnny Doesn't Flap: NT is OK!" (Russian: «Почему Джонни не машет руками. НТ (нейротипичный) – это окей!»). An autistic child narrates about his neurotypical friend Johnny; his mother explains that Johnny is NT, has no autism, so his brain works differently — "И это нормально" (*and that's OK*). Each vignette inverts a common judgment made about autistic people: [msg #317, #318]
1. **Punctuality** — Johnny is due at 4:00 but sometimes comes at 3:58 or 4:03; the narrator gave him a watch for his birthday, but the "problem" persists — "ужасно с пунктуальностью" (*terrible with punctuality*), yet they have fun.
2. **Routine** — Johnny sometimes wants checkers first and then cards, sometimes the reverse; he can't keep the same order every time.
3. **Eye contact** — Johnny looks you straight in the eye when talking, which can be very uncomfortable; he doesn't do it out of spite, he's just like that.
4. **Scripts** — Johnny watches the same cartoons but never repeats the intro word for word; the narrator doesn't know if he even memorised it — he pays attention to strange things.
5. **Emotional expression** — when something wonderful happens, Johnny doesn't flap his hands or jump; he just stretches the corners of his mouth and opens his eyes a little wider. Maybe he doesn't know how to show emotions.
6. **Special interests** — Johnny has no topic he knows everything about (World War II, dinosaurs, trams); he seems uninterested in the detailed information shared with him and is unlikely to become an expert in anything — but he's a good guy.
7. **"Functioning"** — Johnny functions very well at school, understands rules and does tasks, but asked simple information about the school — e.g. which company made the lift, the computer or the cameras — he stares blankly; he understands some things well and has trouble with others, "Но это так у всех" (*but that's how it is for everyone*).
8. **Meltdowns** — Johnny never has a tantrum when something terrible happens, such as the fire alarm going off or art class being suddenly cancelled; he is afraid of what other people will think; the narrator thinks he locks his feelings inside, but that's just how he copes.
9. **Literal language** — Johnny says the maths test was "проще пареной репы" (*simpler than a steamed turnip*, i.e. very easy) and "Учитель метал гром и молнии" (*the teacher was hurling thunder and lightning*); the narrator tries to explain that a turnip has nothing to do with a maths test, but Johnny doesn't seem to understand he should say what he means. Eventually the narrator decodes his strange phrases.
10. **Social drive** — at recess Johnny always wants to play with other children and never retreats into his own world; the narrator wonders whether he ever has time to sit and think about his favourite advert or metro announcement — "Возможно, он слишком зациклен на общении" (*maybe he is too fixated on socialising*).
11. **Conclusion** — being friends with an NT child is interesting; he has many oddities that can annoy until you get used to them. Mum says everyone's brain is built differently and these differences are not bad. "Я думаю, что быть НТ – это нормально" (*I think being NT is OK*). [msg #318]

The channel's verdict: some sketches may be outdated in places, but most still seem valuable. [msg #317]

## Detailed insights

### Stereotypes of autism, present and past
The channel opens its stereotype series with "Что же такое «аутизм»?" (*So what is "autism"?*), citing three screen images: Sheldon from "The Big Bang Theory", "Rain Man", and the red-haired chess player of "The Queen's Gambit" (pictured in the post's photo). [msg #324]
- **Today's stereotype:** people giving monologues about trains, physics or the wings of Drosophila; their gaze goes past the interlocutor; they flap their hands strangely and switch their face abruptly from a frozen mask to delight; they chop out the truth on the verge of insult and behave wildly inappropriately in company; they prefer to talk only about their bizarre hobbies or sit with them in a corner.
- **The image of the past:** a quiet *boy*, naive in emotions and wooden in body, who lines things up in a row, remembers the phone number of a girl from kindergarten and throws a tantrum if the usual course of things is disrupted — whether he is 5 or 35.
- In both images autistic people are invariably either geniuses or unfit for life, and more often both.
- **The truth** is much more complex, between the lines and in shades — "целый спектр, который можно и не заметить невооружённым глазом" (*a whole spectrum that may go unnoticed by the naked eye*). [msg #324]

### Masking (camouflaging)
Masking is when an autistic person adjusts to people around them so as not to stand out — implementing the behaviour expected of them. The person observes, memorises and repeats various scripts in various situations. Examples Maria gives: they know that to seem pleasant you must smile; you must be able to sustain small talk in a new group; when someone looks upset, you must express sympathy through certain phrases. [msg #360]

Masking takes a lot of energy. So it is good when the person can be themselves without judgment: communicating by text rather than meeting in person, putting on headphones when tired of conversation, leaving a friendly gathering after 15 minutes because it became too anxious. [msg #360]

Why it is so costly, per the Autism Awareness Day post: in the general population communication happens through speech, and basically speech is a tool for transmitting not so much knowledge as socially significant information. Social signals are complex, multi-component and context-dependent — very hard to master when your thinking is "мелкое ситечко с пошаговой обработкой" (*a fine sieve with step-by-step processing*). If you learn fast, grasp things on the fly and forget ~nothing, you're lucky and masking will be easier — but it is still wildly costly, and a special recovery system is necessary. For the burnout mechanism of masking, see reason 3 of *Four reasons the "high-/low-functioning" label fails*. [msg #534]

### "Simple and vivid" explanation of ASD for people off the spectrum
On World Autism Awareness Day (2 April), the author offered how she would explain ASD simply to someone outside the spectrum, in four theses: [msg #534]
1. **"Быть человеком с РАС — не выбор и не воспитание, это генетика"** (*being a person with ASD is not a choice and not upbringing, it's genetics*). This genetics can affect memory and thinking, speech, processing of signals from different senses, experiencing and expressing emotions, and even immunity. Against this, attempts to "be like everyone" are titanic labour — like a foreign language, another culture, another planet. [msg #534]
2. **"Любить уединение не значит любить одиночество"** (*loving solitude does not mean loving loneliness*). Statistics for 2024: one person in 36 has ASD, i.e. one such person next to 35 others (commenters add: next to 99). Imagine how lonely that can be and how hard it is to find your pack. It is even harder when the world is too "noisy" with sounds, light, colours, smells and other details and one regularly very much needs to go to one's burrow to wait out the storm or reboot. Special interests — especially beloved hobbies one can pursue and be filled by for a very long time — are common in ASD, but closeness to people is still necessary. When communicating with an autistic person it is especially valuable to remember that communication is not only words (the masking mechanism above belongs to this thesis). [msg #534]
3. **"Люди на спектре — столь же разные, как и вне"** (*people on the spectrum are just as diverse as those outside it*) — see *Eight dimensions in which people on the spectrum differ*. [msg #534]
4. **"Сильные стороны будут бросаться в глаза, но и слабые — настоящая Ахиллесова пята"** (*strengths will catch the eye, but the weaknesses are a real Achilles' heel*). Both are noticeable to most people around because they are very unusual. Unable to recognise them, others may catch only a vague sense of non-dangerous otherness. Whether the person becomes an unreachable star for envy and admiration or an outcast eccentric from a far-off farmstead — very pleasant to turn to on business but very uncomfortable to be around otherwise — depends on how the person can fit into social hierarchical games. Portraits offered: a brilliant mathematician, surgeon or scientist with an equally dreamy expression; a charismatic orator with crisp diction who backstage twirls hair and a spinner, gazing into emptiness; a talented dancer, singer or athlete who after a performance vanishes for months from all social life except super-close ties; Elon Musk, Dr. House, Sherlock Holmes, Sheldon Cooper, "Rain Man", the hero of "Atypical", or your acquaintance — a mysterious DJ/programmer/psychologist with a penetrating gaze. "Все они имеют особое излучение" (*they all have a special radiance*). [msg #535]

### Understanding before judgment (the parenting carousel's framing)
Introducing the NEN carousel, the author argues: sometimes we are raised to express opinions categorically and without being asked, as if it were our civic duty to set a neighbour — and their children, and their mother — on the right path; she calls this an atavism of Soviet times. Perhaps we should sometimes pause and ask ourselves: do I know enough about what is happening in this person's life to hammer advice into them? It might become the last nail — especially for a mother without the privileges of nannies and free social support. And if we want to help so much, why not simply offer help — "Вечер радикальных идей, дамы и господа" (*an evening of radical ideas, ladies and gentlemen*), she adds ironically. [msg #631]

### History: two women forgotten in autism research
A post forwarded from the channel "Читаю мысли" (for 8 March) tells of two women whose contribution is often skipped in articles that mostly cite men. [msg #533]
- **Grunya Sukhareva** — a psychiatrist after whom Moscow's Children's Psychiatric Hospital No. 6 is named. She was the first to describe children whose behaviour did not fit the manifestations of schizophrenia; she wrote an article that was translated into German a year later but then somehow got lost. All this in the 1930s, whereas the first papers by Asperger and Kanner date to 1943–44. [msg #533]
- **Lorna Wing** — a British physician who studied autism in parallel with Kanner and Asperger and almost all the time pushed the idea that one should not consider separate narrow forms of autism or go into rigid categorisation. She first introduced and popularised the term "Asperger syndrome" for high-functioning autistic people, and from the 1970s proposed the term ASD, which came into use with DSM-5 in 2013. She founded the National Autistic Society in England and raised a daughter with ASD. [msg #533]

### Media representation and how it evolved
The channel calls the series "The Pitt" phenomenal and says it radically overturns cinema's ideas of autism of the last 20 years. Taylor Dearden's performance as Dr. Melissa King is described as one of the most thoughtful portrayals of an autistic character on contemporary TV: she plays the doctor with subtlety, humanity and precision, avoiding many usual clichés and TV tropes about autism that shaped film and TV for decades. Instead of treating autism as a gimmick, a superpower or a set of stereotypes, "The Pitt" gives a character who feels like a real person: smart, awkward, capable, emotionally perceptive, at times overwhelmed and deeply human. The actress says: "Нейроотличия постоянно неверно понимаются и оцениваются с внешней стороны — без погружения в то, что происходит внутри" (*neurodivergence is constantly misunderstood and judged from the outside, without immersion in what happens inside*) — and so she deliberately moves away from familiar tropes. [msg #692]

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The empathy findings are framed as initial states ("изначально") that depend on gender socialisation and become closer to typical with age; the link between high empathic accuracy and reduced cognitive empathy/empathic concern is hedged by Rita as "Возможно, частично поэтому" (*possibly partly for this reason*), and the shutdown mechanism rests on self-reports of some autistic people. [msg #359]
- The two views of ASD coexist: the deficit model is the one in DSM-5 and ICD-11; the channel favours the difference model based on growing research, not on consensus. [msg #359]
- Prevalence: the CDC 2024 figure is 1 in 36; commenters suggested 1 in 99 — (!) UNCLEAR: the source does not reconcile these numbers. [msg #534]
- The timeline of subtype removal: DSM-IV replaced in 2013 and ICD-10 in 2023 with a footnote "до недавнего законопроекта" (*until a recent bill*) — (!) UNCLEAR: the footnote's meaning (presumably regarding Russian adoption of ICD-11) is not explained. [msg #372]
- Myth 4's claim that "research shows" working conditions matter more than job content is stated without a named study. [msg #360]
- Origins: #359 says autism arises during intrauterine development; #534 and the NEN card say it is genetic/congenital — complementary statements, not a contradiction in the source. [msg #359, #534, #625]
- Some sketches of the "Johnny" book are acknowledged as outdated in places. [msg #317]
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the NEN carousel promised five myths; only three are in the reposted images. The literalness video (#621) is unavailable. [msg #631, #621]
- Lorna Wing popularised "Asperger syndrome" for high-functioning autistic people, while the channel elsewhere argues that "high-functioning" is a bad label and that such subtypes were dropped from manuals — a historical development, not a contradiction. [msg #533, #402, #372]
