# The double empathy problem: mutual misunderstanding between autistic and neurotypical people

## What this covers

A four-part article series by the channel author, "Аутизм и проблема двойной эмпатии" ("Autism and the double empathy problem"), plus an afterword. It contrasts the long-dominant "mind-blindness" account of autism with Damian Milton's double empathy theory, walks through the experiments on how autistic (РАС) and neurotypical (НТ) people understand each other and form first impressions, and closes with how both sides can build a "two-way road". The author describes the series as her original article, compiled over a month from many sources with her own translation of the theory [msg #420]. According to the source this matters because the findings "shake the usual foundation" and remove from autistic people the sole responsibility for unsuccessful social contact [msg #420].

## Core ideas

- **Misunderstanding is mutual, not an autistic deficit.** Milton's double empathy theory holds that communication breakdowns between neurotypical and autistic people are a two-sided problem of mutual understanding; difficulty in understanding and empathising was found mainly between members of different groups, not within them [msg #420]. Full detail in *Mind-blindness model vs double empathy problem*.
- **Difference itself makes understanding harder.** The more different we feel from someone, the harder it is to find common language, form an accurate picture of them and, above all, to be motivated to do so [msg #425].
- **Neurotypical people have blind spots too.** Studies show neurotypical people misread autistic people's emotions, expressions and experience; autistic pairs communicate as effectively as neurotypical ones, and quality drops only in mixed groups [msg #425, #426]. Full detail in *Six research findings*.
- **Snap judgements are a major contributor.** Neurotypical people form negative first impressions of autistic people within seconds, driven by non-verbal self-presentation, and these impressions are very stable [msg #432]. Full detail in *Sasson et al. 2017 three-experiment design*.
- **The gap can be bridged from both sides** through refusing hasty conclusions, developing executive functions, reinterpreting behaviour that only looks like disinterest, and spreading knowledge [msg #443]. Full detail in *Two-way road*.
- **The deeper issue is untested worldviews.** In the author's view the double empathy problem highlights a more general failure to test the epistemic status of our beliefs, which closes us to other people's perspective; openness and empathy can be learned starting with kindness to oneself [msg #444].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Mind-blindness model vs double empathy problem
**The older model (mind-blindness).** In the mid-1980s the prominent researcher Simon Baron-Cohen claimed that people on the spectrum have "умственной слепотой" ("mind-blindness"), i.e. that what we now know as theory of mind is fundamentally impaired in them. Theory of mind is defined as: "Теория разума — это способность представлять убеждения, эмоции, желания и намерения других людей, а также понимать и предсказывать их поведение." (*Theory of mind is the ability to represent other people's beliefs, emotions, desires and intentions, and also to understand and predict their behaviour.*) [msg #420]

**Its reach.** According to the author, the mind-blindness concept is still taught in many psychology textbooks and is supported by more than 75% of the top scientific articles on theory of mind and autism in Google Scholar. It also guides work in sociology, philosophy, economics, anthropology, robotics and other fields. Through this lens Baron-Cohen and his followers, right up to recent years, saw and explained the "impairments" of autistic people — in empathy, in verbal and non-verbal communication and in social interaction generally — even though the very concept of "autism", its components and its diagnostic criteria have changed enormously over the past 40 years [msg #420].

**The challenge.** From the 1990s autistic activists such as Jim Sinclair argued that their ways of communicating mesh poorly with neurotypical ones. In 2012 the autistic researcher Damian Milton presented the essay "Об онтологическом статусе аутизма: проблема двойной эмпатии" ("On the ontological status of autism: the double empathy problem"). In it he proposed that "сбои в общении между НТ- и РАС-людьми — это двусторонняя проблема, с трудностями взаимо_понимания" (*breakdowns in communication between NT and autistic people are a two-sided problem, with difficulties of mutual understanding*). Milton is described as a British sociologist and social psychologist with academic qualifications in sociology, psychology, philosophy and education, who teaches at the University of Kent, consults for the UK National Autistic Society and advocates for the rights of autistic people, being autistic himself [msg #420].

**What followed.** The essay became a turning point for research on autistic communication. Studies in the following years showed that most autistic people fit into groups without problems, communicate effectively, show social reciprocity and empathise sensitively and accurately — with other autistic people. It turned out that understanding and empathising were hard only for members of different groups, on and off the spectrum [msg #420].

**Why it matters.** These results remove from autistic people the sole responsibility for failed social contact. They also echo neurodiversity principles: autistic people generally have a different approach to working with information at every stage — gathering, processing, storing and exchanging. Milton stresses that "теория соответствует аутистической феноменологии, полученной из инсайдерских источников" (*the theory corresponds to autistic phenomenology obtained from insider sources*), i.e. it reflects the personal experience and perception of people on the spectrum [msg #420].

**Status of the evidence.** Scientific support is growing but not yet robust, and as of 2024 the leading classifications, ICD and DSM, still follow the old reference points in their criteria. M. Lerner, associate professor of psychology, psychiatry and pediatrics at Stony Brook University in New York, is quoted: "Не все исследователи настроены на это новое направление" (*Not all researchers are on board with this new direction*) and "Проблема двойной эмпатии с эмпирической точки зрения является более молодой теорией" (*From an empirical point of view, the double empathy problem is a younger theory*) [msg #420].

### Why difference produces mutual misunderstanding (the mechanism)
The author explains the mechanism as follows. Autism affects how its bearers perceive and interact with the world. She lists the questions on which autistic and non-autistic experience differs noticeably, to a greater or lesser degree [msg #425]:
1. What attention focuses on, and how.
2. What is interesting and what is a waste of effort.
3. How intense something is for the senses.
4. What is important (including what is important to show outwardly) and what is secondary.

The answers to these questions, and the experience accumulated through them, differ between people on and off the spectrum. That difference makes it harder to understand each other and to predict each other at the level of intentions, feelings, experiences and motivations behind actions. Because of this, recent research has shifted from the usual practice of observing autistic people in social situations to studying how autistic people communicate with each other and how neurotypical people perceive them [msg #425].

### Six research findings on mutual misunderstanding
The author numbers the findings of this newer research [msg #425, #426]:

1. **Neurotypical "blind spots".** Neurotypical people also have "слепые зоны" ("blind spots") regarding autistic people — in recognising their emotional state and their lived experience (studies from 2015–2016). In one study where participants conveyed their lived experience and emotions through animation, neurotypical people had trouble decoding autistic people's states; in another, they had trouble accurately interpreting autistic people's facial expressions [msg #425].
2. **"Broken telephone" information transfer (2020).** A study of information passing, modelled on the game "Испорченный телефон" ("broken telephone"), showed that autistic people communicate with one another as effectively as neurotypical people do with one another, and that the quality of transmission and understanding falls only in mixed groups [msg #425].
3. **Rapport in pairs — Crompton and colleagues (2020).** Catherine Crompton and colleagues set out to stress-test the double empathy problem: if the issue is the difference between neuro-groups, contact within a group should be better. Part 1: participants in three groups (NT, autistic, mixed) rated their personal impressions of talking in pairs. Part 2: outside observers from matching groups rated the quality of the first participants' contact from video recordings. Results: self-reported mutual understanding was highest in NT pairs; in autistic pairs it was somewhat lower but still high and clearly higher than in mixed pairs. From the outside, contact quality in mixed pairs also looked the weakest, while autistic pairs gave the impression of more understanding than NT pairs. The author adds a playful question: to what extent is neurotypical people's feeling of being understood linked to their urge to look for hidden meaning between the lines, unlike autistic people — "a topic for further research" [msg #425].
4. **Autistic conversation patterns — Heasman and a colleague (2019).** Observing 30 autistic people communicating during cooperative video games, B. Heasman and a colleague noted distinctive features. "Typical" ping-pong exchange, with regular alternation of turns, happened only when players needed to coordinate actions in the game. The rest of the time, according to the authors, periods of contact and disengagement alternated fairly sharply: active discussion gave way to long silence, or one person dominated the conversation — and participants experienced this as natural and easy. Autistic people also proceeded from a "презумпции общности" ("presumption of commonality"): if nothing contradicted it, they established rapport with each other immediately. Conversely, when they ran into misunderstanding, responses could become rough (the authors recorded "потенциально деструктивные для общения высказывания" — *utterances potentially destructive to communication* — a judgement the channel author notes was made by authors who, as far as she knows, are neurotypical), and people tried to readjust. Lesson: autistic-to-autistic communication has its own pattern that would likely feel unfamiliar to neurotypical people [msg #426].
5. **Five-minute unstructured conversations (2019).** Strangers held a free, unstructured 5-minute conversation and then shared impressions of their partner and rated communication quality. Pairs were homogeneous (22 neurodivergent pairs and 23 neurotypical pairs) or mixed (25 pairs). Participants in all groups rated partners who turned out to be off the spectrum as warmer and more socially skilled. Yet autistic participants preferred interacting with other autistic adults and reported telling them more about themselves than they told neurotypical partners. Neurotypical participants likewise reported wanting to talk more with people of their own group [msg #426].
6. **Autistic people's own reports of comfort.** Autistic people report feeling more comfortable with others on the spectrum: (a) in interviews, 12 participants said they observe more ease and understanding within the spectrum; (b) a study of 12 adolescents (6 on the spectrum and 6 not) showed that they prefer to communicate with a group of their own neurotype and gender when it comes not to requests but to sharing thoughts and experience [msg #426].

**Author's conclusion from the six findings.** It appears that autistic people not only have no problem with empathy (as known from a large 2019 meta-analysis discussed in the channel's earlier "myth 2" post), but show especially pronounced sympathy and understanding towards similar people. This mirrors how neurotypical people respond with empathy to people like themselves, but less, and far from always, to those who differ — something earlier research on empathy for in-group vs out-group members had already shown [msg #426].

### Sasson et al. 2017 three-experiment design on first impressions of autistic people
Part 3 of the series is titled "Спектр и предубеждение" ("Spectrum and prejudice"). Its thesis: quick emotional assessments that neurotypical people form during superficial contact with autistic people appear to contribute noticeably to the difficulty of contact [msg #432].

**Headline finding.** A large 2017 study (Nature Scientific Reports link) showed that people off the spectrum consistently form a negative first impression of autistic people's character traits — even though neuro-status was not discussed in the study at all. The impression forms within a few seconds and is extremely stable: it does not change as interaction continues, persists through repeated interactions interleaved with non-autistic people, and appears in both child and adult age groups, even when neurotypical raters did not know who had a diagnosis. In control groups of neurotypical people this did not happen. The article described three independent experiments with real-world social interaction [msg #432]:

1. **Experiment 1.** 214 people rated autistic people as less socially competent, less likeable and more submissive, and expressed less desire to talk and hang out with them. Ratings of intelligence and trustworthiness, and willingness to sit next to them or live nearby, were on par with neurotypical participants. The cause turned out to be the style of outward social presentation, including in static pictures: the effect appeared with audio and visual information, but not when raters read transcripts of speech [msg #432].
2. **Experiment 2.** 12 autistic and 16 neurotypical people of student age were recorded talking with an experimenter on an everyday topic ("Видел(а) ли ты какие-нибудь интересные фильмы за последнее время?" — *Have you seen any interesting films lately?*). From randomly chosen first-person photos (from the experimenter's viewpoint), 37 participants used a slider between extremes to rate how socially awkward the person was, how approachable, and how open they themselves would be to friendship. Neurotypical raters judged autistic people as less approachable and more awkward than controls and indicated a lower likelihood of friendship with members of the autistic group [msg #432].
3. **Experiment 3.** Participants were autistic school-age children — an age when close attention is often paid to developing social skills. Nonetheless they received low ratings from both adults and peers [msg #432].

**Combined conclusion.** The results of the three independent studies were strikingly consistent despite different samples and methods. Together they are evidence that autistic people's social self-presentation, especially non-verbal — facial expression, body posture and prosody (the pattern of pronouncing words: strength, pitch, breathiness; the source closes this list with "и др.") — is perceived less favourably and goes together with neurotypical people's unwillingness to continue social interaction [msg #432].

**Limitation and next step.** Showing images, even repeatedly, is not equivalent to deeper acquaintance, so the study authors proposed testing in future how impressions change over prolonged interaction [msg #432].

### Rose vs cactus model of neurotypical and autistic friendship
According to some self-reports (the "Learn From Autistics" piece on autism and friendship), it is precisely deeper acquaintance and getting to know each other better that helps neurotypical and autistic people find common ground and warm to each other. This happens despite a difference in friendship styles described in an "Articulate Autistic" article: neurotypical friendship is more like "на розу, которой нужен регулярный уход и внимание" (*a rose that needs regular care and attention*), while autistic friendship is more like "на кактус, который доволен подпиткой время от времени и сохраняет свой цвет независимо от условий внешней среды" (*a cactus that is content with occasional feeding and keeps its colour regardless of the external environment*) [msg #432].

**Why it matters.** In experiment 2, autistic participants reported a much stronger feeling of loneliness than the control group. This agrees with earlier results showing autistic people did not differ from neurotypical peers in their desire for relationships, and suggests that their need for social experience and closeness is deeply unmet [msg #432].

### Two-way road: how to bridge the gap
The final part is titled "Как создать дорогу с двусторонним движением" ("How to build a two-way road") [msg #443].

**Starting diagnosis.** Contact between people on and off the spectrum appears to be exactly the case where "непонятное сочетается с непредсказуемым, это бывает дискомфортным" (*the incomprehensible combines with the unpredictable, and that can be uncomfortable*). With any neural architecture, even once we are sure that people who differ from us pose no harm, sometimes only a special interest — research-driven, trauma-driven or other — makes us voluntarily go deeper into such contact [msg #443].

**Components of bridging, as the source lays them out:**
1. **Empathy beyond first impressions.** Fortunately this is not the limit: as human beings we are capable of empathy even towards those who seem unlike us at first impression, by refusing hasty conclusions [msg #443].
2. **Developing executive functions.** The same executive functions that help us make weighed decisions and function in other areas of life support this. The author notes the irony that the need to develop executive functions is something neurotypical and autistic people share, only in different contexts [msg #443].
3. **An evolutionary framing.** Empathy is presumed to have evolved as a separate superstructure whose main function is to foster cooperation and the protection of the weak and suffering; the author suggests we may now be at the stage of evolution where it is time to take the next step [msg #443].
4. **Changing training programmes.** Researchers are already acting: changes are being made to the training curricula of the National Autistic Society, to the ATLASS training run by Studio3, and to the Synergy programme developed by AT-Autism. Researchers have also set the task of further evaluating these programmes from the perspectives of both autistic people and practitioners [msg #443].
5. **Distinguishing real disinterest from apparent disinterest.** Jaswal and Akhtar (2018) emphasise the difference between social disinterest and what merely looks like it to neurotypical people. Example 1: autistic people say that avoiding eye contact helps them avoid overload from part of the incoming information and concentrate during a conversation. Example 2: pointing in children has two functions, "императивную («дай») и декларативную («смотри»)" (*imperative ("give") and declarative ("look")*). It is commonly believed that autistic children do not use the gesture because they are not interested in drawing others' attention to their discoveries. However, in autistic children the gesture is absent altogether — even in the "give" sense — so it is simply not an active part of their "vocabulary", rather than a sign of disinterest [msg #443].
6. **Learning and telling others more.** On our side, regardless of which side of the spectrum each of us is on, we can learn and talk more about all of this [msg #443].

**Outcome the author expects.** Equally two-way efforts to narrow the gap in perceiving the world have a very good chance of making the "трудности перевода" (*lost in translation / difficulties of translation*) merely a "вопросом времени… которое мы уделяем друг другу" (*matter of time… that we give to each other*) [msg #443].

### Testing the epistemic status of one's worldview and learning empathy through self-kindness
In the afterword the author argues the following chain [msg #444]:
1. **Interaction is by definition two-sided.** Social interaction involves more than one person, so communication difficulties can arise not only from one person's behaviour but also from the perception, judgements and social decisions made by the people on the other side.
2. **The general problem behind double empathy.** In the author's own view, the double empathy problem highlights a more general problem: we stop stress-testing the epistemic status of our worldview — what we believe and, crucially, why: on what basis and by what we are guided. This is what closes us to new information and to the possibility of seeing a situation through another person's eyes and truly understanding them.
3. **Why the test feels frightening.** Such testing can be scary, but only at first — while there is little experience of colliding with real and diverse life and while inner supports are weak, which can make it seem that "другой» значит «опасный" (*"other" means "dangerous"*).
4. **The cost of not testing.** "Можно быть уверенным в одном и том же от младенчества до последних дней и так и не понять, что, на самом деле, мы никогда не уходили далеко от дома и не знаем, как устроен мир за его пределами." (*One can be sure of the same thing from infancy to one's last days and never understand that, in fact, we never went far from home and do not know how the world beyond it works.*)
5. **The practice.** Hence the importance of talking with living people instead of with matrices created long ago in one's head, switching on openness to many-sided experience and readiness to empathise. This can be learned, starting with kindness to oneself — the author links Kristin Neff's book on self-compassion here (see *Tools and resources mentioned*).

## Detailed insights

### Value of the theory despite its weaknesses
The author acknowledges many open questions about the theory of double — "perhaps even two-way" — empathy: different studies include slightly different aspects in it, and the number of studies on the topic is very small. Even so, the theory has already prompted questions that let us move "в сторону от слепой стигматизации — той, где есть безупречные и неправильные" (*away from blind stigmatisation — the kind where there are the flawless and the wrong*), which she sees as infinitely valuable [msg #443].

> "Проблема двойной эмпатии с эмпирической точки зрения является более молодой теорией" (*From an empirical point of view, the double empathy problem is a younger theory*) — M. Lerner [msg #420]

> "Чем сильнее мы с кем-то ощущаем различия, тем сложнее может быть находить общий язык, создавать друг о друге верное представление — и, главное, иметь к тому мотивацию." > "Социальное взаимодействие по определению предполагает участие более чем одного человека." (*Social interaction by definition involves more than one person.*) — the author [msg #444]

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The author herself states that scientific support for the theory is growing but not yet robust, that ICD and DSM criteria (as of 2024) still follow the old model, that different studies include slightly different aspects in the theory, and that there are only a handful of studies [msg #420, #443].
- In the Crompton study, self-reported understanding was highest in NT pairs, while outside observers saw more understanding in autistic pairs than in NT pairs; the author offers only a tongue-in-cheek hypothesis (NT people's search for hidden meaning between the lines) and labels it a topic for further research [msg #425].
- In the 2019 conversation study, all groups rated non-autistic partners as warmer and more socially skilled, even though autistic participants preferred and disclosed more to autistic partners [msg #426].
- The characterisation of rough responses in autistic conversations as "potentially destructive" is flagged by the author as the view of authors who, to her knowledge, are neurotypical [msg #426].
- The first-impressions study used images and short recordings; its authors note this is not equivalent to deeper acquaintance and call for testing over prolonged interaction [msg #432].
- The evolutionary account of empathy is marked by the author as presumptive ("Предположительно", *presumably*) [msg #443].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the 2020 rapport study lead is spelled "Кэтрин Комптон" in #425 and "Кэтрин Кромптон" in #432; the linked Frontiers paper and the podcast picture ("Dr Catherine") suggest the same person [msg #425, #432].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the source names the in-group/out-group empathy studies and the 2019 meta-analysis only by link and reference, without describing their methods or numbers [msg #426].
- The author promises a later article on the neurology and neurobiology of autism and invites readers to share examples of the double empathy concept influencing educational programmes; neither is part of this topic's content [msg #443].
