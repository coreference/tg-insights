# Evidence-based therapy approaches and choosing help

## What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel author (Human Gazing Upon Machine) shared about specific evidence-based or research-backed approaches to help: Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) and its six-process model, Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy (IBCT), and a warning about potentially dangerous psychotherapies. It also covers practical routes to help: a grounding practice for anxiety, panic and sleeplessness, a guide for choosing an anxiety self-help book, and free lecture series and programs on bipolar disorder and youth mental health. The author's framing is pragmatic: she looks for methods with scientific backing (searching sources linked to PubMed), but reserves judgement until she has examined them closely [msg #321, #337, #668].

## Core ideas

- **ACT replaces the chase for happiness with values-based action.** Instead of running the "hedonic race" towards an idealised self and circumstances, ACT asks you to map your values and act in line with them while going through life's difficulties; it also teaches skills to step out of the flow of emotions and observe them (see *ACT hexaflex*) [msg #337].
- **Psychological flexibility is the hub of ACT**, built from six processes grouped into three pillars: be here now, open up, do what matters [img #339, img #340].
- **IBCT is an evidence-based couple therapy** that uses functional analysis of how partners' behaviour affects each other, focusing on vulnerabilities, the cycles that trigger them, and partners' responsiveness to each other's needs [msg #321].
- **Not every hard moment in therapy means harm, but some therapies are harmful.** Temporary worsening, painful memories and strong emotions are normal; yet some approaches remain in use despite unproven effectiveness or being declared dangerous, unethical or even banned by professional associations [msg #668].
- **Self-help works best when matched to your kind of problem**: an anxiety book should fit your anxiety type and come from a competent, ethical author [msg #142].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### ACT hexaflex: six core therapeutic processes
Source: a page from Russ Harris's illustrated ACT manual, photographed by the author under the caption «Ключевые процессы ACT» ("Key processes of ACT") [msg #338, img #339]. The book text states that the six core therapeutic processes of ACT are contact with the present moment (контакт с настоящим), cognitive defusion (когнитивное разделение), acceptance (принятие), self-observation (самонаблюдение), values (ценности) and committed action (проактивность). Before examining them in order, the book shows a diagram of them that, in its words, was light-heartedly dubbed the ACT hexaflex (гексафлекс ACT) [img #339].

The hexagon places **psychological flexibility** in the centre, with the motto "Be here, open up and do what matters" (Будь здесь, откройся и делай то, что имеет значение — printed across several lines in the diagram). The six vertices, each with its short instruction from the diagram [img #339]:
1. **Contact with the present** (контакт с настоящим) — «Будь здесь и сейчас» (*Be here and now*).
2. **Values** (ценности) — "Decide what really matters" (*Реши, что по-настоящему важно*, as printed across lines in the diagram).
3. **Committed action / proactivity** (проактивность) — «Сделай то, что нужно» (*Do what is needed*).
4. **Self-observation / self-as-context** (самонаблюдение) — "the part of the mind that notices what is happening" (*Часть сознания, замечающая происходящее*, printed across lines).
5. **Cognitive defusion** (когнитивное разделение) — "Watch your thoughts" (*Наблюдай за своими мыслями*, printed across lines).
6. **Acceptance** (принятие) — «Откройся» (*Open up*).

### ACT triflex: three pillars of psychological flexibility
A second image reduces the six processes to a triangle with **psychological flexibility** in the centre and three corners, each corner flanked by two processes [img #340]:
1. **«БУДЬ ЗДЕСЬ И СЕЙЧАС»** (*Be here and now*, top corner) — formed by contact with the present and self-observation.
2. **«ОТКРОЙСЯ»** (*Open up*, bottom left) — formed by cognitive defusion and acceptance.
3. **«СДЕЛАЙ ТО, ЧТО НУЖНО»** (*Do what is needed*, bottom right) — formed by values and committed action (проактивность).

This grouping mirrors the hexaflex motto "be here, open up and do what matters" [img #339, img #340].

### Two-part grounding practice for anxiety, panic and insomnia
**Purpose / when to use:** for people ending the day who want to slow down, who cannot fall asleep because of anxiety, or who are "right now somewhere in the zone of rising panic". The author notes the practice may be familiar, but she added atmosphere and clarifications she considers important; she also recorded an audio guide for it in May and reposted it in September because it seemed relevant again [msg #68].

**Part one (preparatory) — paced breathing:**
1. Close your eyes; put one hand on the centre of your chest and the other on your belly [msg #68].
2. Take a deep breath with both chest and belly for a count of 4, then hold for 2 [msg #68].
3. Exhale slowly for a count of 6, and again hold at the end for 2 [msg #68].
4. Repeat the whole cycle three more times (four cycles in total) [msg #68].

**Part two — a descending scan through the senses**, alternating between the outside world and the body:
1. **Sight (outside):** open your eyes, slowly sweep your gaze around the room and pick out 5 objects in the space around you [msg #68].
2. **Body (inside):** move your attention into your body and notice 4 sensations in it [msg #68].
3. **Hearing (outside):** turn outwards again, listen and find 3 sounds [msg #68].
4. **Taste:** lick your lips and feel their taste; then run something over them that will give another taste and lick them again (i.e. two tastes) [msg #68].
5. **Smell (outside):** what smell do you notice right now? If there is none, bring the crook of your elbow to your nose and "listen" to the smell of your own skin, or open a container with a favourite scent and breathe it in [msg #68].

(!) INCOMPLETE: the audio accompaniment the author mentions was not available in the source, so any additions it contained are not captured; the attached photo is decorative and contains no instructions [msg #68].

### IBCT standard protocol
The author quotes the description of the standard IBCT protocol [msg #321]:
1. **Assessment / feedback phase:** «4 сеанса для фазы оценки/обратной связи…» (*4 sessions for the assessment/feedback phase…*). Her aside: it is unhurried, "okay".
2. **Active treatment:** «20–22 сеанса активного лечения, обычно проводимого еженедельно; интервалы между сеансами становятся более широкими ближе к концу терапии» (*20–22 sessions of active treatment, usually weekly; the intervals between sessions get wider towards the end of therapy*). Her estimate: about half a year of therapy; she ironically notes she calculated the cost and "was happy for the therapists".

## Detailed insights

### Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT): what it offers
- **Against "ghostly happiness".** The author describes ACT as an approach that lets you step away from hunting an illusory happiness — the picture where "we all stand beautiful in white in ideal circumstances and as the best version of ourselves". According to her, it is exactly from that peak that we fall within moments into sadness and anxiety — if we even manage to reach the peak. She calls these the "traps" of happiness (discussed in an earlier post of hers) [msg #337].
- **Values map instead of the hedonic race.** Instead of running in the hedonic race, ACT proposes drawing a map of your values and acting in agreement with them while passing through life's difficulties [msg #337].
- **A tough values game.** ACT includes a "harsh but very interesting" game-practice that helps you define very clearly what matters to you and what you could in fact give up and still go on living. (!) INCOMPLETE: the game itself is not described [msg #337].
- **Observing emotions from the shore.** ACT has a set of techniques for stepping out of the stream of emotions and watching them, giving yourself a breather "on the shore of these waves" and opening the possibility of making decisions consciously. The author confirms the association with mindfulness: some parts of ACT can be assembled into common mindfulness techniques [msg #337].
- **The author's own state.** She has long been able to enter — "or rather exit" — a particular state during strong stress: like surfacing out of physical sensations into a calm "outside" from which she can observe and describe anything evenly and without distress, "even if it is burning pain". She could never pass this skill on so others could repeat it; the Harris book promises instructions. She jokes that a meditating monk "goes out of himself" and Anger from *Inside Out* also "goes out of himself" (the Russian phrase vykhodit iz sebya means both stepping outside oneself and losing one's temper) — "but there is a nuance" [msg #337].
- **Where she wants to apply it.** Being able to explain this has become relevant in her work and personal life: helping with impulsivity (including in ADHD), social anxiety (in autism spectrum conditions) and the confusion of feelings with thoughts in many different bodily states. She also notes a conference talk on ACT for anxiety in "Asperger syndrome" (which she points out is part of the autism spectrum) [msg #337].

### Integrative Behavioral Couple Therapy (IBCT)
- **How the author found it.** When a relationship with someone close had "not yet cracked, but already crunched", she searched her extensive memory archive for repair models, but what she had tried did not work for that situation. She then searched in Phind, which she describes as "like ChatGPT, only the sources are more selective": most answers to her queries came with PubMed links. That is how she found IBCT, "a fairly new kind of systemic therapy" [msg #321].
- **Definition.** IBCT is an evidence-based therapy method for couples by clinical psychologists A. Christensen and N. Jacobson. It uses functional analysis to study how partners' behaviour influences each other and the relationship as a whole [msg #321].
- **Mechanics as the author sees them at the start (two components):** (1) analysis of each partner's vulnerabilities and of the cycles — the moments when those vulnerabilities get hit (she comments that this is suitably "systemic"); plus (2) the needed response of one partner to the needs of the other [msg #321].
- **What IBCT promises to address:**
  - why the same quarrels happen again and again;
  - how to keep small incompatibilities from causing big problems;
  - which communication approaches in a couple really work for resolving conflicts;
  - how to solve problems together and make positive changes (the source list ends with an open-ended "и др.") [msg #321].
- **Open question.** The author does not yet know how IBCT differs from classic systemic family therapy; she guesses there may be more emphasis on emotional processes and needs, and plans to check [msg #321].
- **Her stance.** «Моя птичка-скептичка уже чирикает» (*My little sceptic-bird is already chirping*), but, as always, she believes you need to get to know a method more closely before drawing conclusions [msg #321].
- **Authors and evidence.** Andrew Christensen is a clinical psychologist and professor at UCLA; the late Neil S. Jacobson was a clinical psychologist and professor at the University of Washington. IBCT is supported by research, including a large clinical trial funded by the US National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH). Results: more than 2/3 of participating couples stayed together and showed significant clinical improvement at the end of therapy and two years after it [msg #321].

### Potentially dangerous psychotherapies
A post forwarded from the channel «Чистые Когниции» (Pure Cognitions) makes these points [msg #668]:
- Psychotherapy is rarely simple: temporary worsening, painful memories and strong emotions are characteristic of it. This is natural.
- Still, it is important to distinguish normal difficulties from genuinely dangerous methods — sometimes the therapeutic approach itself causes harm.
- Some kinds of psychotherapy continue to be used although their effectiveness is unproven, or they have been recognised as dangerous and even banned by professional associations.
- The authors updated their earlier article and expanded the review of practices recognised as harmful and unethical.

- **NAMI "Ending the Silence"** — a free online program by the US National Alliance on Mental Illness for children and teenagers. It aims to raise awareness of key features of this age and the difficulties young people may face. It includes a 50-minute educational video, 12 live (personal) stories and additional videos on the most common difficulties, covering in particular self-harm, emotion regulation and coping with difficult life situations. According to the author, it gives the whole basis on mental disorders and age-specific challenges, ways of coping, and a supportive community. A presentation can be requested directly on the first page ("Bring NAMI Ending the Silence to your school or youth group"). The author's hedge: the language is "presumably" simple English, possibly also available in others [msg #43, img #43].

The outcome figures (over 2/3 of couples together and clinically improved, at end of therapy and two years later) are reported by her from IBCT's own materials [msg #321].
