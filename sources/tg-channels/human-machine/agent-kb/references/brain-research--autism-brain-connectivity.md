# Brain connectivity and subtypes in autism

## What this covers

This topic gathers the channel author's long-form series on the neuroscience of autism spectrum disorder (ASD, "РАС"): where the connectivity theory came from (Tsien's cell-assembly logic, Hebb, Herbert, Just and Minshew), the proposed developmental mechanism (reduced synaptic pruning), the two competing/complementary theories of under- and hyperconnectivity, why results contradict each other, the 2023–2024 findings on the salience network in infants at risk, the study that split autistic people into four subtypes from resting-state fMRI, and a 2025 news post on how the immune molecule IL-17 explains the fever effect in autism [msg #456, #457, #458, #463, #464, #519, #521, #525, #526, #541].

According to the source, connectivity theories "сейчас … играют одну из центральных ролей" in understanding the biology of autism, and the move from a single ASD-vs-neurotypical contrast to connectivity-based subtypes matters because it lets diagnosis and support be tailored to people's actual profiles instead of treating everyone the same [msg #463, #519, #525].

## Core ideas

- **Autism as a difference in brain connectivity.** The current connectivity theory holds that the clinical symptoms seen in autistic people arise from a particular synchronization and coordination between brain regions; compared with the statistical majority, connections between distant regions are reduced and between neighbouring regions intense [msg #456].
- **Reduced pruning as a mechanism.** Synaptic pruning in early childhood is less extensive in autistic people, leaving a denser local network; this is offered as an explanation for both strengths (perception, pattern recognition, detail memory) and difficulties (social interaction, sensory hypersensitivity, epilepsy risk) [msg #457].
- **Results conflict, so the field moved to subgroups and individuals.** Group studies give contradictory findings (some find no ASD/NT difference at all), because individual connectivity varies and imaging is sensitive to method; the answer has been to study infants, longitudinal samples and subtypes [msg #457, #458, #463, #464].
- **Early attention trade-off.** In infants at elevated likelihood of autism the salience network is more strongly connected to sensorimotor areas than to prefrontal "social" areas, which the author summarizes as attention to sensations and movement borrowing from social attention [msg #463, #464].
- **Four connectivity subtypes, and only 13.4% of atypical links relate to symptoms.** A study using ABIDE data separated shared from individual-specific connectivity, found three dimensions and four subtypes with their own genetic and behavioural signatures; most atypical connections lie within normal neural variation, and differences are about complex network interaction rather than "more" or "less" connectivity overall [msg #521, #525].
- **Immune molecules can change autistic behaviour.** IL-17 released during some infections suppresses an overactive somatosensory region (S1DZ in mice), which may explain why many autistic children's behavioural symptoms temporarily ease during fever [msg #541].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Theory of connectivity (Tsien) and cortical layer logic
**Origin.** In experiments since 2005, Joe Z. Tsien found that, to encode the experience of fear, certain brain cells use a strategy "от частного к общему" (from the particular to the general). He supposed this might reflect a general mathematical principle of how cell assemblies (ensembles) are organized in the brain, and in 2015 he proposed the "теорию связности" (theory of connectivity) [msg #456].

**Cell assembly defined.** A cell ensemble is a network of neurons that has become a "сыгранным оркестром" (a well-rehearsed orchestra): their repeated simultaneous activation during some process strengthened the excitatory synaptic connections between them. In the author's footnote, the concept was introduced by the Canadian neuropsychologist D. O. Hebb (Hebb 1949), the author of the postulate “Нейроны, которые загораются вместе, связываются друг с другом” ("Neurons that fire together, wire together"); in building the theory of connectivity Tsien relied heavily on Hebb's work [msg #456].

**What the theory claims.**
1. **Scope:** it explains how the brain organizes its cells for perception, for forming knowledge and memories, and for flexible behaviour during learning (in the broad sense) [msg #456].
2. **The rule:** the brain uses a simple mathematical permutation logic based on powers of two, written in the source as "N = 2i–1". (!) UNCLEAR: the text says the logic is "based on a power of two" but the formula appears as "2i–1"; the exponent formatting may have been lost, and the post does not explain what N and i stand for [msg #456].
3. **Generality:** this logic occurs in different parts of the brain in different animals and matters for processing various types of information, including emotional and social signals [msg #456].
4. **Built into development:** even when certain receptors necessary for learning and memory are removed in adults, the logic still works, which suggests it is built into our development (the author cites her reference [11]) [msg #456].

**How the cortex uses it, by layer.**
- **Upper layers (2/3):** connect randomly and deal with specific, detailed information, helping in tasks such as recognizing new patterns. According to the post, this randomness also explains why we need sleep to consolidate memories [msg #456].
- **Deeper layers (5/6):** are more organized and process general information, controlling motivation, emotions and behaviour [msg #456].

### Connectivity theory of autism: history and core claim
**Foundations (early 2000s).** Applying connectivity thinking to autism became possible thanks to researchers of the early 2000s [msg #456]:
1. **Martha Herbert** was the pioneer. Her work, at the forefront of combining clinical observation with advanced neuroimaging to study the neurological basis of ASD, first began to shift autism brain research toward connectivity theory. She argued for a system-level analysis of the brain, in contrast to the more traditional, localized views of brain dysfunction in autism [msg #456].
2. **Marcel Just and Nancy Minshew**, in work from 2004–2008, proposed that autism can be understood as a state of disrupted connectivity between different brain regions [msg #456].

Since then this approach has become an important research area for understanding the neural basis of autism; from this point on in the series, "connectivity theory" means the theory in the ASD context [msg #456].

**Core claim.** The connectivity theory currently proposes that the clinical symptoms autistic people can have are caused by a particular synchronization and coordination between their brain regions. It was advanced and tested through research on two kinds of connectivity [msg #456]:
- **structural connectivity** — the physical connections between regions;
- **functional connectivity** — the degree of their synchronized activity.

**Finding.** Compared with the statistical majority, people with ASD were found to have reduced connectivity between distant brain regions and intense connectivity between neighbouring ones [msg #456].

### Synaptic pruning "hedge" model and its consequences
**Mechanism.** In early brain development, especially from birth to about two years, the brain grows rapidly and forms numerous neurons and synapses. Neurons are nerve cells that pass information via electrical and chemical signals; synapses are the places where those signals are transmitted (a small gap between parts of neighbouring neurons) [msg #457].

**The hedge metaphor.** If brain growth is compared with a hedge, it normally grows wild and is later trimmed: in neurotypical development synaptic pruning ("прунинг") reduces the number of synapses, driven by the child's experience and actions, which optimizes the brain's work. In autistic people this pruning is less extensive, producing a denser, less streamlined hedge with more branches [msg #457].

**Consequences proposed in the post** (the author uses hedged language — "может", "may"):
1. **More activity in certain regions** — the abundance of neurons and synapses leads to greater brain activity in some areas, which may enhance sensory perception and pattern recognition [msg #457].
2. **Sensory hypersensitivity** — this can show up as hypersensitivity to light, sounds or textures [msg #457].
3. **Synesthesia and unusual associations** — described as a pleasant thing from the point of view of creativity and vividness of perception: synesthesia, or simply the ability to create unusual associations "и даже быть подозреваемым в употреблении интересных веществ" (and even to be suspected of using interesting substances — a joke by the author) [msg #457].
4. **Cognitive strengths** — in cognitive processing it may lead to enhanced memory and to abilities in detail-oriented problem solving [msg #457].
5. **Weak long-range links** — another supposed feature: while neighbouring areas are tightly interwoven, distant areas are weakly connected and interact weakly, which may explain why tasks such as social interaction can be harder [msg #457].
6. **Epilepsy** — increased neural activity and connections may also contribute to conditions such as epilepsy, in which excessive electrical activity in the brain causes seizures [msg #457].

### Highways vs small towns metaphor
The author's second metaphor: the neurotypical brain has "шоссе" (highways) — efficient long-distance neural pathways — while the autistic brain has more "маленьких городов" (small towns) — localized, dense neural activity. It summarizes the distant-underconnectivity / local-hyperconnectivity picture [msg #457].

### Underconnectivity theory vs hyperconnectivity theory
Written as a quick follow-up note answering readers' questions on the previous article. Both theories propose that brain regions are connected and interact differently in people on and off the spectrum [msg #463].

1. **Underconnectivity theory ("теория пониженной связности").** In people with ASD, brain regions that are *far* from each other interact *less* and therefore less efficiently — reduced functional connectivity between distal regions. Given which regions sit at such distances, this may lead to problems in social communication and cognition [msg #463].
2. **Hyperconnectivity theory ("теория гиперсвязности").** People with ASD have an *increased* number of connections in *neighbouring* (proximal) brain regions compared with neurotypical people. This may lead to narrow specialization in information processing, and from there to stronger focus on details at the expense of broader, context-aware understanding. The theory fits the intense focus and restricted interests often observed in ASD [msg #463].

**How the debate went.** There were many proposals that the autistic brain is less connected than the neurotypical one in some places, while being more connected elsewhere. Researchers converged on "both", but soon disagreed on *where* — results were affected now by participants' age, now by experience, "то ещё миллион всего" (now by a million other things). Regions under suspicion included [msg #463]:
- the default mode network (DMN);
- the anterior cingulate cortex and the prefrontal cortex (PFC);
- the amygdala;
- sensory areas (SC).

The proposed fix was to exclude some of the confounding factors — for example, by studying infants [msg #463].

### Salience-network findings in infants at familial risk
**The network.** The latest data (2023–2024) found significant differences in brain structures of infants at risk of developing ASD, especially in the **salience network (SN)** — the network that detects and filters environmental stimuli, lets one separate the main from the secondary in the information flow, and directs attention accordingly. Per the author's footnote, the SN includes parts of the inner (medial) surface of the cortex — the anterior cingulate gyrus, the anterior part of the insula and the pre-supplementary motor areas — plus nodes in the amygdala, hypothalamus, ventral striatum, thalamus and specific brainstem nuclei [msg #463].

**The UCLA Health finding** [msg #463]:
- Infants with the usual population likelihood of ASD had stronger SN connections with **prefrontal** regions, which are critical for social attention and interaction.
- Infants with elevated likelihood had stronger SN connections with **sensorimotor** regions, where primary processing of movement and sensory information happens.

**Conclusion drawn.** In potentially neurodivergent people, "внимание к ощущениям и движениям берёт в кредит у внимания к социальным аспектам" (attention to sensations and movements borrows on credit from attention to social aspects) [msg #463].

**Prediction at age one.** This finding successfully predicted the children's behaviour at one year [msg #464]:
- the first group (lower likelihood of autism) showed a better ability to share with others what captured their attention — an important precursor of social and communicative skills;
- the second group showed increased sensory sensitivity — a common condition in autism, with very strong (compared with neurotypical people) reactions to sounds and other environmental sensory stimuli.

**Caveat from another study.** Another study, based on individual differences in brain connectivity patterns, pointed out that these results are ambiguous and the picture is subtler: both reduced and increased connectivity have been reported in autistic people in different brain regions and at different developmental stages (by EEG and fMRI), because those studies focused on groups of participants and could not assess individual characteristics [msg #464].

**Next step in the field.** In newer research, one group of authors (2023) finally separated which differences belong to ASD-vs-NT and which connectivity differences exist *within* the group of autistic people, while others showed that fMRI can be used to distinguish subtypes among autistic participants, and identified four [msg #464].

### Four-step method for identifying connectivity-based autism subtypes
**The study.** In March 2023 researchers at Weill Cornell Medicine published what the author calls a very important paper. Comparing not only neurotypical and neurodivergent groups with each other but also people within the spectrum, they identified clear differences in how, in autistic people [msg #519]:
1. certain brain regions interact with one another;
2. which genetic features lie behind this;
3. how people manifest in communication and in repetitive actions.

This yielded four groups of people on the spectrum — which, the author stresses, is about more than the amount of support needed or external measures of how far someone outwardly does not experience difficulties (her strikethrough version: "научился маскироваться и выгорать незаметно для окружающих" — has learned to mask and burn out unnoticed by others) [msg #519].

**Data.** 254 people with ASD and 295 neurotypical people from the Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange (ABIDE); a large dataset collected with resting-state functional MRI to analyse patterns of brain connections [msg #521].

**The steps:**
1. **Compare NT and ASD.** This revealed some connections that were broadly altered in ASD as a whole. The researchers acknowledged that these general patterns cannot explain the differences in symptoms and behaviour *within* the ASD group — which is why the next steps were needed [msg #521].
2. **Compare within the ASD group.** The data were split into two categories: **shared connectivity patterns**, present more universally across people with ASD, and **individual-specific connectivity patterns**, unique patterns present in some autistic people but not in others [msg #521].
3. **Identify the key dimensions.** Within the individual-specific patterns, mathematical methods such as extracting an orthogonal basis revealed three main dimensions — a kind of cluster of how strongly particular regions are connected to each other [msg #521]:
   - the social communication cluster;
   - the repetitive behaviour cluster;
   - the speech (language) and cognitive flexibility cluster.
4. **Group by unique connectivity profile and confirm with genetics and behaviour.** Each autistic person was placed along the three dimensions according to how strongly the regions contributing to them were connected; people with similar profiles were grouped into four subtypes using clustering algorithms. Each subtype thus had its own neural basis — patterns of how certain brain networks (such as the default mode network and the salience network) are functionally connected. To check accuracy, the subtypes were cross-matched with behavioural data (questionnaires) and genetics: each subtype's connectivity profile turned out to have unique genetic markers and behavioural patterns, which both confirmed the models and gave the classification a biological basis [msg #521].

**Why it matters.** This step-by-step comparison let the study move from a broad ASD-vs-NT contrast to a fine, connection-based understanding of subtypes within the spectrum [msg #521].

### Consequences of hyperconnectivity (three effects)
A distinguishing feature found in all identified ASD subtypes is hyper- and hypoconnectivity of certain parts of the brain compared with the population majority [msg #526].

**Hyperconnectivity** — more neural connections than usual. The author draws three consequences [msg #526]:
1. processing of the corresponding information is more intense, which can lead to getting stuck/looping ("застреванию/зацикливанию") and to overloads, and sometimes to epileptic foci;
2. "generation of content" in those places is faster;
3. if the extra connection is *between* regions rather than within one, their associations happen more often — synesthesia, lateral thinking and other creativity may sit exactly here.

**Hypoconnectivity** — the reverse [msg #526].

**Network plasticity.** The brain's ability to create an unusual number of connections was discovered earlier in studies of injuries ("травм"). It is part of the broader concept of network plasticity — the part of neuroplasticity that concerns specifically the connections between parts of the brain (and does not include, for example, the creation of new neurons) [msg #526].

**Expression in behaviour.** How strongly these "special effects" show up in behaviour depends, among other things, on the body's biochemical resources at a given moment, but the main point, per the author, is that in ASD there is "прошивка" (firmware — a built-in wiring) for it [msg #526].

### Five components of language processing plus motor planning
The author adds definitions that, she notes, the researchers did not spell out but which are needed to understand the speech and other features of the subgroups [msg #526]:

- **Social affect ("социальный аффект").** In the study, the specifics of social interaction were measured by this parameter. It measures how unusual a person's eye contact, facial expressions, initiation of joint attention and other behaviours are [msg #526].
- **Verbal IQ** (verbal intelligence) — the ability to understand and reason using concepts formulated in words; more broadly, it is connected with problem solving, abstract thinking and working memory [msg #526].
- **Language processing** — the ability to understand, interpret and produce (the author emphasizes "produce" with "(!)") language in oral, written and sign form. It includes five kinds of processing; the examples are the author's own (she invites corrections) [msg #526]:
  1. **Phonological** — recognizing the sounds of speech. Example: distinguishing the words кот (cat) and год (year) [msg #526].
  2. **Morphological** — understanding the structure of words and their components, such as suffixes. Example: understanding that «стульчик» means "a little chair" (стул = chair + diminutive suffix) [msg #526].
  3. **Syntactic** — mastering grammatical rules to build sentences. Example: understanding that "the dog chases the cat" differs from "the cat chases the dog" [msg #526].
  4. **Semantic** — extracting meaning from words and sentences. Examples: distinguishing the senses of «ключ» in "the conversation went in an unusual key" vs "she plays in the bass clef"; and «лук» in "please buy onions" vs slang "check out my look" [msg #526].
  5. **Pragmatic** — interpreting language from context, tone and social cues. Example: the sarcasm in «Только вас мне ещё и не хватало!» ("You're the last thing I needed!") [msg #526].
  - **Plus motor planning:** together with these components, the ability to form coherent sentences through motor planning — articulation for speech, or other movements for sign language [msg #526].

### IL-17 variants, brain sites and behavioural effects (fever effect)
A news post on new research into the cytokine IL-17, adding to growing evidence that immune molecules can influence behaviour during illness. Scientists from MIT and Harvard ran two studies and found that interleukin-17 can either trigger a feeling of anxiety or stimulate prosocial behaviour, depending on which version of the molecule it is and where it acts [msg #541]:

1. **IL-17A and IL-17C — amygdala (fear and anxiety areas).** They bind receptors IL-17RA and IL-17RE, raise neuronal excitability and, as a result, cause a feeling of anxiety [msg #541].
2. **IL-17E (also called IL-25) — somatosensory cortex.** Here, on the contrary, it lowers neuronal excitability, binding receptors IL-17RA and IL-17RB [msg #541].

**What it may explain.** Why we feel anxious or withdrawn during illness (trying to protect ourselves from other people and other people from ourselves); it may also shed light on side effects of drugs that specifically block IL-17 in autoimmune diseases [msg #541].

**The fever effect.** The lead authors, Gloria Choi and Jun Huh, became interested in IL-17 several years earlier when they found it is involved in the "fever effect": large-scale studies of autistic children showed that in many of them behavioural symptoms temporarily weaken during fever [msg #541].

**Mechanism shown in mice (2019).** Choi and Huh showed that in some infections IL-17 is released and suppresses a small area of the mouse cortex called S1DZ [msg #541]:
- S1DZ is a close analogue of human Brodmann area 3a, located in the somatosensory cortex and involved in the sense of touch, proprioception (the position of the body in space and the effort for physical actions needed to coordinate voluntary movement), and neuropathic skin pain.
- Excessive activation of neurons in this area produced autism-like behavioural symptoms in mice, including repetitive behaviour and reduced sociability.
- Therefore, lowering neuronal activity in this cortex via IL-17 can reduce these manifestations.

**Current and future work.** Choi's lab is further mapping the locations of IL-17 receptors and molecules, focusing on S1DZ. Ultimately, a better understanding of these neuroimmune interactions may help researchers develop new treatments for difficulties in autism or depression [msg #541].

## Detailed insights

### Why connectivity results contradict each other
The author lists the reasons in her Part 2 [msg #457]:
1. **Individual uniqueness.** Every person's combination of behaviour, experience and genetics is unique, and ASD is no exception, so variability extends to individual connectivity patterns. Results of different experiments currently differ, and some find no difference at all between ASD and NT brains (her refs [1][3][4]).
2. **Subgroups.** Some researchers therefore study connectivity in subgroups of autistic people, considering age, sex and genetic mutations, which requires direct evaluation ([3]). Example: mutations in the autism-linked genes MET and CNTNAP2 produce connectivity patterns different from those of people without these mutations.
3. **Need for big data and smaller clusters.** Large databases, including the Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange, can help pinpoint groups of autistic people with different connectivity patterns. Some researchers have started looking for smaller-scale clusters, for example focusing on brain regions that control speech.
4. **Imaging method sensitivity.** Brain imaging results depend on magnetic field parameters, the experimental approach and data-analysis methods, besides standard problems such as head movement. Example: connectivity between some regions is stronger when participants' eyes are open.
5. **Medication.** Psychotropic drugs such as fluoxetine (Prozac) or methylphenidate (Ritalin) can also change connectivity structure.
6. **Cross-sectional design.** The most valuable data come from studies that track connectivity in the same people over time. One of the few such studies suggests that connections between some brain networks strengthen from early to late adolescence in typical people but stay stable in autistic people; some studies of adolescents may give more information ([3]).

### Future research directions for connectivity studies
From Part 3 of the series [msg #458]:
1. **Study autistic toddlers before therapy.** Researchers can gain insight by studying autistic toddlers before they receive therapy. A sleep study of 2–4-year-olds shows that in autistic toddlers connections between some brain regions turn out unusually *weak*, not strong.
2. **Link connectivity to other mechanisms.** Existing research stresses the need to connect the disrupted-connectivity hypothesis with other proposed mechanisms of ASD: theory of mind (analysed in an earlier channel post), executive dysfunction, and altered complex information processing. Understanding connectivity anomalies within neurobiological frameworks requires collaborative studies using a wider range of methods ([1]).
3. **Fill fundamental gaps.** The field struggles to interpret connectivity data because of unresolved assumptions and knowledge gaps; more basic research is needed to understand how the commonly used measures (DTI, fMRI, EEG) relate to other levels of analysis.
4. **Combine imaging methods.** Some researchers argue that combining imaging methods gives richer data as well as more reliable results. The most common method now is fMRI; others include magnetoencephalography (MEG), which tracks fluctuations in the brain's magnetic fields, and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), which maps structural connections.
5. **Larger multimodal and developmental studies.** Specialists address these difficulties with larger multimodal studies that combine functional and anatomical connectivity analyses; understanding the developmental trajectory of brain connectivity is one of the most important topics for future research ([4]).
6. **Fix the analysis problem.** A major problem is how exactly to analyse the images. Machine learning is now used to classify participants as autistic based on their connectivity patterns, but different algorithms can give contradictory results, and researchers often cannot replicate their own findings when applying their methods to a new dataset.

The author added that, fortunately, a large study at a British university at the beginning of the year proposed a new classification of ASD ("да, снова!" — yes, again!) based on neural patterns, to be covered in later posts [msg #458].

### Heterogeneity: how big are the neurodivergent differences really?
Part 2 of the subtype series (the author jokes she had to fight neural-network hallucinations to read the study) [msg #525]:
- **The 13.4% finding.** In the researchers' view, the most important conclusion is that among the atypical functional connections in people with ASD, "только 13.4% оказались связанными с симптомами" (only 13.4% turned out to be linked to symptoms) [msg #525].
- **Combinations matter.** Most variations fell within the range of standard neural variability and produced ASD symptoms only in specific combinations with atypical connections in particular areas — the default mode network, the thalamus, primary sensorimotor areas, the dorsal raphe of the brainstem, with the source itself ending the list with "др" (and so forth) [msg #525].
- **Many paths of adaptation.** The results show the brain can adapt to what happens inside and outside through various (literally neural) paths; in this context ASD is not a homogeneous condition but a spectrum ("гетерогенное", heterogeneous) [msg #525].
- **Same symptoms, different networks.** The researchers allow that symptoms of ASD that look similar at first glance may be tied to different pictures at the level of neural networks — which, in the author's view, shows the importance of fine differential diagnosis [msg #525].
- **Compensatory patterns.** They also think some connectivity patterns may be compensatory, helping people cope with symptoms (to be covered in later posts). Hence different subtypes may need quite different support, depending on their unique connectivity profiles [msg #525].
- **Nuance, not quantity.** Last but not least, the differences between neurodivergent and neurotypical people lie in nuances — complex interaction of neural networks, "а не просто о том, что при РАС глобально связей «больше» или «меньше»" (and not simply that in ASD there are globally "more" or "fewer" connections) [msg #525].

### Why subtypes matter (the author's framing)
The author explains the shift: the latest ASD research no longer studies autism as a single phenomenon but goes down to details and notices different configurations. This shift aims to recognize unique cognitive profiles of ASD and approach people's needs more finely — "без того, чтобы подбирать всех под одну гребёнку" (without lumping everyone together). She is openly enthusiastic and expects effects on diagnosis and support, and invites readers who know autism closely to make "educated guesses" about how brain regions and their contacts may be reflected in a person's state and behaviour. The post's glossary: НТ = neurotypical, НО = neurodivergent, РАС or "спектр" = autism spectrum disorder (counted as neurodivergent). The attached image is a decorative illustration of 21 numbered, differently shaped leaves with no text content [msg #519, img #519].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- **Hedged mechanisms.** The pruning-based consequences (perception, synesthesia, memory, social difficulty, epilepsy) are stated with "может" / "предполагается" (may / is supposed); they are hypotheses, not established facts [msg #457].
- (!) CONTRADICTION: the general connectivity picture says distant regions are *under*connected and neighbouring ones *over*connected, yet a sleep study of 2–4-year-olds found unusually *weak* connections in autistic toddlers, the 2024 infant study found *stronger* SN–sensorimotor coupling, and another study notes that both reduced and increased connectivity appear in different regions and ages. The source presents these as unresolved [msg #456, #458, #463, #464].
- (!) UNCLEAR: in Part 3 the author says the new neural-pattern classification came from "одном из британских университетов" (a British university) at the beginning of the year, while the subtype series attributes the four-subtype study to Weill Cornell Medicine (March 2023), and the source list also gives a September 2023 Biological Psychiatry paper and a December 2023 press release. The source does not reconcile institution and dates [msg #458, #464, #519].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the numbered references [1], [3], [4] and [11] cited in the connectivity series are not included in the source messages [msg #456, #457, #458].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the descriptions of the four individual subtypes, and the promised posts on compensatory connectivity, are announced but not present in this topic's messages [msg #525, #526].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the formula "N = 2i–1" is given without explanation of its terms [msg #456].
- **Group vs individual data.** Group-based EEG/fMRI studies cannot assess individual features, which is offered as the reason for ambiguous results [msg #464].
- **Replication.** Machine-learning classification of autism from connectivity is unstable across algorithms and often fails to replicate on new datasets [msg #458].
- **Animal model.** The S1DZ/IL-17 mechanism was shown in mice; the human link is by analogy to Brodmann area 3a, and treatments are a hoped-for future outcome [msg #541].
- **The author's own additions.** The language-processing examples in Part 3 are the author's own, and she invites corrections; the definitions of social affect, verbal IQ and language processing are her additions because the researchers did not describe them [msg #526].
