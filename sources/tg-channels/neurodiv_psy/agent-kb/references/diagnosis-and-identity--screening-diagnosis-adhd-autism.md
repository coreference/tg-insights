# Screening and diagnosis of adult ADHD and autism

## What this covers

This part collects the channel's starting points for adults who suspect ADHD or autism: a curated map of Russian-language resources for screening and finding informed help, an explanation of why ADHD often surfaces in young adulthood (especially in women), and a full translation of Samantha Craft's unofficial checklist of autistic traits, which is described as especially important for recognising autism in women. According to the source, the Russian-speaking space still has few specialists informed about autism and ADHD, so self-screening and crowd-sourced specialist lists are a practical first step [msg #10, #149].

This part covers four posts reshared by the channel. The largest is a full Russian translation of the UK ADHD Foundation's guide *СДВГ у взрослых. Гайд для успешной жизни с СДВГ* (ADHD in adults: a guide to living successfully with ADHD). It covers self-screening with the ASRS, how to get diagnosed, the DSM-5 criteria, executive functions and coping strategies, sleep, diet, exercise, stress, relationships, medication and therapy. Another post lists eight science-based ADHD screening instruments and includes a psychiatrist's view on why self-screening is still worth doing. One post is about autistic girls and women being diagnosed late and misdiagnosed. The last summarises a large Swedish study showing that ADHD and autism diagnoses have risen sharply while measured symptom levels have not [msg #209, #233, #255, #337].

According to the sources, this matters for several reasons. Myths about ADHD still stop people understanding how it affects them. Understanding why one struggles opens the way to many coping approaches. Missed diagnosis in women harms their mental and physical health. Talk of an "epidemic" is not supported by the data [msg #209, #233, #337].

## Core ideas

- **Few informed specialists, so start with screening tools and community lists.** The author notes that in the Russian-speaking space there are still not many specialists informed about autism and ADHD, so getting to the right psychiatrist may be hard; screening tests and "people's lists" of informed professionals help bridge that gap (full list in *Resource map*) [msg #10].
- **ADHD is often discovered at 18–25 because external structure disappears.** School, university, clubs, sports sections and even certain relationships provide a ready-made structure and routine; when these vanish, the person must structure life themselves, which for someone with ADHD can feel impossible — and this is when many people, especially women, discover their ADHD [img #73].
- **Autism assessment must look across the lifespan.** When considering an autism-spectrum diagnosis, childhood, adolescence, young adulthood and current age should all be considered, because many autistic people learn communication rules — sometimes to extremes — so the primary sign may be that they are still learning social rules, i.e. still masking or trying to fit in [msg #149].
- **Craft's checklist is a conversation starter, not a diagnostic tool.** It is one person's opinion based on 8+ years of near-daily contact with diagnosed and self-identified autistic people; it is especially useful for women but usable by any gender [msg #149].

- **Self-screening is useful but is not diagnosis.** Psychiatrist Stepan Krasnoshchekov says screening helps a person suspect a disorder, gauge their symptoms and decide whether to see a psychiatrist. A negative test should not stop someone with symptoms and suffering from seeing a specialist. The diagnostic interview is the main diagnostic method [msg #255].
- **ADHD is distinguished from ordinary inattention by degree, persistence since childhood and current impairment.** Everyone is sometimes inattentive, hyperactive or impulsive, and stress or other conditions can mimic these traits. In ADHD, though, the traits are more pronounced and present since childhood. Whether you have ADHD depends on how consistently they show up and whether they cause difficulties now [msg #209].
- **Managing ADHD is more than medication.** The guide treats daily, gradual lifestyle change, executive-function strategies, sleep, diet, exercise, stress reduction and relationship work as essential. It calls medication "one of a whole range of strategies" [msg #209].
- **ADHD does not define you.** The guide's signature line is that ADHD is a lens through which you see the world. It takes a strengths-based approach: hyperactivity can be energy, impulsivity can be decisiveness, and mind-wandering can be imagination and curiosity [msg #209].
- **Autism in women is missed and misdiagnosed.** The cited figure is that 80% of women do not get a correct autism diagnosis before 18, because of stereotypes and biases among specialists, parents and teachers. Psychiatrists often label them with social anxiety, BPD, OCD or attachment disorder instead [msg #233].
- **Diagnoses rose; symptoms did not.** A Swedish twin study of 9,870 adolescents found no rise in autism symptom levels and only a small rise in ADHD symptoms among girls, while diagnoses soared. The authors conclude that talk of an autism or ADHD "epidemic" is unfounded [msg #337].

### Samantha Craft's autistic traits checklist
**What it is and who made it.** An **unofficial** checklist ("**неофициальный** чеклист") created by Samantha Craft, who was diagnosed with Asperger's syndrome in 2012 and now identifies as autistic. Her middle son is autistic, as are 10,000+ people she knows and calls friends. She holds a master's degree in education and has no academic degree in psychiatry or psychology; her expertise is lived experience on the spectrum and as a parent of a child diagnosed with Asperger's. She created the list to help medical professionals recognise autism; for in-depth information the post refers to her book "Everyday Aspergers", noting that at the time the book was written Asperger's had not yet been removed from the DSM. The translation was forwarded from the Telegram channel Спектр нейроотличности (Spectrum of Neurodifference); the original source is myspectrumsuite.com [msg #149].

**Suggested use (scoring rule).** Tick all items that apply to the person. If there are 75–80% matches in **each** section, then you *may possibly* want to consider the probability that the person *may be* autistic — the hedges are the source's own ("*возможно*… *может быть* аутистом") [msg #149].

**How to apply it.** Consider childhood, adolescence, young adulthood and current age, not only the present. This matters especially for communication difficulties: many autistic people learn communication rules, sometimes to extremes, so the primary sign may be that they are *still* learning social rules — in other words, still masking or trying to fit in [msg #149].

**Disclaimer (from the source).** It is one person's opinion; it is not meant to replace the DSM-V definition of ASD and is not an official diagnostic tool. Thousands of people worldwide have used it together with DSM-IV or DSM-V and the guidance of a professional mental health specialist. It is described as an important tool, especially for women, though people of any gender can use it. It is based on 8+ years of almost daily contact with people diagnosed with or self-identified as autistic. It does not include everything; some people will fit the categories and not be autistic. It is intended as a springboard for discussion and raising awareness [msg #149].

### Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale (ASRS) — screening procedure (guide version)
**Purpose:** a self-assessment for adults who wonder whether they have ADHD. It comes from Kessler et al. (Psychological Medicine) [file #209].

**How to complete and score it:**
1. Fill in Parts A and B by marking with a cross the answer that fits you best on a five-point frequency scale: Никогда (never), Редко (rarely), Иногда (sometimes), Часто (often), Очень часто (very often) [file #209].
2. Score Part A. If **four or more marks fall in the blue (shaded) cells**, you have signs characteristic of ADHD and further assessment is needed [file #209].
3. Part B does not decide the result. It can give additional information and add context [file #209].

### Executive functions model
**Definition:** executive function is a set of cognitive skills that everyone has. They help us plan, organise ourselves and use goal-directed behaviour to do everything we need to do in life. They also help us control the attention, impulses and emotions that can stop us starting and/or finishing those actions. We use these skills every day to learn, work and cope with daily life. Difficulties with them can stop us concentrating, following instructions and handling emotions [file #209].

**The eight component skills shown in the guide:**
1. **Flexible thinking:** the ability to adapt to the unexpected.
2. **Emotional control:** the ability to describe feelings and regulate them.
3. **Impulse control:** thinking before acting.
4. **Organisation:** keeping track of things mentally and physically.
5. **Working memory:** holding information in mind.
6. **Self-monitoring:** keeping track of one's own actions.
7. **Planning and prioritising:** being able to set a goal and make a plan to reach it.
8. **Task initiation:** being able to take action to get started. [file #209]

**Working memory, "one of the most important executive functions":** a system for temporarily storing and handling the information needed for complex thinking such as learning, reasoning and understanding. It helps us choose and start information-processing functions, such as retrieving relevant information from long-term memory. There are two types:
- **Non-verbal working memory** develops in childhood. It lets us hold in mind what we see and hear around us. Through it we learn to imitate others, anticipate consequences, develop self-awareness, manage time and delay gratification.
- **Verbal working memory** is the ability to talk to oneself. As it develops, children learn self-control and problem-solving. It helps us make plans, follow rules, think rationally, and plays an important role in moral judgement.
ADHD can cause problems in both areas [file #209].

**Emotional self-regulation, "another central executive function":** emotions arise whether we want them or not and can drive behaviour. Repeated triggers provoke strong feelings of sadness, loss, disappointment, anger or joy. Triggers include reactions to criticism, humiliation, unmet needs, or strong happiness when expectations are met. Verbal and non-verbal working memory help regulate emotions in six ways:
1. Using imagination to feel less angry or sad.
2. Soothing oneself to reduce the impact of strong emotions.
3. Bargaining with oneself to motivate oneself internally.
4. Distracting oneself from what triggers the emotion.
5. Thinking about and expressing emotions in socially acceptable ways (the transcription is garbled here: "думать, ать свои эмоции социальносоциально приемлемыми").
6. Talking to oneself about what one feels. [file #209]

**Common executive-function problems in adults with ADHD.** The guide presents these as a self-reflection prompt: "which of these matter for you?"
1. Difficulties with motivation and organisation.
2. Putting things back in their place and not losing them.
3. Being unable to concentrate.
4. Difficulties solving problems.
5. Failing to foresee consequences.
6. Difficulties taking in new information.
7. Problems with forecasting.
8. Time management, meeting deadlines and estimating time take a lot of effort or do not work at all.
9. Acting too fast and impulsively without fully thinking through the consequences.
10. Difficulty prioritising work or duties.
11. Hard to start a conversation and keep attention during it.
12. Rigid thinking, and difficulty evaluating the results of one's own actions.
13. Weaker memory for past or current events, and difficulty remembering details.
14. Difficulty controlling emotions, which can lead to outbursts such as anger or crying. [file #209]

### Five strategies for executive function difficulties
**Purpose:** to compensate for executive functions that do not work well "inside" by moving them into the outside world. Each strategy below is reproduced fully [file #209].
1. **Externalise what does not work internally.** Reduce the amount of information kept in your head. Move key information into a physical form you can reach, for example a notebook you carry at work, or the phone's Notes app. Write down important information or instructions you receive regularly and immediately. This becomes your **external working memory**, so always keep it with you. The notes can later be expanded into daily goals or a long-term work plan [file #209].
2. **Train external motivation.** Create regular, repeating forms of external motivation. For a long task, break it into smaller parts or time blocks and reward yourself after each section or block. The reward can be as simple as a cup of coffee or listening to music [file #209].
3. **Externalise time.** Make time more tangible with timers, clocks, and reminders on the computer or phone. The more clearly physical reminders make you aware that time is passing, the more they help you manage time effectively [file #209].
4. **Externalise organisation.** Build visual systems that work for you: planners, calendars, card files and daily to-do lists. Use a highlighter to mark key information in documents. Keep a diary where you record expected difficulties as well as tasks to be done. Pitfall: there are many systems, so be ready to change your organisational tools if they do not suit you [file #209].
5. **Organise your environment.** Ask how distracting your surroundings are, especially your workplace, and what you can change. Options: move your desk away from the window or from where colleagues gather and chat; set up blocking on the computer or phone so irrelevant information does not appear on screen; put the phone on silent or voicemail and plan to check it only at set times of day; consider noise-cancelling headphones or screens around your work area [file #209].

### Four ideas for better sleep
**Context:** most working-age adults need 7–9 hours of sleep a night. People with ADHD often struggle to keep a regular night-time routine, are easily distracted, and find it hard to switch off in bed when the past day or tomorrow is still racing in their head. ADHD medication (especially stimulants) and late-evening caffeine (tea, coffee, fizzy drinks, chocolate) can add to this, as can anxiety, depression or substance misuse combined with ADHD. Poor sleep lowers energy, mood, performance and enjoyment of life. It reduces concentration and increases working-memory difficulties, procrastination and lack of motivation, especially at work. In ADHD it worsens existing difficulties. First step: if you have had sleep difficulties for a long time or notice signs of a sleep disorder, tell your doctor and the ADHD clinic (or, depending on the country, a psychiatrist). If you take ADHD medication, discuss its effect on sleep and whether to change it. The self-help measures below are called гигиена сна (sleep hygiene) [file #209].

1. **Keep a sleep routine.** A successful bedtime routine depends on consistency: go to bed at the same time every night, weekends included, and get up at the same time every morning. Stick to it even if you are not tired at bedtime. In the evening try relaxing activities such as a bath, reading or audio recordings. Mechanism: a bath raises body temperature, and it drops after you get out. This supports the natural physiological drop in temperature that happens as the body falls asleep [file #209].
2. **Don't force yourself to fall asleep.** Instead of lying awake worrying or ruminating, get up and do something relaxing, and go back to bed when you feel tired again. Keep a notebook by the bed and write down what worries you; knowing you will not forget it tomorrow helps you feel calmer. Try meditation or breathing exercises before sleep, such as Dr Andrew Weil's **4-7-8 technique**: breathe in for 4 seconds, hold for 7, breathe out for 8, and repeat several times. Plan the evening so you think only about good things in bed, such as three things you were grateful for today. The more positive the thoughts, the less stress just before sleep [file #209].
3. **Make the bedroom good for sleep.** Audit the bedroom for distractions. Is it too hot or too cold? Is there outside noise, and can it be reduced? Is it dark enough and is the light dimmed? (Blackout curtains or blinds are a good purchase.) Are the walls in muted, dark tones? Are your belongings out of sight? Is your sleepwear comfortable, or does it raise body temperature? Does the mattress help or hinder? It should be firm enough to support the body evenly, but one that is too firm makes you feel you are lying on top of it rather than comfortably. How many screens are in the bedroom, and can they be removed? [file #209]
4. **Prepare your body for sleep during the day.** Plan regular exercise so you feel more tired in the evening, but not too close to bedtime, because that can leave you more alert. Do not use alcohol to fall asleep: although a sedative, it can seriously harm the quality and quantity of sleep, and a lot of it before bed makes sleep lighter and restless, so you do not feel rested next day. Avoid caffeine less than **eight hours** before sleep; its stimulating effect varies by person, but leave enough time between the last dose and bedtime. Avoid eating late and plan the evening meal earlier. Try relaxing practices such as aromatherapy or massage. Set aside time to reflect on the day and think about tomorrow; to-do lists or diaries help offload thoughts before sleep [file #209].

### What ADHD is, according to the guide
ADHD is described as a neurological condition. The NHS estimates it affects about **3% of the population**. Its three main features are inattention, hyperactivity and impulsivity, and many doctors also recognise emotional-regulation difficulties and executive-function difficulties as characteristic. ADHD can last a lifetime, and its manifestations can change with age [file #209].

Each person with ADHD is unique: a combination of genetics and environment, such as upbringing, school life, family, society and specific experiences. ADHD may therefore affect you differently from someone else with ADHD who has a different history. Some people's ADHD is thought to be linked to physical problems such as **asthma** (Cortese et al.) and **migraine** (Hansen et al., 2018). Some women have more difficulty with ADHD because of **hormonal changes** (Young et al., 2020, expert consensus on females with ADHD) [file #209].

Living with ADHD can be a serious source of worry for many. Daily changes to habits that reduce stress are an integral part of living well with ADHD. Change should be gradual, but should start now. To understand which lifestyle choices to make, find out how ADHD affects you personally [file #209].

### Sleep disorders associated with ADHD
The sleep cycle, as the guide explains it: on falling asleep, body temperature drops, heart rate slows and brain activity changes. We first enter the first stage of non-REM (NREM) sleep, the lightest stage, from which we wake easily. Sleep then deepens through two more NREM stages until REM sleep, the stage where dreaming starts [file #209].

The three sleep disorders the guide links to ADHD:
1. **Sleep apnoea:** breathing stops during the night, causing broken sleep and tiredness on waking. Research shows it is more common in people with ADHD than in the general population (Oğuztürk et al.). Loud snoring can be a sign.
2. **Circadian rhythm sleep disorders:** the body runs on a daily cycle tuned to light and dark over 24 hours. When darkness falls, the body releases melatonin, which signals it to prepare for sleep. With a circadian rhythm disorder, the body may be out of sync and may not release melatonin at the right time, so falling asleep is harder. Bright bedroom light or artificial blue light from laptops, tablets and phones can contribute.
3. **Restless legs syndrome (RLS):** a strong urge to move the legs in bed and during sleep, constant discomfort in the limbs, and itching, pain, pulling or throbbing sensations in the legs. Studies of RLS and ADHD found it may affect **up to 44%** of people with ADHD (Cortese et al., Sleep). [file #209]

### Diet, eating and ADHD
**General healthy-eating guidance** (NHS England guide, last revised 2019, as relayed):
- Eat 5 portions of varied fruit and vegetables a day. They should make up more than a third of daily food.
- Starchy foods (potatoes, bread, rice, pasta) should also be about a third, included at every meal, preferably high-fibre versions (wholegrain, potatoes with skin). They are a good energy source.
- Dairy or alternatives (soy drinks, lower-fat cheese, skimmed milk) are sources of protein and calcium. Choose low-fat, low-sugar options. Two to three portions a day provide all the calcium needed. For vegans or the lactose-intolerant: leafy greens, broccoli, oranges, and calcium-fortified soy milk, rice drinks or yoghurts.
- Protein: fish, eggs or meat. Pulses, lentils and beans are effective meat alternatives that contain less fat and a higher amount of fibre.
- Two portions of fish a week, ideally oily. If you do not eat fish, nuts and seeds supply omega-3, or consider supplements.
- Eat less high-fat, high-salt, high-sugar food (chocolate, cakes, biscuits, sugary soft drinks, butter).
- Drink plenty: 6–8 glasses a day. Water, low-fat milk and low- or no-sugar drinks including tea and coffee are healthier.
- About 2,000 kcal a day for women and about 2,500 kcal for men. [file #209]

**Diet self-check (past week, yes/no):**
1. Five portions of fruit and vegetables every day?
2. Starchy carbohydrates at every meal?
3. Two to three portions of dairy every day?
4. Two portions of fish or alternatives each week?

**ADHD and weight.** Research shows that ADHD is associated with different eating patterns, physical activity levels and sleep behaviour. Together with genetic risk, these may be linked to weight changes not seen in people without ADHD. A 2016 meta-analysis (Cortese et al., American Journal of Psychiatry) found that about **28% of adults with ADHD meet obesity criteria vs 16%** of people without ADHD [file #209].

**Seven factors that may link ADHD and eating habits:**
1. Impulsive eating without regard to type or calories.
2. Skipping meals or forgetting to eat, which increases hunger and possibly leads to overeating.
3. ADHD medication can suppress appetite, leading to undereating during the day and overeating when the medication wears off in the evening or at night.
4. Difficulty planning and structuring time, buying ingredients and cooking, which leads to overuse of fast food or high-calorie snacks.
5. Feelings of anxiety, depression or boredom.
6. Difficulty sticking to diet plans, especially ones that require preparation and study, record-keeping, nutrition knowledge and cooking.
7. Sensory difficulties, which can lead to picky eating. [file #209]

**Mechanism:** inattention and impulsivity are core ADHD features and can affect diet (Kittel-Schneider et al., 2021). People with ADHD may overeat, snack heavily between meals or eat late at night because of poor impulse control, low mindfulness while eating and poor planning. These traits are also linked to a preference for high-carbohydrate foods and foods that bring short-term satisfaction [file #209].

**Five ways to improve daily eating:**
1. Plan several mini-meals through the day. This can reduce the restlessness that leads to impulsive overeating.
2. Create a healthy "food environment" at home, for example by not buying high-sugar, high-calorie snacks.
3. Remember that the results of dietary change are not immediate. This helps you stick to plans and not give up too easily or quickly.
4. Replace high-calorie snacks with a regularly restocked supply of nutritious no-prep snacks, such as boiled eggs, yoghurt, apples or oranges.
5. Plan weekly shopping on a fixed day, then set aside time to cook healthy meals that can be frozen and reheated during the week. Don't shop hungry. [file #209]

### Exercise and fitness
**Benefits for people with ADHD**, as listed: it keeps the body healthy, improves impulse control and working memory, improves executive function, and reduces stress and anxiety. Exercise can stimulate release of neurotransmitters such as **dopamine**, which affects the ability to focus and concentrate. Generally, exercise strengthens the heart and improves circulation. It increases blood flow and lowers blood pressure, which raises oxygen levels and helps reduce the risk of heart problems (high cholesterol, coronary heart disease, heart attack). It also supports good mental health and sound sleep [file #209].

**Dose (NHS England, adults 19–64):** some physical activity every day. On average at least **150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity a week**, about 30 minutes a day, five days a week. On at least two days a week, include strengthening exercises for all major muscle groups (legs, hips, back, chest, shoulders, arms). Match level and intensity to your fitness. If in doubt, limited in ability, pregnant or recently given birth, consult a doctor before starting a new regime [file #209].

**Five tips for starting exercise:**
1. **Be ready to experiment** to find the right activity. If some options do not suit you, do not give up entirely; switch to another type of exercise.
2. **Find a workout or sports buddy**, because a friend can help you keep to the schedule.
3. **Make a schedule:** find a convenient time of day and remove all obstacles, for example by laying out workout clothes the night before or keeping a gym bag in the car.
4. **Vary your routine** so you do not lose interest or focus halfway. For example, use interval training in the gym (alternate short bouts of running or cycling with short bouts of strength work), or alternate swimming and running on different days.
5. **Reward yourself**, especially at the start, because immediate reward can be decisive for persistence. Pick something you usually look forward to and do not allow it until after the workout, such as a hot bath. [file #209]

### Daily stress-reduction strategies
Relationships, money, career, budgeting and emergencies can disrupt the routines and structure people with ADHD depend on. The guide "strongly recommends" spending time "daily — yes, every day" on caring for your body, because physical health affects mental health as much as the reverse. Method: start with one strategy and gradually add more. Not all will suit you. Be patient and "train" your mind to build these habits, making them daily or weekly over three to six months. Benefits take **at least three months** to show, so use visual reminders around the house (a cork noticeboard, a poster schedule) and smartphone reminders at set times. The promised benefits: less chance of becoming irritable, impatient or short-tempered; better focus, concentration, self-motivation and a sense of control over your life; better mood and help with depression [file #209].

The strategies:
1. **Brisk walking, swimming, jogging or cycling** also reduce anxiety, lift mood and improve motivation.
2. **Yoga** is an established stress-reduction method that lets you "listen" to what your body says it needs. There are many good online materials, and 10 minutes a day is possible.
3. **Hobbies and interests.** Hyperfocus is described as a product of the "interest-based nervous system". Many people with ADHD can concentrate for long periods on something that genuinely interests them, though life also demands attention to everyday necessities. A hobby you enjoy, whether light (crafts, reading) or active (sport, community interests), relaxes and energises, and gives you "your time".
4. **Meditation.** The guide advises practising deep breathing for several months before starting meditation. Meditation need not mean sitting still; mindfulness can be practised while walking at a slow, smooth pace.
5. **Deep breathing.** Very slow deep breathing for 3–5 minutes a day, gradually building up to four times a day. It can be done almost anywhere: at the office or on public transport.
6. **Other strategies.** Find what genuinely helps you relax: baths or massage, reading or audiobooks, creative activities, art, theatre, or volunteering and working on causes you care about (climate change, care of older people, animal protection, local charity events). [file #209]

### Relationships and ADHD
The guide says everyone needs to feel loved, understood and accepted by the people who matter, and warm connections matter for mental health, intimacy, work and social life. ADHD can strongly affect relationships. You may have felt misunderstood. People with limited knowledge of ADHD may find people with ADHD hard to be around, and you may have faced rejection, leaving you feeling misunderstood, impatient or disappointed. People with ADHD often feel overwhelmed by daily demands, including maintaining relationships. Constant apologising, or remarks that make you feel incapable or ashamed, can lead to withdrawing from social situations and not starting or maintaining relationships for fear they will fail [file #209].

**How ADHD affects relationships (six mechanisms):**
1. Poor memory and organisation can be read as lack of interest, for example missing a social event or forgetting a birthday or anniversary. A non-ADHD partner may feel resentful at doing a disproportionate share of household planning and organisation.
2. Leaving tasks to the last minute can increase stress for others, especially at work.
3. Avoiding multi-step tasks or not finishing tasks can be seen as selfishness.
4. Excessive talking and interrupting can be read as lack of interest in others' thoughts and opinions.
5. Emotional-regulation difficulties can make people feel uncomfortable or afraid to be around you.
6. Impulsivity: thoughtless remarks or actions can offend and cause conflict, and impulsive behaviour such as unplanned spending can affect close relationships. [file #209]

### Medication
The guide says medication has been shown to help many adults with ADHD (Hechtman, 2008). It helps the brain work better and improves the ability to focus and remember information. It reduces impulsivity and helps with emotional regulation and some aspects of executive function [file #209].
- **Titration:** the psychiatrist starts at a low dose and may raise it over several months until it works effectively. Track any changes in symptoms and possible side effects. Your doctor should discuss these at the start of dose-finding or when switching medication, so you know what to watch for.
- Every brain is unique, so comparing yourself with other people with ADHD will not help. Discuss concerns with your doctor and give yourself time to go through the process.
- Approved ADHD medications act for different lengths of time. Some are taken once a day, others several times. Know how and when to take your specific drug. You may need it only for a certain period each day, which for most people is working hours.
- There are **two kinds: stimulant and non-stimulant.** The doctor advises which suits you based on how ADHD affects you and your medical history.
- **"REMEMBER":** pharmacotherapy is one of a whole range of strategies and should be used as such; do not rely on medication alone. Drugs can cause side effects such as dizziness, drowsiness and visual disturbances. You should be informed about them, and if they occur, avoid potentially dangerous activities such as driving or operating machinery. [file #209]

### CBT, coaching and peer feedback
When choosing a psychologist, make sure they have appropriate training, qualifications and experience and are registered with a professional body. Also ask about their knowledge and track record with ADHD clients. For some people, a trusted friend "with a critical eye" who gives genuinely honest, objective feedback is enough to help make desired changes. Cognitive behavioural therapy, dialectical behaviour therapy, coaching and group coaching can help you start making life choices that help you manage ADHD [file #209].

### Late discovery and the strengths reframe ("Thriving with ADHD — good news!")
For many adults who learn late that they have ADHD, the discovery can bring both **relief and sadness** as they think about how life might have gone had they known earlier. The guide says criticising yourself or others, or blaming others for not knowing why you struggled, will not help. What matters is what YOU do NOW: this is your chance to learn to manage your ADHD and take responsibility for regulating it, so you can keep living, working and pursuing your goals [file #209].

Applying the strategies and changing lifestyle brings improved wellbeing and growing confidence in managing daily life. A positive, solution-focused attitude is learned and takes time. Be patient and kind to yourself, cultivate a sense of humour, and value the people and things that matter. Explaining to loved ones why some things are hard and what you are doing about it helps a great deal, so ask for understanding and support. But **responsibility for managing ADHD lies with you**: a specialist can offer medication, but lifestyle changes depend on you. The changes take time to become habits. You will still make mistakes and still struggle with some things, and you will find new ways to cope. Remind yourself you are doing your best, and celebrate achievements and progress [file #209].

**Strengths reframe:** once you manage ADHD successfully, you may see ways it can help you. Hyperactivity can be seen as energy, drive and enthusiasm. Impulsivity can show as decisiveness, a willingness to take risks and seize opportunities. Inattention and mind-wandering can be seen as an imagination that seeks ideas, inspiration and new experience, and as curiosity. ADHD does not define you; it explains how your unique mind works and how you interact with the world and the people in it [file #209].

### Why self-screening still makes sense (psychiatrist's view)
The channel *Где моё внимание?* (Where's my attention?) observes that most people who first suspect ADHD go and google tests. Every honestly made test ends by saying its results are not enough for a diagnosis and a specialist is needed. Is there any point taking them, then? Psychiatrist **Stepan Krasnoshchekov** says yes. Self-administered testing is called **screening**. It helps a person:
1. Suspect a disorder.
2. Assess their symptoms.
3. Understand whether they need to see a psychiatrist.
Tests can also be **a useful tool for self-reflection**, for analysing one's life by area and period [msg #255].

His key caveat: **if a person has symptoms and suffering but the test shows no ADHD, they should still see a specialist.** The specialist conducts a diagnostic interview, the main method of diagnosing ADHD. It can detect the syndrome even if the test missed it, or find something else that explains the problems [msg #255].

### Eight science-based ADHD screening tools
The post presents these as the most common science-oriented screening tests for ADHD. Each is described as follows [msg #255]:
1. **ASRS-5, the WHO screening questionnaire.** A scale for diagnosing ADHD in adults, developed jointly with the World Health Organization and researchers from New York and Harvard. It consists of 18 ADHD criteria from DSM-5. Available as a Russian online test and an English file.
2. **Wender Utah Rating Scale (WURS) for adult ADHD self-assessment.** Used to assess whether an adult had ADHD symptoms **in childhood**. It helps match childhood symptoms with adult ones, but only as far as the person remembers their childhood well (a stated limitation). Russian online test and English file.
3. **DIVA-5 diagnostic interview.** A structured diagnostic assessment designed specifically for ADHD in adults. To assess the person's experience on each of the 18 ADHD symptom criteria in childhood and adulthood, it gives a list of concrete, realistic examples of current and past behaviour. Russian file.
4. **Brown scale for executive functions and attention.** Assesses a broader, less obvious range of executive dysfunctions and pinpoints problem areas in **six domains: activation, attention, effort, emotional regulation, memory and action**. The channel's own Russian translation plus the English original.
5. **ADHD Rating Scale-IV with prompts (Шкала для оценки СДВГ у взрослых-IV с подсказками).** An 18-item scale based on DSM-5 criteria that helps assess ADHD symptom severity. The first 9 items assess inattention, the other 9 hyperactivity-impulsivity. Russian file.
6. **Russell Barkley's checklist** from his book on coping with adult ADHD («Совладание с СДВГ у взрослых»). Described as a scientifically grounded, valid screening for adult ADHD symptoms from one of the leading experts. It can help identify ADHD deficits and indicate the need for a full diagnostic interview with a psychiatrist. Russian file.
7. **Conners' Adult ADHD Rating Scales (CAARS–S:L).** Helps determine the presence and severity of adult ADHD symptoms. It has **eight areas**: (a) inattention/memory problems; (b) hyperactivity/restlessness; (c) impulsivity/emotional lability; (d) problems with self-concept; (e) DSM-5 inattentive symptoms; (f) DSM-5 hyperactive-impulsive symptoms; (g) DSM-5 total ADHD symptoms; (h) ADHD index. The channel's Russian translation plus the English original.
8. **ACE+ diagnostic interview for adult ADHD.** A semi-structured clinical interview that helps health professionals identify ADHD in adults. During it the psychiatrist assesses core ADHD symptoms in adulthood and childhood and the degree of their negative impact on the person's life. Russian file. [msg #255]

Note the distinction the list implies: items 3 (DIVA-5) and 8 (ACE+) are clinician-led interviews, not self-tests, while the others are rating scales or checklists [msg #255].

### Why diagnoses rise while symptoms do not (Swedish CATSS study)
An author of the channel *Аутизм и СДВГ в опыте и книжках* (Autism and ADHD in experience and books) shares a large study by researchers from the University of Gothenburg and Karolinska Institutet. Its finding: the number of people diagnosed with ADHD and autism grew enormously over two decades, while the number of people with the matching symptoms stayed stable [msg #337].

**Study design:** data from the Child and Adolescent Twin Study in Sweden (CATSS), in which parents answered questions about their children's ASD and ADHD symptoms by age 18. The sample was **9,870 people born 1993–2001**. ASD symptoms were assessed with **12 questions matching DSM-IV criteria**. ADHD symptoms were assessed with **17 items of the ABCL/18-59**, which measures attention and executive-function problems **but not hyperactivity** [msg #337].

**Results:**
- **Autism:** over the nine-year period, no increase in mean ASD symptom levels in the whole sample or separately in boys or girls. This agrees with an earlier study of roughly the same group, which found stable autism symptoms in nine-year-olds over a decade despite rising clinical diagnoses.
- **ADHD:** boys' symptom levels stayed stable. Girls showed a statistically significant though small rise: the share of girls reaching the **90th percentile** of ADHD symptoms grew on average **5.3% per year**, while boys' share stayed unchanged. The post's author says it is hard to draw firm conclusions, so this remains uncertain. She adds a hopeful speculation, explicitly her own: perhaps culture has begun to let girls express themselves a bit more and not internalise everything. [msg #337]

**Five hypotheses for why diagnoses rise but symptoms do not** (factors that "most likely" contribute):
1. **Broader understanding of diagnostic criteria:** more people now fall under the definitions of ASD and ADHD than before.
2. **Greater awareness:** parents, teachers and doctors recognise the signs better, so children are assessed and diagnosed more often.
3. **Diagnostic substitution:** people who used to receive other diagnoses (for example developmental delay or, say, BPD) now receive an ASD diagnosis.
4. **Lower diagnostic threshold:** current ASD and ADHD diagnoses are made on fewer symptoms than before (evidence cited: Arvidsson et al., 2018 for ASD; Kazda et al., 2023 for ADHD).
5. **Changed perception of problems:** parents today more often see ASD and ADHD symptoms as complicating their child's life, even at the same symptom severity. [msg #337]

**Conclusion:** the authors' overall conclusion is that talk of an autism or ADHD "epidemic" is unfounded. The post's author names the next open question: how to manage diagnosis and treatment when one diagnosis covers people with a huge range of problems and impairments [msg #337].

## Evidence and numbers

- ADHD prevalence: about 3% of the population (NHS England estimate, as cited in the guide) [file #209].
- Obesity: about 28% of adults with ADHD vs 16% without ADHD (Cortese et al., 2016 meta-analysis) [file #209].
- Restless legs syndrome: up to 44% of people with ADHD (Cortese et al., Sleep) [file #209].
- Sleep need: 7–9 hours a night for most working-age adults [file #209].
- Autism prevalence estimates: 0.2% in the late 1970s. By the early 2020s, 2.8% in the USA, and in Sweden 5.8% of adolescent boys and 4.0% of adolescent girls [msg #337].
- Mean age at ASD diagnosis rose from 9.6 years (1998) to 14.5 years (2018); the growth is most visible in adolescence and adulthood [msg #337].
- ADHD diagnosis among US children: 6.1% in 1997–98 to 14.2% in 2021–22. In Sweden in 2022, 10.5% of adolescent boys and 6.0% of girls had an ADHD diagnosis [msg #337].
- 80% of women are not correctly diagnosed with autism before 18 (claim by Nerdivergent; no study named in the post) [msg #233].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The Craft checklist is explicitly unofficial, one person's opinion, made by someone without a psychiatry or psychology degree; it does not replace DSM-V criteria, is not a diagnostic tool, does not cover everything, and produces false positives (some people fit and are not autistic). Even the scoring rule is hedged ("possibly… may be") [msg #149].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the posted cards cover sections A–H, J, an unlettered "Confusion" section and an optional executive-functioning section; there is no card labelled Section I. The "Confusion" section is probably Section I, but its letter is not shown on the card (it reads "Секция : Растерянность") [img #152].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: in the optional executive-functioning section, item 6 is missing (numbering jumps from 5 to 7) and the last item is unnumbered [img #150].
- (!) UNCLEAR: whether the "75–80% matches per section" rule applies to the optional executive-functioning section is not stated [msg #149, img #150].
- The checklist was written while Asperger's syndrome was still a DSM diagnosis, so some terminology is dated [msg #149].
- The specialists on the Pure Cognitions list may not diagnose autism or ADHD [msg #10].
- The ADHD screening test estimates risk only; it is not presented as a diagnosis [msg #10].
- The 18–25 discovery claim comes from a shared meme-tweet, not from cited research [img #73].

- **Conflict of interest:** the ADHD guide was funded by Takeda UK, a pharmaceutical company, whose specialists reviewed it; one of the recommended websites is Takeda-funded [file #209].
- **UK-specific route:** the diagnostic pathway (GP referral, NHS) applies only to the UK. The translator notes that elsewhere the procedure differs, for example going to a psychiatrist directly [file #209].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the transcription of the ASRS form does not show which items form Part A or which cells are shaded. The interpretation text mentions "Постоянно" (constantly), which is not on the five-point answer scale [file #209].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the predominantly inattentive subtype is described as involving difficulties with attention "or poor impulse control", which does not fit the subtype's name. This is likely a translation slip [file #209].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the "12 tips" for relationships appear as 16 items in the transcription with scrambled numbering [file #209].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: one of the six emotion-regulation methods is garbled in the transcription ("думать, ать свои эмоции социальносоциально приемлемыми способами") [file #209].
- Screening tests are not diagnostic. A negative result does not rule out ADHD, and the WURS depends on how well one remembers childhood [msg #255].
- In the Swedish study, the ADHD measure did not cover hyperactivity, and symptoms were parent-reported. The rise in girls' ADHD symptoms is small and, in the post author's words, uncertain.
