# Stimming: functions, types, stim toys and responding to harmful stims

## What this covers

This file covers a single forwarded post from the channel «Нейросеть» (Neuroset), which links to a Telegraph longread «стимминг» written by Юрий Каан (Yuri Kaan). The post credits editing to Элия and Дана, illustrations to Альт and design to Астер. The article defines stimming, explains why neurodivergent (especially autistic) people stim, gives examples across six sensory categories, covers stim toys and how to pick one, separates stimming from tics and from ADHD fidgeting, and gives three recommendations for when stimming becomes harmful. The source says this matters because stimming is a necessary self-regulation tool that is often misunderstood, mocked, punished or banned. [msg #60] [article #60]

## Core ideas

- **Everyone stims; neurodivergent people need to.** Stimming is not exclusively a neurodivergent trait. The difference is that neurodivergent people, especially autistic people, need it to get through certain moments in life more easily. [article #60]
- **Stimming has several functions.** It helps with self-regulation, emotional expression, communication and distraction. It can also be done purely for pleasant sensation (sensory seeking, sensory joy). See *Functions of stimming*. [article #60]
- **Stims don't have to look "nice".** Each person's stimming looks different, and acceptance should not depend on looking cute or socially acceptable. [article #60]
- **Stimming is defined by function, not by form.** The same action (such as jumping) is a stim only when it serves emotional expression, self-regulation or a similar purpose. [article #60]
- **Stimming can be harmful.** It can then be met with understanding, a calmer environment and safer substitutes, never with punishment. See *Three-step response to harmful stimming*. [article #60]

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Definition and functions of stimming
The article defines stimming as «повторяющиеся действия, включающие любые органы чувств, призванные помочь с саморегуляцией, выразить эмоции, коммуницировать или отвлечься» (*repetitive actions involving any of the senses, meant to help with self-regulation, to express emotions, to communicate or to distract oneself*). It names these functions: [article #60]

1. **Self-regulation and preventing meltdown.** Active stimming can stop sensory overload from escalating into a meltdown. The mechanism given is that comfortable, familiar actions can, in some cases, distract a person from the discomfort they are feeling. [article #60]
2. **Expressing emotions and communication.** Autistic people often find it hard to say in words what they feel, especially during strong emotions such as joy, anger or hurt. In those moments stimming can support their communication. The first illustration in the article shows this: a person clapping and flapping with joy when handed a gift. [article #60]
3. **Distraction.** Distraction is part of the definition. It is also the mechanism behind the anti-meltdown function, since familiar actions pull attention away from discomfort. [article #60]
4. **Sensory seeking.** Stimming can happen "without any particular reason", simply because the person wants a particular pleasant stimulation. The article says this is especially typical of autistic people because of their sensory seeking (сенсорный поиск): deliberately seeking sensory stimulation that feels comfortable to them. [article #60]
5. **Sensory joy.** Autistic people also experience sensory joy (сенсорная радость), when certain stimuli bring very positive emotions and sensations. Stimming may be done precisely to reach that state. [article #60]

### Six sensory categories of stims (examples)
The article says stimming can involve any of the five senses and look completely different in every person, and that it covers only the main examples. It groups them into six categories: [article #60]

1. **Visual (Визуальные):** watching spinners rotate, kaleidoscopes, lava lamps, flowing water, moving clouds, glittering rhinestones and colours pleasing to the eye. [article #60]
2. **Auditory (Звуковые):** repetitive music, listening to musical instruments, certain kinds of noise such as the sound of the sea, and sounds like rustling leaves or rain tapping on glass. [article #60]
3. **Tactile (Тактильные):** tapping, pop-its, slimes, soft toys, tight hugs, squeezing stim toys, biting one's lip, playing musical instruments (for example plucking guitar or ukulele strings), being in water, toe-walking and hand-flapping. [article #60]
4. **Gustatory/oral (Вкусовые/оральные):** chewing on something, repeating sounds, words and phrases, and eating particular foods (for example spicy or sweet). [article #60]
5. **Olfactory (Обонятельные):** inhaling particular smells, such as clean laundry or scented candles. [article #60]
6. **Vestibular (Вестибулярные):** rocking, spinning, pacing back and forth, swinging on swings and jumping on a trampoline. The third illustration shows a joyful girl jumping on a trampoline. [article #60]

The article adds that these actions are by no means always stimming. Plain jumping differs from stim-jumping in that the stim is needed «для выражения сильных эмоций, саморегуляции или другой похожей причины» (*to express strong emotions, for self-regulation or another similar reason*). [article #60]

### Stim toy selection criteria
Stim toys (стим игрушки) are objects created for the purpose of stimming. There are a great many of them. Adults use them too, not only children, because stimming as a whole is not an exclusively childhood experience. Popular examples named are tangles (танглы), stim bracelets (стим-браслеты) and chewable jewellery (жевательные украшения). Most are inexpensive and can be ordered online or found in shops. Objects that were not made as stim toys can also be used if they help the person stim. [article #60]

When choosing one, the article says to base the choice on three things: [article #60]
1. **Texture** of the toy. [article #60]
2. **Type of stimulation** it provides. [article #60]
3. **Convenience in different places.** Some toys are quiet and won't distract anyone, so they can be carried to school or work. Others may be too loud and/or too big to carry around. [article #60]

### Stimming vs tics vs fidgeting
**Stimming vs tics.** The article refers back to the channel's earlier article on tic disorders. It says stimming and tics are often confused but are easy to tell apart: [article #60]
- *Control:* stimming is usually voluntary («добровольно»), while tics are uncontrollable actions. [article #60]
- *Triggers:* stimming happens more often when a person is stressed, nervous or over-excited by their surroundings. Tics occur both in response to triggers and with no visible reason. [article #60]

**Stimming vs fidgeting.** According to experts the article cites (not named), actions that use senses other than the ones the main task needs can improve concentration in people with ADHD. The example given is listening to music while reading a textbook. These secondary actions, performed alongside a main task, are called fidgeting (фиджетинг). The summary distinction is that fidgeting is more typical of ADHD and helps with concentration, while stimming is a self-regulation tool. In some people the two overlap, so they can be hard to tell apart, but their main purpose differs. [article #60]

### Three-step response to harmful stimming
Stimming can be painful and cause physical harm. The article's examples of what someone might do during sensory overload or a meltdown are: hitting themselves on the head, pulling out hair, biting nails, scratching, rubbing or picking skin, screaming loudly, biting themselves, and banging their head against hard objects. The fourth illustration accompanies this section. In such cases it recommends: [article #60]

1. **Create a calm environment and remove or accommodate triggers.** Lower the stress level with a calm setting. Track what leads the person to self-injurious behaviour. Then try either to keep them out of those situations or to give them accommodations so they cope better. The example given is noise-cancelling headphones for people sensitive to loud sounds. [article #60]
2. **Never punish stimming, especially dangerous stimming.** The reasoning is that «Никто не будет причинять себе боль без веской причины, и если бы человек мог не стиммить таким образом, он бы этого и не делал» (*No one would hurt themselves without a good reason, and if the person could avoid stimming this way, they wouldn't do it*). The article therefore calls for understanding and support. [article #60]
3. **Find a replacement for destructive stimming.** Buy various stim toys to try. Look at examples of harmless stimming. Gradually try to learn to use them. When the dangerous stim happens again, remind the person of your practice sessions and, if needed, stim yourself. The stated mechanism is that many people instinctively copy behaviour they see, which can lead to safer stimming in the future. [article #60]

## Detailed insights

### Social perception of stimming and stim toys
- **Internet videos give a skewed picture.** Blog videos can make stimming seem always cute and broadly socially acceptable. In reality every neurodivergent person's behaviour differs, and «стимминг не должен выглядеть «привлекательно», чтобы быть принятым» (*stimming should not have to look "attractive" to be accepted*). [article #60]
- **Popularity trivialises stim toys.** Some stim toys become very popular with young people, including neurotypical children. They then often come to be seen as a silly amusement rather than a necessary self-regulation tool. [article #60]
- **Example: the fidget spinner bans.** When fidget spinners surged in popularity, dozens of schools in America banned them from classrooms entirely, because children threw them and distracted each other. The author's lesson: if stim toys were taken seriously, neurodivergent children who use them as intended would not have to lose them completely. [article #60]

### Closing position
The article concludes that stimming is an important way for autistic and other neurodivergent people to self-regulate. It says people should not be scolded for stimming or pushed to hide it, because it is a necessary tool of communication and self-expression. It adds that neurodivergent traits should not be mocked or stigmatised, and that understanding and mutual support should replace condemnation and rejection. [article #60]

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The "experts" behind the fidgeting/ADHD concentration claim are not named, and no study is cited. [article #60]
- The article hedges that the stimming/fidgeting line can blur in some people, and that stimming is "most often" (чаще всего) voluntary, not always. [article #60]
- The original spells «амовыражения» [sic] (for самовыражения, self-expression). [article #60]
- The article says stimming is "usually voluntary". Its harm-reduction advice also says that if the person could avoid stimming this way, they would not do it. (!) UNCLEAR: the source does not reconcile how voluntary harmful stims during a meltdown are. [article #60]
- The four article illustrations contain no text. Image 1 shows joyful hand-clapping when receiving a gift and image 3 shows jumping on a trampoline. The content of images 2 and 4 was not checked. [article #60]
