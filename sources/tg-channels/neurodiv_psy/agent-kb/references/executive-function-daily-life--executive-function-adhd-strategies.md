# Executive dysfunction, ADHD skills training and daily-life strategies

## What this covers

This part covers three things the channel (@ia_psy) taught: (1) CBT-based skills training for adults with ADHD, in particular the protocol by Steven Safren and colleagues — why it exists, how it is organized and which skills it trains; (2) a meme-post on executive dysfunction making people avoid even things they want to do; (3) a card series "СДВГ и деньги" (ADHD and money) explaining why people with ADHD overspend and listing concrete strategies for money management. According to the source this matters because ADHD affects many areas of life — attention, self-organization, emotions, relationships, household skills — and medication alone often does not give people the practical strategies they need; with money specifically, ADHD traits often leave people without savings or in debt [msg #54, #57, #173].

This part covers four bodies of material. First, a full Russian translation (forwarded from the channel "Чистые Когниции") of the ADHD Foundation UK guide titled "СДВГ у взрослых" (ADHD in adults), subtitled as a guide to living successfully with ADHD, which covers self-screening, how diagnosis works, DSM-5 criteria and subtypes, executive functions, five externalisation strategies, sleep, diet, exercise, daily stress reduction, relationships, medication, CBT/coaching and a strengths-based outlook [msg #209]. Second, the channel's cleaning hacks for neurodivergent people [msg #302]. Third, a two-part post on ADHD and motivation: why starting is the hardest step, why it happens, what motivates the ADHD brain, and how to use that [msg #323, #324]. Fourth, a two-part post on time blindness in ADHD and autism with what helps [msg #340, #341].

Why it matters according to the source: the guide says ADHD can affect physical and mental health and that myths still prevent people from understanding what ADHD is and how to cope; problems with self-motivation are reported by up to 94% of people with ADHD and are "одна из самых болезненных проблем" (one of the most painful problems); and time blindness causes chronic lateness and missed deadlines [msg #209, #323, #340].

## Core ideas

- **Medication is not enough on its own, and is not always available.** Even when medication reduces ADHD symptoms, many adults with ADHD still lack concrete skills and strategies for handling existing problems; skills training fills that gap [msg #54, #57].
- **The Safren protocol is a leading CBT skills-training format** — small groups, two ADHD-specialist facilitators, 8–12 weeks, training planning, organization, overwhelming tasks, attention, distractibility, procrastination, rest planning, environment organization and emotional regulation (full detail in *Frameworks*) [msg #55, #56, #57].
- **Improvement is real but non-linear.** Participants report coping better with daily tasks, but because ADHD is lifelong and involves several brain systems, there are ups and downs [msg #57].
- **Executive dysfunction does not discriminate between tasks.** People with ADHD may avoid not only boring or disliked tasks but also things that bring joy or are necessary [msg #58].
- **ADHD money problems have identifiable mechanisms** — impulsivity, poor organization, forgetfulness, weak non-verbal working memory (cannot vividly recall past consequences or picture future ones) and emotional dysregulation — and each mechanism has matching strategies [msg #173, #174].

- **Executive functions are the core of adult ADHD difficulties, and the remedy is to externalise them.** What does not work "inside" (working memory, motivation, sense of time, organisation) should be moved outside into notes, rewards, timers, visual systems and an arranged environment (see *Five strategies for executive-function difficulties*) [msg #209].
- **ADHD management is lifestyle plus support, not only medication.** The guide repeatedly says medication is one strategy among many; sleep, diet, exercise, daily stress reduction, relationships, CBT/coaching are all part of management, and change should be gradual but should start now [msg #209].
- **Starting is the hardest step.** For the ADHD brain the most effortful step is to start, to move a task "с мертвой точки" (off dead centre); once moving, continuing is easier (see *Boulder model*) [msg #323].
- **The ADHD brain is motivated by pressure, interest, competition, novelty and play, not by distant importance.** Because of dopamine/reward differences, working memory problems and time blindness, long-term goals and "important for the future" barely work; tools should make the task immediate, social, interesting or playful [msg #324].
- **Time blindness is a symptom, not a diagnosis, and it differs between ADHD and autism.** In ADHD it comes from unstable alertness, working memory and impulse inhibition; in autism from a different integration of time, rigidity and sensory factors. The remedy is to make time visible and measured [msg #340, #341].
- **No shame.** Both the cleaning post and the guide stress self-compassion: being unable to clean "не делает вас ужасным человеком" (does not make you a terrible person), and in the guide what matters is what you do now, not blaming yourself or others [msg #302, #209].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Safren CBT skills-training protocol for adults with ADHD
**What and why.** Skills training for adults with ADHD is offered for situations where medication is unavailable or insufficient. The channel explains that ADHD affects attention, self-organization, emotions and relationships, and household skills; that even after taking medication to reduce symptoms many adults with ADHD still do not have specific skills and strategies to deal effectively with their existing problems; and that medication is not always accessible — "многие препараты невозможно достать в России и некоторых других странах" (*many drugs cannot be obtained in Russia and some other countries*) — while for some people medication simply does not work. "В таких случаях на помощь приходит тренинг навыков." (*In such cases skills training comes to the rescue.*) [msg #54, #57] The card series opens with the title "Тренинг навыков для взрослых с СДВГ" (*Skills training for adults with ADHD*) [img #53].

**Origin.** One of the most popular such trainings is the training for adults developed by Steven Safren and colleagues within the cognitive-behavioural (CBT) approach. In building the protocol the authors relied on three sources: research results, their own clinical experience of working with people with ADHD, and the requests and feedback of people with ADHD themselves [msg #55, #57].

**Format.**
1. **Small groups.** Work usually takes place in small groups — "до 10 человек" (*up to 10 people*) per the card, "до 10-12 человек" (*up to 10–12 people*) per the text post (see *Caveats*) [msg #55, #57].
2. **Two facilitators.** Participants are accompanied by two facilitators who are specialists in working with ADHD [msg #55, #57].
3. **Duration 8–12 weeks.** Over 8–12 weeks the participants meet regularly to share experience with each other and learn specific skills — i.e. the group is both a learning space and a peer-exchange space [msg #55, #57].

**Skill modules trained** (the full list from the card, supplemented by the text post) [img #56, msg #57]:
1. **Organization and planning skills** (навыки организации и планирования) — the core difficulty the channel names for ADHD: organizing and planning one's life.
2. **Working with overwhelming tasks** (работа с подавляющими задачами) — tasks that feel too large or heavy to start.
3. **Managing multitasking activity** (управление многозадачной деятельностью).
4. **Choosing a suitable solution when none of the solutions is ideal** (выбор подходящего решения, когда ни одно из решений не идеально) — decision-making under imperfect options.
5. **Attention-switching and emotional-regulation skills** (навыки переключения внимания и эмоциональной регуляции); the text post adds work with mood problems (проблемы настроения).
6. **Organizing space / the environment** (организация пространства; "организовывать среду вокруг себя" — *organizing the environment around oneself*).
7. **Planning rest** (планирование отдыха).
8. **Managing distractibility** (управление отвлекаемостью), together with managing attention in general.
9. **Managing procrastination** (управление прокрастинацией).
(!) INCOMPLETE: the source names the modules but does not describe the techniques taught within each one [img #56, msg #57].

**Outcome.** Participants report that after skills training they cope better with everyday tasks and responsibilities. However, the author warns that ADHD is a complex condition that involves several parts of the brain and accompanies a person throughout life, so "улучшения не всегда линейны: бывают взлеты и падения" (*improvements are not always linear: there are ups and downs*) [msg #57].

### ADHD money-management strategies (card series "СДВГ и деньги")
**Purpose / why spending is hard with ADHD.** The cover card asks "Почему мы столько тратим и как остановиться?" (*Why do we spend so much and how do we stop?*) [img #173]. The author explains the mechanisms behind poor financial planning and unwise spending in ADHD [msg #173, #174]:
- **Impulsivity, difficulties with organization, forgetfulness and other problems** mean people with ADHD more often end up without savings or even in debt — not to mention the difficulties of getting an education and keeping a job, which also affect income.
- **Weak non-verbal working memory** — the working memory responsible for visual and any other non-speech images. Because of it a person with ADHD may find it hard to remember what their impulsive purchases led to in the past and to clearly imagine the consequences that may follow if they spend all their savings right now.
- **Emotional dysregulation** — people with ADHD often suffer from it, and many unwise purchases are made under the influence of various emotions.
The strategies below are grouped into four blocks, each addressing these mechanisms.

**Block 1 — Visualization and external cues (визуализация и внешние подсказки)** — compensates for weak non-verbal working memory [img #175]:
1. **Replay the past on an imagined screen.** Recall situations in which it is hard for you to look ahead, and imagine some visual device — a TV, a monitor or the like. Try to picture a similar situation from the past as vividly and in as much detail as possible, and what it led to. The aim is to make the consequences of past impulsive spending concrete and visible.
2. **Use tangible visual and physical cues** that help fight the urge to spend: keep a photo of the desired goal (what you are saving for), stick reminder stickers, or, conversely, find a picture that shows "не надо" (*don't do it*).

**Block 2 — Self-regulation (саморегуляция)** — addresses emotional spending [img #176]:
1. **Track emotions and desires** with different tools, for example a mood diary.
2. **Act when you notice the danger state.** If you realize that right now your mood is gone and the desire to spend money is too strong, you can: seek support from close people; use emotional-regulation exercises (for example breathing techniques); talk to yourself and try to talk yourself out of it.
3. **Raise stress tolerance in general:** sleep properly, eat properly, do physical exercise, limit caffeine, alcohol and other substances.

**Block 3 — Asking for help is not shameful (искать помощи не стыдно)** [img #177]:
1. **Involve a close person in budgeting.** Perhaps someone close can help you draw up a budget (all monthly expenses and payments), or at least sit next to you while you do it, and remind you to pay bills. (Sitting next to someone while they do a task is the body-doubling idea named in the topic scope; the card describes it without using that term.)
2. **Automate payments.** Most payments can now be automated in online-banking apps, so they do not depend on memory.
3. **Look for free resources if you have financial problems:** debt restructuring, credit counselling, budgeting help. The card itself ends this list with "и т.д." (an open-ended "etcetera"), so these three are the only ones the source names.

**Block 4 — Limiting spending and saving (ограничение расходов и сбережения)** [img #178]:
1. **Save at least 10% of income** if possible. Create a forced-saving system: have the online-banking app debit a fixed amount every month or whenever the salary arrives.
2. **Remove easy access to your card:** turn off autofill and remove "default" cards in online shops, so that buying requires extra steps.
3. **Do not lend money to anyone** — and if you did lend, consider it a gift from you.
4. **Unless absolutely necessary, decline offers to open a credit card**, and all the more so to take a microloan (микрозайм).

### Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale (ASRS) self-screening
Purpose: a self-screening for adults who wonder whether they have ADHD; the guide's first part is aimed at such readers [msg #209]. Instructions: fill in parts A and B, marking the most fitting answer with a cross. Answer options: Никогда (never), Редко (rarely), Иногда (sometimes), Часто (often), Очень часто (very often). Scoring: if four or more marks fall into the blue cells of part A, you have signs characteristic of ADHD and further assessment is needed; part B can give additional information and context [file #209].
(!) INCOMPLETE: the transcription does not show which cells are blue or exactly which questions belong to part A vs part B, so the scoring cannot be fully reproduced from the source.

The 18 questions ("How often…") [file #209]:
1. Do you have trouble wrapping up the final details of a project once the challenging parts are done?
2. Do you have difficulty getting things in order when a task requires organisation?
3. Do you have problems remembering appointments or obligations?
4. When a task requires a lot of thought, do you avoid or delay getting started?
5. Do you fidget or squirm with hands or feet when you have to sit for a long time?
6. Do you feel overly active and compelled to do things, as if driven by a motor inside?
7. Do you make careless mistakes when working on a boring or difficult project?
8. Do you have difficulty keeping attention during boring or repetitive work?
9. Do you have difficulty concentrating on what people say to you, even when they speak to you directly?
10. Do you misplace or have trouble finding things at home or at work?
11. Are you distracted by activity or noise around you?
12. Do you leave your seat in meetings or other situations where you are expected to remain seated?
13. Do you feel restless or fidgety?
14. Do you have difficulty unwinding and relaxing when you have free time?
15. Do you feel you talk too much in social situations?
16. In conversation, do you finish other people's sentences before they can finish them?
17. Do you have difficulty waiting your turn when turn-taking is required?
18. Do you interrupt others when they are busy?

Interpretation the guide gives: if you answered mostly "Часто", "Очень часто" or "Постоянно" (often, very often, constantly), take note — but the guide doubts anyone answers "never" to any question. So the answer to whether you have ADHD lies in how consistently and regularly inattention, impulsivity and hyperactivity show up in your life, and whether they currently cause difficulties. If you still think you may have ADHD, the guide suggests getting assessed [file #209].
(!) CONTRADICTION: the interpretation page mentions an answer "Постоянно" (constantly) that is not among the five answer options on the scale.

What separates ADHD from ordinary inattention: everyone is sometimes inattentive, hyperactive and impulsive, and physical and mental conditions (and stress) can produce similar symptoms — trouble concentrating, forgetfulness, impulsivity, restlessness. Simply put, in ADHD these characteristics are more pronounced and have been present since childhood; ADHD can last a lifetime and its manifestations can change with age [file #209].

Reasons why diagnosis can be useful (four) [file #209]:
1. You can explore what you do well, which life areas may be hard, and develop self-help strategies.
2. You clarify whether current difficulties are caused by ADHD and not by another condition.
3. You learn whether you may have ADHD together with another disorder.
4. You can find out whether there are ADHD treatments that may suit you.

### Preparing for an ADHD assessment
Purpose: the route to diagnosis; the guide marks this section as relevant only for the UK, and a translator's footnote says in other countries the order may differ, for example going to a psychiatrist [file #209].
1. **See your GP (терапевт) and ask for a referral for ADHD assessment.** Be as informed about ADHD as possible beforehand. Be ready to justify why you think you have ADHD, perhaps referring to signs from the ASRS, explaining how it affects your life and discussing your childhood and adolescent experience [file #209].
2. **The GP rules out physiological causes** and may use their own screening tools at this stage; the GP knows who in the local NHS service should refer you. The GP cannot diagnose ADHD and cannot prescribe medication without recommendations from a mental-health specialist trained in ADHD. Some people with ADHD have other mental-health problems that also need to be understood and treated by a specialist [file #209].
3. **Before meeting the specialist, think about:**
   - Your medical history, including any difficulties and treatment for physical and mental health; bring records if you have them. Do not worry if you have none — the specialist can learn a lot from a conversation and history-taking.
   - Your experience at school and university.
   - If comfortable, talk to trusted friends and family, share what you learned about ADHD and ask their opinion; with your permission the psychiatrist may want information from them as part of assessment, so raise it early.
   - ADHD can run in families, so find out whether there are family cases of ADHD and/or specific learning difficulties or mental-health problems [file #209].
4. **Assessment itself** explores your development, mental health and medical history, so the psychiatrist understands ADHD's impact on your life and can recommend support. The specialist then decides whether your symptoms meet clinical criteria — most likely DSM-5 [file #209].
5. **After diagnosis you can begin to explore** five things: the advantages of your strengths; support at home and at work; coping skills; medication to ease ADHD symptoms; coaching or therapeutic support. If you decide not to be formally assessed, or are waiting, you can still explore all of these yourself except medication [file #209].

### DSM-5 ADHD criteria and subtypes
DSM-5 lists eighteen symptoms: nine in "Inattention" and nine in "Hyperactivity or impulsivity" [file #209].

Inattention (each "often"):
1. Fails to give close attention to details or makes careless mistakes at work — overlooks or misses details, work is inaccurate.
2. Has difficulty sustaining attention in tasks, e.g. during lectures, conversations or lengthy reading.
3. Does not seem to listen when spoken to directly, even with no obvious distraction.
4. Does not follow through on instructions and fails to finish work duties — starts something, loses focus and gets sidetracked.
5. Has difficulty organising tasks and activities — sequential tasks, keeping materials/belongings in order, misses deadlines, manages time poorly.
6. Avoids or dislikes tasks requiring sustained mental effort, e.g. preparing reports or filling in forms.
7. Loses things necessary for tasks or activities, e.g. wallet, purse, phone, glasses.
8. Is easily distracted by extraneous stimuli; in adults this can be unrelated thoughts.
9. Is forgetful in daily activities, e.g. returning calls, paying bills, making appointments [file #209].

Hyperactivity + impulsivity (each "often"):
1. Fidgets or taps hands and feet or squirms in the seat.
2. Leaves the seat where remaining seated is expected, e.g. in the office or in meetings.
3. Feels restless.
4. Rarely able to engage in activities quietly.
5. Is "on the go", acting as if "driven by a motor"; cannot sit still or feel comfortable still for long, e.g. at meetings or restaurants.
6. Talks excessively.
7. Blurts out an answer before the question is finished, e.g. cannot wait for their turn in conversation.
8. Has difficulty waiting, e.g. standing in a queue.
9. Interrupts or intrudes on others — butts into what others are doing or takes over [file #209].

Three DSM-5 subtypes discussed if the specialist finds enough symptoms [file #209]:
1. **Combined type** — you meet criteria in both the inattention and the hyperactivity/impulsivity groups.
2. **Predominantly inattentive type** — described as people who mainly have difficulty with attention or poor impulse control; according to the guide it is most common in adolescents and adults. (!) UNCLEAR: the translation mentions "плохой контроль импульсов" (poor impulse control) in the inattentive subtype, which seems inconsistent with the subtype's name.
3. **Predominantly hyperactive type** — you do not have enough inattention symptoms for the combined type; the main difficulties are in hyperactivity and impulsivity.

### Executive function components map
The guide defines executive function as a set of cognitive skills everyone has that help us plan, organise ourselves and use goal-directed behaviour to do everything we need in life; they help us control attention, impulses and emotions that may stop us from starting and/or finishing these actions. We use them daily to learn, work and manage everyday life; when they are impaired it can be hard to focus, follow instructions and cope with emotions [file #209]. The eight components:
1. **Гибкое мышление** (flexible thinking) — ability to adapt to the unexpected.
2. **Эмоциональный контроль** (emotional control) — ability to describe and regulate feelings.
3. **Контроль импульсов** (impulse control) — thinking before acting.
4. **Организованность** (organisation) — keeping track of things mentally and physically.
5. **Рабочая память** (working memory) — holding information in mind.
6. **Самоконтроль** (self-monitoring) — ability to track one's own actions.
7. **Планирование и расстановка приоритетов** (planning and prioritising) — can identify a goal and make a plan to reach it.
8. **Инициация выполнения задач** (task initiation) — can take action to get started [file #209].

**Working memory**, called one of the most important executive functions, is a system for temporarily holding and managing information needed for complex thinking — learning, reasoning, understanding; it helps choose and launch processing such as retrieving relevant information from long-term memory. Two types:
- **Non-verbal working memory** develops in childhood and lets us hold in mind what we see and hear around us. Through it we learn to imitate others, anticipate consequences, develop self-awareness, manage time and delay gratification.
- **Verbal working memory** is the ability to talk to oneself. When it develops in children they learn self-control and problem-solving; it helps us make plans, follow rules, think rationally, and plays an important role in moral judgement.
ADHD can cause problems in both [file #209].

**Emotional self-regulation**, "another central executive function": emotions arise whether we want them or not and can drive behaviour; repeated triggers produce strong sadness, loss, disappointment, anger or joy — e.g. reactions to criticism, humiliation, unmet needs, or strong happiness when expectations are met [file #209].

### Six ways working memory helps regulate emotions
Verbal and non-verbal working memory help regulate emotions in these ways [file #209]:
1. Using imagination to be less angry or sad.
2. Soothing ourselves to reduce the impact of strong emotions.

### Five strategies for executive-function difficulties
Purpose: the guide's core self-help toolkit for executive dysfunction. The common logic is externalisation — move the function into physical form [file #209].
1. **Вынести во вне то, что не выполняется внутри (externalise what does not work inside).** What: reduce the amount of information held in your head. How: put key information into a physical, accessible form — e.g. carry a notebook at work, or use the phone's Notes and regularly and immediately write down important information or instructions received. Why: it becomes your external working memory, so always keep it with you. Extension: these notes can then be expanded into daily goals or a long-term work plan [file #209].
2. **Тренировать внешнюю мотивацию (train external motivation).** Create regularly recurring forms of external motivation: for a long task, break it into smaller parts or time blocks and reward yourself after each part or block — it can be as simple as a cup of coffee or listening to music [file #209].
3. **Экстернализация времени (externalise time).** Make time more tangible with timers, clocks, computer or phone reminders. The more clearly you perceive time passing via physical reminders, the more it helps you manage time effectively [file #209].
4. **Экстернализация организации (externalise organisation).** Create visual systems that work for you: planners, calendars, card files, daily to-do lists. Use a highlighter to mark key information in documents. Keep a diary where you write anticipated difficulties as well as tasks to be done. Caveat: there are many systems, so be ready to change your organisational tools if they do not suit you [file #209].
5. **Организуйте среду вокруг себя (organise the environment around you).** Ask how distracting your surroundings, especially the workplace, are and whether you can change that. Options: move your desk away from the window or from where colleagues gather and chat; set up blocking on computer or phone so irrelevant information does not appear on screen; put the phone on silent or to voicemail and plan to check it only at set times of day; consider noise-cancelling headphones or screens around your workspace [file #209].

### Four ideas for better sleep
Context: people with ADHD very often have sleep difficulties; most working-age adults need 7–9 hours a night. Poor sleep harms energy, mood, performance and enjoyment of life. People with ADHD sometimes struggle to keep a regular sleep schedule, are easily distracted and struggle to switch off in bed; a head still churning with the day's events or tomorrow's thoughts makes falling asleep harder. Contributors: ADHD medication, especially stimulants, and late-evening caffeine (tea, coffee, fizzy drinks, chocolate); mental-health difficulties such as anxiety, depression or substance misuse combined with ADHD also hamper falling asleep and sleep quality. First, if you have long-term sleep problems or recognise signs of a sleep disorder, tell your doctor and ADHD clinic (in other countries, for example, a psychiatrist); if you take ADHD medication, discuss how it may affect sleep and whether to change it. Self-help steps are sometimes called sleep hygiene (гигиена сна) [file #209].
1. **Keep a sleep routine.** Successful bedtime depends on consistency: go to bed at the same time every night including weekends and get up at the same time each morning, even if you do not feel tired at bedtime. In the evening try relaxing activities — a bath, reading, listening to audio. Why a bath works: it raises body temperature, and after you get out the temperature falls, which supplements the physiological cooling that should happen as the body falls asleep [file #209].
2. **Do not force yourself to sleep.** Instead of lying awake worrying or ruminating, get up and do something relaxing; return to bed when tired again. Keep a notebook by the bed and write down what worries you — knowing you will not forget it tomorrow helps you feel calmer. Try meditation or breathing exercises before bed; a popular one is the 4-7-8 technique by Dr Andrew Weil: inhale for 4 seconds, hold for 7, exhale for 8, repeat several times. Plan the evening so you think only of good things in bed, e.g. three things you were grateful for today — the more positive the thoughts, the less stress right before sleep [file #209].
3. **Create a bedroom environment conducive to sleep.** Audit the bedroom for distractions: Is it too hot or cold? Is there outside noise and can it be reduced? Is the room dark enough, the light dimmed (blackout curtains or blinds are a good purchase)? Are the walls in muted, dark tones? Are your things put away out of sight? Is your sleepwear comfortable or does it raise body temperature? Does your mattress help — it should be firm enough to support the body evenly, but too firm makes you feel you are perched on top rather than lying comfortably? How many screens are in the bedroom and can they be removed? [file #209]
4. **Prepare your body for sleep during the day.** Schedule regular exercise so you feel more tired in the evening, but not too close to bedtime, as it may have the opposite effect. Do not use alcohol to fall asleep — although sedating, it can seriously harm sleep quality and quantity; a lot of alcohol before bed makes sleep lighter and more restless. Avoid caffeine less than eight hours before bed; the stimulant effect varies by person but leave enough time between the last caffeine and bedtime. Avoid eating late and plan the evening meal earlier. Try relaxing practices such as aromatherapy or massage. Schedule time to reflect on the past day and think about tomorrow; to-do lists or diaries help offload thoughts before sleep [file #209].

Sleep background in the guide: when we fall asleep body temperature drops, heart rate slows and brain activity changes. We enter the first, lightest NREM stage, from which we are easily woken; then two more NREM stages until REM, the stage where we dream. Poor sleep lowers concentration, increases working-memory difficulties, tendency to procrastinate and lack of motivation, especially at work; in ADHD poor sleep worsens existing difficulties [file #209].

Sleep disorders that may be linked to ADHD [file #209]:
- **Sleep apnoea** — breathing stops during the night, causing fragmented sleep and tiredness on waking; studies found it more common in people with ADHD than in the general population; loud snoring can be a sign.
- **Circadian rhythm sleep disorders** — the body runs on a 24-hour cycle tuned to light and dark (circadian rhythm); at dark the hormone melatonin is released to signal preparation for sleep. With a circadian disorder the body may be out of sync and not release melatonin at the right time, so falling asleep is harder. Bright light in the bedroom or artificial blue light from laptops, tablets or phones can contribute.
- **Restless legs syndrome (RLS)** — strong urge to move the legs in bed and during sleep, constant discomfort in the limbs, itching, aching, pulling or throbbing sensations in the legs; research found it may affect up to 44% of people with ADHD.

### Weekly diet self-check and five ways to improve daily eating
Background recommendations the guide gives (NHS England healthy eating guide, last revised 2019): eat 5 portions of varied fruit and vegetables daily — more than a third of daily food. Starchy foods should also be a third: include starchy carbohydrates (potatoes, bread, rice, pasta) in every meal, preferably high-fibre versions such as wholegrain or potatoes with skin; they are a good energy source. Dairy or alternatives (soy drinks, lower-fat cheese, skimmed milk) are important sources of protein and calcium; choose low fat and low sugar; two to three portions a day provide all the calcium needed. Vegans or the lactose intolerant can get calcium from green leafy vegetables, broccoli and oranges, and calcium-fortified soy milk, rice drinks or yogurts. Good protein sources: fish, eggs or meat; pulses, lentils or beans are effective meat alternatives with less fat and more fibre. Two portions of fish a week, ideally oily; if you do not eat fish, nuts and seeds can supply omega-3, or consider supplements. Eat less of foods high in fat, salt and sugar (chocolate, cakes, biscuits, sugary soft drinks, butter). Drink plenty of fluid — 6 to 8 glasses a day; healthier options are water, low-fat dairy, low- or no-sugar drinks including tea and coffee. Women are recommended about 2,000 calories a day, men about 2,500 [file #209].

Self-check — over the last week did you (yes/no) [file #209]:
1. Eat five portions of fruit and vegetables every day?
2. Include starchy carbohydrates in every meal?
3. Eat two to three portions of dairy every day?
4. Eat two portions of fish or alternatives every week?

If "yes" — you are doing great; if "no", the guide moves on to how ADHD affects eating.

Factors that may link ADHD and eating habits [file #209]:
- Impulsive eating without regard to type of food or calories.
- Skipping meals or forgetting to eat, leading to stronger hunger and possibly overeating.
- ADHD medication may suppress appetite, leading to undereating in the day and overeating when the medication wears off in the evening or at night.
- Difficulties planning and structuring time, buying ingredients and cooking, leading to overuse of fast food or high-calorie snacks.
- Feelings of anxiety, depression or boredom.
- Difficulty following diet plans, especially those requiring preparation and study, record-keeping, nutrition knowledge and cooking.
- Sensory difficulties can lead to picky eating.

Mechanism: inattention and impulsivity are core ADHD features and can affect diet. People with ADHD may overeat, snack excessively between meals, binge or eat late because of impulse-control difficulties, low mindfulness while eating and poor planning; these traits are also linked to preferring high-carbohydrate foods or foods that give short-term satisfaction [file #209].

How to improve daily eating [file #209]:
1. Plan several mini-meals through the day — this can reduce the restlessness that may lead to impulsive overeating.
2. Create a healthy "food environment" at home, e.g. do not buy high-sugar, high-calorie snacks.
3. Remember that results of changing diet are not immediate — this helps you stick with plans and not give up too easily or quickly.
4. Replace high-calorie snacks with a regularly restocked supply of nutritious snacks needing little preparation: boiled eggs, yogurt, apples or oranges.
5. Plan weekly grocery shopping on a fixed day each week, then set aside time to cook healthy meals that can be frozen and reheated during the week. Do not shop hungry.

### Five tips to start exercising
Benefits for people with ADHD listed by the guide: helps keep the body healthy; improves impulse control and working memory; improves executive function; reduces stress and anxiety; exercise can stimulate release of neurotransmitters such as dopamine, which affects the ability to focus and concentrate. Generally, regular exercise strengthens the heart and improves circulation, increases blood flow and lowers blood pressure, raises oxygen levels and helps lower the risk of heart disease (high cholesterol, coronary heart disease, heart attack); it also supports psychological health and sound sleep. NHS England recommends adults 19–64 do some physical activity every day: on average at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity a week, about 30 minutes a day five days a week, with strengthening exercises working all major muscle groups (legs, hips, back, chest, shoulders and arms) on at least two days. Level and intensity should match your individual fitness; if in doubt, limited in ability, pregnant or recently given birth, consult a doctor first [file #209].

The five tips [file #209]:
1. **Be ready to experiment** to find the activity that suits you; if some options do not work, do not quit altogether — switch to another kind.
2. **Find a workout buddy** — a friend helps you keep to schedule.
3. **Make a schedule**: find a convenient time of day and remove obstacles, e.g. lay out workout clothes the night before or keep a sports bag in the car.
4. **Vary your routine** so you do not lose interest or focus halfway: e.g. interval training in the gym alternating short bursts of running or cycling with short strength sessions, or alternating swimming and running on different days.
5. **Reward yourself**, especially when starting: immediate reward can be the deciding factor in persistence. Pick something you usually look forward to and do not allow it until after the workout, e.g. a hot bath.

### Daily stress-reduction strategies
The guide says all these difficulties can make life with ADHD stressful; relationships, money, career, budgeting and force majeure can seriously disrupt life, routine and the structure we depend on. It strongly recommends caring for your body "daily — yes, every day", because physical health affects mental health just as mental affects physical. Method: start with one strategy, then gradually add more; not all will suit you; be patient and "train" your mind to build these daily habits. Expected results: lower likelihood of becoming irritable, impatient or short-tempered; better ability to focus, concentrate, motivate yourself and feel in control of your life whatever is happening around you; better mood and coping with depression [file #209].

The strategies [file #209]:
1. **Brisk walking, swimming, jogging or cycling** — also reduce anxiety, lift mood and improve motivation.
2. **Yoga** — a well-established stress reducer that lets you "listen" to what your body says it needs; many good online materials; you can practise 10 minutes a day.
3. **Hobbies and interests** — hyperfocus is a product of the "нервной системы, основанной на интересе" (interest-based nervous system): many people with ADHD can concentrate for long periods on something that truly interests them, though life also requires attending to everyday matters. A hobby you enjoy is very relaxing and energising — light (needlework, reading) or active (sport, community interests) — giving you "your time" aimed purely at wellbeing and enjoying life.
4. **Meditation** — the guide advises practising deep breathing for several months before starting meditation; meditation need not mean sitting still — you can practise mindful walking at a slow, smooth pace.

6. **Other strategies** — think what really helps you relax: a bath or massage; reading or audiobooks; creative activities, art, theatre; volunteering or working on causes you care about, e.g. climate change, elderly care, animal protection, local charity events.

### Boulder model of task initiation and four hard-to-start task types
Purpose: explains why starting is the hardest part for the ADHD brain [msg #323]. The channel's image: imagine you must roll a huge boulder from one place to another. Moving it from rest takes a great deal of effort, but once it rolls, continuing is easier. Sometimes a task is so interesting that the boulder seems to fly downhill by itself; if the task is too heavy, it is as if we push it uphill the whole way. The hardest and most energy-consuming step for the ADHD brain is "начать, сдвинуть дело “с мертвой точки”" (to start, to get the matter off dead centre). The felt consequence: the brain generates many ideas and tasks, things pile up, anxiety grows, yet starting remains impossible [msg #323].

Starting is especially hard when the task is [msg #323]:
1. **Boring or routine** — e.g. washing windows or doing homework.
2. **Too big or stretched over time** — writing a thesis, training before a hike.
3. **Almost finished** — a ready text or design that only needs edits.
4. **Evokes unpleasant emotions** — something we already failed at before.

### Why motivation is hard in ADHD (three causes)
[msg #324]
1. **Dopamine and the reward system.** In ADHD the brain needs more dopamine to feel the desire to act. So long-term goals, delayed rewards and abstract "this is important for the future" almost do not work — they do not stimulate the brain enough to act right now.
2. **Working memory problems and time blindness.** People with ADHD find it hard to hold past experience and use it for the future — e.g. hard to recall how convenient it is when lunch for work is prepared in advance. Tomorrow, a month or a year all feel equally "far", so everything is postponed "для будущего меня" (for future me).
3. **Executive functions.** Motivation often rests on emotion regulation and planning; when these work unstably, sustaining inner drive without external support becomes almost impossible.

### What motivates the ADHD brain
[msg #324]
1. **Pressure.** To cope with stress the nervous system releases dopamine and adrenaline; dopamine then helps the under-stimulated brain focus on the task. Three kinds:
   - **Interpersonal** — accountability to people whose expectations matter (e.g. a boss or academic supervisor).
   - **Time** — the deadline is tomorrow and completion really will be checked.
   - **Financial** — fines and debts, nothing to feed the family; the channel notes this works more weakly.
2. **Interest or passion.** A favourite activity can switch on hyperfocus, but remove personal interest and the process "freezes". Interesting things release much-needed dopamine — which is why we can do interesting things for hours but cannot wash the dishes.
3. **Competition or challenge.** For some people, competing with others shifts attention from how hard it is to start to the wish to win.
4. **Novelty.** New tasks, projects and ideas give a dopamine surge, but novelty fades quickly and new stimuli must be sought.
5. **Humour and play.** Fun, pleasant tasks motivate better and are easier; adding a game element or humour makes the task "живой" (alive) and attractive.

### Eight ways to use knowledge of ADHD motivation
[msg #324]
1. **Promise yourself a short stretch** (a couple of minutes) of work. This lowers the tension of initiation because you only need to endure a little. Example from the author: promise to fold laundry while one song plays; then the brain may get drawn in.
2. **Break tasks into small steps** — same principle: easier to talk yourself into small tasks than one huge one.
3. **Add a game element or challenge**, e.g. compete with friends.
4. **Make yourself accountable to others** — a promise to a friend or colleague often works better than a promise to yourself; let others set you a deadline and actually ask about progress.
5. **Try body doubling**: work alongside another person, even online; it reduces procrastination and helps you stay in the process.
6. **Add interests and values to the task** — e.g. not just studying how the heart functions, but doing it to write a fanfic about cardiologists or to help loved ones avoid a heart attack.
7. **Remember working-memory limits and use visual reminders**: notes, pictures, trackers.
8. **Remind yourself of the result awaiting you and the emotions** you will feel — pride, relief, joy. Usually people remember stories of success and failure and are motivated by them; people with ADHD have to remind themselves additionally.

### Cleaning hacks not invented by neurotypicals

5. **Give things clear places.** It matters to understand why and where you will put socks or plates, otherwise they will end up anywhere each time. ### Time blindness: definition, manifestations, causes and aids
**Definition.** Also called "временнУю слепоту", "слепоту ко времени" or "временнУю агнозию" — difficulty managing time, understanding how fast it passes and how long a task may take. The person relies on external clocks and timers rather than an inner sense of time. "Это не отдельный диагноз" (it is not a separate diagnosis) but a symptom of ADHD and some other neurodivergences. Typical experiences: you look at the clock — plenty of time; you get distracted for a couple of moments and you are late again; you sit down for a "20-minute task" and get up six hours later. Not only people with ADHD experience it — people with other neurodivergences do too [msg #340].

**In ADHD (Marx et al., 2021, as cited by the channel):** [msg #341]
1. Hard to accurately estimate short intervals (e.g. "count 30 seconds").
2. Tendency to overestimate long intervals (it seems 30 minutes passed when only 10 did).
3. Losing the sense of time when absorbed.
4. Difficulty estimating how long a task will take, leading to lateness and deadline problems.
5. Difficulties planning the day and following routines (sleep, eating, regular tasks).
Why: in ADHD the brain finds it harder to maintain a stable level of attention and "собранности" (alertness), with working-memory and impulse-inhibition difficulties. When attention "drops out" or, conversely, hyperfocus sets in, the brain tracks time worse; impulsivity also prevents stopping to estimate in advance how long a task will really take [msg #341].

**In autism (Casassus et al., 2019, as cited by the channel):** less clear-cut; manifestations vary greatly between people [msg #341].
1. Some feel time very precisely, others easily get lost in it.
2. It may be hard to feel how much time has passed even when knowing the number ("15 minutes passed" but no inner sense).
3. "Now", "in 3 hours", "tomorrow" feel like abstract concepts and numbers.
4. Hard to keep schedules, start and finish things on time.
5. Getting stuck between tasks (e.g. finished cleaning and cannot start cooking).
6. Under sensory or emotional overload time may seem too fast or too slow.
Why: for many autistic people time feels less "intuitive"; the autistic brain literally integrates time differently. Working-memory features, difficulty switching between tasks (cognitive rigidity) and sensory features play a role; the brain does not always give a clear inner signal like "10 minutes passed", so estimates are less accurate. Stress, sudden changes of plan and sensory overload can "compress" or "stretch" subjective time, making the sense even less reliable [msg #341].

**What helps (seven aids)** [msg #341]:
1. **Measure the real durations of tasks** (how long I eat, get ready, walk to work) to understand your own time patterns.
2. **Make time more "visible"**: timers (visual and ordinary), clocks in several rooms, hourglasses, the Pomodoro method.
3. **Planners, apps and visual schedules** that show when and how long a task takes — Google Calendar, TimeBloc, Sectograph, a notebook, a board.
4. **Alarms and reminders**: "start now", "finish now", "get ready for the next task".
5. **Divide tasks into small chunks** (I wash dishes 10 minutes, read 15 minutes).
6. **If short on time, avoid highly stimulating activities** (e.g. games before bed).
7. **Self-care, rest and emotion regulation** improve attentiveness and help track time better.

### The guide's framing of ADHD
The foreword by Dr Tony Lloyd says the booklet aims to give basic knowledge and practical strategies for living successfully with ADHD, and is also for those who first wonder whether they have ADHD and are deciding whether to seek formal diagnosis. Despite much UK research and online information, myths and misconceptions persist and stop people understanding what ADHD is, how it affects them and how to cope. ADHD can create problems in daily life, but there are many examples of ADHD strengths when we understand it and learn to cope. The guide proposes an inspiring, supportive, strengths-based approach that helps you recognise and realise your potential and find solutions where you struggle. Signature line: "СДВГ не определяет вас, это призма, через которую вы воспринимаете мир, в котором мы живем" (ADHD does not define you; it is the prism through which you perceive the world we live in) [file #209].

Definition and prevalence as given: ADHD is a neurological condition — attention deficit hyperactivity disorder; NHS England estimates it occurs in 3% of the population. It is characterised by three main features — inattention, hyperactivity and impulsivity — but many doctors also recognise difficulties with emotional regulation and with "executive functions" as characteristic [file #209].

How ADHD affects you individually: living with ADHD can be a serious source of worry; daily lifestyle changes aimed at reducing stressors are an integral part of living successfully with ADHD; change should be gradual but start now. Medication helps many, but managing ADHD is more than taking medication. Every person with ADHD is unique — a combination of genetics and environment (upbringing, school life, family, society and specific experiences) — so ADHD's effect on you may differ from someone else's. In some people ADHD is thought to be linked to physical problems such as asthma and migraine, and some women have more difficulties with ADHD due to hormonal changes [file #209].

### Medication
The guide states medication has been shown to help many adults with ADHD: it helps the brain function better, improves focus and memory, reduces impulsivity, helps with emotional regulation and some aspects of executive function. On starting, the psychiatrist begins "titration": you start at a low dose and over several months the dose may be raised until it works effectively, while tracking symptom changes and possible side effects; the doctor should discuss these at the start of dose-finding or when switching drugs so you know what to look for. Every brain is unique, so comparing yourself with other people with ADHD does not help; discuss concerns with your doctor and give yourself time. Approved ADHD medications act for different durations — some once a day, others several times a day; you need to know how and when to take your specific drug; you may need it only for a particular period each day, for most people while at work. There are two kinds: stimulant and non-stimulant; the doctor advises based on how ADHD affects you and your medical history. "Remember" box: pharmacotherapy is one of a range of strategies and should be used as such — you should not rely only on medication. Drugs can cause side effects such as dizziness, drowsiness and visual disturbances; you should be informed, and if they occur avoid potentially dangerous activities such as driving or operating machinery [file #209].

### CBT and coaching
When choosing a psychologist, make sure they have appropriate training, qualifications and experience and are registered with a professional body; also ask about their knowledge and successful experience with ADHD clients. For some people a trusted friend "с критическим взглядом" (with a critical eye) who gives honest objective feedback is enough to help make desired changes. CBT, dialectical behaviour therapy, coaching and group coaching can help you start making life choices that help you manage ADHD successfully [file #209].

### Thriving with ADHD — the good news
For many adults who learn late that they have ADHD it can be both a relief and a source of sadness when they reflect on how life might have gone had they known earlier. Criticising yourself or others, or blaming others for not knowing why you struggled, will not help; what matters is what YOU do NOW — your chance to learn to control your ADHD and take responsibility for regulating it, so you can keep living, working and pursuing your goals and interests. Applying the strategies and changing lifestyle, you will notice better wellbeing and growing confidence in managing daily life. A positive, solution-focused attitude is learned and takes time: be patient and kind to yourself, cultivate a sense of humour, value the people and things that matter to you [file #209].

Explaining to loved ones why some things are hard and what you are doing about it can help a lot; ask for understanding and support. Family and friends help you adjust, but responsibility for managing ADHD lies with you: a specialist can offer medication, but lifestyle changes depend on you. Changes take time to become habits; you will still make mistakes and keep struggling with some things, and will find new ways to cope. Reframes the guide offers: hyperactivity can be seen as energy, drive and enthusiasm; impulsivity as decisiveness — willingness to take a risk and seize an opportunity; inattention and mind-wandering as imagination seeking ideas, inspiration, new experiences, and as curiosity. ADHD is an explanation of how your unique mind works, how you interact with the world and the people in it. Remind yourself you are doing your best; mark achievements and progress [file #209].

## Evidence and numbers

- Up to 94% of people with ADHD have problems motivating themselves (channel's figure; source of the statistic not named) [msg #323].
- NHS England estimates ADHD in 3% of the population [file #209].
- A 2016 meta-analysis (Cortese et al., American Journal of Psychiatry) cited by the guide found about 28% of adults with ADHD meet obesity criteria vs 16% of people without ADHD; research links ADHD with different eating patterns, physical activity levels and sleep behaviour that, with genetic risk, may relate to weight changes not seen in people without ADHD [file #209].
- Research cited by the guide: up to 44% of people with ADHD may have restless legs syndrome; sleep apnoea is more common in ADHD than in the general population [file #209].
- Sleep need: 7–9 hours for most working-age adults; caffeine cutoff 8 hours before bed [file #209].
- Exercise: 150 minutes/week moderate activity (about 30 min × 5 days) plus strength on 2 days for adults 19–64 (NHS England) [file #209].
- Diet: 5 fruit/veg portions, 2–3 dairy portions, 2 fish portions a week, 6–8 glasses of fluid; about 2,000 kcal women / 2,500 kcal men [file #209].
- Habit timeline: benefits of stress strategies appear after at least three months; habits form over three to six months [file #209].
- The guide's reference list includes Kessler et al. (ASRS), DSM-5 (APA 2013), de la Peña et al. 2020 on the inattentive subtype, Cortese et al. on asthma and on RLS, Hansen et al. 2018 on migraine comorbidity, Young et al. 2020 on females with ADHD, Oğuztürk et al. on sleep apnoea, Kittel-Schneider et al. 2021 on non-mental diseases associated with ADHD, Hechtman 2008 on treatment of adult ADHD, plus The Sleep Charity and Medical News Today pages [file #209].
