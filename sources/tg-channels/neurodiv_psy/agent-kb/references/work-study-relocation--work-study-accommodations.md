# Neurodivergent people at work and university: job fit and accommodations

## What this covers

This topic gathers everything the channel taught about autistic and ADHD people in two settings: higher education and employment. It covers the research picture (lower graduation rates, very high unemployment), the concrete difficulties people meet (adaptation, social life, sensory overload, onboarding, hiring interviews), which kinds of work tend to fit, a needs pyramid derived from a study of autistic women at work, the role of values and special interests in motivation, and a practical toolkit of accommodations that a person can arrange without disclosing a diagnosis. According to the source, the core problem is not neurodivergence itself but a mismatch between the person and the environment — and that mismatch can be reduced [msg #200, #306, #371, #406, #413].

## Core ideas

- **Neurodivergence does not by itself prevent work — mismatch does.** Research cited by the channel author says most autistic adults with low support needs and most adults with ADHD adapt well and perform in very diverse career areas when they choose suitable work; what hinders them is the mismatch between environment and person, and accommodations measurably raise productivity and job satisfaction [msg #406].
- **The labour market does not reflect this.** Unemployment among autistic adults is 50–80% and ADHD adults are 2–3 times more likely to be jobless; the hiring process itself tests social conformity rather than job skills (see *Causes of unemployment and job loss*) [msg #406].
- **At university, studying is the easier part; adaptation and social life are the hard parts.** Autistic students graduate far less often (39% vs 59%), yet 48% are satisfied with their academic success; the main difficulties are adaptation and social life (see *Autism and higher education*) [msg #201, #202, #203, #204].
- **The main task at work is not to burn out.** In the autistic women's study, avoiding (primarily neurodivergent) burnout is the base on which everything else rests; sensory fit and fit to the cognitive profile are must-haves [msg #371].
- **Connection of work to personal interests and values is a "should", not a "nice to have".** Long-term endurance on willpower alone does not work for autistic people and is "a guaranteed path" to burnout and depression [msg #371].
- **In post-Soviet realities, official accommodations are almost impossible to request; ask for adjustments as personal preferences, without naming a diagnosis**, and use self-arranged tools (see *Accommodations without disclosure*) [msg #413].
- **Jobs reported as fitting share flexibility, autonomy, concreteness, impact, few people and variety** — readers' jobs almost never involved strict organisational requirements or routine, unchanging processes [msg #306].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Autism and higher education - difficulties and self-help accommodations
Purpose: a card series by the channel author, based on a conference talk about her own experience of getting a university degree as an autistic person, describing what autistic students struggle with and what to do about it. Title card: «АУТИЗМ И ВЫСШЕЕ ОБРАЗОВАНИЕ СЛОЖНОСТИ И АККОМОДАЦИИ» (*Autism and higher education: difficulties and accommodations*) [msg #200, img #200].

The author's own verdict: university was still better than school for her, even though she was dying of anxiety every exam session: «В моем опыте университет все еще был лучше, чем школа, хотя каждую сессию я умирала от тревоги» (*In my experience university was still better than school, although every exam session I was dying of anxiety*) [msg #200].

**Background data** (card 2): compared with non-autistic peers, autistic school pupils prepare less for university admission and less often enter full four-year bachelor's programmes. During study they more often experience higher levels of loneliness, anxiety and depression. Graduation rates of autistic students are markedly lower (39%) than for peers in the general population (59%) or students with other types of disability (50%), per Petcu, Zhang & Li (2021) [img #201].

**Difficulty area 1 — Adaptation** (card 3). One of the main trials is the adjustment period, made up of five components [img #202]:
1. A new environment and new people.
2. Different rules of studying than at school.
3. Moving away from home and losing one's support system.
4. Difficulties with household skills (cooking, cleaning).
5. Dormitories and living together with strangers.

**Difficulty area 2 — Social features** (card 4). Often this is the main area of difficulty [img #203]:
1. Trouble making friends.
2. High social anxiety and emotional dysregulation.
3. Lack of knowledge about autism among the people around (Hillier et al., 2018).
4. Bullying (Connor, 2013).

**Difficulty area 3 — Academic features** (card 5). With studying itself things are much better: «С самой учебой все намного лучше» (*With studying itself everything is much better*). Recent research shows 48% of autistic students are satisfied with their academic success (Jackson et al., 2018). The difficulties that remain (Gurbuz et al., 2019) [img #204]:
1. Speed of information processing.
2. Sensory overload during classes.
3. Time management.
4. Difficulty asking questions.
5. Oral presentations and oral answers.
6. Group work.
7. Motivation.

**How to help yourself** (cards 6–7) — ten self-help accommodations [img #205, #206]:
1. Research as much information about the university as possible in advance and find the curriculum — this reduces the shock of the new environment and new rules.
2. Find a psychologist, counsellor or coach who will support you, work on emotional regulation and help you, for example, compose a letter to a teacher.
3. Ask teachers for the slides of their classes, or photograph them during class (compensates for processing speed).
4. Ask classmates for their lecture notes — «возможно, за ответную помощь или шоколадку :)» (*perhaps in exchange for help or a chocolate bar :)*).
5. Bring fidget toys, headphones and similar items.
6. Don't forget to take breaks, eat and drink.
7. Don't be shy about leaving the class if you feel unwell.
8. Try to negotiate written instead of oral examination in some subjects.
9. Look for support groups inside or outside the university.
10. Ask for help and advice about difficulties — this can be done in Telegram groups or on Reddit.

**New challenges** (card 8): the final card, titled «НОВЫЕ ЧЕЛЛЕНДЖИ...» (*New challenges...*), shows a news headline (NY Post) next to a ChatGPT screen: a Purdue University professor was accused of being an AI bot because her e-mail lacked "human warmth", and answered «Я просто аутичные» (OCR of the Russian rendering; *I'm just autistic*). The lesson implied by the card is that in the age of chatbots autistic people's direct, low-affect written style can be mistaken for AI text — a new kind of misunderstanding [img #207].

### Five job areas reported as fitting ADHD/ASD
Origin: the author of the channel *как помыть посуду* (forwarded by this channel) asked readers what jobs they do and how well they fit their ADHD/ASD, then grouped the many answers by type of work. A side observation: there were almost no positions with strict requirements on how work is organised and almost none involving routine and an unchanging work process [msg #306].

1. **IT: testing, working with text, analytics, programming.** Main traits: clear requirements and understandable processes; a need for attention to detail; a minimum of distractions, especially in technical tasks; often the possibility to work independently or with minimal social interaction. Why it suits neurodivergent people: such positions allow entering hyperfocus and being more productive than usual; repeatability, structure and clarity of actions lower anxiety before new tasks; remote work [msg #306].
2. **Creative and visual professions: artist, web designer, perfumer's assistant, stylist.** Shared traits: visual or tactile perception; the chance to express oneself and be creative; often project or freelance work with a flexible schedule. Why it suits: creativity can help cope with emotional tension; a flexible schedule allows accounting for energy patterns, sensory overload and planning problems [msg #306].
3. **Education and languages: tutor and teacher, translator.** Shared traits: often one-to-one interaction; passing on knowledge about your special interest; the possibility to build the learning process your own way. Why it suits: teaching gives a sense of being useful to society and to a specific person. Minus: problems with self-organisation may arise, and teaching in a school can create problems because of the "group" nature of the work (overload, difficulties with interactions) [msg #306].
4. **Working with living beings and manual work: veterinary medicine, gardening, taxidermy, jewellery making.** Shared traits: working with one's hands, with "reality", not only mental activity; often physical activity; involvement of the senses. Why it suits: less abstraction, more concreteness; moving and working with one's hands in natural conditions reduces tension and anxiety; working with animals means no masking and pleasant interaction [msg #306].
5. **Independent, flexible employment: entrepreneurship, sales.** Shared traits: being your own boss or working in an informal structure; being able to influence the volume of tasks and when they are done; more freedom, fewer routine demands from above. Why it suits: neurodivergent people often find it hard to adapt to rigid structures and bureaucracy, whereas here they can build work processes completely around themselves [msg #306].

### Six principles for choosing work with ADHD/ASD (job bingo chart)
From the common traits of readers' jobs, the *как помыть посуду* author compiled (and, she notes, supplemented herself) a list of traits of an "ADHD/ASD-friendly" job, drawn as a picture titled (in OCR-garbled form) *Main principles for choosing a job with ADHD/ASD*. She suggests using it as a bingo chart when looking for a new job — ticking off how many principles a vacancy meets [msg #306, img #306].

1. **Flexible schedule and autonomy** — so that you can work in moments of concentration and rest when overloaded without consequences for the work [img #306].
2. **Not detached from your plans** — the work should be linked to your interests and plans; otherwise there will not be enough energy left for self-realisation and leisure, which leads to burnout [img #306].
3. **Minimum bureaucracy and abstraction** — it is easier to work with concrete things, the senses, visuals or data than with abstract requirements [img #306].
4. **About impact** — the work should produce a visible result and give a feeling of being needed by people and of the work's importance. Being an insignificant cog in the system will not work for long [img #306].
5. **Alone or one-to-one** — fewer people means less anxiety, less masking, less overload. A small team is better than a large staff (the card reads «Маленький коллектив • большой штат», the symbol between the two presumably meaning "better than" — (!) UNCLEAR: OCR lost the comparison sign) [img #306].
6. **Creativity and variety** — routine, monotonous work is tiring, while varied and creative tasks sustain interest in the work, pushing back the feeling of "rusting" («заржавления») [img #306].

### Pyramid of autistic people's needs in the work environment
Origin: preliminary results of a study by the author of the channel *Об аутичных женщинах, по которым не скажешь* (*About autistic women you wouldn't guess are autistic*), forwarded here. For some respondents many workplace needs are systematically unmet, which leads to overloads and burnouts. Others managed to set up or find a suitable environment and processes, thereby lowering the frequency of burnouts and staying longer at the same company. The author arranged the needs as a pyramid with layers from critical (must have) to desirable (nice have); she had not yet collected anything for "nice have", so the pyramid is truncated (the top is a question mark). She asks readers what they want but would give up without changing the job itself [msg #371, img #371].

The base, per the author: «основная задача на работе у нас - не выгореть» (*our main task at work is not to burn out*) — first neurodivergent burnout, then professional burnout, though in an autistic person's experience the two seem tightly intertwined [msg #371].

Layers, from bottom to top [img #371]:

**Layer 1 — Physical environment: fit to the sensory profile (MUST HAVE; level of physiological needs).** Task: prevent or reduce the frequency of overloads and burnout. Needs [img #372]:
1. The ability to control the space: lighting, sound, temperature, smells, layout.
2. Space and time for timely sensory deprivation.
An "office sensory deprivation kit" (the card notes it was generated with ChatGPT): earplugs; sunglasses with side shields; noise-cancelling headphones; a tube hoodie / hood; a sleep and pause mask; a weighted lap pillow; compression gloves; an ointment or barrier spray / odour neutraliser. A subscriber joked that the kit should also include a wallet with money to buy all this [img #372].

**Layer 2 — Organisation, processes, atmosphere: fit to the cognitive profile and to motivation features (productivity cycles) (MUST HAVE; level of physiological needs).** Task: prevent/reduce overloads and burnout and raise the effectiveness of work. Needs [img #373, #374]:
1. Ability to control the schedule: hybrid, remote work, extra days off, alternating days of activity and types of tasks (for example, a day of teamwork, then a day of quiet solo work from home), a flexible length of the working day depending on individual activity cycles [img #373].
2. Ability to control the types of tasks and formats of work based on one's strengths, competences, inclinations and limitations [img #373].
3. An inclusive atmosphere/culture: an accepting team informed about neurodivergence — the possibility of NOT masking all the time [img #373].
4. Detailed onboarding of a new autistic employee; reducing uncertainty in tasks, feedback and planning [img #373].
5. Finding ways of assigning tasks that do not provoke demand avoidance — the card offers tentative options with question marks: free choice from a list? finding a person as supervisor with whom there is a match in values and approaches, if it did not work out with the direct manager? [img #374]
6. Ability to control task deadlines and avoid hard deadlines [img #374].
7. Ability to control hyperfocus, including with external tools [img #374].
8. Ability to control the style and frequency of communication — as a rule it should be structured, have a clear goal and meaning, and be time-limited [img #374].

**Layer 3 — Meanings: fit of the work or of individual tasks to the person's values and/or interests (SHOULD HAVE; level of psychology and identity).** Task: maintaining motivation, overcoming barriers, increasing (preserving) productivity [img #371, #375]. The author stresses that this is not "nice have" but "should": the work should relate to you personally. This noticeably lowers stress and the probability of burnout — provided the physiological-level needs are met. Achieving goals and getting results can itself be a special interest; a special interest is not necessarily a field of activity or your current hobby: «Это что-то, контактируя с чем, вы чувствуете прилив сил, вдохновение, радость или просто покой» (*It is something in contact with which you feel a surge of energy, inspiration, joy or simply calm*). This does not mean you must monetise your hobby; but if your work contains something that resonates with you personally and brings joy, life becomes much easier. Patience and moving purely on willpower do not work for autistic people in the long term — it is possible, but it is a guaranteed path to burnout and depression [msg #371].

Example from a study participant whose value is helping people (consciously adding meaning to everyday hard tasks): she dislikes phoning both strangers and acquaintances, but sometimes has to call colleagues in other cities to agree changes to ticket or hotel bookings. It becomes easier to contact them first when she realises she is helping them; she is not calling to chat about the weather but with a concrete question needing a concrete answer: «Вот это меня смиряет с» … «необходимостью идти на контакт» (*This reconciles me with the need to make contact*) [img #375].

**Layer 4 — NICE HAVE:** empty, marked "?" — no data yet [img #371].

### Causes of unemployment and job loss in autistic and ADHD adults
From the channel author's post "(Не)трудоспособные: нейроотличия и работа" (*(Un)employable: neurodivergence and work*) [msg #406].

Weaker zones for **autistic people**:
1. Insufficient or poor-quality onboarding (help adapting) at a new workplace.
2. Not understanding the unwritten rules of social interaction.
3. Mismatched communication styles and misunderstanding from those around.
4. Uncomfortable sensory features of the environment.
5. Stigma around autism.

Additional difficulties for **people with ADHD**:
1. Difficulty planning and organising time, missing deadlines — especially when there are no clear instructions about deadlines and consequences.
2. Chronic lateness.
3. Impulsivity, which is easily read as unreliability.
4. Fast burnout from work processes not designed for their pace and way of concentrating.

**One more, equally important cause — the hiring process itself:** «сам процесс найма устроен так, что оценивает не рабочие навыки, а соответствие ожиданиям общества и компании» (*the hiring process itself is designed to assess not work skills but conformity to the expectations of society and the company*) — predictability, visible friendliness, the ability to talk about yourself quickly and smoothly. An autistic or ADHD person can have the needed skills and completely fail the interview, because the interview tests the wrong abilities [msg #406].

**The remedy:** accommodations — flexible schedule, the possibility of remote work, clear written instructions instead of vague oral ones, structured onboarding. When they appear, results change noticeably: both productivity and job satisfaction rise [msg #406].

### Accommodations without disclosure - four-area toolkit
Context (channel author): for a neurodivergent person already on the labour market, «просить у работодателя официальные аккомодации в постсоветских реалиях почти нереально» (*asking the employer for official accommodations in post-Soviet realities is almost unrealistic*). Disclosing a diagnosis often equals the risk of not getting an offer or being refused under a plausible pretext. The most one can afford at an interview is to carefully ask about conditions ("is the schedule flexible", "how is feedback organised") and look at the conditions potential colleagues work in. If already employed, explain your neurodivergent traits as personal features or preferences **without naming the diagnosis**: «я продуктивнее работаю, когда слушаю музыку» (*I work more productively when I listen to music*), «мне иногда важно походить по кабинету туда-сюда и подумать» (*sometimes it's important for me to walk back and forth around the office and think*). The toolkit below does not depend on whether the company is ready to talk about neurodivergence; some items you do yourself, others you can propose as a way to "help you understand tasks better and cope better" [msg #413].

**Area 1 — Job search** [msg #413]:
1. Courses and mentoring to build the skills needed for job search and interviews; or work on these competences with a coach, career consultant or psychologist; if money is short, YouTube has many free videos on passing interviews. (The author's own upcoming webinar/course with Карина Аруштова is mentioned here.)
2. Ask acquaintances for advice, watch videos, read advice on writing CVs and cover letters. It is always good if a knowledgeable person looks over your CV — or, as a last resort, an AI chatbot if nobody is around (the author notes the AI conversation is complicated and does not go into it). The key is to understand what exactly the recruiter/employer wants to see.
3. Remember you are not obliged to match a vacancy 100%; autistic black-and-white thinking can get very much in the way here.
4. Don't be afraid to embellish your work experience: «Нормально написать себе 9 месяцев работы в компании вместо 6» (*It's normal to write 9 months of work at a company instead of 6*).
5. During the interview, if possible, assess how well the workplace fits you on various indicators: noise and light levels, the team, workload, number of interactions per day.

**Area 2 — Sensory and emotional needs** [msg #413]:
1. Noise reduction: noise-cancelling headphones and earplugs. Popular options: Silensys headphones and Loop earplugs; cheaper: ordinary construction ear defenders or earplugs sold everywhere.
2. Light: ideally the ability to control lighting; in reality, photochromic glasses (lenses that change colour depending on lighting).
3. Breaks in quiet places to avoid overstimulation — scout in advance where you can escape to: outside, a storeroom, the toilet or another unpopulated place.
4. Bring fidget toys for stimming; you can choose something as inconspicuous as possible.
5. Tools for mood monitoring and emotional regulation: mood-tracking apps (Finch — the author: «оч люблю!» (*really love it!*) — How We Feel, Daylio and others) and mindfulness/relaxation apps (Headspace, MindShift and others).

**Area 3 — Social adaptations** [msg #413]:
1. Ideally, informing employers and colleagues about neurodivergence, support and emotional regulation. In reality, disclosure sometimes leads to dismissal — but you can talk about your traits without the diagnosis, e.g. «Знаешь, я не очень хорошо понимаю сарказм, не мог бы ты говорить более прямо, что тебе не нравится?» (*You know, I don't understand sarcasm very well, could you say more directly what you don't like?*).
2. Acceptance and respect from colleagues — something to use as a criterion when choosing a team/company.
3. Socially oriented coaching or psychological counselling to acquire communication skills and navigate workplace relationships and etiquette.
4. Speech-to-text programs if dictating is easier than typing (many operating systems have this built in today).
5. Augmentative and alternative communication devices that help people with limited verbal skills communicate.
6. Friends/acquaintances (or, again as a last resort, AI chatbots) who can help decode what colleagues want from you when you can't figure it out yourself.

**Area 4 — Workplace routine, tasks and related communication** [msg #413]:
1. Apps for task management, planning, prioritisation and decision-making, such as Todoist, Google Calendar, Sectograph and many others.
2. A fixed or flexible work schedule, depending on your needs.
3. Ask for concrete feedback — you can ask for examples of what was great and what was not, and next steps.
4. If relations with colleagues and manager are normal, ask them to set clearer tasks and deadlines; if not, make the vague task as clear as possible yourself (see *Making a vague task concrete*).

### Making a vague task concrete
When a manager gives a fuzzy instruction and you cannot ask them to clarify, break it into concrete actions yourself. The author's example (with a joke as the last step): «мне сказали быть организованнее, что я могу сделать? 1. завести трекер задач; 2. записать, что от меня ожидают в ближайшую неделю; 3. поплакать» (*I was told to be more organised, what can I do? 1. set up a task tracker; 2. write down what is expected of me in the coming week; 3. have a cry*) [msg #413].

## Detailed insights

### Stand-up comedy as a fit for an autistic person: the case of Татьянида
The needs-pyramid post includes an interview with the autistic stand-up comedian Татьянида (channel НЕЙРОТРОЕЧНИЦА!), asked how she came to stand-up, whether it contains her special interests and whether it matches her values [msg #371].
- **Why she started:** she took stand-up courses to lower the intensity of her anxiety about doing something wrong in communication with people (without understanding what) and embarrassing herself. For her it was like deciding to skydive: what could be scarier than going on a small bar stage where nobody cares about you, telling 10 jokes, and nobody laughing. The point, she says: «всем страшно, все шутят в тишину, одна и та же шутка заходит в одном зале и не заходит в другом» (*everyone is scared, everyone jokes into silence, the same joke lands in one room and not in another*) — i.e. failure is normalised and not personal [img #376].
- **Why it turned out to fit:** «я шла за опытом позора на сцене, а получила Джек-пот» (*I went for the experience of shame on stage and got the jackpot*): humour and joke writing are a very clearly described and regulated thing, with room to play for someone with ASD. Her colleague keeps a spreadsheet measuring the level of laughter for each joke at each show. There are clear schemes and rules: how jokes are written, how many minutes a set takes, how to dress, in what order everything goes; even crowd work and improvisation have a set of conventions and rules [img #377].
- **Special interest link:** she prefers writing jokes because it combines her interests in studying people's behaviour and reactions and in conveying information as a unit of content (video, poem, joke, reel). Fitting a thought into the form of a joke is "like Tetris or match-three" [img #377].
- **Outcome:** she writes jokes "for the drawer" more often than she performs, but each performance is a small feat for her and she is terribly proud of herself — «А я мало от чего получаю яркие эмоции» (*And I get vivid emotions from few things*) [img #377].
Lesson in context: a field that looks socially terrifying can fit an autistic person when it has explicit rules, measurable feedback and overlaps with special interests and values (exposure to social fear, studying human reactions) [msg #371, img #376, #377].

### Reframing the question
The channel author's hopeful conclusion: the situation is gradually changing and can be influenced — more people learn that everyone and every brain is different and that neurodivergence exists at all, and work formats are changing too. The question «что со мной не так» (*what is wrong with me*) is gradually giving way to «что подходит и не подходит именно мне» (*what does and doesn't suit me specifically*) [msg #406].

## Evidence and numbers

All figures as cited by the channel author in the employment post [msg #406]:
- Research shows most autistic adults with low support needs (the author notes official level divisions may not reflect real needs) and most adults with ADHD adapt well to the work environment across very diverse careers when they choose suitable work; successful employment significantly improves quality of life for both groups.
- 50 to 80% of autistic adults have no job; the figure varies greatly by country and counting methodology.
- In the UK only 22% of autistic people are officially employed — the lowest rate among all groups of disabled people — while more than 80% of non-disabled people work.
- Adults with ADHD have a 2–3 times higher risk of being unemployed than neurotypical people, and a 60% higher probability of losing an existing job.
- About 67% of people with ADHD are in full-time employment versus 87% of people without ADHD.
- With accommodations, both productivity and job satisfaction grow noticeably.

University figures are given in *Autism and higher education* (39% / 59% / 50% graduation; 48% academic satisfaction) [img #201, #204].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The workplace needs pyramid is explicitly based on **preliminary** results of a study of autistic women; the "nice have" layer is empty and the author asks readers for input [msg #371].
- The job areas and six principles come from an informal reader survey on another channel, and the author admits she supplemented the principles with her own ideas (shown as struck-through "дополнила от себя") [msg #306].
- Unemployment figures for autistic adults vary strongly by country and methodology (50–80%); the channel author also notes that official support-level divisions may not reflect real needs [msg #406].
- In the education area, teaching in a school is flagged as a possible problem (group work, overload) despite education being listed as a fitting field [msg #306].
- The advice to "embellish" work experience (9 months instead of 6) is the channel author's own pragmatic recommendation [msg #413].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the "small team vs large staff" comparison sign on the principles card was lost in OCR [img #306]. The Purdue professor's quote is rendered in OCR as «Я просто аутичные» (grammatically garbled; meaning "I'm just autistic") [img #207].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the employment post promises a follow-up post on accommodations; #413 serves that role. The webinar content (stages of the job-search cycle and difficulties at each stage) is only announced, not explained [msg #406, #413].
