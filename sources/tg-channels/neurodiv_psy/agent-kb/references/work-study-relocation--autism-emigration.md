# Autism and emigration

## What this covers

This topic gathers two posts. The first is a three-part post by the channel author, an autistic woman who had moved out of her home country more than a year before. It covers why immigration is especially demanding for autistic people, which countries' immigration laws exclude autistic applicants, and what autistic immigrants themselves recommend [msg #390, #391, #392]. The second is a guest post by Fridrich, an AuDHD psychologist, giving a five-point checklist of "small" needs to check before choosing a country, plus a closing argument that one's own needs, not other people's norms, decide quality of life [msg #399, #400]. The author notes that much of the first post also applies to people who moved to another city or region, not only another country: "В чем-то пост будет актуален и для людей, которые переехали в другой город/регион!" (*In some ways the post will also be relevant for people who moved to another city/region!*) [msg #390].

## Core ideas

- **Immigration is a test at the limit of capacity for autistic people.** The author asks the reader to picture an airport at rush hour, an unfamiliar city, foreign speech from every side and no familiar landmarks. Finding your way in such a situation is hard for anyone, but for autistic people it "может быть настоящим испытанием на пределе возможностей" (*can be a real test at the limit of one's capacity*) [msg #390].
- **The load is multi-layered and usually unsupported.** Immigrants have to learn a new language and culture and also get past significant social, sensory and bureaucratic obstacles, "зачастую без какой-либо поддержки и понимания со стороны принимающего государства" (*often without any support or understanding from the receiving state*) [msg #390].
- **Some countries' immigration laws are ableist.** Canada, New Zealand and Australia have historically excluded autistic people as a "burden" on healthcare (see *Discriminatory immigration laws*) [msg #391].
- **People cope, and autistic immigrants have practical recommendations** on routine, money, sensory strategies, language, community and autistic organisations (see *Six recommendations*) [msg #392].
- **Small, specific needs decide whether life in a new place is bearable.** Fridrich argues that emigrant chats talk about work, study and language, but for autistic people it is the combination of "small things" (climate and microclimate, food, medicine, community, places to rest) that "может сделать жизнь невыносимой" (*can make life unbearable*) [msg #399].
- **Your own needs are valid even if others find them silly**, because you, not they, live with them every day (see *Choosing for yourself*) [msg #400].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Eight unique difficulties for autistic immigrants
Purpose: the author's list of what autistic people face most often when moving ("Вот с чем сталкиваются аутичные люди чаще всего при переезде" — *Here is what autistic people most often face when moving*) [msg #391].

1. **Sensory overload at every step.** Airports, train stations, new neighbourhoods and a huge number of unfamiliar sounds, smells and people. Without one's usual self-regulation tools and without a familiar space to hide in, this drains a person very quickly [msg #391].
2. **Breakdown of familiar routines.** The author calls routine "зачастую основа психологической устойчивости аутичного человека" (*often the foundation of an autistic person's psychological stability*). Moving "обнуляет все" (*resets everything to zero*): the daily schedule, usual routes, favourite places [msg #391].
3. **Loss of the support system.** Family, old friends, a doctor you trust, specialists who know you all stay behind. Building a new system in the new place takes time and energy, and there is often too little of both already [msg #391].
4. **Unfamiliar social rules.** Every culture has its own unwritten norms: how to greet people, how directly you can speak, whether to kiss when meeting, when silence is appropriate and when it isn't. What worked at home can be perceived completely differently in the new place [msg #391].
5. **Language barrier.** It is not only about grammar. There are idioms, sarcasm, hints and meaningful pauses. On top of that come differences in auditory processing, and the fact that autistic people "зачастую и на родном языке половину информации не успевают понять" (*often fail to take in half the information even in their native language*) [msg #391].
6. **Bureaucracy.** Filling in documents, visa interviews, phone calls to government services, waiting in queues. The author sums up the oral side as "Устное общение, кошмар, ужас, стресс" (*Oral communication, nightmare, horror, stress*) [msg #391].
7. **Access to specialised help.** Finding a psychotherapist or psychiatrist who works with autistic adults is hard even at home. In a new country you also have the language barrier, not knowing how the system works, and often long waiting lists or high prices [msg #391].
8. **Double stigma.** Being autistic already means meeting incomprehension, and so does being an immigrant. Together the two factors create extra vulnerability [msg #391].

### Six recommendations from autistic immigrants
Purpose: what autistic immigrants themselves most often recommend ("Вот что чаще всего рекомендуют сами аутичные иммигранты") [msg #392].

1. **Create a routine.** Even small daily habits help lower anxiety. Bring familiar and favourite things such as plush toys and collectible items to help you feel more comfortable [msg #392].
2. **Work out where your money will come from.** A benefit (for refugees) or remote work is very important for getting through the first months in the new place more easily [msg #392].
3. **Plan sensory strategies.** Look for quiet places in advance. Carry your usual self-regulation items, for example sunglasses, sunscreen with a comfortable texture, or headphones [msg #392].
4. **Learn the language gradually.** Don't rush, because Google Translate can handle most tasks. If you have shutdowns, carry pictures or cards in the needed language. Learn at your own pace, and "не ожидайте, что вы быстро заговорите, если вы не особо много разговариваете и на родном языке" (*don't expect to start speaking quickly if you don't talk much in your native language either*) [msg #392].
5. **Find your people.** There are often immigrant communities where you are moving. Don't expect to make friends with locals straight away. Start with compatriots, with whom you at least share a language. Immigrant chats also contain a lot of useful information, with the caveat that "иногда бывают неприятные разборки" (*sometimes there are unpleasant squabbles*) [msg #392].
6. **Write to autistic organisations and communities** (if there are any). They help you find community, rights protection and resources [msg #392].

### Fridrich's five things autistic people should check before emigrating
Purpose and context: guest author Fridrich reads emigrant chats from time to time and sees that they skip things that turned out to matter to him. People there usually write about work, study or language. He does not say these are unimportant, but argues that autistic people should pay attention to a set of things whose combination "может сделать жизнь невыносимой" (*can make life unbearable*). His examples come from emigrating from Ukraine to Austria (Vienna) in 2022 and from Austria to Spain in 2026. He has also moved within his country and to Latvia [msg #399].

1. **Sensory needs (climate and microclimate).** If you are sensitive to heat and/or cold, the climate can become real torture. But climate is not the whole story. In Vienna almost no homes have air conditioning, and he "буквально страдал каждое лето" (*literally suffered every summer*). In Spain it is much hotter, but there are air conditioners. His conclusion is that the microclimate decides as much as the climate, "вообще я бы сказал, что он решает куда больше" (*actually I'd say it matters far more*). Lesson: check indoor conditions, not only the weather [msg #399].
2. **Food.** If you are a selective eater like him, you may find there is nothing for you to eat. Money can solve this, as it can many needs, especially sensory ones, but not everyone has money. In Vienna he had "практически нечего есть" (*practically nothing to eat*), "как бы странно это не звучало" (*however strange that may sound*). Check the other country's food habits and whether Ukrainian products, or whatever products you are used to, are available [msg #399].
3. **Medical care.** As a trans man, access to hormone replacement therapy was very important to him, but the point is wider: many people take psychiatric medication or other drugs. He advises carrying a supply for six months, or even a year for medications you take permanently, because getting them in the new country may be difficult. On the other hand, in some countries (Austria, Germany) they may be covered by insurance, which can lower living costs a lot. In Austria even gender transition can be done through insurance. In some countries it may be easier, or harder, to get a diagnosis if you don't have one yet. He "brought" his ASD diagnosis with him and was assessed for ADHD (and prescribed medication) in Vienna [msg #399].
4. **Community.** It is important to have your own people, ideally before you leave: people who already know how things work and/or speak the language and can support you. He says this matters especially for queer people, because in emigrant chats a shared emigration experience "вообще не равен отсутствию цисгетеронормативности и рефлексии привилегий" (*is not at all the same as an absence of cis-heteronormativity, or as reflecting on one's privilege*) [msg #399].
5. **Opportunities for rest.** Nature is very important to him, and Vienna is far from green. He was going crazy from the lack of enough trees, shade and nooks: "это город – одно большое открытое пространство" (*this city is one big open space*). At first this may seem like nonsense, he says, but combined with other unmet sensory needs, having no way to recover and rest "делает из жизни один большой мелтдаун" (*turns life into one big meltdown*) [msg #399].

## Detailed insights

### Discriminatory immigration laws
The author writes that immigration law in several countries has historically not accepted autistic people, treating them as a "обузой" (*burden*) on the healthcare system [msg #391]:
- **Canada:** autistic migrants have been refused citizenship or even deported on the assumption that they would put too much load on healthcare. Reforms announced in 2018 were supposed to make access easier for people with disabilities, but problems remain [msg #391].
- **New Zealand:** permanent resident status is effectively out of reach for autistic people who are judged to need "значительной поддержке" (*significant support*) [msg #391].
- **Australia:** strict "медицинские критерии" (*medical criteria*) apply. If an "чрезвычайно высокая" (*extremely high*) load on the social system is expected, no visa is granted, and autistic applicants often fall under these restrictions [msg #391].

These laws are harshly criticised as a form of "системного эйблизмa" (*systemic ableism*) [msg #391].

**The workaround and the author's verdict.** If you really want to go to one of these countries, there is a way out: don't mention ASD, and don't ask the state for any support until you have citizenship. The author's verdict: "Но это такой себе выход" (*But that's a so-so way out*) [msg #391].

### The author's conclusion
Autistic immigrants carry a double load: adapting to a new culture while living with autistic traits. The author's view is that with support, changes in legislation and growing acceptance they can build their lives successfully and share their experience with other immigrants and autistic people. She ends with an affirmation: "этот мир принадлежит вам так же, как и нейротипичным людям, и вы имеете право на комфортную жизнь!" (*this world belongs to you just as much as to neurotypical people, and you have the right to a comfortable life!*) She also asks readers what else they would recommend to autistic people moving to another country or region [msg #392].

### Choosing for yourself: your needs decide quality of life
Fridrich's overall message is that your needs ("именно ваши!" — *yours specifically!*) are important even if the people around you find them silly or not worth attention. Everyone around him loved Vienna and stared wide-eyed when he answered that what bothered him was too few trees. But, he points out, "они-то спросили и забыли, а мне (и вам) с этим условным отсутствием деревьев сталкиваться каждый день своей жизни" (*they asked and forgot, while I (and you) have to face this figurative lack of trees every day of our lives*) [msg #400].

The practical rule that follows: the fact that a place suits everyone around you, or that something like language doesn't matter to you at all, is "не повод этих всех слушать и не выбирать себя" (*not a reason to listen to all of them and not choose yourself*). Choose the place against your own list of needs, not against the general consensus [msg #400].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The legal situations in Canada, New Zealand and Australia are given as the author's summary, with no sources, dates of the current rules or further detail. Only the 2018 Canadian reform is dated, and the author says problems persist after it [msg #391].
- Hiding an ASD diagnosis to get through immigration is described as possible but explicitly not endorsed ("такой себе выход" — *a so-so way out*) [msg #391].
- Fridrich's checklist is drawn from his personal experience (Vienna vs Spain) and from his position as a trans, queer, AuDHD person. He notes that money can solve many of the food and sensory problems, but not everyone has it [msg #399].
- The two posts give slightly different advice on community. The channel author suggests starting with compatriots in immigrant chats [msg #392]. Fridrich warns that for queer people emigrant chats may be cis-heteronormative, and recommends having your people in place before you leave [msg #399]. These are different emphases, not a direct contradiction.
