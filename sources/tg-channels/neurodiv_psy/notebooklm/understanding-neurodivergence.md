## The neurodiversity paradigm, terminology and myths about autism

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's foundational vocabulary post ("Про нейроразнообразие и нейроотличия" — "About neurodiversity and neurodivergence") with its summary card set "Нейроразнообразие: основные термины" ("Neurodiversity: basic terms") [#12] [#13], plus three later reposts: a myth-busting card about what autistic people can be and do [#38], a reposted rebuke of functioning comparisons between autistic/ADHD people [#195], and a post on autism awareness vs acceptance with a study summary on neurodiversity training for hospital staff [#304]. The author explains why the new words are needed at all: the words "нейроразнообразие", "нейроотличный" and "нейротипичный" are increasingly heard, mostly when discussing autism and ADHD, and the post sets out what they mean and why "new words to talk about familiar conditions" are used [#12]. According to the source, the stakes are the quality of life of neurodivergent people: much of their suffering stems from discrimination and non-inclusive environments, and how people talk about them signals respect or its absence [#12] [#19].

### Core ideas

- Brains vary like eye and hair colour. Neurodiversity is the natural variety in how brains are built and work; the idea of a single "normal" or "healthy" brain type is treated as a stereotype that does not benefit society [#12] [#14].
- Not every neurodivergence is a disease. In the neurodiversity paradigm only conditions that interfere with life and cause suffering, and that can be separated from the person (e.g. epilepsy), are diseases; autism and ADHD shape the whole personality, cannot be separated or cured, and are accepted and adapted to instead (see *Neurodiversity paradigm criterion*) [#12].
- Much of the difficulty is social. Many autistic, ADHD and dyslexic people are hindered not by their condition but by discrimination and the lack of an inclusive environment [#12].
- Support is still needed. The paradigm does not deny difficulties or the need for medical/psychological help; inclusion work and clinical help serve the same goal (see *Critics of the movement and the author's answer*) [#12].
- Words signal respect. Using "neurodivergent" does not deny diagnoses; using the words neurodivergent people ask for shows you don't see them as mere "deviations from the norm" [#19].
- Stereotypes and comparisons invalidate. Autism is compatible with many traits people assume it excludes [#38], and one autistic/ADHD person's capability is no yardstick for others [#195].
- Acceptance goes beyond awareness. Awareness is knowing autistic people exist; acceptance is respecting, accommodating and valuing them, and training can shift professionals toward it [#304].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Neurodiversity paradigm criterion: which neurodivergences are diseases
Purpose: to distinguish, within the paradigm, between neurodivergences that should be treated as illnesses and those that are ways of being. The source gives two categories, each with an example and reasoning [#12].
1. Neurodivergences that are diseases. Only those forms of neurodivergence "которые мешают людям жить" (which prevent people from living) and cause them suffering are considered diseases. Example: epilepsy can be called a disease because people with epilepsy suffer from seizures that worsen their quality of life. Such diseases can easily be separated from the person's personality: "Если избавить человека от эпилепсии, то личность его фундаментально не поменяется" (If you rid a person of epilepsy, their personality will not fundamentally change) [#12].
2. Neurodivergences that are not diseases. Other neurodivergences affect all spheres of a person's life. Autism and ADHD influence how a person perceives the world, thinks and communicates, what interests them, what comes easily and what does not. They are "невозможно отделить от личности человека" (impossible to separate from the person's personality) in a way that leaves the person still themselves. In the paradigm such conditions are not considered illnesses and also cannot be cured; the person and those around them can only accept the neurodivergence and adapt to it [#12].
Corollary given by the author: many autistic people, and people with ADHD or dyslexia, are troubled "не их состояние" (not by their condition) but by discrimination and the absence of an inclusive environment; suffering can result from barriers imposed by social norms, leading to social isolation and inequality [#12].

#### Classification of neurodivergences by origin
The source divides neurodivergences into three kinds [#12] [#17]:
1. Innate (врожденные) — e.g. autism, ADHD, dyslexia (and others, as the source itself says).
2. Acquired (приобретенные) — e.g. brain injuries, conditions caused by long-term alcohol use (and others, as the source itself says).
3. A combination of innate and acquired factors.

#### Conditions commonly counted as neurodivergences
The author notes that classifications differ from one another, but they often include the following nine groups [#12] [#18]:
1. Autism spectrum disorders (РАС, ASD).
2. Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (СДВГ, ADHD).
3. Down syndrome.
4. Specific learning disorders — dyslexia, dysgraphia, dyscalculia.
5. Dyspraxia.
6. Intellectual developmental disorders.
7. Tourette syndrome.
8. Sensory processing disorders.
9. Mental disorders such as bipolar disorder (БАР), obsessive-compulsive disorder (ОКР) and others.

#### Awareness vs acceptance

1. Awareness — Kaylynn Partlow: "Осведомлённость об аутизме — это когда ты знаешь, кто я..." (Autism awareness is when you know who I am...). Building on Patrick Casale, the post's author frames awareness as knowing that autistic people exist [#304].
2. Acceptance — Partlow: "Принятие аутизма — это когда ты рад видеть меня" (Autism acceptance is when you are glad to see me). Acceptance is "нечто гораздо большее" (something much more); it says: "Аутистичные люди заслуживают уважения, адаптации и того, чтобы их ценили такими, какие они есть" (Autistic people deserve respect, accommodation and to be valued as they are). Concretely it means: neurodivergence-affirming stance, listening to autistic voices, supporting self-advocacy, making the world inclusive and breaking stigma — a shift "от «исправления» к адаптации" (from "fixing" to adaptation) and respect for different ways of thinking and communicating [#304].
The author considers it important to keep talking about what happens in the neurodivergent community and to advocate for a world where autistic people can live fully, authentically and safely [#304].

### Detailed insights

#### Definitions
- Neurodiversity (нейроразнообразие) is the diversity of brain structure and functioning in different people. The author's analogy: just as people differ in eye and hair colour, brain functioning can differ too [#12] [#14].
- The neurodiversity movement is built on this idea of variability. Its supporters advocate civil rights, equality, respect and full social inclusion for neurodivergent people. The notion that there is one "normal" or "healthy" type of brain or neurocognitive functioning is regarded as a stereotype that does not benefit society [#12].
- Neurodivergent (нейроотличный) / neurodivergence (нейроотличие). If a person's brain works substantially differently from the norm accepted in society, the person is called neurodivergent and the state of their brain can be called a neurodivergence [#12] [#15].
- Neurotypical (нейротипичный). People whose brain functioning relatively corresponds to social standards of «нормальности» ("normality"); accordingly they have no mental disorders or other neurodivergences [#12] [#16].

#### Critics of the movement and the author's answer
The author states that the neurodiversity movement has "немало противников" (quite a few opponents). One objection: treating autism or ADHD as natural variations of brain functioning implies that neurodivergent people do not need support. The author's reply is explicit: "*Поддержка нужна*" (Support is needed). Neurodivergent people acknowledge that they have difficulties, but many of these difficulties are tied to discrimination, social expectations and the environment [#12].
The author's position on what to do: it is important to fight for greater awareness of neurodivergence, reduced stigma, inclusive environments and accommodations. Until society achieves this, it is "особенно важно" (especially important) for neurodivergent people to receive medical and/or psychological help. In the end the fight for inclusion and acceptance and medical/psychological help serve "одной и той же цели" (one and the same goal) — improving neurodivergent people's quality of life [#12].

#### Why the terminology matters
The final card of the terms set argues: using the term "нейроотличный" (neurodivergent) does not mean denying diagnoses and the difficulties associated with them. By using the words neurodivergent people ask you to use, you show that you respect them and do not regard them simply as «отклонения от нормы» ("deviations from the norm") that need to be fitted to society's standards [#19].

#### Functioning comparisons invalidate others
A post reposted from the channel "Нейропанк" (tags #нейропанк_аутизм, #нейропанк_напоминания — autism, reminders), translating a social-media post by purpleflamebOi, rebukes the line «У меня аутизм и СДВГ, и я все равно все ещё могу делать это, отмазки кончились!!» ("I have autism and ADHD and I can still do this, no more excuses!!"; English original: "I have autism and ADHD yet I can still do this, you have no excuse !!"). The answer: "Заткнись. Не все функционируют так, как ты." (Shut up. Not everyone functions the way you do.) Not everyone can get employed the way you can; not everyone can go through life the way you do; "Не все такие, как ты." (Not everyone is like you.) The lesson: one neurodivergent person's level of functioning, employment or daily coping cannot be used as a standard to dismiss another's difficulties as "excuses" [#195].

### Evidence and numbers

Study on autistic-doctor-led neurodiversity training in an Australian hospital. The forwarded post says a recent study in Australia showed how neurodiversity training helps doctors better understand and accept autistic people; the author translated the study's summary card, which is based on work by Bernard S. et al. published in *Neurodiversity* (2025) and titled "What happens when autistic doctors teach hospital teams about neurodivergence?" - *Why it was done:* negative attitudes (prejudices) toward autistic people can hinder quality medical care. Neurodiversity training could help, but a hospital in Western Australia was not doing it. - *How it was done:* the study leads met with hospital teams and learned what they thought about the training; feedback showed they wanted to learn more about experience of working in the hospital. Three teams of doctors and nurses went through the training. ## AuDHD: how ADHD and autism overlap and conflict

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's material on the co-occurrence of ADHD (СДВГ) and autism: comorbidity statistics in children and adults, shared genetic factors, the traits the two conditions share versus those specific to each, the opposing needs that make people with both (AuDHD, АуСДВГ) feel like a walking contradiction, the distinction between special interests, hyperfixations and hyperfocus, why AuDHD is usually more impairing than either condition alone, and why rest is especially hard with AuDHD. The author writes from personal experience — she says she is both autistic and has ADHD — and stresses that the two diagnoses are "in some ways very similar and in some ways completely opposite", which is exactly what makes life at their intersection difficult [#26, #70].

### Core ideas

- ADHD and autism frequently co-occur. One and the same person can be both autistic and have ADHD (the author gives herself as an example); the conditions share many traits but are opposite in others [#26].
- The co-occurrence is a new research area. Until 2013 the two were considered mutually exclusive diagnoses ("if you have ADHD you cannot have autism, and vice versa"); recent research shows they occur together very often [#70].
- Shared traits, different causes. Many difficulties (executive function, social cognition, interoception, emotional regulation) appear in both, sometimes for different underlying reasons [#26, #67].
- Opposing needs create internal conflict. Autistic needs (routine, the familiar, a constant special interest, plans kept) collide with ADHD tendencies (disorganization, craving novelty, switching interests, lateness) [#68, #70].
- AuDHD is usually harder than either alone, and ADHD symptoms are usually addressed first [#69, #70].
- Three different "interest/focus" phenomena — special interest (autism, lifelong), hyperfixation (ADHD, months), hyperfocus (ADHD, hours) — are often confused and should be distinguished [#33, #37].
- Rest is hard with AuDHD because one part needs stimulation and fun while the other needs calm and quiet [#361].

#### Eight overlapping traits of ADHD and autism
A text list (author: neurodivergent_insights, posted by the channel) explaining in more depth what ADHD and autism share [#26]:
1. Executive-function difficulties — problems organizing tasks, with object permanence, memory, concentration, decision-making and attention regulation [#26].
2. Social cognition — both groups have difficulties, but for different reasons. In people with ADHD the difficulties may stem from inattention and impulsivity; in autistic people, from trouble intuitively perceiving social cues, body language and understanding subtext [#26].
3. Interoception problems — interoception is the ability to sense the signals your body sends (hunger, thirst, the need to go to the toilet, emotions). It matters for emotional regulation: a person with interoceptive difficulties finds it harder to understand and respond flexibly to their internal states and emotions [#26].
4. Emotional-regulation difficulties — due to various factors (brain functioning, interoception, rigidity and sensory particularities), regulating emotions may be harder [#26].
5. Increased risk of OCD — given as 30–50% [#26].
6. Alcohol dependence — 5–10 times more common in people with ADHD, and 2 times more common in autistic people [#26].
7. Increased risk of self-harm and suicidal behaviour [#26].
8. A higher share of queer people among autistic people and people with ADHD [#26].

A shorter version of the shared-traits list appears on the AuDHD cards and in the AuDHD post: difficulties with executive functioning (attention, organization/self-organization, decision-making and other functions); communication particularities, "хоть и разного рода" (though of different kinds); emotional-regulation problems; difficulties understanding the body's internal signals (hunger, thirst, when it's time to go to the toilet); sensory particularities; altered perception of time; hyperfocus; stimming; and other traits [#67, msg #70]. Note that this version adds altered time perception and hyperfocus, which are not in the eight-item list [#67].

#### AuDHD as a "walking contradiction": four opposing pairs
The card states that people with AuDHD sometimes feel "как ходячее противоречие" (like a walking contradiction), because autistic and ADHD traits pull in opposite directions. Each pair: autism side ↔ ADHD side [#68]:
1. Striving for routine and order ↔ difficulties with organization and planning. The person needs order but struggles to create it [#68, msg #70].
2. Wish to stick to familiar things ↔ craving for new experiences. The post words it as the ADHD "жажда спонтанности и новых впечатлений" (thirst for spontaneity and new impressions) versus the autistic drive to keep to what is already familiar [#68, msg #70].
3. Need for a constant special interest ↔ switching interests to stay engaged [#68].
4. Distress when others break plans (e.g. being late) ↔ difficulty arriving on time oneself [#68].

The author adds that these differences "могут сильно затруднять существование человека с ауСДВГ" (can make the existence of a person with AuDHD very difficult) [#70].

#### Special interest vs hyperfixation vs hyperfocus
Purpose: the three terms come up constantly in discussions of ADHD and autism, and the last two even sound alike; the series (title card "Специнтересы, гиперфиксации или гиперфокус?" — *Special interests, hyperfixations or hyperfocus?*) explains how to tell them apart [#33, msg #37].

1. Special (особый/специальный) interest — autism, long-term.
   - What: a strong, long-lasting interest (often lifelong) that autistic people are very focused on [#34, msg #37].
   - Function: helps autistic people understand the world around them and regulate their emotions; can help perceive the world and better understand emotions and relationships [#34, msg #37].
   - Significance: incredibly important to autistic people and often felt as part of their identity; usually a big source of joy [#37].
   - Social side: sometimes it can be the only topic an autistic person is able to talk about, especially if they are not very good at masking [#37].
   - Life impact: often stays for life and can influence the choice of university, job and similar [#37].
   - Shape over time: focused, narrow and long-term; never disappears completely but is not necessarily equally intense in different periods of life [#37].
2. Hyperfixation — ADHD, days to months.
   - What: an intense, all-consuming interest lasting from a few days to a few months (sometimes people speak of several years) [#35, msg #37]. (The card says "до нескольких месяцев (или лет)" — up to several months or years.)
   - Objects: shows, film franchises, books, ideas, food and even people [#37].
   - Mechanism: during a hyperfixation you absorb information at enormous speed and the brain gets the stimulation it was lacking — the card frames it as helping the brain cope with understimulation (недостимуляция). When the novelty wears off, interest gradually fades [#35, msg #37].
   - Who: usually associated with ADHD but can occur in other neurodivergent people [#37].
3. Hyperfocus — ADHD, hours.
   - What: a short (up to a few hours) feeling/state of deep concentration on something, during which it is almost impossible to tear yourself away, and you neglect all other needs including basic ones — food, sleep and work [#36, msg #37].
   - Object: you can hyperfocus on your hyperfixation, but also on other things, for example cleaning or reading a story [#37].
   - Who: usually linked to ADHD but can occur in other neurodivergent people [#37].

Key distinguishing axis: duration (lifelong → months → hours) and nature (an identity-level interest vs a novelty-driven interest vs a state of concentration). Hyperfocus is a state that can attach to a hyperfixation or to anything else, whereas special interests and hyperfixations are interests [#37].

### Detailed insights

#### What "AuDHD" means and the history of the diagnosis
АуСДВГ (AuDHD) is the term used to denote autism + ADHD together, because these neurodivergences often occur together and share many traits [#65, msg #70]. The author explains that research on autism together with ADHD is a very new field: until 2013 they were considered two mutually exclusive diagnoses — if you had ADHD you could not have autism, and vice versa. The latest research shows that autism and ADHD very often occur together [#70]. She writes as "человек, который сам живет с ауСДВГ" (a person who herself lives with AuDHD) and knows first-hand what living at the intersection of these neurodivergences is like [#70].

#### Rare "cancelling out" vs the usual heavier burden
In rare cases autism and ADHD symptoms seem to cancel each other out: a person with AuDHD may have less visible social difficulties thanks to (ADHD) sociability, or a weaker drive toward routine because of disorganization and spontaneity [#70]. But most often people with AuDHD have a harder time than people with only autism or only ADHD, and experience more serious functional impairment [#69, msg #70]. In such cases the usual advice is to work first on reducing ADHD symptoms — for example by trying medication, individual psychotherapy or skills training [#69, msg #70].

#### Why rest is hard with AuDHD
A post forwarded from the channel Доказательная Жабля describes the author's weekend spent hyperfocusing on rest for neurodivergent people, trying to understand why rest is so hard with AuDHD [#361]. Her description of the mechanism:
- It is as if two different people live inside — "одному нужна стимуляция и веселье, а другому — покой и тишина" (one needs stimulation and fun, the other calm and quiet) [#361].
- When you try to do nothing, the brain immediately starts throwing up ideas [#361].
- When you try to do something, you fall into thoughts unrelated to the task [#361].

### Evidence and numbers

- 22–83% of autistic children meet diagnostic criteria for ADHD (estimates; Sokolova et al., 2017, as cited by the author) [#26, img #66, msg #70].
- 30–65% of children with ADHD have pronounced / clinically significant levels of autistic traits (Sokolova et al., 2017) [#26, img #66, msg #70].
- In adults, the co-occurrence rate is 20–37%; the author notes there is still little research on adults and these are estimates from some studies [#66, msg #70].
- ADHD and autism share about 50–72% of contributing genetic factors [#66, msg #70].
- OCD risk is increased (30–50%) in ADHD and autism [#26].
- Alcohol dependence: 5–10 times more common with ADHD; 2 times more common in autistic people [#26].
- Before 2013 the two diagnoses were considered mutually exclusive [#70].

## Lesser-known autistic traits and co-occurring conditions

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's posts on how heterogeneous autism is and on traits and conditions that often accompany it but are less talked about: pathological demand avoidance (PDA), the so-called "sensitivity to injustice", dyspraxia (motor-coordination difficulties), asexuality, premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD), and the shared experiences of growing up autistic. The author (an autistic psychologist with diagnosed ASD + ADHD) frames these posts as aids to self-understanding and self-acceptance: knowing that not every autistic person has every trait, and that some struggles (clumsiness, avoidance, low sexual interest) have names and explanations, helps people accept a diagnosis and seek help [#299, #319].

### Core ideas

- Autism is heterogeneous; nobody has every trait. Many autistic people doubt their diagnosis because they don't see the full set of possible symptoms in themselves (e.g., everything except a need for routine), and this blocks them from accepting it and getting help. The author lists her own "missing" traits to counter this (see *Autistic traits the author does not have*) [#299].
- Autism contains coexisting opposites. Seemingly contradictory traits can sit in the same person — e.g., loving novelty yet needing parts of routine unchanged (see *Coexisting opposites*) [#264].
- PDA is a lack of ability, not stubbornness — a coping problem driven by a need for control and certainty, which cannot be fixed by orders or sanctions [#196].
- "Injustice sensitivity" is not a moral compass but cognitive inflexibility — black-and-white thinking multiplied by difficulties with cognitive empathy; it makes autistic people neither better nor worse than neurotypical people [#311, #312, #313, #314, #315].
- Dyspraxia is not "just clumsiness" — it is a brain-based difficulty planning and coordinating movement, reported in up to 80% of autistic people and up to 50% of people with ADHD, and it shapes how a person moves, which activities they join and how they perceive themselves and the world [#318, #319].
- Asexuality is markedly more common among autistic people, but autism does not cause asexuality — they are independent, valid identities [#386, #387].
- PMDD is far more common in neurodivergent people — up to 92% of autistic and up to 46% of menstruating people with ADHD experience PMDD symptoms [#63].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Autistic traits the author does not have (11-point self-inventory)
Purpose: to show, using her own case as someone diagnosed with ASD (+ADHD), that a person can be autistic while lacking many textbook autistic traits — "возможно, это поможет вам в принятии своего состояния" (maybe this will help you accept your condition). The traits she does NOT observe in herself [#299]:
1. No speech or intellectual developmental impairment, which occurs in some autistic people. She has a rhotacism ("картавлю" — can't roll her R's) but thinks it is unlikely to be linked to autism. She was not delayed as a child and, according to her parents, never had skill regression [#299].
2. She can mask and make eye contact when needed — though most often she does not want to [#299].
3. She can take turns in a dialogue and ask many questions about the other person. She now has no problem with talking nonstop about her interest without noticing what the other person thinks — although this did happen in her teens. She can sustain a topic the other person chose even if it doesn't interest her, or at least listen [#299].
4. People don't complain that she is overly direct in a way perceived as rude (e.g., saying what she thinks), so she counts this difficulty as absent [#299].
5. She usually understands hints, though she prefers people to say clearly in words what they want [#299].
6. No difficulty understanding proverbs, sayings, idioms and metaphors, especially in her native language [#299].
7. She feels emotional empathy in response to someone's pain and discomfort — sometimes even too strong, so she cannot watch certain videos or films if she notices a character's discomfort [#299].
8. No alexithymia: thanks to years of psychotherapy she distinguishes her emotions well [#299].
9. Good imagination: as a child she acted out many stories with toy animals and dolls, wrote stories and drew imaginary scenes [#299].
10. No (noticeable to her) behavioural rituals; no need to do things the same way. She can take different routes to different shops and then cook different food [#299].
11. Flexible attitude to her schedule: she gets up and goes to bed at different times and has no fixed daily routine — though she says it is more honest to say that ADHD makes any schedule hard for her. However, she can feel discomfort if she has planned rest and is asked to urgently do some tasks [#299].

The diagnostic rule she attaches: for a diagnosis you must have noticeable difficulties in the social sphere and patterns of restricted, repetitive or inflexible behaviour or interests — but not every autistic person has all existing manifestations of autism, because "Наши мозги и истории развития слишком сильно отличаются, чтобы мы все вели себя одинаково" (our brains and developmental histories differ too much for us all to behave the same way). She invites autistic readers to share which traits they don't have, and promises a similar post about ADHD [#299].

#### PDA profile — six characteristic features
PDA (ПИТ, pathological demand avoidance) was first described by British professor Elizabeth Newson in the early 1980s; the term has since been used by other researchers, but PDA is still not included in any official diagnostic manual (DSM-5 or ICD-10). Newson's position: "Речь идет о серьезной копинговой проблеме, которую необходимо признать. Эта проблема – отсутствие способности, а не упрямство. Эту проблему нельзя решить «сказав, что делать» или с помощью каких-то санкций" (This is a serious coping problem that must be recognised. It is a lack of ability, not stubbornness. It cannot be solved by "telling them what to do" or by sanctions) [#196].

Features of people with a PDA profile, per the forwarded post [#196]:
1. Resistance to ordinary demands of life — getting out of bed, brushing teeth and similar basic tasks.
2. Using social strategies to avoid demands — distraction, excuses, little jokes, talking one's way out ("забалтывание").
3. Sociability without understanding social context — these are the sociable autistic people of whom it is often said that they "don't look autistic".
4. Impulsivity and frequent mood changes.
5. Finding comfort in role play and pretending.
6. Obsessive behaviour, obsession with other people. (The author jokes, crossed out, that she recently dived deep into the lore of the band "Ранетки".)

Proposed cause: "Говорят, что от ПИТ страдают те, кому не хватает контроля над ситуацией и условиями" (It is said that those who lack control over the situation and conditions suffer from PDA) — note the hedge "говорят" (it is said) [#196].

#### PDA survey of 240 people — needs and self-reports
An express survey of 240 people with PDA cited in the forwarded post (published on aspergers.ru) found [#196]:
1. 90%: "Мне необходим контроль над собственной жизнью" (I need control over my own life).
2. 88%: "Мне нужно знать, что происходит / я не могу терпеть неопределённость" (I need to know what's happening / I can't tolerate uncertainty).
3. 87%: "Мне нужен доступ к тихим местам" (I need access to quiet places).
4. 72%: "Избегание требований мешает мне делать то, что нравится" (Demand avoidance stops me doing things I like). The author's crossed-out aside: once she could not sit down to her thesis for a month because she was writing her second novel.
5. 24%: "Я способен работать, чтобы получать заработок" (I am able to work to earn a living).
6. 84%: "Я могу достичь многого, но мне нужны пространство и автономия, чтобы делать это по-своему" (I can achieve a lot, but I need space and autonomy to do it my own way).

#### Injustice sensitivity as cognitive inflexibility
Purpose: to re-examine the common claim that autistic people have a special "sensitivity to (in)justice". The channel author presents the view of Megan (The Neurocuriosity Club, YouTube) as one she found very interesting [#315].

1. The usual description. Injustice sensitivity is attributed to autistic people as a heightened emotional reaction to any situation the person perceives as wrong and unfair toward someone (or something). It is usually described as a positive trait that helps autistic people be aware of and give more weight to equality, fair treatment and other aspects of justice [#315].
2. Justice is subjective. Ideas of justice are shaped by upbringing and personal values. No two autistic people are alike, so no two ideas of justice are alike. Example: a person may believe all wasps are evil and deserve destruction — for them "уничтожение ос = справедливость" (destroying wasps = justice), and the existence of wasps is unfair to the people they sting [#312, #315].
3. So the reaction is to personal beliefs, not objective injustice. Autistic injustice sensitivity does not reflect a reaction to real, objective injustice but to the person's individual ideas of what is "good" and "bad", "moral" and "immoral". Megan stresses that autistic people are not innately more just or ethical: "Дело не в справедливости как таковой" (it's not about justice as such), but about a stable, inflexible idea of "how it should be" and following one's convictions [#313, #315].
4. The underlying trait is cognitive inflexibility (rigidity): a thinking style in which it can be hard to switch between ideas, points of view and ways of solving problems. Formula from the cards: "Когнитивная негибкость = черно-белое мышление + особенности эмпатии" (cognitive inflexibility = black-and-white thinking + empathy differences). Its two components [#314, #315]:
   - Black-and-white thinking — a thinking distortion present in some autistic people: perceiving situations, people or events in extreme categories — "всё или ничего" (all or nothing), "так или никак вообще" (this way or not at all), "хорошо или плохо" (good or bad). It can promote rigid rule-following: if there is a rule, it must not be broken under any circumstances. Hence nuances, doubts and "grey zones" are hard to perceive [#315].
   - Empathy differences characteristic of some autistic people can make it hard to understand what others think and why they make particular decisions — so looking at a problem from different points of view is not easy [#315].
5. Conclusion. Per Megan, injustice sensitivity is not a moral compass but cognitive inflexibility — "черно-белое мышление помноженное на трудности с когнитивной эмпатией" (black-and-white thinking multiplied by difficulties with cognitive empathy). With this trait one can end up at any end of the political spectrum; it makes us neither better nor worse than neurotypical people — it is just another difference [#315].
6. Practical implication. It is therefore important to reflect on where our beliefs come from and to question our ideas of justice, even when that is hard. The author asks readers which term they prefer: "injustice sensitivity" or "cognitive inflexibility" [#311, #315].

#### Dyspraxia — definition, symptoms and recommendations
Definition: dyspraxia is an impairment of the brain's ability to plan and coordinate physical movements, affecting both gross motor skills (e.g., balance, walking) and fine motor skills (e.g., writing, using cutlery) [#319].
- Gross motor skills cover walking, running, climbing, keeping balance, throwing and catching objects and other large body movements; fine motor skills cover small precise hand movements — knitting and weaving, writing and drawing, grasping small objects with fingers, eating with cutlery [#318].
- Mechanism: research suggests dyspraxia may be linked to features of brain maturation, including insufficient activation of brain areas involved in motor learning, and differences in development of the motor and sensory pathways of the nervous system [#319].
- Exclusion: motor difficulties caused by other conditions and features — such as cerebral palsy (ДЦП), multiple sclerosis, hypermobility — do not count as dyspraxia [#319].

Symptoms in children [#319]:
1. Delay in early motor milestones (crawling, walking, toilet training).
2. Difficulty going up and down stairs.
3. Poor balance — the child may bump into objects, fall often or seem clumsy.
4. Difficulty with sports and exercises such as cycling, jumping, catching, throwing or kicking a ball; the child may avoid these activities because of coordination difficulties.
5. Poor handwriting, difficulty drawing/colouring and using scissors compared with peers.
6. Difficulty dressing, doing up buttons, brushing teeth and tying shoelaces.

Symptoms in adults may look like [#319]:
1. Poor posture.
2. Problems maintaining balance and with movement; gait peculiarities.
3. Difficulty with hand-eye coordination (catching a ball, playing musical instruments or video games).
4. Fast fatigue when doing habitual actions.
5. Trouble learning new motor skills (new gym exercises, dancing).
6. Difficulty writing or using a keyboard.
7. Difficulty with self-care and housework.

Self-screening: if you suspect dyspraxia you can take the free online NeuroKnowHow Adult Dyspraxia Questionnaire (in English), which helps assess dyspraxic traits from childhood to the present. Caveat: a questionnaire is not enough for a diagnosis, and the author is not sure adults can even obtain this diagnosis in Russia [#319].

Recommendations (six) [#319]:
1. Read more about other people's experience.
2. Seek help from an occupational therapist (эрготерапевт) — a specialist who helps develop motor and everyday-living skills.
3. Look for physical activities you find interesting — even simple regular exercise helps improve balance and confidence in your body.
4. Take up something that develops fine motor skills: drawing, knitting, beadwork.
5. Use adaptive tools when needed: special grips for pens and pencils to make writing more comfortable, non-slip mats, writing software (e.g., speech-to-text).
6. Adapt the environment: e.g., buy protective impact-absorbing corner guards for furniture and door frames.

#### Asexuality — definition and spectrum
The author wrote this two-part post to link autism awareness (April 2) with International Asexuality Day, to explain how the two relate [#386].

Definition: asexuality is "*отсутствие сексуального влечения*" (the absence of sexual attraction) or "*сниженное сексуальное влечение*" (reduced sexual attraction) to other people — so it covers both a total absence and a lowered level of attraction [#386]. The asexual spectrum includes people who:
1. feel no desire at all to have sex with others [#386];
2. feel such desire only in certain periods [#386];
3. feel sexual attraction only to people with whom they have formed close, trusting relationships [#386].

Clarifications:
- Asexuality does not mean an absence of romantic feelings — asexual people can want closeness, relationships and love; the absence concerns sexual attraction, not the wish for intimacy [#386].
- It does not mean a uniform attitude to sex: for some sex is a matter of indifference, some don't mind having it occasionally, and some feel disgust toward it [#386].
- It is not considered an illness or a paraphilia and does not need treatment. Most researchers treat it as a sexual orientation, as natural as any other [#387].
- Some survivors of sexualised violence identify as "asexual", but trauma is usually not considered the root cause of asexuality [#387].

#### Four possible reasons asexuality is more common in autistic people
The author offers several possible reasons, each illustrated with quotes from autistic people (from Reddit) [#387]:
1. Difficulty recognising one's emotions and bodily sensations. Autistic people more often cannot understand or name what feelings and sensations they have, which also affects understanding attraction: "Я не совсем понимаю, что такое сексуальное влечение… Я никогда не испытываю сексуального влечения к конкретным людям" (I don't quite understand what sexual attraction is… I never feel sexual attraction to specific people).
2. Sensory features. For some autistic people sex is too overloading or, conversely, not stimulating enough: "Я нахожу секс одновременно скучным и перегружающим." (I find sex both boring and overwhelming.)
3. Social norms. Autistic people are often less oriented toward social norms around sex and relationships, which lets them explore their identity more honestly: "Аутичные люди… подвергают сомнению социальные нормы и не следуют им автоматически." (Autistic people… question social norms and don't follow them automatically.); "Мы больше склонны саморефлексии и интроспекции." (We are more inclined to self-reflection and introspection.); "Я, честно говоря, не понимаю, какое отношение секс имеет к любви." (Honestly, I don't understand what sex has to do with love.)
4. Social motivation. Communication difficulties and frequent rejection can lead an autistic person to seek social contact less overall and therefore not look for sexual partners, which can also lead to self-identifying as "asexual": "Многие аутичные люди ведут более асоциальны или избегают отношений… поэтому у них нет интереса и к сексуальным отношениям" (Many autistic people lead more asocial [lives] or avoid relationships… so they have no interest in sexual relationships either) [sic in source grammar].

Conclusion: this is not a cause-and-effect relationship — autism does not cause asexuality; they are independent, valid identities. Research also found that asexual autistic women reported greater sexual satisfaction in their relationships (asexuality being a spectrum) and lower anxiety than other groups, so "асексуальность может быть источником спокойствия и гармонии" (asexuality can be a source of calm and harmony) [#387].

### Detailed insights

#### Coexisting opposites in autism
A translated card (original by @allforthedopamine) titled "Реальность аутизма: сочетающиеся противоположности" (the reality of autism: coexisting opposites) lists four pairs [#264]:
1. You can chat easily for a long time with a couple of people, but communication with most people is very hard.
2. You express lots of emotion about special interests, but struggle to show emotions in other situations.
3. High pain threshold, but a light touch can feel painful.
4. You love novelty, but certain parts of routine must stay unchanged.

The author adds a fifth example in the caption: you listen to music at full volume, but if the neighbours suddenly breathe loudly — unbearable (sensory sensitivity depends on whether the sound is self-chosen) [#264].

#### PDA — the author's personal experience
The author of the forwarded post (Аспергейша) notes the irony that she long avoided writing about PDA, because reading up on it would make her recall all the situations in her life when she avoided demands, which is painful. #### Dyspraxia — the author's own story
The author has always noticed she is very clumsy: as a child she struggled in PE, fell on flat ground, danced badly, didn't play team games and could tell right from left only by a mole on her left hand. She also had fine-motor problems: terrible, untidy handwriting, messy eating with spoon and fork (often helping with her hands), wearing Velcro sneakers for a long time because laces were hard, and even difficulty controlling her tongue muscles (she still has a rhotacism). She does not know whether she has dyspraxia, but now knows what it is and that it is not "just clumsiness": it affects how we move, which activities we engage in, and thus how we perceive ourselves and the world — and the more we know about dyspraxia and motor difficulties, the better we can help ourselves and other people. The common joke about neurodivergent people constantly bumping into furniture has a basis [#318, #319].

#### PMDD
PMDD (ПМДР) is a cyclical, hormonally driven mood disorder — in effect a very severe form of premenstrual syndrome. Women with it describe their life like this: "Раз в месяц я будто нажимаю на кнопку самоуничтожения и наблюдаю, как наполненная и приносящая удовлетворение жизнь разрушается вокруг меня" (Once a month it's as if I press a self-destruct button and watch a full and satisfying life collapse around me); then at some point the state passes and for another two weeks they try to pick up the pieces. The forwarded post (Чистые Когниции) points to its article on symptoms, causes and treatments [#64].

- ★ Pure Cognitions (Чистые Когниции) — article on PMDD (premenstrual dysphoric disorder) (article) — Article by Anna Bondarenko (edited by Olga Kravchenko) on the symptoms, causes and treatment of PMDD, a severe hormonal mood disorder, as a co-occurring condition in neurodivergent people. Shared by the channel as further reading. [#64] → <https://teletype.in/@purecognitions/qhvE8wH-wIt>
- ★ IDRlabs asexuality test (Russian version) (tool) — Online test for asexuality that the post offers to readers; the author says she does not know how good it is and asks readers to suggest a better one. [#387] → <https://www.idrlabs.com/ru/asexuality/test.php?>
- ★ NeuroKnowHow — Adult dyspraxia questionnaire (tool) — Free online test in English that helps assess dyspraxic traits from childhood to the present. Offered as a first step for those who suspect dyspraxia, with the caveat that it is not a diagnosis. [#319] → <https://neuroknowhow.com/dyspraxia/adult-dyspraxia-questionnaire/>
- ★ PDA profile questionnaire (aspergers.ru) (tool) — Self-assessment questionnaire for the PDA (pathological demand avoidance) profile, offered to readers who recognise themselves in the description. It is a self-check, not a diagnosis. [#196] → <https://www.aspergers.ru/node/469>
