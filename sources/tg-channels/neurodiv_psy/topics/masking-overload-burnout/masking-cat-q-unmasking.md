# Masking, the CAT-Q questionnaire and unmasking

## What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel taught about autistic masking (camouflaging): the hidden cost of masking for the masker, a complete Russian-language self-report tool for measuring it (the CAT-Q, translated and adapted by the channel author Юлия Ан with editor Фрэнки Друг), and the practice of unmasking — showing neurodivergent traits more openly, and accepting that some people will then dislike you. According to the source, masking is a survival mechanism learned especially by people diagnosed late or not at all, it is associated with psychological stress and problems in functioning, and the lower one's masking score the better [msg #52, #192, #258, #259].

## Core ideas

- **Invisible autism is not mild autism.** When others say "you don't look autistic", it is because the person has spent most of their life minimising autism's impact on others — for others' sake — while feeling it twice as strongly themselves [img #52].
- **Masking can be measured.** The CAT-Q estimates how much a person over 16 masks and/or compensates for autistic traits in social interaction; it is not a diagnostic tool and its Russian version is not standardised, so results need caution [msg #189, file #192].
- **More masking is worse, not better.** In the questionnaire's interpretation, higher scores reflect "better" masking but are linked to psychological stress and functional problems, so ideally one's score should be as low as possible [file #192].
- **Masking was learned for survival.** Undiagnosed autistic people quickly learned to adjust their personality to others to fit in, because their behaviour was treated as unacceptable; as adults they keep believing they must mask to be liked [img #258, #259].
- **Unmasking means accepting being disliked.** One of the hardest parts of unmasking is learning not to be liked; being disliked is not your responsibility and does not mean you are wrong or guilty [img #257, #260, #261].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### CAT-Q — Camouflaging Autistic Traits Questionnaire (Russian version)
**What it is and who it is for.** «Опросник камуфляжа аутичных черт (Camouflaging Autistic Traits Questionnaire, CAT-Q)» (Camouflaging Autistic Traits Questionnaire) is a questionnaire about camouflaging — masking — in the social behaviour of people over 16. It helps assess how strongly you mask and/or compensate for your autistic traits during social interactions. The author notes it is especially relevant for women and nonbinary people. In the questionnaire's own description it is a self-report method used to assess strategies of compensating for and masking autistic traits during social interactions. Original authors: Laura Hull et al.; translation and adaptation by Юлия Ан; editor Фрэнки Друг [msg #189, file #192].

**What it is not.** «Опросник не служит для диагностики аутизма.» ("The questionnaire is not for diagnosing autism.") It helps one explore one's individual autistic features. The author stresses that results should be treated with caution because «опросник не был апробирован и стандартизирован на русскоязычной выборке!» ("the questionnaire has not been tested and standardised on a Russian-speaking sample!") [msg #189].

**How to fill it in.** Read each statement and choose the answer that best describes your experience during social interactions, marking the matching cell with a tick or a cross. The form has fields for name (or pseudonym) and date of completion [file #192]. The 7-point answer scale:
1. Strongly disagree («Совершенно не согласен») — 1 point
2. Disagree — 2 points
3. Partly disagree — 3 points
4. Can neither agree nor disagree — 4 points
5. Partly agree — 5 points
6. Agree — 6 points
7. Fully agree («Полностью согласен») — 7 points
[file #192]

**The 25 statements** (English rendering of the Russian items; reverse-scored items marked *, factor in brackets: C = Compensation, M = Masking, A = Assimilation) [file #192]:
1. When I talk to someone, I consciously copy their body language or facial expression. [C]
2. I monitor my body language and facial expression to seem relaxed. [M]
3. I rarely feel the need to pretend (play a role) in order to get through a social situation.* [A]
4. I have developed a script that I follow in social situations. [C]
5. I repeat phrases I have heard from others in exactly the form I first heard them. [C]
6. I adjust my body language and facial expression so as to look interested in the person I am talking to. [M]
7. In social situations I feel as if I am "playing a role" rather than being myself. [A]
8. During social interactions I use behaviour patterns I learned by watching other people interact. [C]
9. I always think about the impression I make on other people. [M]
10. I need other people's support in order to take part in communication. [A]
11. I practise my facial expressions and body language so that they look natural. [C]
12. I do not feel the need to look other people in the eyes if I do not want to.* [M]
13. I have to force myself to interact with people when I am in social situations. [A]
14. I have tried to improve my understanding of social skills by watching other people. [C]
15. I monitor my body language and/or facial expression to look interested in the person I am talking to. [M]
16. In social situations I try to find ways to avoid interacting with other people. [A]
17. I have studied the rules of social interaction to improve my social skills. [C]
18. I am always aware of the impression I make on other people. [M]
19. I feel I can be myself when I am with other people.* [A]
20. I study the facial expressions and movements/gestures of people interacting with each other while watching TV, films or reading fiction. [C]
21. I adjust my body language and facial expressions so as to look relaxed. [M]
22. When I talk with people, I feel the conversation flows naturally.* [A]
23. I have spent some time learning social skills from TV series and films, and I try to apply them in communication. [C]
24. In social situations I do not pay attention to my gestures/movements and facial expression.* [M]
25. In social situations I feel as if I am pretending to be "normal". [A]

### CAT-Q scoring and interpretation
1. **Score each answer 1–7**, where 1 = "Strongly disagree" and 7 = "Fully agree" [file #192].
2. **Reverse-score the starred items 3, 12, 19, 22 and 24** (7 down to 1): for these, 7 = "Strongly disagree" and 1 = "Fully agree". These are the statements phrased in the non-masking direction (e.g. feeling one can be oneself, conversation flowing naturally), so agreeing with them means less camouflaging [file #192].
3. **Add up all 25 items.** The total ranges from 25 to 175; a higher score reflects «лучшую маскировку» ("better masking") [file #192].
4. **Apply the threshold:** «Общий балл 100 или выше указывает на то, что вы маскируете аутичные черты.» ("A total score of 100 or higher indicates that you mask autistic traits.") [file #192]
5. **Read the meaning of a high score correctly:** higher scores are associated with psychological stress and problems in functioning, «поэтому в идеале вам нужно, чтобы ваш балл был как можно ниже» ("so ideally you want your score to be as low as possible") [file #192].
6. **Interpret with caution**, because the questionnaire is not standardised for a Russian-speaking sample [file #192].
7. **Optionally compute the three factor subscores** (next framework) and compare them, and the total, with the English-sample means (see *Evidence and numbers*) [file #192].

### CAT-Q three-factor structure
The 25 items split into three factors, each describing a different kind of camouflaging strategy [file #192]:
1. **Compensation («Компенсация») — items 1, 4, 5, 8, 11, 14, 17, 20 and 23 (9 items).** Strategies used to actively compensate for difficulties in social situations. Examples given: copying body language and facial expressions, learning social cues from films and books [file #192].
2. **Masking («Маскировка») — items 2, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21 and 24 (8 items).** Strategies used to hide autistic characteristics or to portray a non-autistic persona. Examples given: changing the face and body so they look confident and/or relaxed, forced eye contact [file #192].
3. **Assimilation («Ассимиляция») — items 3, 7, 10, 13, 16, 19, 22 and 25 (8 items).** Strategies used to try to fit in with others in social situations. Examples given: pretending, avoiding interaction, or forcing oneself to interact with others [file #192].

### Unmasking — what it is and what it involves
**Definition.** The author defines unmasking: «анмаскинг (или размаскировка) - это процесс, при котором мы стараемся меньше скрывать свои нейроотличные черты в общении» ("unmasking is the process in which we try to hide our neurodivergent traits less in communication") [msg #257].

**Concrete unmasking behaviours named by the author** [msg #257]:
1. Stimming more («больше стиммим»).
2. Putting in earplugs when it is noisy.
3. Asking again when something is unclear.

**The inner critic that persists.** The author reports noticing that she masks less in many situations, yet a voice in her head still demands: «"Смотри в глаза!", "Следи за лицом", "Убери фиджет в рюкзак, а то что люди подумают!"» ("Look them in the eyes!", "Watch your face", "Put the fidget in your backpack, or what will people think!"). The lesson: reducing masking behaviourally does not immediately silence the internalised demands to mask — the same demands that CAT-Q items about eye contact and facial monitoring measure [msg #257].

**Unmasking is a choice of priorities.** The author asks readers whether they are trying to mask less, whether they currently have other priorities, or whether masking is for them «вопрос выживания» ("a matter of survival") — i.e., she frames unmasking as optional and context-dependent, not an obligation [msg #257].

**The "learning not to be liked" sequence** (carousel by @elenacarrOll, translated by the author) [img #257, #258, #259, #260, #261]:
1. **The hardest part:** «Одна из самых непростых вещей в анмаскинге - это научиться не нравиться людям» ("One of the most difficult things in unmasking is learning not to be liked by people") — rendered here from the card text spread over lines [img #257].
2. **Where masking came from:** those of us whose autism went undiagnosed for a long time (or still is undiagnosed) very quickly learned to adjust our personality to those around us in order to fit in; masking became a survival mechanism [img #258].
3. **Why it mattered:** this survival mechanism can be very important for autistic people because we learn that our behaviour is often considered unacceptable in society [img #259].
4. **The belief that outlives its usefulness:** when we grow up, we often keep holding the belief that we need to mask in order to be liked by people [img #259].
5. **Why the first unmasked steps feel unsafe:** we may feel especially unsafe the first time we risk showing our real self and not being liked; for some this means dropping a "pleasant person" mask that has been taking root for 20–30 years [img #260].
6. **The reframe:** although unmasking may seem terribly frightening and uncomfortable, being disliked by someone is not your responsibility; nor does it mean you are wrong or guilty in some way. It is part of life, and you are doing the right thing by unmasking [img #261].

## Detailed insights

### The hidden cost of "not looking autistic"
A translated comic by Nicole (Sensory Stories) captures the core mechanism of masking's cost. To "Они: Ты не выглядишь аутичной!" ("Them: You don't look autistic!"), the speaker answers that you don't see it because her autism mainly affects *her*: she spent most of her life minimising its impact on others — «ради них, не ради себя» ("for their sake, not for mine") — and so now the other person doesn't see it, «но я ощущаю в два раза сильнее» ("but I feel it twice as strongly"). The lesson: the invisibility of autistic traits is produced by effort that is shifted onto the autistic person, and the cost to them grows rather than disappears. The channel posted it under the tags #поддержка (support) and #аутичныемемы (autistic memes) [img #52].

### Who masks more, according to the CAT-Q materials
The author states that measuring camouflaging is especially relevant for women and nonbinary people [msg #189]. The English-sample norms reproduced in the questionnaire (see *Evidence and numbers*) show autistic women and nonbinary people with the highest total scores [file #192].

## Evidence and numbers

**Mean CAT-Q scores on an English-speaking sample** (from the questionnaire's last page; format autistic | non-autistic) [file #192]:
- Women — total 124.35 | 90.87; Compensation 41.85 | 27.18; Masking 37.87 | 34.69; Assimilation 44.63 | 29.00.
- Men — total 109.64 | 96.89; Compensation 36.81 | 30.06; Masking 32.90 | 36.34; Assimilation 39.93 | 30.48.
- Nonbinary — total 122.00 | 109.44; Compensation 43.50 | 35.48; Masking 36.06 | 38.70; Assimilation 39.88 | 35.26.

Reading the table as given: autistic groups score higher than non-autistic groups on the total, Compensation and Assimilation in every gender group; on the Masking factor, autistic women score higher than non-autistic women, while autistic men and autistic nonbinary people score lower than their non-autistic counterparts [file #192].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The CAT-Q is explicitly not a diagnostic instrument, and the Russian version has not been tested or standardised on a Russian-speaking sample; both the post and the form ask for cautious interpretation [msg #189, file #192].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the form names the threshold "100 or higher = masking autistic traits", but the English-sample mean for non-autistic nonbinary people (109.44) is above 100, so the threshold does not by itself separate autistic from non-autistic respondents; the source does not comment on this [file #192].
- (!) CONTRADICTION (minor): the form credits "Laura Hull et al., 2018" on every page, while the reference list gives the validation paper as Hull et al. (2019) [file #192].
- The questionnaire calls a higher score "better masking" yet says the goal is a score as low as possible because higher scores go with stress and functional problems — "better" refers to masking effectiveness, not wellbeing [file #192].
- The author frames unmasking as context-dependent: for some readers other priorities may come first, or masking may be a matter of survival [msg #257].
