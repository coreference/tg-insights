# Lesser-known autistic traits and co-occurring conditions

## What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's posts on how heterogeneous autism is and on traits and conditions that often accompany it but are less talked about: pathological demand avoidance (PDA), the so-called "sensitivity to injustice", dyspraxia (motor-coordination difficulties), asexuality, premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD), and the shared experiences of growing up autistic. The author (an autistic psychologist with diagnosed ASD + ADHD) frames these posts as aids to self-understanding and self-acceptance: knowing that not every autistic person has every trait, and that some struggles (clumsiness, avoidance, low sexual interest) have names and explanations, helps people accept a diagnosis and seek help [msg #299, #319].

## Core ideas

- **Autism is heterogeneous; nobody has every trait.** Many autistic people doubt their diagnosis because they don't see the full set of possible symptoms in themselves (e.g., everything except a need for routine), and this blocks them from accepting it and getting help. The author lists her own "missing" traits to counter this (see *Autistic traits the author does not have*) [msg #299].
- **Autism contains coexisting opposites.** Seemingly contradictory traits can sit in the same person — e.g., loving novelty yet needing parts of routine unchanged (see *Coexisting opposites*) [msg #264].
- **PDA is a lack of ability, not stubbornness** — a coping problem driven by a need for control and certainty, which cannot be fixed by orders or sanctions [msg #196].
- **"Injustice sensitivity" is not a moral compass but cognitive inflexibility** — black-and-white thinking multiplied by difficulties with cognitive empathy; it makes autistic people neither better nor worse than neurotypical people [msg #311, #312, #313, #314, #315].
- **Dyspraxia is not "just clumsiness"** — it is a brain-based difficulty planning and coordinating movement, reported in up to 80% of autistic people and up to 50% of people with ADHD, and it shapes how a person moves, which activities they join and how they perceive themselves and the world [msg #318, #319].
- **Asexuality is markedly more common among autistic people**, but autism does not cause asexuality — they are independent, valid identities [msg #386, #387].
- **PMDD is far more common in neurodivergent people** — up to 92% of autistic and up to 46% of menstruating people with ADHD experience PMDD symptoms [msg #63].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Autistic traits the author does not have (11-point self-inventory)
Purpose: to show, using her own case as someone diagnosed with ASD (+ADHD), that a person can be autistic while lacking many textbook autistic traits — "возможно, это поможет вам в принятии своего состояния" (maybe this will help you accept your condition). The traits she does NOT observe in herself [msg #299]:
1. **No speech or intellectual developmental impairment**, which occurs in some autistic people. She has a rhotacism ("картавлю" — can't roll her R's) but thinks it is unlikely to be linked to autism. She was not delayed as a child and, according to her parents, never had skill regression [msg #299].
2. **She can mask and make eye contact** when needed — though most often she does not want to [msg #299].
3. **She can take turns in a dialogue and ask many questions about the other person.** She now has no problem with talking nonstop about her interest without noticing what the other person thinks — although this did happen in her teens. She can sustain a topic the other person chose even if it doesn't interest her, or at least listen [msg #299].
4. **People don't complain that she is overly direct** in a way perceived as rude (e.g., saying what she thinks), so she counts this difficulty as absent [msg #299].
5. **She usually understands hints**, though she prefers people to say clearly in words what they want [msg #299].
6. **No difficulty understanding proverbs, sayings, idioms and metaphors**, especially in her native language [msg #299].
7. **She feels emotional empathy** in response to someone's pain and discomfort — sometimes even too strong, so she cannot watch certain videos or films if she notices a character's discomfort [msg #299].
8. **No alexithymia**: thanks to years of psychotherapy she distinguishes her emotions well [msg #299].
9. **Good imagination**: as a child she acted out many stories with toy animals and dolls, wrote stories and drew imaginary scenes [msg #299].
10. **No (noticeable to her) behavioural rituals**; no need to do things the same way. She can take different routes to different shops and then cook different food [msg #299].
11. **Flexible attitude to her schedule**: she gets up and goes to bed at different times and has no fixed daily routine — though she says it is more honest to say that ADHD makes any schedule hard for her. However, she can feel discomfort if she has planned rest and is asked to urgently do some tasks [msg #299].

The diagnostic rule she attaches: for a diagnosis you must have noticeable difficulties in the social sphere **and** patterns of restricted, repetitive or inflexible behaviour or interests — but not every autistic person has **all** existing manifestations of autism, because "Наши мозги и истории развития слишком сильно отличаются, чтобы мы все вели себя одинаково" (our brains and developmental histories differ too much for us all to behave the same way). She invites autistic readers to share which traits they don't have, and promises a similar post about ADHD [msg #299].

### PDA profile — six characteristic features
PDA (ПИТ, pathological demand avoidance) was first described by British professor Elizabeth Newson in the early 1980s; the term has since been used by other researchers, but PDA is still not included in any official diagnostic manual (DSM-5 or ICD-10). Newson's position: "Речь идет о серьезной копинговой проблеме, которую необходимо признать. Эта проблема – отсутствие способности, а не упрямство. Эту проблему нельзя решить «сказав, что делать» или с помощью каких-то санкций" (This is a serious coping problem that must be recognised. It is a lack of ability, not stubbornness. It cannot be solved by "telling them what to do" or by sanctions) [msg #196].

Features of people with a PDA profile, per the forwarded post [msg #196]:
1. **Resistance to ordinary demands of life** — getting out of bed, brushing teeth and similar basic tasks.
2. **Using social strategies to avoid demands** — distraction, excuses, little jokes, talking one's way out ("забалтывание").
3. **Sociability without understanding social context** — these are the sociable autistic people of whom it is often said that they "don't look autistic".
4. **Impulsivity and frequent mood changes.**
5. **Finding comfort in role play and pretending.**
6. **Obsessive behaviour, obsession with other people.** (The author jokes, crossed out, that she recently dived deep into the lore of the band "Ранетки".)

Proposed cause: "Говорят, что от ПИТ страдают те, кому не хватает контроля над ситуацией и условиями" (It is said that those who lack control over the situation and conditions suffer from PDA) — note the hedge "говорят" (it is said) [msg #196].

### PDA survey of 240 people — needs and self-reports
An express survey of 240 people with PDA cited in the forwarded post (published on aspergers.ru) found [msg #196]:
1. 90%: "Мне необходим контроль над собственной жизнью" (I need control over my own life).
2. 88%: "Мне нужно знать, что происходит / я не могу терпеть неопределённость" (I need to know what's happening / I can't tolerate uncertainty).
3. 87%: "Мне нужен доступ к тихим местам" (I need access to quiet places).
4. 72%: "Избегание требований мешает мне делать то, что нравится" (Demand avoidance stops me doing things I like). The author's crossed-out aside: once she could not sit down to her thesis for a month because she was writing her second novel.
5. 24%: "Я способен работать, чтобы получать заработок" (I am able to work to earn a living).
6. 84%: "Я могу достичь многого, но мне нужны пространство и автономия, чтобы делать это по-своему" (I can achieve a lot, but I need space and autonomy to do it my own way).

### Injustice sensitivity as cognitive inflexibility
Purpose: to re-examine the common claim that autistic people have a special "sensitivity to (in)justice". The channel author presents the view of Megan (The Neurocuriosity Club, YouTube) as one she found very interesting [msg #315].

1. **The usual description.** Injustice sensitivity is attributed to autistic people as a heightened emotional reaction to any situation the person perceives as wrong and unfair toward someone (or something). It is usually described as a positive trait that helps autistic people be aware of and give more weight to equality, fair treatment and other aspects of justice [msg #315].
2. **Justice is subjective.** Ideas of justice are shaped by upbringing and personal values. No two autistic people are alike, so no two ideas of justice are alike. Example: a person may believe all wasps are evil and deserve destruction — for them "уничтожение ос = справедливость" (destroying wasps = justice), and the existence of wasps is unfair to the people they sting [msg #312, #315].
3. **So the reaction is to personal beliefs, not objective injustice.** Autistic injustice sensitivity does not reflect a reaction to real, objective injustice but to the person's individual ideas of what is "good" and "bad", "moral" and "immoral". Megan stresses that autistic people are not innately more just or ethical: "Дело не в справедливости как таковой" (it's not about justice as such), but about a stable, inflexible idea of "how it should be" and following one's convictions [msg #313, #315].
4. **The underlying trait is cognitive inflexibility** (rigidity): a thinking style in which it can be hard to switch between ideas, points of view and ways of solving problems. Formula from the cards: "Когнитивная негибкость = черно-белое мышление + особенности эмпатии" (cognitive inflexibility = black-and-white thinking + empathy differences). Its two components [msg #314, #315]:
   - **Black-and-white thinking** — a thinking distortion present in some autistic people: perceiving situations, people or events in extreme categories — "всё или ничего" (all or nothing), "так или никак вообще" (this way or not at all), "хорошо или плохо" (good or bad). It can promote rigid rule-following: if there is a rule, it must not be broken under any circumstances. Hence nuances, doubts and "grey zones" are hard to perceive [msg #315].
   - **Empathy differences** characteristic of some autistic people can make it hard to understand what others think and why they make particular decisions — so looking at a problem from different points of view is not easy [msg #315].
5. **Conclusion.** Per Megan, injustice sensitivity is not a moral compass but cognitive inflexibility — "черно-белое мышление помноженное на трудности с когнитивной эмпатией" (black-and-white thinking multiplied by difficulties with cognitive empathy). With this trait one can end up at any end of the political spectrum; it makes us neither better nor worse than neurotypical people — it is just another difference [msg #315].
6. **Practical implication.** It is therefore important to reflect on where our beliefs come from and to question our ideas of justice, even when that is hard. The author asks readers which term they prefer: "injustice sensitivity" or "cognitive inflexibility" [msg #311, #315].

### Dyspraxia — definition, symptoms and recommendations
**Definition:** dyspraxia is an impairment of the brain's ability to plan and coordinate physical movements, affecting both gross motor skills (e.g., balance, walking) and fine motor skills (e.g., writing, using cutlery) [msg #319].
- Gross motor skills cover walking, running, climbing, keeping balance, throwing and catching objects and other large body movements; fine motor skills cover small precise hand movements — knitting and weaving, writing and drawing, grasping small objects with fingers, eating with cutlery [msg #318].
- **Mechanism:** research suggests dyspraxia may be linked to features of brain maturation, including insufficient activation of brain areas involved in motor learning, and differences in development of the motor and sensory pathways of the nervous system [msg #319].
- **Exclusion:** motor difficulties caused by other conditions and features — such as cerebral palsy (ДЦП), multiple sclerosis, hypermobility — do not count as dyspraxia [msg #319].

**Symptoms in children** [msg #319]:
1. Delay in early motor milestones (crawling, walking, toilet training).
2. Difficulty going up and down stairs.
3. Poor balance — the child may bump into objects, fall often or seem clumsy.
4. Difficulty with sports and exercises such as cycling, jumping, catching, throwing or kicking a ball; the child may avoid these activities because of coordination difficulties.
5. Poor handwriting, difficulty drawing/colouring and using scissors compared with peers.
6. Difficulty dressing, doing up buttons, brushing teeth and tying shoelaces.

**Symptoms in adults may look like** [msg #319]:
1. Poor posture.
2. Problems maintaining balance and with movement; gait peculiarities.
3. Difficulty with hand-eye coordination (catching a ball, playing musical instruments or video games).
4. Fast fatigue when doing habitual actions.
5. Trouble learning new motor skills (new gym exercises, dancing).
6. Difficulty writing or using a keyboard.
7. Difficulty with self-care and housework.

**Self-screening:** if you suspect dyspraxia you can take the free online NeuroKnowHow Adult Dyspraxia Questionnaire (in English), which helps assess dyspraxic traits from childhood to the present. Caveat: a questionnaire is not enough for a diagnosis, and the author is not sure adults can even obtain this diagnosis in Russia [msg #319].

**Recommendations (six)** [msg #319]:
1. Read more about other people's experience.
2. Seek help from an occupational therapist (эрготерапевт) — a specialist who helps develop motor and everyday-living skills.
3. Look for physical activities you find interesting — even simple regular exercise helps improve balance and confidence in your body.
4. Take up something that develops fine motor skills: drawing, knitting, beadwork.
5. Use adaptive tools when needed: special grips for pens and pencils to make writing more comfortable, non-slip mats, writing software (e.g., speech-to-text).
6. Adapt the environment: e.g., buy protective impact-absorbing corner guards for furniture and door frames.

### Asexuality — definition and spectrum
The author wrote this two-part post to link autism awareness (April 2) with International Asexuality Day, to explain how the two relate [msg #386].

**Definition:** asexuality is "*отсутствие сексуального влечения*" (the absence of sexual attraction) or "*сниженное сексуальное влечение*" (reduced sexual attraction) to other people — so it covers both a total absence and a lowered level of attraction [msg #386]. The asexual spectrum includes people who:
1. feel no desire at all to have sex with others [msg #386];
2. feel such desire only in certain periods [msg #386];
3. feel sexual attraction only to people with whom they have formed close, trusting relationships [msg #386].

Clarifications:
- Asexuality does **not** mean an absence of romantic feelings — asexual people can want closeness, relationships and love; the absence concerns sexual attraction, not the wish for intimacy [msg #386].
- It does **not** mean a uniform attitude to sex: for some sex is a matter of indifference, some don't mind having it occasionally, and some feel disgust toward it [msg #386].
- It is not considered an illness or a paraphilia and does not need treatment. Most researchers treat it as a sexual orientation, as natural as any other [msg #387].
- Some survivors of sexualised violence identify as "asexual", but trauma is usually not considered the root cause of asexuality [msg #387].

### Four possible reasons asexuality is more common in autistic people
The author offers several possible reasons, each illustrated with quotes from autistic people (from Reddit) [msg #387]:
1. **Difficulty recognising one's emotions and bodily sensations.** Autistic people more often cannot understand or name what feelings and sensations they have, which also affects understanding attraction: "Я не совсем понимаю, что такое сексуальное влечение… Я никогда не испытываю сексуального влечения к конкретным людям" (I don't quite understand what sexual attraction is… I never feel sexual attraction to specific people).
2. **Sensory features.** For some autistic people sex is too overloading or, conversely, not stimulating enough: "Я нахожу секс одновременно скучным и перегружающим." (I find sex both boring and overwhelming.)
3. **Social norms.** Autistic people are often less oriented toward social norms around sex and relationships, which lets them explore their identity more honestly: "Аутичные люди… подвергают сомнению социальные нормы и не следуют им автоматически." (Autistic people… question social norms and don't follow them automatically.); "Мы больше склонны саморефлексии и интроспекции." (We are more inclined to self-reflection and introspection.); "Я, честно говоря, не понимаю, какое отношение секс имеет к любви." (Honestly, I don't understand what sex has to do with love.)
4. **Social motivation.** Communication difficulties and frequent rejection can lead an autistic person to seek social contact less overall and therefore not look for sexual partners, which can also lead to self-identifying as "asexual": "Многие аутичные люди ведут более асоциальны или избегают отношений… поэтому у них нет интереса и к сексуальным отношениям" (Many autistic people lead more asocial [lives] or avoid relationships… so they have no interest in sexual relationships either) [sic in source grammar].

Conclusion: this is **not** a cause-and-effect relationship — autism does not cause asexuality; they are independent, valid identities. Research also found that asexual autistic women reported greater sexual satisfaction in their relationships (asexuality being a spectrum) and lower anxiety than other groups, so "асексуальность может быть источником спокойствия и гармонии" (asexuality can be a source of calm and harmony) [msg #387].

## Detailed insights

### Coexisting opposites in autism
A translated card (original by @allforthedopamine) titled "Реальность аутизма: сочетающиеся противоположности" (the reality of autism: coexisting opposites) lists four pairs [img #264]:
1. You can chat easily for a long time with a couple of people, but communication with most people is very hard.
2. You express lots of emotion about special interests, but struggle to show emotions in other situations.
3. High pain threshold, but a light touch can feel painful.
4. You love novelty, but certain parts of routine must stay unchanged.

The author adds a fifth example in the caption: you listen to music at full volume, but if the neighbours suddenly breathe loudly — unbearable (sensory sensitivity depends on whether the sound is self-chosen) [msg #264].

### Growing up with autism — starter pack
A meme card "Взросление с аутизмом: стартер-пак" (growing up with autism: starter pack), captioned "your experience may differ", lists typical experiences [msg #228, img #228]:
1. Easier to talk with adults than with other children.
2. Anxiety, depression and other mental-health issues.
3. Pacing back and forth, swinging on swings for hours, spinning on the spot.
4. 3:00 AM — a terrible sleep schedule.
5. Parents in denial of your problems.
6. No friends.
7. A gifted child, or barely scraping a C ("едва тянешь на 3").
8. Rejection and/or bullying.
9. Everything is bad with cleaning, cooking, hygiene.
10. Practising social interactions and "stealing" mannerisms of characters and friends.
11. Eating the same thing for months/years.
12. Parents consider you a "бытовым инвалидом" (household invalid — unable to manage everyday life).

### PDA — the author's personal experience
The author of the forwarded post (Аспергейша) notes the irony that she long avoided writing about PDA, because reading up on it would make her recall all the situations in her life when she avoided demands, which is painful. Since childhood she tried to evade others' demands: she always felt she would fail anyway and was afraid of being seen; evading made her feel as if she became invisible, at least for a while. She still feels bad recalling this. Lesson implied by the post: avoidance is rooted in fear and a sense of inevitable failure, consistent with Newson's "lack of ability, not stubbornness" [msg #196].

### Dyspraxia — the author's own story
The author has always noticed she is very clumsy: as a child she struggled in PE, fell on flat ground, danced badly, didn't play team games and could tell right from left only by a mole on her left hand. She also had fine-motor problems: terrible, untidy handwriting, messy eating with spoon and fork (often helping with her hands), wearing Velcro sneakers for a long time because laces were hard, and even difficulty controlling her tongue muscles (she still has a rhotacism). She does not know whether she has dyspraxia, but now knows what it is and that it is not "just clumsiness": it affects how we move, which activities we engage in, and thus how we perceive ourselves and the world — and the more we know about dyspraxia and motor difficulties, the better we can help ourselves and other people. The common joke about neurodivergent people constantly bumping into furniture has a basis [msg #318, #319].

### PMDD
PMDD (ПМДР) is a cyclical, hormonally driven mood disorder — in effect a very severe form of premenstrual syndrome. Women with it describe their life like this: "Раз в месяц я будто нажимаю на кнопку самоуничтожения и наблюдаю, как наполненная и приносящая удовлетворение жизнь разрушается вокруг меня" (Once a month it's as if I press a self-destruct button and watch a full and satisfying life collapse around me); then at some point the state passes and for another two weeks they try to pick up the pieces. The forwarded post (Чистые Когниции) points to its article on symptoms, causes and treatments [msg #64].

## Evidence and numbers

- PMDD: up to 92% of autistic people and up to 46% of menstruating people with ADHD experience PMDD symptoms (no source study named) [msg #63].
- Dyspraxia: up to 80% of autistic people (Cassidy et al., 2016) and up to 50% of people with ADHD (Goulardins et al., 2017) have motor-skill impairments matching dyspraxia, "according to some studies" [msg #319].
- Asexuality: about 0.4%–1% of the general population vs 5–15% of autistic adults, by various estimates [msg #387].
- George & Stokes (2017): about 8.25% of autistic men identify as asexual vs 0.8% of neurotypical men; 22% of autistic women vs 1.5% of neurotypical women [msg #387].
- PDA survey figures: see *PDA survey of 240 people* [msg #196].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- PDA is not in any official diagnostic manual (DSM-5 or ICD-10); its cause ("lack of control") is stated as hearsay ("говорят") [msg #196].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the PDA "effective approaches" image was not available [msg #196].
- The injustice-sensitivity reframing is Megan's view, presented by the author as "probably" ("Вероятно") underlying the trait; the author leaves the terminology question open to readers [msg #315].
- Dyspraxia prevalence figures are "up to" figures from "some studies"; a questionnaire cannot diagnose, and adult diagnosis may be unavailable in Russia [msg #319].
- The asexuality reasons are offered as possible ("может быть несколько"), not established; the relationship is explicitly not causal. The author does not vouch for the linked asexuality test [msg #387].
- The PMDD figures in #63 are given without a named study [msg #63].
- The "starter pack" is a meme and the author reminds readers their experience may differ [msg #228].
- The author's no-traits list is one person's self-report; she notes some judgments rely on others (parents' memory, people not complaining) [msg #299].
