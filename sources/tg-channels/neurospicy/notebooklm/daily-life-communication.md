## Accommodations, self-checks and daily supports

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's practical material on adapting everyday life for autistic and ADHD people: a forwarded essay arguing that each person needs their own accommodations and that weaknesses can be compensated through collaboration; a card of accommodation examples; a multi-card "autistic state self-check" covering energy, emotions, sensory, physical and other needs; a card series on why food and eating are hard for autistic people (including "safe food"); a card series on jobs that fit autistic traits; and the channel author's recommendation of a free AI support chatbot. According to the source, it matters because generic advice often fails neurodivergent people, who then blame and push themselves, worsening the situation [#266].

### Core ideas

- Everyone is unique — including in weaknesses and needed accommodations. Uniqueness shows not only in rare abilities but in vulnerabilities and in the accommodations a person needs; so methods that help others may not help you, and this is not a failure of effort [#266].
- Collaboration can compensate deficits. If something does not work for you at all, look for people with whom it will work; even two people with deficits in the same areas can compensate each other [#266].
- Check in systematically. When an autistic person feels unwell, a structured self-check of energy, emotions, sensory, physical and other needs helps locate what is missing and what to do about it [#468, #469, #470, #471, #472, #473, #474].
- Eating difficulties in autism have concrete causes — sensory hypersensitivity, safe foods that stop feeling safe, interoception problems, executive function problems, changing food properties, demand avoidance, social demands at the table, GI problems and coordination [#699, #700, #701, #702, #703, #704, #705, #706].
- Autistic traits fit specific jobs. The cards map ten occupations to the traits that make them suitable (routine, attention to detail, pattern-finding, limited social interaction, hyperfocus) [#579, #580, #581, #582, #583, #584, #585].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Compensating deficits through collaboration
Source: a forwarded essay from the channel Кораблик Леськи titled "Жить- сложно." ("Living is hard.") [#266].

1. Recognise the concrete difficulties. The author's worked example is running your own social-media channel: you may achieve some success and then hit "сплошную стену" ("a solid wall"), losing the ability to keep developing it without really understanding why, since "всё же вроде нормально было" ("everything seemed fine"). This is especially true if you are not a "нейротипиком в вакууме" ("neurotypical in a vacuum") who has no trouble with everyday activity, motivation, concentration and putting thoughts into words. The listed difficulties [#266]:
   - Executive dysfunction and insecurity may not even let you start writing.
   - "Мы можем раниться об негативный фидбек от читающих" ("We can get hurt by negative feedback from readers").
   - We may be unable to keep up regular activity — and regularity is the key to a channel's growth.
   - Even shaping a thought so that it is comfortable to read is far from a simple task.
   - We may simply be unable to stand some kind of activity at all — for example writing texts or choosing illustrations.
   The author adds that there are "many, MANY" other difficulties beyond these [#266].
2. Understand why generic methods fail. The internet has an abundance of methods for overcoming difficulties, including neurodivergence-related ones, but if you have tried some you probably noticed they work well in maybe a third of cases. The reason given: literally every person is unique — contrary to the "ты никто и звать тебя никак" ("you are nobody and your name is nothing") messages many heard while growing up [#266].
3. Accept that uniqueness includes weaknesses. The catch is that uniqueness is expressed not only in "я могу то, чего не может почти никто больше" ("I can do what almost nobody else can") but also in weaknesses, vulnerabilities and "необходимых нам аккомодациях и адаптациях" ("the accommodations and adaptations we need"). The author rewrites the phrase ironically: "I can do what almost nobody else can — including falling into a non-functional state from something so trivial, in others' opinion, that I myself won't understand what's going on" [#266].
4. Avoid the self-blame trap. The danger is that we start blaming ourselves or pushing ourselves, aggravating an already difficult situation, with the thought "ну ведь остальным помогает – я просто плохо стараюсь!" ("it helps everyone else — I'm just not trying hard enough!"). The main thing is to accept the simple fact that we are all different [#266].
5. Find people with whom it works. "Если у вас что-то вот совсем-совсем не получается либо вызывает ощутимые сложности – попробуйте найти тех, вместе с кем у вас это получится." ("If something really doesn't work for you at all or causes noticeable difficulty, try to find those with whom it will work.") Reasoning: we are social beings, and many of us may need some form of interaction with others to unfold our strengths [#266].
6. Mutual compensation, even with shared deficits. The author's key claim: even with deficits in the same areas, collaboration can compensate them and produce "две здоровые, сильные личности" ("two healthy, strong personalities"). The author announces future posts on how people can cooperate to mutual benefit when both sides are aware of their deficits and ready to help each other [#266].

#### Autistic state self-check (Самопроверка аутичного состояния)
A card series by neurodivergent_lou, forwarded from Нейр∞тлично [#468]. Purpose: a set of questions to go through when you are not feeling right, each paired with concrete options. The sections in order:

1. "Каков мой уровень энергии?" (What is my energy level?) The card shows a row of batteries from almost empty (red) to full (green), with two branches [#469]:
- *Low battery* → ask: can you set aside time for rest or for an activity that will help restore your strength? The author's current favourite ways to recover: colouring, listening to music, walks in nature and watching series; and also simply lying in a dark room without any expectations — "тоже прекрасно" ("also wonderful") [#469].
- *High battery* → ask: how can you keep conserving your energy? Examples: take regular breaks, do not mask autistic traits when it is safe, and use your energy level to take care of yourself [#469].

2. "Как я себя чувствую?" (How do I feel?) Identify the emotion using an emotion wheel. The words on the wheel include: rejected, anxious, scared, mad, hurt, threatened, insecure, distant, guilty, anger, confused, despair, lonely, sad, surprise, startled, amazed, bored, disgust, excited, disappointed, joyful, awful, proud, avoidance, peaceful, optimistic [#470]. Then ask what usually helps you when things are hard. If it is hard to come up with options, the author shares what she uses for anxiety [#470]:
- Temperature — hold an ice cube or rinse your face with water.
- Intense exercise — running up and down stairs, jumping and similar.
- Paced breathing — inhale for 4 seconds, hold the breath for 4 seconds, exhale for 4 seconds.
- Progressive muscle relaxation — tense each muscle group in turn for 5 seconds, then relax it.

3. "Удовлетворяю ли я свои сенсорные потребности?" (Am I meeting my sensory needs?) First determine the direction [#471]:
- *If you are overloaded:* sit in a dark, quiet room; wear loose, comfortable clothes; use sunglasses and noise-cancelling headphones; try different kinds of stimming.
- *If you are understimulated:* use a weighted blanket; jump; watch a visually stimulating video; light a scented candle; hug.

4. "Как удовлетворяются мои физические потребности?" (How are my physical needs being met?) — part one [#472]:
- *Am I getting enough fluids?* If not, try drinking something — not necessarily water, choose whatever is comfortable for you — or ask someone to help you with a drink.
- *Did I get enough sleep, or do I feel tired?* If short on sleep, set aside time for a short nap. Sometimes, when there are sleep problems, sticking to a regular schedule helps.
- *Am I getting enough food?* Can you get something to eat that is comfortable and safe? Or ask someone to help with food? Setting a regular alarm to remind yourself to eat may be useful.

5. Physical needs — part two [#473]:
- *Have I taken care of my physical health?* Have you taken the medications you need? Can you ask for help getting them? Do you feel ill or are you in pain? Can you meet those needs?
- *Would light exercise help?* The card stresses that exercise may not be accessible or helpful for everyone. But if it helps you, can you take a walk or even do a seated exercise using a YouTube video?
- *Have I taken care of my hygiene needs?* "Здесь нет никакого осуждения" ("There is no judgement here"), but for some people taking care of hygiene can improve mood. Did you brush your teeth? Comb your hair? Shower? Can you make these activities more comfortable from a sensory point of view?

6. "Как обстоят дела с моими другими потребностями?" (How are my other needs?) [#474]:
- *Have I spent enough time alone? Or do I need to meet people to recharge?* Can you schedule this into your timetable?
- *Can I change my physical space?* For example turn off bright light, draw the curtains, remove excess things, reduce noise in the room. Can you adjust the temperature?
- *Have I spent time on my special interests?* If it is hard to find time for them, there are ways to integrate special interests into daily routine — for example watching a video about your interest while brushing teeth or making tea, or listening to a podcast while commuting.

#### Five + four reasons eating is hard for autistic people
A card series by neurodivergent_lou titled "Аутичны и голодны? Но никакая еда не кажется подходящей..." ("Autistic and hungry? But no food seems right...") [#699]. The reasons, in card order:

1. Sensory hypersensitivity. Autistic people are sometimes hypersensitive to sensory stimuli — textures, smells, colours — which can lead to aversion to certain foods. Eating food with an unsuitable texture, taste or smell can be "невероятно мучительным или болезненным" ("incredibly tormenting or painful") for autistic people — "это не просто дискомфорт" ("it is not just discomfort"). This differs from a neurotypical person simply disliking some food [#700].
2. Safe food that stops feeling safe. Some autistic people use the term "безопасная еда" ("safe food") or "та же самая еда" ("the same food") for food that brings comfort, causes no sensory problems and to which they are attached. But sometimes even safe food stops feeling safe — perhaps because we eat it so often that one day we get completely sick of it. Then we have to search for a new safe food; we can be very hungry while no food seems suitable — the situation the series is named after [#701].
3. Interoception problems. Interoception is the body's ability to provide information about its internal state; through it we feel body signals such as hunger. Autistic people often have problems with it, so it can be hard to tell when we are hungry and when full — which means we may eat less or more than others [#702].
4. Executive function difficulties. Executive functions are the set of skills that help us carry out everyday tasks and regulate and control our actions. Problems with them can make cooking difficult and complicate planning one's diet; it may also be hard to find motivation and energy for the task. As a result one has to rely on microwave food or easy-to-make dishes. Decision-making is also hard [#703].
5. Changing textures and smells vs need for predictability. Food textures and smells can change often, which is stressful for an autistic person who relies on predictability. Example: strawberries may have one taste on one day and a completely different taste and smell on another. Autistic people may value predictability because it helps them cope with a world that seems unpredictable [#704].
6. The eating environment and demands. For autistic people the eating environment can feel very overloaded and sometimes full of expectations. Autistic people — especially those with PDA (pathological demand avoidance, "ПИТ") — can struggle when eating feels like a "требование" ("demand"). This includes the "внутреннее требование" ("internal demand") — the feeling of hunger itself [#705].
7. Social demands at the table. There may be a need to make conversation at the table, hold eye contact or sit still. Autistic people sometimes find small talk hard, and sitting still is hard because one is in pain or needs stimulation for regulation [#706].
8. Gastrointestinal problems. Autistic people more often face GI problems, which can create difficulties around eating and pain [#706].
9. Body coordination. Coordination problems can make using a knife and fork or a spoon yet another obstacle to eating [#706].

## Autistic social communication and processing

### What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel Нейропряность shared (mostly forwarded from Нейр∞тлично and translated from the autistic author neurodivergent_lou) about how autistic people experience, process and conduct social interaction: the hidden days of processing after any interaction, delayed emotional processing, voice volume and tone, auditory processing difficulties, ready-made self-advocacy scripts, and the myth that autism is the opposite of empathy. It also includes a bot for checking whether messages sound toxic, a quote on honesty that is direct yet kind, and a quote distinguishing limerence from healthy love. The source's stated reason this matters: much of the cost of socialising for autistic people is invisible to others ("Это часть жизни аутистов, которую люди не видят" — "this is a part of autistic life people do not see"), so autistic people are misread as rude, bored, stubborn or unempathetic when they are actually processing differently [#411, #454, #742, #285].

### Core ideas

- Socialising continues after it ends. For an autistic person each social interaction is said to require 3–5 days of processing afterwards; masking during it adds a need for days of rest without expectations (see *Post-interaction processing*) [#410, #411, #413].
- Emotions may be processed with a delay of days to years, so an autistic person may feel an emotion without knowing its cause or even which emotion it is [#416].
- Voice differences are misread. Volume regulation, unwritten volume rules, monotone or unusual stress, and over-imitation while masking can be read as boredom, disinterest, rudeness or sarcasm; autistic people attend more to words, neurotypicals more to tone (see *Autism and tone of voice*) [#451–#457].
- Auditory processing difficulties show in six recognisable ways, from saying "What?" to buy time to misheard words (see *Six signs*) [#738–#744].
- Self-advocacy can be scripted. Sixteen ready phrases let an autistic person ask for processing time, written information, exit signals, email instead of calls, and explain stimming, eye contact, burnout and unmasking [#627–#635].
- "Autism is the antonym of empathy" is a harmful generalisation. Empathy has at least three types — cognitive, affective and compassionate — and people mean very different things by the word [#285].
- Honesty does not require cruelty, and healthy love does not consume your functioning the way limerence does [#802, #723].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Post-interaction processing in autism (neurodivergent_lou)
Purpose: to explain to non-autistic people (and to autistic readers themselves) why socialising costs far more than the visible event. The carousel opens with the headline claim "Для аутичного человека каждое социальное взаимодействие требует обработки в течение 3-5 дней..." ("For an autistic person every social interaction requires processing over 3–5 days…") [#410]. Its components, in the order of the cards:

1. Socialising = the event + the following days of processing. The author believes people do not realise that for autistic people social interaction includes not only the actual social part but also processing of the interaction in the following days. This is a part of autistic life others do not see; if you socialise, you are forced to deal with the days of processing that follow [#411].
2. "Looking fine" hides real difficulty. Autistic people may seem to be coping normally while internally experiencing real difficulties. Everyday actions such as communication take an "incredible" amount of energy, and little time may remain for other things because so much time goes into making sense of what happens every day [#412].
3. Masking during interaction and its recovery cost. The author constantly monitors how she behaves in social interactions, compares her behaviour with others' and imitates them. Autistic masking can be completely exhausting; after masking for a long time she needs several days of "отдохнуть без ожиданий" ("rest without expectations") — defined as a state in which you are not required to perform any tasks, be productive or meet any standards. Otherwise she risks driving herself into burnout [#413].
4. Overthinking and re-reading what was said. After communicating she tends to think too much and re-interpret what she said; sometimes it simply occurs to her that something she said 5 days ago could actually have been misinterpreted. Her explanation: autistic people communicate differently from the majority from childhood and are often misunderstood, which creates negative experiences — so the re-checking is learned from a history of being misread [#414].
5. Learning not to trust one's own perceptions. She also believes autistic people learn not to trust their own sensations, including sensations about how they communicate, which can make them truly doubt themselves and how they behave in society [#415].
6. Delayed emotional processing. Autistic people sometimes have a delay in processing emotions: they may not process emotions right after socialising, and it can take several days, weeks, months or even years to realise and process what they feel in response to certain situations. It can show up as feeling an emotion without knowing what caused it, or not even knowing exactly which emotion it is [#416].

#### Autism and tone of voice (neurodivergent_lou)
Purpose: to explain voice-related differences that cause misunderstandings in both directions. The carousel is titled "Аутизм и тон голоса" ("Autism and tone of voice") [#451]. Components:

1. Regulating volume is hard. Autistic people sometimes find it hard to regulate their voice volume — it can be difficult to tell how loudly they are speaking and therefore to control it. Sensory overload is a second cause: when ambient sounds feel amplified, they may speak louder to drown out the sensory noise around them [#452].
2. Unwritten volume rules are hard to read. Likewise, social rules about voice volume in particular situations can be hard to understand. Her example: there is an unspoken rule that you must not speak loudly in a library, but if you are playing football you are expected to shout — she adds the hedge "по крайней мере, так мне кажется" ("at least, that is how it seems to me") [#453].
3. Autistic tone is misread by others. Because an autistic person's tone differs from what others are used to — for example monotone or stress on particular sounds — it is perceived as boredom, disinterest or even rudeness. Autistic people may pay more attention to what exactly is said than to the tone it is said in, whereas neurotypical people may, conversely, give more weight to tone than to the words themselves [#454].
4. Masking by imitating speech can backfire. As part of masking, an autistic person may try to imitate the speech of non-autistic people, having realised that their autistic traits do not help them get what they need. For the author this sometimes made her voice excessively expressive and sound sarcastic, because she tried too hard to imitate other people's manner of speaking — "Получается обратный эффект." ("The opposite effect results.") [#455].
5. Neurotypical tone is hard to interpret. Autistic people can find it hard to interpret neurotypical people's tone of voice; tone may contain hidden social hints that are difficult to catch. They often need such cues to be expressed clearly and directly [#456].
6. Special interests change the voice. When talking about her interests, she usually starts speaking fast with emphasis on many words, and her pitch goes higher and lower because she is experiencing joy from her special interest [#457].

#### Six signs of auditory processing difficulties (neurodivergent_lou)
Purpose: to list "Варианты проявления трудностей с обработкой аудиальной информации..." ("ways auditory processing difficulties can show up…") so that behaviours are not misread [#738].

1. Saying "What?" even when you heard exactly what was said. Processing what she hears takes her quite a long time, so to give herself more time she sometimes says "что?" even when she did hear; this buys time to process and think before answering. Pitfall: she sometimes confuses people by answering before they have finished repeating — she simply needed more time [#739].
2. Using subtitles for TV or video. She relies on subtitles to take in information more easily and not miss anything, because she reads faster than she processes sound. Subtitles can also serve as visual reinforcement of what is heard [#740].
3. Difficulty with phone calls. Autistic people can find phone calls hard; one possible reason is auditory processing difficulty. On a call you rely on sound alone, without, for example, the lip-reading available face to face, which makes taking in information harder [#741].
4. Difficulty with verbal instructions. People with auditory processing problems may struggle to follow directions and instructions given orally — for example directions for getting somewhere, or oral instructions at school or work. This complicates everyday life. Others sometimes assume the person is deliberately making trouble or being stubborn, when in fact they are struggling to process the instructions [#742].
5. Difficulty holding a conversation in noisy places. In noisy places it is especially hard to take in what others say. Autistic people may find it hard to tell whether this comes from autism-typical sensory overload or from auditory processing problems that prevent perceiving speech in a noisy environment [#743].
6. Frequently mishearing words or sounds. Words or sounds may be misheard, especially similar-sounding ones: it can seem one word was said when it was actually a completely different similar-sounding word, which can make communication confusing [#744].

#### Self-advocacy scripts for autistic people (neurodivergent_lou)
Purpose: phrases the author relies on as an autistic person ("Скрипты самоадвокации на которые я полагаюсь как аутичный человек…" — "self-advocacy scripts I rely on as an autistic person…") to state needs and pre-empt misreadings [#627]. Each script is given with the need it addresses (the scripts are short; the "why" is the need they name):

1. Asking for processing time: "Sometimes it's hard for me to process information; could you give me time to think about it?" [#628]
2. Explaining headphones and stim toys: "I rely on my headphones and stim toys as disability aids. I'm still listening. These aids help me take in information more easily." — "Я все еще слушаю." ("I'm still listening.") pre-empts the assumption that using them means inattention [#628].
3. Naming worry about what to say: "I usually worry about what to say to strangers, especially in new situations. What should I say to that person?" — asking for a prompt rather than freezing [#629].
4. Checking emotions directly: "It's hard for me to interpret body language and facial expressions — are you upset?" [#629]
5. Asking for an end time: "Could you tell me roughly when this will end? It's not because I don't want to spend time with you; it just helps me prepare." [#630]
6. Agreeing on an exit signal: "Can we agree on a sign or word I can use so you know I'm overwhelmed and need to leave the situation?" [#630]
7. Clarifying what kind of support is wanted: "Do you want me to give you advice, or would you prefer I just listen to what you want to say?" [#631]
8. Disclosing burnout and limited energy: "Right now I'm experiencing autistic burnout and my energy is very limited." [#631]
9. Pre-arranging meltdown help: "If I have a meltdown, could you help me move to a quiet place, find noise-cancelling headphones, and not touch me?" [#632]
10. Explaining unmasking: "I'm experimenting with unmasking my autistic traits. This means I may look more autistic, because I no longer suppress my traits." [#632]
11. Protecting routine: "I rely on routine that helps me cope and stay calm, even if my routine isn't quite clear to you. Please don't interrupt my activities." [#633]
12. Flagging a high pain threshold: "I have a high pain threshold, which means I may not realise that I'm in pain or that something is wrong." [#633]
13. Asking for written information: "It's hard for me to remember information — could you write it down for me, or let me write it down?" [#634]
14. Explaining lack of eye contact: "I don't make eye contact because it is hard for me. I'm not being rude, and we can still communicate as usual." [#634]
15. Asking for orientation in a new situation: "I'm struggling in a new situation. Could you tell me what is happening and what I should do?" [#635]
16. Asking for email instead of calls: "I have trouble with phone calls because I can't understand what is being said to me; could we communicate by email?" (this links to sign 3 of auditory processing) [#635]

The carousel has 8 cards with two phrases each, i.e. 16 scripts in total [#628, #629, #630, #631, #632, #633, #634, #635].

#### Three types of empathy (Aleksei Sannikov)
Purpose: psychologist Алексей Санников argues against common generalisations — that people with BPD (borderline personality disorder) lack empathy, that "РАС это антоним эмпатии" ("ASD is the antonym of empathy"), and that empathy means feeling the same as the other person. His opinion: "Я считаю, что даже на бытовом уровне, делать таких обобщений не стоит." ("I believe that even at an everyday level such generalisations should not be made.") His reasoning: people put completely different meanings into the word "empathy", and when asked to clarify they get flustered and cannot always give a clear definition of what they mean in that specific case — while the term is multifaceted. Etymology: from Greek ἐν "in" + πάθος "passion, suffering, compassion, feeling, sympathy". He quotes the Wikipedia definition: "Осознанное сопереживание текущему эмоциональному состоянию другого человека без потери ощущения происхождения этого переживания." ("Conscious co-experiencing of another person's current emotional state without losing the sense of the origin of that experience."); an empath is a person with a developed capacity for empathy. Empathy is currently divided into several types [#285]:

1. Rational / intellectual (cognitive) empathy. The other's state is primarily analysed and studied: we compare the person's behaviour with their actions and with knowledge of how people act when experiencing a given emotion. It rests on one's own experience of similar feelings; facial expressions, gestures, gaze and voice matter greatly for determining what the other is feeling [#285].
2. Emotional / affective empathy. Based on the mechanism of psychological contagion: a person feels another's emotions but cannot (or not always) name them. Such people, he says, have a special nervous system and can experience others' pain as their own personal tragedy — unsurprisingly, they experience significant emotional and nervous overload. He invites the reader to notice the "huge difference" between types 1 and 2 [#285].
3. Compassionate empathy (empathic concern). The wish to help and support a person. A curious effect: at that moment there may be no understanding of what is happening with them, nor co-feeling; yet the heaviness of the other's emotion can feel painful to someone with this form developed, and it prompts them to help [#285].

#### Compassionate-empathy offer of help (Aleksei Sannikov)
What to do when compassionate empathy makes you want to help [#285]:
1. Offer solutions or ask directly — "Чем тебе помочь?" ("How can I help you?").
2. If the other person cannot answer, ask yourself: "Что помогло мне в подобной ситуации?" ("What helped me in a similar situation?") or "Что могло бы мне помочь?" ("What could have helped me?").
3. Turn the answers into concrete offers, for example: (a) I can just be next to you; (b) I can listen to you; (c) I can do something (e.g. give an umbrella in a downpour). The source ends the list with "... и т.п." ("…and so on"), i.e. the options are open-ended [#285].

## ADHD traits: rejection sensitivity, daydreaming and body doubling

### What this covers

This topic collects the channel's posts on experiences that are specific to (or especially common in) ADHD and AuDHD, and on one practical tool for them. It covers rejection sensitive dysphoria (RSD) as a 24-sign checklist, maladaptive daydreaming and hyperphantasia, how ADHD shapes reactions to conflict, body doubling as a way to get past task-initiation paralysis, and a humorous illustration of how ADHD and autism combine in focus needs. According to the source, these traits matter because they create thought-and-emotion overload in conflicts, can interfere with concentration and daily tasks, and can lead people to break off relationships or leave work and school to avoid rejection [#42, #155, #267, #431].

### Core ideas

- RSD shows up as a wide pattern of avoidance, monitoring and self-criticism, not just as hurt feelings. The translated cards list 24 signs ranging from people-pleasing and over-apologising to perfectionism and quitting jobs or friendships to avoid rejection (full checklist below) [#267, #268, #269, #270, #271, #272].
- Maladaptive daydreaming and hyperphantasia are two related phenomena — absorbing fantasy that can disrupt life, and unusually vivid mental imagery — which the channel says are more common in women and in people with ADHD, and are double-edged: a source of creativity and sometimes a protective mechanism, but also a drain on concentration [#155].
- ADHD can significantly affect how a person reacts to arguments and confrontation, producing overload from thoughts and emotions; the author's framing is that conflict is a normal part of life and nobody resolves every problem perfectly [#42].
- Body doubling lets people with ADHD/AuDHD borrow external momentum and structure instead of relying on willpower: it is easier to start acting by mirroring someone else who is working [#431, #830].
- AuDHD combines contradictory needs — the ADHD side needs stimulation (music) to focus, while the autistic side needs that stimulation to be very specific and predictable [#818].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Signs of rejection sensitive dysphoria (24-sign checklist)
Purpose: a self-recognition checklist for rejection sensitive dysphoria (cards titled «Признаки чувствительной к отвержению дисфории» — "Signs of rejection sensitive dysphoria"). It was originally made by neurodivergent_lou; the channel forwarded a Russian translation from the channel Нейр∞тлично («Перевод поста neurodivergent_lou» — "translation of a post by neurodivergent_lou") as six cards of four signs each. The cards give the signs only; they do not give a scoring rule, a threshold number of signs, or coping advice. The signs, grouped by card in their original order:

Card 1 [#267]:
1. Breaking off relationships with friends, leaving a job or leaving school in order to avoid rejection — pre-emptively exiting before rejection can happen [#267].
2. Constant re-checking of oneself and self-doubt — repeatedly second-guessing one's own actions and judgements [#267].
3. Being a strong people-pleaser (to avoid rejection) — accommodating others so they have no reason to reject you [#267].
4. Searching for constant reassurance/confirmation [#267].

Card 2 [#268]:
5. Experiencing deep emotions, or numbness, in response to a refusal or a perceived refusal — the reaction can be either an emotional flood or shutting down [#268].
6. Interpreting the slightest change in someone's behaviour as rejection [#268].
7. Complete hyperfocus on rejection or perceived rejection — the rejection (real or imagined) takes over attention [#268].
8. Often feeling "cringe" or being easily embarrassed [#268].

Card 3 [#269]:
9. Criticising oneself in order to avoid being rejected by others — getting the criticism in first [#269].
10. High expectations of oneself to compensate for perceived rejection [#269].
11. Constantly watching other people to detect signs that they are rejecting us [#269].
12. A constant need for approval [#269].

Card 4 [#270]:
13. Reacting to rejection in a way that others consider "extreme" [#270].
14. Difficulty doing tasks where other people might notice us — being observed raises the fear of judgement [#270].
15. Comparing oneself with others [#270].
16. Avoiding conflicts [#270].

Card 5 [#271]:
17. Avoiding situations in which we could be criticised or rejected [#271].
18. Excessive apologising in order to avoid rejection (the card is illustrated with the word "SORRY") [#271].
19. Over-analysing texts and emails to find signs that we are being rejected [#271].
20. Anxiety about making mistakes [#271].

Card 6 [#272]:
21. "Getting stuck" in thoughts about past cases of rejection (illustrated with a "PAST" signpost) — rumination on old rejections [#272].
22. Significant mood changes because of perceived or actual rejection [#272].
23. Being a perfectionist [#272].
24. Difficulty maintaining relationships because of one's reaction to rejection or perceived rejection [#272].

A recurring thread across the cards is that the trigger is often *perceived* («предполагаемый») rather than actual rejection, and that many signs are strategies aimed at avoiding rejection (people-pleasing, apologising, self-criticism, avoidance, perfectionism) [#267, #268, #269, #271, #272].

#### Maladaptive daydreaming and hyperphantasia profile
Purpose: to help readers recognise themselves; the post opens by asking whether you have ever sunk into a fantasy world so deeply that you lost track of time, and says this may be a sign of maladaptive daydreaming and hyperphantasia [#155]. The post is structured in five blocks:

1. What it is.
   - Maladaptive daydreaming («Дезадаптивное мечтание») — «интенсивное, захватывающее фантазирование, которое может мешать повседневной жизни» (intense, absorbing fantasising that can interfere with everyday life) [#155].
   - Hyperphantasia («Гиперфантазия») — «способность создавать невероятно детальные и яркие мысленные образы» (the ability to create incredibly detailed and vivid mental images) [#155].
2. Who encounters it more often.
   - More common in women [#155].
   - Often seen in people with ADHD [#155].
   - May be connected with neurodivergence in general [#155].
3. Interesting facts (as presented by the channel, without named studies).
   - A possible link with oestrogen levels [#155].
   - Research is said to show increased activity in specific brain areas — the default mode network («сеть пассивного режима работы - DMN») [#155].
   - May be linked to changes in how dopamine and serotonin work [#155].
4. Impact on life (both positive and negative).
   - A source of creativity and unique thinking [#155].
   - Can interfere with concentration and everyday tasks [#155].
   - Sometimes acts as a protective mechanism under stress or trauma [#155].
5. Famous "dreamers." Some science-fiction writers probably had this ability — the channel hedges with «вероятно» (probably) and names no one [#155].

#### Body doubling (бадди) for task initiation
Purpose: a method for people with ADHD/AuDHD («СДВГ/РАСДВГ») to cope with difficulties caused by executive dysfunction, especially the paralysis of choosing a task and starting the next steps [#431].

- What it is. The channel's term «Бадди» comes from "body double" («двойник»). It means working together with another person while each does various tasks; a later post defines it as «когда два человека одновременно работают "бок о бок", каждый над своей задачей» (when two people work "side by side" at the same time, each on their own task) [#431, #830].
- How it is done. In the channel's own practice it can be done over voice and/or video call; physical co-presence is not required [#431].
- Why it works (the channel's explanation). It helps overcome the paralysis people with ADHD/AuDHD often feel when choosing a task and starting the next steps, «так как нам бывает легче начать действовать повторяя за кем-то другим» (because it is sometimes easier for us to start acting by copying someone else) [#431]. The second post adds that a structured body-doubling setup lets people lean on external structure instead of willpower [#830].
- What it can be used for. Anything: work, cleaning, sport, or anything else [#830]. The target is specifically the tasks «которые ты вроде бы знаешь как сделать, но просто не можешь начать» (that you seemingly know how to do but just can't start) — sometimes work or study, sometimes taking a shower or even (not) simply getting out of bed [#830].
- Conditions that matter. The channel stresses it is very important that this happens without pressure and in an atmosphere of acceptance. In the example space described (see *Tools and resources mentioned*), nothing is demanded of participants, each person always decides how much and how to participate, and care toward oneself and each other is a core value [#830].

#### Body-double request format
When looking for a body-doubling partner (the channel's "Поиск бадди" / "find a buddy" thread), a request should state four things [#431]:
1. The desired activity — what you want to work on.
2. The "meeting place" — where the session will happen (e.g. a voice/video channel).
3. The current day/time — when you want to do it.
4. How long the activity will last — the planned duration.

### Detailed insights

#### ADHD and conflict
The channel author Настя writes that managing conflicts can be a difficult task for adults with ADHD, because ADHD can significantly affect our reaction to arguments and confrontations, creating an overload of thoughts and emotions in these situations [#42]. Her stance: «конфликт - это нормальная часть жизни, и никто не совершенен в разрешении каждой проблемы» (conflict is a normal part of life, and no one is perfect at resolving every problem). She advises being patient with yourself and focusing on improving your skills if you feel you are lacking in this area [#42]. Conflict avoidance also appears as one of the RSD signs (sign 16 above) [#270].

#### AuDHD focus quirk: music
A humorous post illustrates how ADHD and autism combine in one person. The ADHD side says: «Мы не можем фокусироваться без музыки, нам нужна музыка» (We can't focus without music, we need music). Once the music is on, the autistic side answers: «Замечательно, но нам нужна определенная музыка с определенным ритмом» (Great, but we need specific music with a specific rhythm) — and demands that endless YouTube live stream with a single melody on repeat [#818]. The lesson the joke carries is that in AuDHD the ADHD need for stimulation to focus is satisfied only within the autistic need for sameness and predictability.
