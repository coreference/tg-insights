## Autistic burnout: definition, causes and warning signs

### What this covers

This topic gathers everything the channel posted on what autistic (and, broadly, neurodivergent — autistic and ADHD) burnout is, how researchers define and provisionally diagnose it, what causes it, what it looks and feels like from the inside, how it differs from ordinary burnout, depression-like illness and "laziness", and which checklists and scales a person can use to track it. According to the source, it matters because the state is "deeply exhausting", is still treated by society as an inconvenience rather than a debilitating struggle, is easily missed or pushed through, and does not respond to the ordinary neurotypical advice to "rest more" [#28, #149, #150, #388].

### Core ideas

- Autistic burnout is a distinct syndrome, not ordinary burnout. It arises from chronic life stress plus a mismatch between expectations and abilities without adequate support; it is pervasive, long-lasting (usually more than 3 months) and involves exhaustion, loss of functioning and reduced tolerance to stimuli (see *Raymaker definition*) [#392, #28].
- It is a community-coined term, not an official diagnosis, but a real, distinct phenomenon. Research (Raymaker et al. 2020) found people describe the same causes and symptoms, distinct from depression and occupational burnout, though doctors may diagnose it as stress, anxiety, depression or chronic fatigue (see *Six-question article*) [#28].
- The cause is the environment, not autism itself. Burnout develops because autistic needs are not met in a neurotypical society: masking, minimising needs, lack of support, social, sensory and daily-life demands exceeding capacity [#432, #388].
- The neurodivergent nervous system carries a higher baseline stress load and recovers less easily, so stress accumulates day after day until the system shuts down (see *The neurodivergent stress day*) [#446].
- Hallmark signs are extreme exhaustion that sleep does not fix, sensory intolerance, executive-function collapse, skill regression, social withdrawal and loss of the ability to mask [#147, #388, #392, #398].
- Simple rest does not cure it: the nervous system gets stuck in hyper- or hypoarousal and has to be helped manually back to safety before rest and activity can help [#388, #446].
- Tracking helps: a concrete list of signs helps a person not miss burnout or try to push through it, and the ABSC checklist gives a score to compare over time [#150, #31].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Raymaker et al. (2020) definition of autistic burnout
The channel gives this research definition: autistic burnout is a syndrome caused by chronic life stress and a mismatch between expectations and abilities in the absence of adequate support. It is characterised by three features:
1. Pervasive, long-term exhaustion — "долгосрочным (обычно более 3 месяцев)" (*long-term (usually more than 3 months)*).
2. Loss of functioning.
3. Reduced tolerance to stimuli.
[#392]

The channel's earlier introduction (#28) frames it in similar words: a unique and deeply exhausting state that primarily affects people with autism (РАС) and ADHD, arising either from prolonged stress or from an acute stressful experience, especially one tied to the need to adapt to the surrounding neurotypical world. It contrasts it with the general phenomenon of burnout in a world where the constant pursuit of efficiency and productivity has become the norm [#28].

#### "What is autistic burnout?" — the six-question article (Dr Alice Nicholls, translated by the channel)
Post #28 links to a Telegraph longread «Что такое Аутистическое выгорание?», translated and adapted for the channel by Настя and Рина from an original article on dralicenicholls.com. It is organised around six questions; each answer is reproduced below [#28].

Q1. What is autistic burnout? The term "autistic burnout" was coined by members of the autistic community to describe an experience that is common among autistic people; it is a shared problem and, the article says, one of the most discussed topics in Facebook groups for autistic people. Definition: "Аутистическое выгорание описывает опыт крайнего истощения у аутистов после периода длительного стресса или острого стрессового переживания." (Autistic burnout describes an experience of extreme exhaustion in autistic people after a period of prolonged stress or an acute stressful experience.) [#28]

Q2. What are its symptoms? Autistic people experience burnout differently, but their descriptions share common features. The four main symptoms [#28]:
1. Extreme fatigue. The degree varies. People may feel constantly tired or perceive even the smallest tasks as a huge effort; some are so exhausted that it is hard even to get out of bed.
2. Loss of skills. Many describe losing cognitive skills or a decline in mental activity: greater difficulty finding words, processing information, solving problems or understanding new concepts. Some describe "мозгового тумана" (brain fog) — everything seems unclear and thinking takes effort.
3. Decline in social skills. Those who were previously highly social may find they no longer want to take part in as many social events and find it hard to keep conversations at their former level. Those for whom social situations were already hard may feel unable to manage even the most basic conversations and may even lose the ability to speak. Autistic people who usually hide their traits (use masking) may find they can no longer do it, or that it tires them even after short periods.
4. Increased perceptual (sensory) sensitivity. Existing difficulties with certain sounds and textures may intensify, and some people start noticing sensitivities they never noticed before. The article's examples: the noise of artificial lighting in the office starts to bother the person; or the pattern on the wallpaper; they may feel anxiety or anger when touched; they may become ever more aware of how uncomfortable their clothes are.

Q3. What causes it? — the stress-accumulation mechanism. Autistic burnout is the result of long-term stress in an autistic person: a combination of stressors plus the absence of sufficient support or relief leads to unbearably high stress and, eventually, burnout. The mechanism, step by step [#28]:
1. Under moderate or high stress for a long time, the ability to cope with that stress starts to deteriorate.
2. We can handle short-term increases in stress, but we need periods of relatively low stress to recover and return to our baseline stress level.
3. Without low-stress periods we keep "carrying" our stress with us, so every additional stressor has a cumulative effect and even small stressors can feel unmanageable.
4. Ultimately, if nothing is done, we become exhausted and can no longer function at the same level.

Autistic people are at especially high risk of stress for reasons directly and indirectly caused by being autistic in a neurotypical world — on top of all the everyday stressors neurotypical people also face. The stressors the article lists ("вот лишь несколько" — *just a few*) [#28]:
1. Masking (маскировка).
2. The double empathy problem (проблема двойной эмпатии).
3. Perceptual sensitivity and unmet sensory needs.
4. Executive-function difficulties.
5. Difficulty accepting change.
6. Life events.
7. Daily hassles.
8. Expectations (at work, in the family, in society).

In addition, autistic people meet obstacles when seeking support to manage their stress. The four barriers to support listed [#28]:
1. Gaslighting.
2. Insufficient boundary-setting / self-advocacy.
3. Inability to take a break.
4. Lack of external resources / support services.

Q4. Is it a diagnosis? Although autistic burnout is widely discussed among autistic adults on social media, it has not been officially recognised by medical professionals or by either of the two diagnostic manuals. People who go to their doctor with its symptoms may instead be diagnosed with stress, anxiety, depression, chronic fatigue or occupational burnout. Researchers have begun studying it as a concept; because with any new term people using the same words may mean different things, Raymaker et al. (2020) interviewed members of the autistic community and analysed social-media content describing autistic burnout. They found that people describing autistic burnout spoke of the same distinctive causes and symptoms, and concluded that the community is describing a specific phenomenon distinct from both depression and occupational burnout [#28].

Q5. How does it relate to depression? Autistic burnout can be accompanied by depression but is a separate phenomenon. Historically it may have been understood as depression, and the article tells readers they may even have been diagnosed with depression when their symptoms would have been better understood as autistic burnout. It quotes Tony Attwood (2014, p. 100), who described one of several causes of depression in autism as "Обусловлено умственным и эмоциональным истощением от общения, невозможностью терпеть интенсивную чувствительность к восприятию и чрезмерными изменениями, а также когнитивным стилем, фокусирующимся на том, что может пойти не так, что приводит к пессимистическому взгляду на жизнь" (Caused by mental and emotional exhaustion from socialising, inability to tolerate intense perceptual sensitivity and excessive change, and a cognitive style focused on what might go wrong, leading to a pessimistic outlook on life). The article reads this quote as showing how autistic burnout can lead to depression and where the two states are similar. Practical consequence: where low mood appears alongside signs of autistic burnout, it is important to treat both. Standard CBT for depression usually encourages people to gradually take up more pleasant and productive activities, which can cause problems if the sources of the burnout have not been addressed [#28].

Q6. How does it relate to occupational burnout? The article quotes the WHO description of occupational burnout: "Чувство истощения энергии или изнеможения; увеличенное эмоциональное отчуждение от работы или чувство негативизма или цинизма по отношению к своей работе; и сниженная профессиональная эффективность." (Feelings of energy depletion or exhaustion; increased mental distance from one's job or feelings of negativism or cynicism related to one's job; and reduced professional efficacy.) (WHO website, 2021). Autistic burnout is the result of long-term stress in an autistic or neurodivergent person; what makes it different is the type of stress. A person with occupational burnout is chronically stressed by pressures at work; a person with autistic burnout is chronically stressed by the pressures of living and working in a neurotypical world and of coping with social and sensory stressors. Work stress can contribute to autistic burnout, but its causes go beyond professional problems and are tied specifically to the difficulties of being autistic in a neurotypical society [#28].

Conclusion of the article: autistic burnout is often discussed in the autistic community; it is not a recognised diagnosis, but people talking about it appear to be discussing the same thing. It is an experience of extreme fatigue, loss of cognitive and social skills and heightened perceptual sensitivity, caused by prolonged stress often stemming from the difficulties of being autistic in a neurotypical world. It differs from depression and from occupational burnout, but autistic people are also at risk of both [#28].

#### Higgins et al. (2021) provisional diagnostic criteria
Purpose: provisional criteria for recognising autistic burnout, translated by the channel from an Embrace Autism infographic. All of 1 and 2 plus at least one item from 3 must be present [#392]:
1. Significant mental and physical exhaustion ("Значительное умственное и физическое истощение"; the card shows it as a battery at 1%) [#392].
2. Interpersonal withdrawal ("Межличностная отстранённость"; the infographic card renders it as increased social withdrawal) [#392].
3. One or more of the following:
   - A. Significant reduction in functioning in social, occupational, educational, academic, behavioural or other important areas of life.
   - B. Confusion, difficulties with executive functions and dissociative states (card: "more problems with executive functions").
   - C. Intensification of autistic traits and reduced ability to mask or hide autistic characteristics (card: "reduced ability to camouflage").
[#392]

How to use it:
1. Tick every symptom that has bothered you more than usual over the last two weeks [#31, #386].
2. Add up the ticks to get a total score [#31].
3. Repeat later and compare the total with your future results, to track changes over time — the channel later nudges readers: "Давно ли вы проверяли сколько баллов у вас по чек-листу аутичного выгорания?" (How long ago did you check your score on the autistic burnout checklist?) [#31, #386].

#### Core and additional characteristics of neurodivergent burnout (with causes)
The channel author's own overview post. Definition: burnout in people with autism and ADHD is characterised by pervasive, long-term exhaustion, loss of functionality and increased difficulty managing everyday tasks. It is largely the result of neurodivergent people living in a world not adapted to them; lack of awareness of neurodivergence, social expectations and demands, and the need to mask lead to an accumulation of life stressors [#388].

Core characteristics [#388]:
1. Chronic exhaustion — constant, deep tiredness that does not improve with rest.
2. Reduced tolerance to stimuli — heightened sensitivity to sensory input such as light, sound and touch.
3. Intensified executive-function problems — greater difficulty planning, organising, remembering and managing daily tasks.
4. Loss of skills — deterioration of executive functioning, including difficulty thinking, remembering, planning, doing basic self-care and managing daily activities.

Additional characteristics [#388]:
1. Emotional dysregulation — more difficulty managing emotions, leading to outbursts or intense anxiety.
2. Intensification of neurodivergent traits — increased repetitive behaviour, heightened sensory sensitivity, greater difficulty adapting to change.
3. Cognitive impairment — more trouble with memory and concentration, making daily tasks harder.
4. Inability to mask — people often lose or reduce the ability to mask their traits; the author notes this is one reason burnout is often the time when adults get identified as neurodivergent.
5. Problems perceiving time — greater difficulty sensing the passage of time, leading to procrastination or last-minute stress.
6. Increased impulsivity — which may lead to risky decisions.

Causes [#388]:
1. Masking.
2. Minimising one's needs.
3. Lack of appropriate support and accommodations.
4. Depletion of executive functions.
5. Overall load exceeding capacities and support.
6. Hyperfocus.

Why simple rest does not help (author's explanation) [#388]:
- Advice to "rest more, stress less" is built for a neurotypical nervous system, which can switch easily between calm/rest and activity/stress; when that system feels "safe" it can launch the body's recovery mechanisms.
- For neurodivergent people this does not work, partly because they only realise they are in neurodivergent burnout after they have already spent all their resources on constant attempts to match the expectations and pace of the neurotypical world.
- As a result the already inflexible nervous system gets stuck in hyperactivation (fight/flight) or hypoactivation (freeze). So, the author says, skills of manually switching the nervous system are needed to help it return to balance, where rest can actually be felt as beneficial.
- "Для нас отдых - это целый осознанный процесс" (For us rest is a whole conscious process) that the nervous system has to be taught — but rest alone will not restore a person from neurodivergent burnout. The author's position: "нейроотличное выгорание - очень комплексное состояние, которое требует комплексного подхода" (neurodivergent burnout is a very complex state that requires a complex approach).

#### Five domains of skill regression (Lindsay M)
From the channel's translation of a longread by Lindsay M on skill regression in autism and/or ADHD. Definition: skill regression is the loss of previously acquired abilities, which can happen for various reasons including environmental changes, stress or mental-health problems; it is not uncommon in autistic and ADHD people at different stages of life. It can show up as a decline in communication (written or spoken), social or daily-living skills, typically during stressful periods — major life changes, increased demands, or overload. It can be especially distressing because it may feel like losing part of one's identity or independence [#398].

Examples by domain [#398]:
1. Communication skills
   - Verbal/written regression: someone who used to speak or write in full sentences may struggle to, preferring single words or gestures.
   - Social cues: difficulty recognising or responding to social cues that were previously understood, such as eye contact or turn-taking in conversation.
2. Social skills
   - Withdrawal from peers: someone who used to enjoy socialising may start avoiding group and even one-on-one activities, leading to isolation.
   - Difficulty initiating conversations: someone who could start conversations or keep up small talk may become more passive, waiting for others to involve them.
3. Daily-living skills
   - Self-care: someone who could maintain personal hygiene, choose clothes and dress may find they cannot manage these tasks at their usual pace.
   - Cooking: someone who used to help with cooking, grocery shopping and menu planning may lose interest and stop taking part, relying entirely on partners or food delivery.
4. Executive-functioning skills
   - Time management: someone who managed their schedule well may start missing deadlines or forgetting important dates.
   - Planning: someone who could plan a project effectively may struggle to identify the steps or gather the materials needed to finish it.
5. Emotional regulation
   - More meltdowns: someone who coped well with frustration may have emotional outbursts more often in response to minor setbacks.
   - Difficulty adapting to change: someone who adapted well to changes in routine may struggle even with minor adjustments, leading to anxiety and/or shutdown, and may start avoiding routines to prevent this dysregulation.

Causes of skill regression: Lindsay M says that across years of research and clinical work she has met many individual contributing factors to acute and chronic regression in neurodivergent people, which seem to group into two main, often interconnected causes: burnout and unmasking [#398].
The channel author adds a third, previously discussed mechanism: state-dependent memory — skills are used while in a special state of consciousness during masking; when burnout hits there is no resource left for masking, and therefore no access to the skills learned in that state [#398].

#### Causes of autistic burnout — explaining it to friends and family (part 1)
Part 1 of a three-part handout for explaining autistic burnout to loved ones. It describes autistic burnout as "медицинское состояние" (a medical condition) caused by prolonged time in stressful conditions where an autistic person cannot cope with the demands placed on them. It stresses that autistic burnout differs from occupational (work) burnout: unlike work burnout, it can be caused by many factors in the social environment [#432].
Causes that "research shows" [#432]:
1. Social demands: too many social events; the need to conform to social norms; a relationship with someone who does not understand autism.
2. Sensory demands: an environment that does not take sensory needs into account.
3. Demands of daily life: an excessive number of errands and administrative tasks.
4. Insufficient support: at work, at school and/or at home.
Key message for the family: burnout is not caused by autism itself — "Оно возникает из-за того, что аутичные потребности не удовлетворяются в нейротипичном обществе" (It arises because autistic needs are not met in a neurotypical society) [#432].

#### Consequences of autistic burnout — first-person checklist for family (part 2)
A fill-in list written in the first person, so an autistic person can show loved ones how burnout affects them [#433]:
1. My autism may become more visible.
2. It is harder for me to mask my autistic traits.
3. I have greater difficulties with verbal communication.
4. I experience stronger emotional dysregulation (e.g. more frequent meltdowns or shutdowns).
5. My mental health worsens (depression and anxiety increase).
6. Suicidal thoughts increase (e.g. thinking about or planning suicide).
7. I often or constantly feel physical and cognitive exhaustion and overload.
8. I become more forgetful, find it harder to concentrate, have "brain fog".
9. I cannot cope with work; I need more time to process information and complete tasks.
10. I have problems organising time.
11. I react more sharply to changes in my daily routine and to unexpected events.
12. My sensory sensitivities increase.
13. Other — a blank line to add individual manifestations.

Important note for the family: the state can change from day to day. On some days there may be more energy and the person may feel closer to their usual self; this does not necessarily mean they have fully recovered, nor that they exaggerated the severity of their state on other days [#433].

#### The neurodivergent stress day and the overtrained-muscle analogy
From the PDF text version of the author's video (posted at readers' request so it can be printed). Purpose: to explain why burnout develops in neurodivergent people and why common advice fails [#446].

1. The myth. There is a mistaken belief that stress strengthens us and helps adaptation. For a neurodivergent person, the author says, stress is destructive and can cause PTSD and complex PTSD [#446].
2. Why the baseline load is higher. The author claims neurodivergent people are physiologically different: a differently built brain, a sensitive nervous system, and a body that produces more cortisol which takes longer to clear, affecting many biological processes. Things that are not stressful for a neurotypical person can be stressful for them, so the stress load is higher at baseline. Neurodivergence also often comes with co-occurring conditions that affect or depend on the nervous system, so stress can quickly have physical health consequences [#446].
3. The analogy. Neurodivergent burnout is the consequence of prolonged time in stressful conditions. Imagine someone training 8–12 hours every day (the author flags these numbers as "чисто мои представления" — *purely my own notion*), constantly stressing the muscles without letting them recover: instead of strengthening, the fibres get injured and break down until the body simply refuses to move. The nervous system is the same: living every day trying to meet neurotypical standards only spends the resources of an already hypersensitive nervous system [#446].
4. The worked example — one day. A stressful situation both neurotypes share: being late for an important meeting (the taxi is late, everything is snowed in). For a neurotypical person the stress may end there and the nervous system returns to normal. For the hypothetical neurodivergent person stress starts in the morning: standing up from lying down causes tachycardia due to comorbid POTS (СПОТ); then overload from the noisy shower and the feel of cream/cosmetics on the face; no appetite for breakfast, so low blood sugar may trigger mast-cell activation with panic-attack-like reactions; noisy family/children add sensory overload; then the taxi is noisy and stuffy and the driver talks non-stop; at the meeting they must explain the delay without it sounding like an excuse, because any explanation becomes an "excuse" in neurotypical eyes; they must talk, and attempts to clarify also bring negative consequences; all the while they hide the need to stim, because they must not show they are neurodivergent — and hiding it also costs energy. After the meeting they must go to a crowded shop or go hungry: noise, light, crowds, constant violation of personal space, children running and shouting; at the checkout, exhausted by heat and sensory overload, they must strain to hear the cashier and answer loudly; carrying heavy bags causes joint subluxations due to hypermobility; at home there are family interactions, chores and noise until late evening [#446].
5. The lesson. Every single situation that day overloaded the nervous system — "Каждая." (Every one.). Day after day, with more or less stress, there is never a chance to recover properly, because a nervous system in survival mode cannot really switch to rest. The outcome is a shutdown of the nervous system because it can no longer cope. The author notes this is a hypothetical example that many will recognise, and some face even heavier loads [#446].
6. What follows for recovery. Once the nervous system has shut down or is stuck in hyper- or hypoarousal, exposing it even to moderate stress is critical, because, like a cornered animal, it reacts to everything as danger — "ДАЖЕ ЕСЛИ ВЫ ГОЛОВОЙ ПОНИМАЕТЕ ЧТО ЭТО НЕ ТАК И ОПАСНОСТИ НЕТ" (EVEN IF YOU UNDERSTAND WITH YOUR HEAD THAT IT IS NOT SO AND THERE IS NO DANGER). We cannot decide how the autonomic nervous system reacts, but we can create conditions in which it first calms down and gains a sense of safety, and only then start doing things; otherwise there is an uncomfortable swing between "заставить себя" (forcing yourself) and "лежать умирать" (lying there dying) [#446].
7. Why common advice fails. Advice like "do sports, go for a walk, have fun, talk to friends" can sound maximally superficial and disrespectful. The author's own case: if constant stress has led to, for example, fibromyalgia and chronic fatigue syndrome (СХУ), physical exercise will not help, because the body reacts to exertion as danger, activity tolerance will not grow and stamina will not increase; she says some recent studies have already refuted the theory that people with ME/CFS need to exercise to expand activity tolerance. What matters more is finding a way to stabilise the nervous system so it feels safe, and only then starting measured activity — a process that takes much longer and requires much more attentiveness to oneself, from oneself and from specialists, but which she calls foundational for her recovery [#446].

### Detailed insights

#### Lou's first-person account: what autistic burnout feels like
A translated series by the autistic creator neurodivergent_lou (via the channel Нейр∞тлично):
- The signature sign: "Я знаю, что у меня аутичное выгорание, когда я чувствую, что могу проспать целый год, и этого будет недостаточно..." (I know I'm in autistic burnout when I feel I could sleep for a whole year and it wouldn't be enough...) [#146].
- What it can include: it is different for everyone, but may include exhaustion; reduced ability to cope with sensory information; losing the ability to do things one used to do. Mechanism: autistic burnout happens when, over time, an autistic person's energy is spent faster than they can replenish it. Some people see it as a survival mechanism — the body shuts down to cope with the constant exhaustion of living in a non-autistic world [#147].
- Recognition and self-neglect: autistic burnout was accepted as a concept only relatively recently, although autistic people have described it for a long time; Lou still thinks society does not understand how dangerous and debilitating it can be. Lou admits not taking her own burnout seriously — expecting to cope when she actually needed to rest and care for herself — which is hard given society's implicit message that to be worthy we must always be productive [#148].
- Not an inconvenience, and not the same as "burnout": autistic burnout is still discussed as an "inconvenience" rather than the debilitating struggle it often is; autistic burnout and what the neurotypical world calls "burnout" are different things, which complicates the discussion further [#149].
- A concrete list of signs helps: having a specific list of autistic burnout signs helped Lou not to miss it or try to push through it. Her list includes feeling she could sleep for years and still be tired; during earlier burnouts she dreamed of sleeping for years and waking up many years later still tired — "Как будто мой мозг подсознательно просил об отдыхе" (As if my brain was subconsciously asking for rest) [#150].
- The level of exhaustion: she cannot really describe it — as if she could sleep for years; she cannot do anything even when she desperately wants to. The exhaustion autistic people feel in autistic burnout often differs from the exhaustion described as burnout in non-autistic society, and she considers this important to remember [#151].
- It is not laziness: autistic burnout has for too long been misread as "laziness" and met with phrases like "we all get a bit tired", "you just need to try and push through", "we all need a bit of time" — which ignore how debilitating and draining it is. "Это не лень." (It is not laziness.) [#152].
- Burnout vs illness confusion: Lou sincerely thought she was ill and it turned out she was showing all the signs of autistic burnout and had badly overstrained; at other times she really was ill and mistook it for autistic burnout. She wonders how this affects autistic people's health — whether they may not realise they are actually ill and not seek help because they wrongly assume it is "just burnout" [#153].

#### Dual masking, double burnout (AuDHD)
A translation of a text by The Ashling about people who are both autistic and ADHD (РАСДВГ / AuDHD): "Двойной маскинг означает, что отдых редко ощущается настоящим." (Dual masking means rest rarely feels real.) What dual masking involves [#726]:
- Autistic traits are smoothed over to seem "polite"; ADHD traits are hidden to look "organised".
- Speech is slowed down so as not to seem too emotional, then speeds up when emotional excitement arrives — followed by guilt.
- Eye contact takes effort; meltdowns/shutdowns are postponed; feelings are processed before being expressed.
- Every movement is rehearsed, every moment replayed; every aspect of oneself is adjusted for others' convenience — "но не для твоего" (but not for yours).
- The text insists this is not overthinking or drama but a matter of survival, and states that research confirms masking autism and ADHD increases burnout, fatigue and emotional strain: "Цена реальна. Ноша тяжела." (The cost is real. The burden is heavy.) and the world does not see it [#726].

#### Executive dysfunction as nervous-system resistance (author's personal observation)
The author shares a thought from personal experience: sometimes executive dysfunction arises from the resistance of the nervous system to a task that feels unsafe. Her example: she hates unloading and putting away dishes from the dishwasher — she dislikes touching freshly washed ceramic/glass, but above all cannot stand the sound of plates and glasses being stacked. Even when someone else does it she leaves the room, because the sound is unbearable, and if she stays long she is overloaded for the rest of the day. Before she had noise-cancelling headphones she resisted the task as much as possible, because it meant sensory torture for her nervous system. The problem almost disappeared once she used headphones and felt that putting dishes away was now safe: she no longer has to force herself, knowing a solution makes the process safe, starting is much easier, and she spends less energy on the task. She adds that executive dysfunction shows even more strongly during burnout, when the nervous system becomes hypersensitive to sensory stimuli, and asks readers whether creating sensory safety improves their executive function [#822].

#### Eating during heavy burnout
The author notes that during severe burnout a "safe food" can become something like macaroni and cheese that one fixates on — which brings nutritional deficits, weight gain and guilt about not eating something healthier [#707].

## Autistic burnout versus depression

### What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel Нейропряность taught about autistic burnout and how to tell it apart from depression (and, in the channel's long article, from occupational burnout). It covers what autistic burnout is and its four core symptoms, the stress-accumulation mechanism that causes it, the stressors and the barriers to support specific to autistic people, its non-diagnostic status, the symptoms it shares with depression, the features more typical of each condition, the different meaning of social withdrawal in each, and why the two need opposite approaches (more activity for depression vs deep rest and unmasking for burnout). The source treats this as practically important: according to neurodivergent lou, the two conditions "обычно требуют разных подходов" (usually require different approaches), and a wrong label can make the person worse; the article warns that people may have been diagnosed with depression when their symptoms "более точно могли бы быть поняты как аутистическое выгорание" (could more accurately be understood as autistic burnout); the #589 post calls recognising the difference "первый шаг к адекватной помощи" (the first step to adequate help) [#461, article #28, msg #589].

### Core ideas

- Autistic burnout is a distinct condition, not a form of depression or of work burnout, though it is often confused with depression — partly because it is still under-researched and not an official diagnosis, so doctors may label it depression and even autistic people may think they just need to try harder [#28, msg #459, #460].
- It is a stress-overload state: prolonged stress without enough periods of low stress, support or relief accumulates until the person can no longer function at their usual level; autistic people face extra stressors from living in a neurotypical world and extra barriers to support [#28, msg #193].
- Its core picture is extreme fatigue, loss of skills, decline of social skills (incl. loss of masking) and heightened sensory sensitivity [#28, img #193].
- The two need different, even opposite, approaches: behavioral activation / increasing activity usually helps depression but can worsen burnout; deep rest helps burnout but can worsen depression [#193, #461, #465, article #28].
- They overlap heavily, and a person can have both at once; burnout can lead to depression, and where low mood accompanies burnout both should be treated [#193, msg #462, article #28].
- Discriminating signs of burnout: a clearer cause, heightened sensory problems, motivation and pleasure preserved but no energy, loss of skills and of the ability to mask; exhaustion is the core feeling. Depression: cause often unclear, low mood as the primary driver [#193, #463, #464, #465].
- Social withdrawal means different things: a maladaptive red flag in depression, an adaptive energy-management strategy in autistic burnout [#589].
- Misdiagnosis has a systemic cause: neurodivergent people meet neurotypical expectations by masking and overcompensating; when they crack, the exhaustion is labelled "depression", which shifts the focus onto the person instead of the expectations that broke them [#790].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Definition of autistic burnout
Community definition (article #28). The post introducing the channel's article describes autistic burnout as a special form of burnout that primarily affects people with ASD ("РАС") and ADHD ("СДВГ") — "уникальное и глубоко истощающее состояние" (a unique and deeply exhausting state) that arises from prolonged stress or an acute stressful experience, especially one connected with the need to adapt to the surrounding neurotypical world. The post frames it against a modern world where constant striving for efficiency and productivity is the norm and many people face burnout [#28]. The article itself (by Dr Alice Nicholls, translated and adapted for the channel by Nastya and Rina) explains that the term "Autistic burnout" was created by members of the autistic community to describe an experience common among autistic people; it is a common problem and one of the most discussed topics in autistic Facebook groups. Its core definition: "Аутистическое выгорание описывает опыт крайнего истощения у аутистов после периода длительного стресса или острого стрессового переживания." (Autistic burnout describes an experience of extreme exhaustion in autistic people after a period of prolonged stress or an acute stressful experience.) [#28]

Research definition (Raymaker et al., quoted in #193). The #193 post, introducing Megan Anna Neff's article, gives this definition: autistic burnout is a syndrome conceptualised as the result of chronic life stress and a mismatch between expectations and abilities, without adequate support. It is characterised by three features: (1) pervasive, long-term exhaustion — typically lasting 3+ months ("обычно 3+ месяца"); (2) loss of function; (3) reduced tolerance to stimuli. Original wording: "Аутичное выгорание - это синдром, концептуализированный как результат хронического жизненного стресса, несоответствия ожиданий и способностей, без адекватной поддержки" (Autistic burnout is a syndrome conceptualised as the result of chronic life stress, a mismatch of expectations and abilities, without adequate support) [#193].

#### Four core symptoms of autistic burnout (article #28)
The article stresses that autistic people experience burnout differently, but their descriptions share common features. The main symptoms are four; each is explained below [#28].
1. Extreme fatigue ("Крайняя усталость"). The degree varies. People in autistic burnout may feel constantly tired or perceive even the smallest tasks as a huge effort; some are so depleted that it is hard even to get out of bed [#28].
2. Loss of skills ("Потеря навыков"). Many describe losing cognitive skills or a decline in mental activity: increased difficulty finding words, processing information, solving problems or understanding new concepts. Some describe a "мозгового тумана" (brain fog) feeling — everything seems unclear and thinking takes effort [#28].
3. Decline of social skills ("Снижение социальных навыков"). This shows up differently depending on the starting point: (a) people who were previously highly social may find they no longer want to take part in as many social events and find it hard to keep up conversations at their former level; (b) people who already found social situations hard may feel unable to cope even with the most basic conversations and may even lose the ability to speak; (c) autistic people who usually hide their traits (use masking) may find they can no longer do so, or that masking tires them out even after short periods [#28].
4. Increased sensory sensitivity ("Увеличение чувствительности к восприятию"). Those who already struggled with certain sounds and textures may find these sensitivities intensify, and some start noticing sensitivities they never noticed before. The article's examples: the noise of artificial lighting in the office starts to bother the person; or the pattern on the wallpaper; they may feel anxiety or anger when touched; they may become ever more aware of how uncomfortable their clothes are [#28].

The article's conclusion summarises: autistic burnout is "переживание крайней усталости, потери когнитивных и социальных навыков, а также усиленной чувствительности к восприятию" (an experience of extreme fatigue, loss of cognitive and social skills, and heightened sensory sensitivity) [#28].

#### Stress-accumulation model of autistic burnout, with stressors and barriers (article #28)
Mechanism. Autistic burnout is the result of long-term stress in an autistic person: a combination of stressors plus the absence of sufficient support or relief produces an unbearably high stress level and, eventually, burnout. The causal chain the article lays out:
1. If we are exposed to moderate or high stress for a long time, our capacity to cope with that stress begins to deteriorate.
2. We can handle short-term spikes of stress, but we need periods of relatively low stress to recover and return to our baseline stress level.
3. Without such low-stress periods we keep "carrying" our stress with us, so any additional stress has a cumulative effect and even small stressors can feel unmanageable.
4. Eventually, if nothing is done, we become exhausted and can no longer function at the same level. [#28]

Why autistic people are at particular risk. They face especially high stress for several reasons caused directly and indirectly by being autistic in a neurotypical world — on top of the everyday stressors neurotypical people face too. The article lists eight sources of stress (introduced as "лишь несколько" — just a few):
1. Masking.
2. The double empathy problem.
3. Sensory sensitivity and unmet sensory needs.
4. Executive function difficulties.
5. Difficulty accepting change.
6. Life events.
7. Daily hassles.
8. Expectations (at work, in the family, in society). [#28]

Barriers to support. In addition, autistic people meet obstacles when seeking support to manage their stress. The article lists four:
1. Gaslighting.
2. Insufficient boundary-setting / self-advocacy.
3. Inability to take a break.
4. Lack of external resources / support services. [#28]
(!) INCOMPLETE: the article lists the stressors and barriers by name only; it does not explain each item further [#28].

Autistic burnout only (right circle), 6 items:

3. Benefit from rest and unmasking — self-care, sensory offloading, dropping the mask and immersion in interests can relieve burnout [#193].

#### Similarities / burnout-specific / depression-specific factors (neurodivergent lou carousel)
A seven-slide carousel by neurodivergent lou, forwarded from the channel Нейр∞тлично. Opening thesis: "Аутичное выгорание часто ошибочно принимают за депрессию..." (Autistic burnout is often mistaken for depression...) [#459, img #459].

Why it matters (slides 2–3). Autistic burnout is still insufficiently studied even though autistic people have discussed it for a long time; because of this it is sometimes confused with depression although they are different states. Even autistic people themselves may not recognise their own burnout and may, for example, believe they are depressed or that they simply «нужно больше постараться» (need to try harder) to feel better. Distinguishing them is important because they usually need different approaches: a person with depression is often advised to increase activity to lift mood and cope with negative thoughts, whereas in autistic burnout raising the activity level can, on the contrary, make the state worse [#460, img #461].

Similarities (slide 4), 6 items:
1. Need for isolation.
2. Feeling of exhaustion.
3. Difficulty with everyday tasks.
4. Emotional dysregulation.
5. Difficulty concentrating.
6. Changes in appetite. [#462]

Factors that may be more specific to autistic burnout (slides 5–6), 6 items:
1. A clearer cause. Example from the slide: you notice that lately you have had a packed schedule ("плотный график") [#463].
2. Heightened sensory problems [#463].
3. Wanting to do things but lacking the energy — motivation and pleasure may still be there, but no strength to act ("Может по-прежнему быть желание что-то" … "делать (может иметься мотивация и" … "удовольствие), но не быть сил для этого.") [#463].
4. Skill loss — e.g. being unable to do things you used to do [#463].
5. Deep rest works better when combined with activities linked to special interests or meeting sensory needs — deep rest can be more effective against burnout in that combination [#464].
6. Exhaustion is the foundational feeling of burnout ("Основополагающим чувством" / "при выгорании является истощение.") [#464].

Factors that may be more specific to depression (slide 7), 3 items:
1. The cause is not always clear [#465].
2. Mood as the primary driver. Mood can change in both conditions, but in depression low mood may be the main cause, whereas in burnout the lack of mood is rather a result of the frustration and exhaustion the burnout produces [#465].
3. Deep rest can worsen depression; increasing the activity level may be more effective instead [#465].

Note the hedging throughout: every factor is framed as "могут быть более" … "специфичны" (may be more specific), not a definitive criterion [#463, img #465].

#### Adaptive vs maladaptive social withdrawal
The #589 post ("Аутичное выгорание vs. Депрессия: Понимание различий в социальном отстранении" — understanding the differences in social withdrawal) argues that the nature of social withdrawal can be the key to telling the conditions apart, because it shows up differently in each [#589].
1. In depression — maladaptive withdrawal. Withdrawal from society is usually treated as a "red flag", a sign the depressive state is deepening. The person isolates because of a lack of interest, pleasure and motivation, which can lead to loss of meaning and a worsening state. The post calls this "дезадаптивное отстранение" (maladaptive withdrawal), which genuinely requires professional intervention [#589].
2. In autistic burnout — adaptive withdrawal. Citing Mantzalas et al. (2022), the post says that for many autistic adults social withdrawal is not avoidance but "адаптивный механизм" (an adaptive mechanism) and a strategic way of managing limited energy, which autistic people spend much faster in social interactions and under sensory load [#589].
3. The energy-cost mechanism. Every social interaction and sensory experience "costs" a certain amount of energy, and for autistic people the price can be disproportionately high [#589].
4. Masking as the stressor. Research (per the post) shows that stress comes from efforts to mask autistic traits and conform to neurotypical norms; constant "камуфляж" (camouflaging) leads to autistic burnout, which may be accompanied by anxiety, depression and even suicidal thoughts [#589].
5. Practical implication. Understanding that withdrawal in burnout is not maladaptation but a strategic form of self-help can lead to more effective support, letting autistic people engage with the world on their own terms in a sustainable way that is good for their wellbeing. Recognising the difference is the first step to adequate help and individualised recovery strategies [#589].

#### Autistic burnout vs occupational burnout (article #28)
The article compares autistic burnout with professional/occupational burnout, using the WHO description of the latter: "Чувство истощения энергии или изнеможения; увеличенное эмоциональное отчуждение от работы или чувство негативизма или цинизма по отношению к своей работе; и сниженная профессиональная эффективность." (A feeling of energy depletion or exhaustion; increased mental distance from one's job or feelings of negativism or cynicism related to one's job; and reduced professional efficacy.) (WHO website, 2021) [#28].
- What they share: both are results of long-term stress [#28].
- What separates them — the type of stress. A person with occupational burnout feels chronic stress from pressures at work; a person with autistic burnout feels chronic stress from the pressures of living and working in a neurotypical world, from the stress of life and of coping with social and sensory stressors [#28].
- Overlap of causes: work stress can contribute to autistic burnout, but its causes go beyond professional problems and are tied specifically to the difficulties of being autistic in a neurotypical society [#28].
- The article's conclusion: autistic burnout differs from both depression and occupational burnout, but autistic people are also at risk of both of these conditions [#28].

#### Five societal expectations placed on neurodivergent people (drcomptonsmith, translated by Rin Bonner)
Thesis: "Нейроотличным людям часто ошибочно ставят диагноз «депрессия»." (Neurodivergent people are often wrongly diagnosed with depression.) This happens because the expectations placed on them do not match how their brain works. Society assumes neurodivergent people can:
1. Manage attention and energy consistently.
2. Tolerate noise, interruptions and changes patiently.
3. Handle social demands without much effort.
4. Keep working under pressure.
5. Self-regulate without accommodations. [#790]

The mechanism of misdiagnosis. Neurodivergent people adapt through masking and overcompensation and so meet these expectations — and the fact that this costs them dearly is rarely taken into account. When it becomes unbearable and "cracks" appear, others see only fatigue, loss of motivation and withdrawal into oneself, and call it "depression". But for many neurodivergent people this is not a loss of interest in life; it is "истощение сил после многих лет, когда человеку приходилось преодолевать себя" (depletion of strength after many years of having to push past oneself). The consequence: "Когда выгорание лечат как депрессию, внимание сосредоточивается на человеке, а не на нейротипичных ожиданиях общества, которые его сломили." (When burnout is treated as depression, the focus falls on the person rather than on society's neurotypical expectations that broke them.) [#790]

### Detailed insights

#### Is autistic burnout a diagnosis? (article #28)
The article answers no: although autistic burnout is often discussed among autistic adults on social media, it has not been officially recognised by medical professionals or by either of the two diagnostic manuals. People who go to their doctors with autistic-burnout symptoms may instead be diagnosed with stress, anxiety, depression, chronic fatigue or occupational burnout. Still, researchers have begun to study it as a concept. Because with any new term there is a risk that people using the same words mean different things, a research group (Raymaker et al., 2020) interviewed members of the autistic community and analysed social-media content describing autistic burnout. They found that people describing autistic burnout talked about the same distinctive causes and symptoms, and concluded that the autistic community was describing a specific phenomenon distinct from both depression and occupational burnout. The article's conclusion: it is not a recognised diagnosis, "но люди, говорящие об этом, кажется, обсуждают одно и то же" (but people who talk about it seem to be discussing the same thing) [#28].

#### How burnout relates to depression (article #28)
- Separate but can co-occur. Autistic burnout may be accompanied by depression, but it is a separate phenomenon. Historically it may have been understood as depression; the reader may even have received a depression diagnosis when their symptoms would more accurately be understood as autistic burnout [#28].
- Burnout as a pathway into depression. Tony Attwood (2014) described several causes of depression in autism; one of them he describes as "Обусловлено умственным и эмоциональным истощением от общения, невозможностью терпеть интенсивную чувствительность к восприятию и чрезмерными изменениями, а также когнитивным стилем, фокусирующимся на том, что может пойти не так, что приводит к пессимистическому взгляду на жизнь" (Caused by mental and emotional exhaustion from socialising, inability to tolerate intense sensory sensitivity and excessive change, and a cognitive style focused on what might go wrong, leading to a pessimistic view of life) (Attwood, 2014, p. 100). The article reads this quote as showing how autistic burnout can lead to depression and where the two states resemble each other [#28].
- Treat both, and beware standard CBT. Where low mood is one of the signs alongside signs of autistic burnout, it is important to treat both conditions. Standard cognitive behavioural therapy for depression usually involves encouraging people to gradually engage in more pleasant and productive activities — which, the article warns, can cause problems if the sources of the burnout have not been addressed [#28].

#### Recurring differential markers across the sources
Pulling the frameworks together, the same discriminators appear in several independent sources: (a) response to activity vs rest — activation helps depression, worsens burnout; rest/unmasking/interests help burnout, deep rest can worsen depression [#193, img #461, img #464, img #465, article #28]; (b) motivation/interest — lost in depression (anhedonia, withdrawal from lack of interest), preserved but unpowered in burnout [#193, img #463, msg #589, msg #790]; (c) sensory sensitivity heightened in burnout [#193, img #463, article #28]; (d) skill loss in burnout, including cognitive skills and the ability to mask [#193, img #463, article #28]; (e) cause — clearer in burnout (accumulated stress, a packed schedule), often unclear in depression [#463, img #465, article #28]; (f) exhaustion as the core of burnout vs mood as the core of depression [#464, img #465, msg #790].

#### Depression can accompany burnout
The sources do not present the conditions as mutually exclusive: the Venn chart states both can exist at once, the article says autistic people are at risk of depression too and that both should be treated, and the #589 post notes that burnout caused by camouflaging may be accompanied by anxiety, depression and suicidal thoughts [#193, article #28, msg #589].
