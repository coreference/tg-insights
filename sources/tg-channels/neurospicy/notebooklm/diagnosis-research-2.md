## Differential diagnosis: autism, ADHD and look-alike conditions

### What this covers

This topic gathers everything the channel posted on telling autism and ADHD apart from conditions that resemble them, and from each other: PTSD and complex PTSD (cPTSD), borderline personality disorder (BPD), bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, schizoid personality disorder, OCD, depression, generalized anxiety disorder, panic attacks and selective mutism. Most of the material is translated: Venn diagrams from Neurodivergent Insights, a self-reflection checklist by Lindsey Mackereth, comparison tables from a psychologist's channel, and research summaries from a psychiatry channel [#43, #387, #544, #592].

The channel's reason for caring: these conditions overlap, so a professional may miss autism when PTSD is present, and "от этого зависит успешность терапевтических вмешательств" (the success of therapeutic interventions depends on this). The same point is made about bipolar disorder, where treatment success, including medication, depends on telling the overlapping traits apart [#43, #44]. A recurring message is that two conditions can be present at once ("Или оба?", *Or both?*), so the question is often "which, or both" rather than "which one" [#387, #544].

### Core ideas

- Overlap is real, so misdiagnosis is common. Autism and ADHD share symptoms with many psychiatric conditions, and they often co-occur with them. Clinicians must screen for both and ask what *causes* a trait, not just whether it is present (see *Why ADHD and autism are hard to tell apart*) [#58, #544].
- The same behavior can have different causes. The Mackereth checklist and the Venn diagrams repeatedly separate traits by motive. Avoiding eye contact because it distracts you points to autism; avoiding it out of distrust points to cPTSD. Repetitive behavior that soothes points to autism; compulsions tied to a specific fear point to OCD [#387, #601].
- Onset and course distinguish conditions. Autism and ADHD are present from childhood or early on. PTSD follows a traumatic event. Bipolar disorder often develops in late adolescence or youth. Schizophrenia symptoms appear in adolescence [#592, #594, #598, #600].
- Co-occurrence is the rule, not the exception. Autism often comes with at least one other condition, bipolar disorder among them. BPD and autism frequently co-occur, and autistic people are more vulnerable to developing BPD [#44, #544].
- Some states look alike but are not disorders. Verbal shutdowns are reactions to overload, not selective mutism. Autistic meltdowns may be mistaken for panic attacks. Autistic burnout is not the same as depression [#736, #482, #595].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Lindsey Mackereth's checklist "Autism or cPTSD?"
What it is and who made it. A checklist that helps sort out the differences between autism and complex PTSD. Its author is Lindsey Mackereth, a licensed mental-health specialist and expert in autism assessment, who built it from ten years of work with neurodivergent people. The channel translated it into Russian as a PDF titled "Аутизм или кПТСР? Чек-лист", subtitled "Инструмент для самопознания, помощи терапевтам и поддержки союзников" (a tool for self-knowledge, for helping therapists and for supporting allies) [#387, file #387].

When to use it. The post lists four uses: (1) self-exploration; (2) understanding loved ones better; (3) as supplementary material for therapy or coaching; (4) when preparing for an autism or mental-health assessment. It can also help therapists, coaches, assessors, medical workers and allies of neurodiverse people and trauma survivors. The post stresses: "это не диагностический инструмент, а скорее помощник для самоанализа" (it is not a diagnostic tool but rather an aid to self-analysis) [#387].

Background page: Autism vs cPTSD. Page 1 contrasts the two conditions on four points [#387]:
- *Autism.* A neurodevelopmental difference, defined as a disorder (autism spectrum disorder) in DSM-5-TR and ICD-11. It is an innate neurodivergence, characterized by five things: (1) social differences; (2) heightened or lowered sensitivity; (3) repetitive movements; (4) specific, focused areas of interest; (5) difficulty with change and disrupted routine. It lasts a lifetime. Difficulties linked to autistic traits can be eased when a person's sensory, communicative, social and psychological needs are understood and accommodated.
- *Complex PTSD.* Not currently in DSM-5-TR but identified in ICD-11. Many symptoms overlap with PTSD. It is acquired through repeated exposure to eight kinds of experience: (1) neglect or trauma, including emotional trauma; (2) bullying; (3) name-calling, being ignored or criticized by mentors or loved ones; (4) discrimination; (5) prolonged crisis situations; (6) persecution; (7) gaslighting or other forms of manipulation; (8) other forms of abuse. In many cases it is treatable: symptoms can often be treated or managed with empirically supported trauma-focused and skill-building psychotherapy.

How to fill it in. For each trait you *currently and most often* identify with, put a tick in the left (lilac) column. Then tick the matching cells in the Autism (green) and/or cPTSD (coral) columns, but only the cells that are colored and contain a checkbox. At the end, count the ticks in each column [#387].

Group A: traits that count for both autism and cPTSD (both cells colored; page 2) [#387]:

3. I spend a lot of time alone compared with others.
4. I often feel I am not like everyone else.
5. It is hard for me to make and/or keep friendships.
6. I like to control events in my life.
7. I experience dissociation (feeling detached from myself, other people or surroundings).
8. I have repetitive body movements or behavioral habits I perform regularly: skin picking, rocking, pacing, making sounds, repeating words, listening to songs on repeat, nail biting, fidgeting.
9. I use repetitive movements or behavior to cope with high stress and/or emotional overload.
10. I tend to experience overload more often than others.
11. I experience my emotions very intensely and/or find it hard to identify them.

The pattern behind the items. Several items come in matched pairs that differ only in motive: eye contact avoided because it distracts (16, autism) vs out of distrust (41, cPTSD); control that prevents sensory overload (31) vs control because of distrust (38); emotions not shared because they are hard to explain (24) vs because of distrust or shame (32); overload from sensory input (25) vs from emotional triggers or flashbacks (51); routines relied on to feel like oneself (14) vs routines not relied on but useful for avoiding hypervigilance triggers (46); time alone for sensory recovery (17) vs feeling safer alone and isolating to avoid pain (40, 42) [#387].

How to read the result. Comparing the columns is for self-analysis only. It is meant to give some idea of whether your symptoms and traits fit autism, complex PTSD or a combination. It is not a diagnostic tool, and there are no "баллов" (points / scores) that lead to a diagnosis or identification. Bring any questions, concerns or insights from it to your next session with a therapist or coach. The author also calls it a great tool to take to an autism or mental-health assessment. The content does not replace professional medical consultation, examination, diagnosis or treatment. Always consult a doctor or other qualified specialist before starting, changing or stopping any health-related treatment [#387].

#### C-PTSD vs BPD table
A two-card comparison from the channel Домик психолога Ани [#297, img #298].

Similarities between C-PTSD and BPD [#297]:
1. Impulsivity.
2. Emotional instability and outbursts of anger.
3. Dissociation and flashbacks.
4. Fear of rejection.
5. Maladaptive ways of coping with emotions and stress.

Differences (C-PTSD on the left, BPD on the right) [#298]:
1. *Relationships.* C-PTSD: a pull toward solitude, difficulty getting close and building relationships, and a marked level of avoidance. BPD: unstable relationships with people and a strong sense of emptiness and loneliness.
2. *Under stress.* C-PTSD: dissociation, depersonalization, irritability and substance use. BPD: hysterics, suicide attempts, risky behavior and self-harm.
3. *Self-esteem.* C-PTSD: stably negative self-esteem. BPD: unstable self-esteem that swings from idealization to devaluation and self-criticism.

#### C-PTSD vs PTSD table
Same source; the post title is "Таблицы сходств и различий К-ПТСР с ПРЛ и ПТСР" (tables of similarities and differences of C-PTSD with BPD and PTSD) [#300].

Similarities between C-PTSD and PTSD [#299]:
1. Flashbacks, intrusive memories, nightmares and insomnia.
2. Memory problems and reduced concentration.
3. Strong fears, a sense of danger, numbness and feeling cut off from others.
4. Guilt and shame, irritability and avoidance.

1. Behavioral disturbances: self-destructive behavior and outbursts of anger.
2. Difficulty maintaining relationships, and a change in one's former personality.
3. Persistent, long-lasting disturbances in the affective sphere: dissociative states and heightened emotional reactivity.
4. Origin: PTSD arises from a single traumatic event. C-PTSD arises from prolonged, repeated traumatic events that continue for months or even years.

#### BPD vs autism Venn diagram ("Misdiagnosis Monday")
Context. This diagram comes from Neurodivergent Insights' "Понедельник неправильных диагнозов" (Misdiagnosis Monday) series, translated by the channel, with an article by dr. Megan Anna Neff. Neff's argument: BPD is often misdiagnosed in autistic women and genderqueer people. It is further complicated because BPD and autism often co-occur and autistic people are more vulnerable to developing BPD, so a person can have both. If the underlying autism is missed, the borderline symptoms usually intensify. Many specialists do not consider autism when diagnosing BPD, so she says autism should always be on the differential list when BPD is suspected, precisely because the autistic neurotype makes people more prone to developing it [#544].

The diagram matches up four themes, then lists the overlap and each side's distinguishing traits [#544]:

1. Identity. BPD: *identity instability*, a persistent pattern of instability in relationships, self-image and mood. Autism: *masked identity*; autistic people who actively mask often have a blurred sense of self and form their identity through interaction with other people.
2. Heightened emotions. BPD: emotional distress caused by attachment trauma, feelings of abandonment, conflicts and reminders of past trauma. Autism: emotional distress caused by unexpected changes, social misunderstandings, and cognitive and sensory overload.
3. Inconsistency in relationships. BPD: intense relationships often accompanied by idealization and devaluation of the partner. Autism: difficulty maintaining friendships, or a tendency to have one intense friendship at a time.
4. Emptiness vs special interests. BPD: *constant emptiness*, a persistent feeling of emptiness, uselessness and lack of purpose. Autism: special interests make up a large part of identity, and without them the person may feel empty and lost.

Overlap (both): (1) dissociation; (2) a blurred sense of self; (3) impaired self-esteem; (4) relationship difficulties; (5) a tendency to systematize and categorize; (6) heightened risk of victimization, suicidality and self-harm; (7) higher rates of eating disorders and substance abuse; (8) difficulty controlling impulses; (9) difficulty regulating intense emotions; (10) paranoid thoughts; (11) rejection sensitivity [#544].

Distinguishing features of BPD: (1) a pervasive fear of being abandoned; (2) destructive impulsivity that arises when the person feels abandoned; (3) projection, often used as a form of communication [#544].

Distinguishing features of autism: (1) a monotropic thinking style; (2) self-soothing through repetition or keeping to routine; (3) unconsciously missing hidden social signals [#544].

Bottom line of the card: "У человека могут быть оба состояния одновременно" (a person can have both conditions at the same time). Its note says the diagram reflects common experiences, individual experience may vary, and it is not meant for diagnosis [#544].

#### OCD vs autism Venn diagram
[#601]
- OCD only (3): (1) repetitive behavior (compulsions) aimed at reducing anxiety linked to obsessions; (2) compulsions are directly tied to obsessions, so the repetitive behavior goes with a specific fear (for example, handwashing targets a fear of contamination); (3) intrusive, unwanted thoughts, urges, sensations and images (obsessions).
- Both (8): (1) insomnia; (2) repetitive behavior; (3) both are considered neurological conditions involving similar neural circuits in the brain; (4) intolerance of uncertainty; (5) different sensory profiles; (6) compulsions, meaning repetitive behavior such as rituals; (7) high risk of substance dependence; (8) looping thinking.
- Autism only (5): (1) special interests; (2) symptoms improve with CBT for OCD adapted for autistic people; (3) repetitive behavior is not tied to obsessions (fear); (4) self-soothing through repetitive behavior, routine and stimming; (5) difficulty with the neurotypical pattern of social communication.

The core differentiator is what the repetitive behavior is for: in OCD it neutralizes an obsession's fear, while in autism it soothes and is not tied to a fear [#601].

(!) CONTRADICTION: compulsions/rituals are placed in the overlap, even though the autism side says autistic repetitive behavior is not tied to obsessions [#601].

#### Selective mutism vs verbal shutdown
Purpose. Both states can make speech impossible, but for different reasons. Selective mutism is an anxiety disorder with stable patterns. Verbal shutdowns are temporary reactions to stress or overload. Translated for the channel from autisticguidetothegalaxy [#736].

Selective mutism, five features: (1) it is an anxiety disorder; (2) speech is possible in some situations but not others; (3) the pattern is stable, safe spaces (such as home) versus unsafe spaces (such as school or work); (4) it most often starts in childhood but can persist into adulthood; (5) later onset is possible but rare [#736].

Verbal shutdown, four features: (1) a reaction to stress or overload; (2) it can happen in any situation where demands, emotions or sensory stimuli overwhelm; (3) speech returns once the nervous system settles; (4) it occurs in autism, ADHD, trauma and other conditions [#736].

How each feels. In selective mutism, the words are there but feel "заблокированными" (blocked); anxiety makes speech impossible. In a verbal shutdown, the brain and body are saving energy and the speech function temporarily "отключается" (switches off) [#736].

Potential triggers of selective mutism: (1) social anxiety; (2) fear of evaluation or embarrassment; (3) talking with authority figures; (4) new or unfamiliar situations [#736].

Potential triggers of a verbal shutdown: (1) sensory overload (noise, light, smell, touch); (2) emotional overload; (3) stress, fatigue or burnout; (4) too many demands at once [#736].

Key differences:
1. *Duration.* Selective mutism is usually long-lasting and situational; verbal shutdowns are usually short and temporary.
2. *Pattern.* Selective mutism follows a stable "safe vs unsafe" pattern; shutdowns can happen anywhere at peak overload.
3. *Trigger.* Selective mutism comes from social anxiety and fear of speaking; shutdowns come from overload, stress or exhaustion [#736].

Why it matters. Knowing the difference helps you explain your experience and helps others respond with patience rather than pressure. Both states need understanding, a safe environment and alternative ways of communicating. Selective mutism is an anxiety disorder, not a trait of autism or ADHD, but an autistic person or a person with ADHD can also have it. In short: "Селективный мутизм = симптом тревожного расстройства. Вербальный шатдаун = реакция на аутистическую перегрузку." (Selective mutism = a symptom of an anxiety disorder. Verbal shutdown = a reaction to autistic overload.) [#736]

Admin's illustrations. The channel admin gives two examples. For selective mutism she thinks of Raj from *The Big Bang Theory*, who could not talk to women because of anxiety. For a verbal shutdown she describes being asked at home where something is and being unable to explain, because there is no energy to form words, so it is easier to get up and show where it is. What she feels is specifically a lack of resource for talking. This also shows a shutdown can happen in a safe place such as home, unlike selective mutism [#736].

### Detailed insights

#### Why ADHD and autism are hard to tell apart
Many neurodivergent people struggle to get an accurate diagnosis, especially with ADHD and ASD. Research shows the two overlap substantially in symptoms and in genetic and neurobiological basis, and many people with ADHD show autistic traits and vice versa [#58].

The post names these points that make them hard to separate:
1. Similar problems with social interaction, communication and emotion regulation.
2. Overlapping difficulties with executive functions, attention and sensory processing.
3. Repetitive behaviors and interests in both groups.
The post ends the list with "и др..." (and the like) without naming further items [#58].

ADHD and ASD also often occur together in one person, which complicates the picture further. The author's conclusion: when assessing either condition, specialists should screen for signs of both and ask the right questions to find out what causes a trait. In her view, only a comprehensive diagnosis can establish a person's precise neurodivergence profile and build the most effective support plan. A longer article by clinical psychologist Anna Neff, "СДВГ vs. РАС", was translated for the channel [#58].

#### Schizophrenia and autism: shared history
Their history is tangled: for many years autism was considered part of the schizophrenia spectrum. In the first edition of the DSM, children with autistic symptoms were labeled as having childhood schizophrenia, and only in 1980 did autism get its own category, in DSM-III. The channel's mini-article covers a brief history of autism, a definition of schizophrenia, the overlapping traits and the differences, stressing that telling them apart matters for accurate diagnosis [#50].

#### Meltdowns mistaken for panic attacks

#### Bipolar I and schizophrenia: one clinical axis (research summary)
Source: the channel Идентификация с дуркой, summarizing a study published on nature.com (article s41398-025-03403-6) [#663].
- The problem. Schizophrenia and bipolar I share symptoms such as hallucinations and delusions, and overlapping phenomenology and neurobiology. When bipolar I has psychotic features, it is often misdiagnosed as schizophrenia. That delays correct treatment and leads to a worse course of illness [#663].
- Schizophrenia is heterogeneous. Patients can have completely different symptoms, from auditory hallucinations and paranoia to marked apathy and detachment, so a single universal profile is almost impossible [#663].
- Method. The researchers used the machine-learning method U-MAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) to simplify and visualize complex data and show how symptoms group into clusters, "превратить хаос множества психиатрических описаний в наглядную карту" (to turn the chaos of many psychiatric descriptions into a clear map). They added network analysis to find which symptoms are central, or dominant, in a disorder's overall picture. The goal was to look at the structure of the disorders through real symptom patterns rather than formal diagnoses [#663].
- Finding 1: schizophrenia. Cluster analysis cleanly separated the negative symptoms (avolition, anhedonia, mutism) from the others (delusions, hallucinations, disorganization, affective disturbances). The negative symptoms correlate strongly with each other and weakly with the rest, suggesting they are a relatively autonomous syndrome block in the pathogenesis of schizophrenia [#663].
- Finding 2: bipolar I. Two clusters emerged: one where patients had almost no psychotic symptoms, and one where most did. This matches the DSM-5 distinction between bipolar I with and without psychotic features. Unlike schizophrenia, though, "ни один отдельный симптом не позволял чётко различить эти две группы" (no single symptom clearly distinguished these two groups). The split rested on a weak, blurry combination of features with no clear "anchor" symptoms. Sub-clustering showed that patients without psychosis also had almost no anxiety symptoms, while anxiety was more common in the psychosis group. Overall, the bipolar I symptom picture was diffuse, with no stable patterns [#663].
- Finding 3: central symptoms. In bipolar I, network analysis found positive symptoms central, especially paranoid delusions. In schizophrenia there was a single patient cluster, and network analysis confirmed negative symptoms are central, which Eugen Bleuler had begun to describe in the 19th–20th centuries. The more negative symptoms a patient had, the more other psychotic manifestations they had too, suggesting negative symptoms may mark a severe course of schizophrenia [#663].

#### Hallucinations in BPD (research summary, part 1)
Source: the channel Идентификация с дуркой [#734].
- Why clinicians call them "pseudohallucinations". In clinical practice, hallucinations in BPD are often labeled "псевдогаллюцинации" (pseudohallucinations), signaling doubt that they are real hallucinations. One reason may be that DSM-5 says that in BPD, hallucinations occur "лишь у некоторых людей в периоды стресса" (only in some people during periods of stress). So clinicians may hesitate to call every positive perceptual disturbance in this group a hallucination [#734].
- What research shows. Research over the past decade shows genuine hallucinations in BPD are not rare, with prevalence of 26–54%. They span several senses: 21–59% are auditory, 30–33% visual, 10–30% olfactory and 13% tactile [#734].
- Chronic, not transient. Auditory hallucinations, the most common, included demeaning and threatening voices lasting for years (on average more than 15) and causing marked distress. This points to a chronic symptom, sharply contrasting with the view of "stress-induced" or "temporary" hallucinations in BPD [#734].
- Definitions. A *pseudohallucination* is when a person hears, sees or feels something but is aware that the experience is subjective ("я слышу голоса, но понимаю, что они в моей голове", *I hear voices but understand they are in my head*); full conviction that it comes from outside is missing. A *hallucination in the strict sense* is perceived as existing objectively in the outside world ("голос исходит из соседней комнаты", *the voice is coming from the next room*). The author concludes that in content, form and psychological impact, BPD hallucinations are close to those in schizophrenia. BPD patients often partly doubt the source, yet still experience the voices as real enough to count as hallucinations [#734].
- Typical features of the voices. They occurred at least once an hour, lasted several minutes, were perceived as inside or near the head, and were about as loud as the patient's own voice [#734].
- Known voices, trauma content. More than half (61%) heard the voices of people they knew, often relatives, abusive figures or significant people from the past, which the author reads as an internalization of traumatic experience. The content was almost always sharply negative: direct insults, accusations, attacks on self-esteem and mockery. Some patients reported voices commenting on their actions in real time, which resembles the "running commentary" in schizophrenia [#734].
- Place on a continuum. Hallucinations occur in 3–6% of the general population and 75% of people with schizophrenia, so BPD sits in an intermediate position on this nosological continuum. By the phenomenology of the hallucinations, BPD patients appear to belong at the severe end of the spectrum [#734].

#### OCD as a bee: how OCD works and how to talk back to it
A translated text by mylovelyocd that explains OCD's mechanism through metaphors and offers replacement phrases [#626].
- Mechanism. OCD is like a bee: it buzzes constantly and keeps the brain on high alert. The more you swat at it, the angrier it gets. The more compulsions you perform, the harsher OCD becomes; the more you argue with it, the more confidently it demands. OCD is also like a thief, stealing moments, pleasure and calm when they cross its path [#626].
- Reframe 1. Instead of "никто больше не думает об этом!" (nobody else thinks about this!), try "я не знаю, что происходит в головах других людей. Все, что я могу, это жить свою жизнь в соответствии с моими ценностями" (I don't know what goes on in other people's heads. All I can do is live my life according to my values) [#626].
- Reframe 2. Instead of "у меня все иначе! Я ужасен!" (it's different for me! I'm terrible!), try "может, у меня все иначе, кто знает. В любом случае, я в безопасности" (maybe it is different for me, who knows. Either way, I am safe) [#626].
- What OCD uses against you. Your past, your thoughts, your diagnoses, and also your lack of diagnoses [#626].
- Content. OCD is complex; obsessions often involve contamination, violence, sexual themes and other things [#626].
- Stance. OCD should not take away life's pleasant moments. It is a liar that will say anything to convince you, but you, not OCD, are responsible for your life, and you deserve happiness [#626].

## Reading autism and ADHD research critically

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's science explainers on the biology of autism — connectivity theories, infant salience-network findings, fMRI-derived autism subtypes and autism genetics — together with its critical breakdowns of specific studies and public claims: the Stanford Z944 mouse study, a study linking autism and vulnerable narcissism, the paracetamol-causes-autism claim, and the idea of Ozempic for ADHD. The recurring lesson according to the source is that autism is heterogeneous, that correlation is not mechanism, that sensational headlines overstate narrow findings, and that a single study's data cannot be generalized to all autistic people [#130, #317, #658, #718, #725, #755, #57].

### Core ideas

- Autism brains differ in connectivity in both directions. Research converged on autism involving both under-connected distant regions and over-connected neighbouring regions, but disagreed about where; infant studies were used to cut out confounds (see *Hypo- vs hyper-connectivity*) [#130].
- In infants at elevated autism risk, attention to sensations and movement seems to "borrow" from social attention: the salience network couples more to sensorimotor areas than to prefrontal social areas [#130].
- Autism can be split into four connectivity-based subtypes, each with its own neural pattern, genetic markers and behavioural profile (see *Four-step subtype pipeline*) [#317].
- Autism genetics is complex and probabilistic: de-novo and polygenic contributions, at least 185 associated genes that are not autism-specific, and incomplete penetrance — autism is "not a binary category" but an interaction of vulnerabilities, compensations and environment [#658].
- Sensational "cure" or "comorbidity" headlines usually rest on narrow studies (mice with one mutation; self-report questionnaires with unbalanced groups) whose limitations the channel lists item by item [#718, #725].
- Paracetamol does not cause autism; the author sees the claim as a correlation confound and as part of a pattern of blaming mothers, comparable to Wakefield's fabricated vaccine study [#755].
- Ozempic for ADHD is still unproven: there is not yet enough research to be confident of its success in correcting ADHD symptoms [#57].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Hypo-connectivity vs hyper-connectivity theories of autism
Purpose: the connectivity theories ("теории связности") propose that brain regions are connected and interact differently in people on the spectrum and outside it; the note says these theories currently play one of the central roles in understanding the biology of autism. The note was written by the author (Настя, forwarded from the channel "Human Gazing Upon Machine") as a quick summary answering readers' questions about a previous article [#130].

1. Hypo-connectivity theory (теория пониженной связности). In people with ASD, brain regions that are *far* from each other interact *less* and therefore less efficiently — reduced functional connectivity between distal regions. Given which regions sit at such distances in the brain, this may lead to problems in social communication and cognition [#130].
2. Hyper-connectivity theory (теория гиперсвязности). In people with ASD the number of connections between *neighbouring* (proximal) regions is *increased* compared with neurotypicals. This may lead to narrow specialization in information processing and hence to stronger focus on details at the expense of broader, context-aware understanding. The note says this theory fits the intense focus and restricted interests often observed in ASD [#130].

How the field moved (the "key findings" as narrated):
- There were many suggestions that the autistic brain is less connected than the neurotypical one in some places but better connected in other places; researchers agreed that both happen, but soon disagreed on *where* — results were influenced sometimes by participants' age, sometimes by experience, "то ещё миллион всего" (sometimes by a million other things). Under suspicion were the default mode network (DMN), the anterior cingulate and prefrontal cortex (PFC), the amygdala and the sensory cortices (SC). It was then decided that some factors should be excluded — for example by studying infants [#130].
- The latest data (2023–2024) found significant differences in brain structures of infants at risk of ASD, especially in the salience network (SN) — the network for detecting and filtering stimuli from the environment, which lets a person separate the main from the secondary in the information stream and direct attention accordingly. A footnote specifies the SN anatomy: it includes parts of the inner (medial) cortical surface — the anterior cingulate gyrus, the anterior insula and the pre-supplementary motor areas — plus nodes in the amygdala, hypothalamus, ventral striatum, thalamus and specific brainstem nuclei [#130].
- UCLA Health found that infants with the population-typical chance of developing ASD had stronger SN connections with prefrontal areas, which are critical for social attention and interaction, whereas infants at elevated risk had stronger SN connections with sensorimotor areas, where primary processing of movement and sensory information happens [#130].
- Conclusion drawn: "похоже, у потенциально нейроотличных людей внимание к ощущениям и движениям берёт в кредит у внимания к социальным аспектам" (it seems that in potentially neurodivergent people, attention to sensations and movements borrows on credit from attention to social aspects). The note continues in the source channel; that continuation is not part of this input — (!) INCOMPLETE [#130].
- The post's photo has no text (illustration only) [#130].

#### Four-step pipeline used to derive autism connectivity subtypes (ABIDE fMRI study)
Purpose: to move from a broad ASD-vs-NT contrast to a fine, connectivity-based understanding of subtypes within the spectrum. The post ("Подтипы РАС: пересборка", *ASD subtypes: reassembly*, part 1, forwarded from "Human Gazing Upon Machine") defines its glossary — НТ = neurotypical, НО = neurodivergent, РАС/спектр = autism spectrum disorder (refers to ND people). Data: researchers examined 254 people with ASD and 295 NT from the Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange, assembling a large dataset with resting-state functional MRI to analyze brain connectivity patterns. The first part of the series is in an earlier post not included here — (!) INCOMPLETE [#317].

1. Compare NT and ASD. Some connections were found to be broadly altered in ASD as a whole. However, the researchers acknowledged that these general patterns cannot explain the differences in symptoms and behaviour *within* the ASD group — i.e., a group average does not describe individuals [#317].
2. Compare within the ASD group. The data were split into two categories: (a) shared connectivity patterns — present more universally in people with ASD; (b) individual-specific connectivity patterns — unique patterns that differ between people within the ASD group [#317].
3. Identify the key dimensions. Within the individual-specific patterns, using mathematical methods such as orthogonal basis extraction, three main dimensions emerged — kinds of clusters describing how strongly particular regions are connected to each other: (i) a social-communication cluster; (ii) a repetitive-behaviour cluster; (iii) a speech and cognitive-flexibility cluster [#317].
4. Group by unique connectivity profile and validate with genetics and behaviour. Along the three dimensions each person in the ASD group was assigned according to how strongly the regions contributing to social communication (and the other dimensions) were connected. People with similar profiles were grouped by clustering algorithms into 4 subtypes, each with a unique neural basis — patterns of how specific brain networks (such as the default mode network, the salience network; the source adds "и др.", i.e. further unnamed networks) are functionally connected. To check accuracy, the subtypes were cross-matched with behavioural assessments and genetic data: each subtype's connectivity profile had unique genetic markers and behavioural patterns, which both confirmed the models and added a biological basis to the classification [#317].

The post does not name or describe the four subtypes themselves — (!) INCOMPLETE [#317].

#### Incomplete penetrance and modifying factors between mutation and phenotype
Purpose: to explain why the same genetic damage leads to autism in one person and not another (forwarded from the channel Идентификация с дуркой) [#658].
- Definition of autism used: a heterogeneous behavioural phenotype characterized by atypical social communication and unusually restricted or stereotyped interests. Its genetics is as complex as that of many other psychiatric disorders [#658].
- Two kinds of genetic contribution: (1) monogenic de-novo factors — mutations that arose for the first time in the person in a gene's structure and are absent in the parents; (2) polygenic factors — many variants across different genes, each contributing a small amount to the formation of behaviour in people with ASD [#658].
- 185 genes, but "associated", not specific: recent Nature work identifies at least 185 genes whose variants are most often found in autistic people. The post stresses the wording "ассоцированность с РАС" (association with ASD) because there is as yet no convincing evidence that these genes are specific to the spectrum and do not appear in, for example, patients with schizophrenia or bipolar disorder (which often happens in other groups) [#658].
- The 36% observation: 36% of mutations that "cut off a significant part of the protein" in undiagnosed people (without an autism diagnosis) fall in the same coding regions of the gene as in autistic patients. At the protein level these mutations very likely cause similar functional consequences — protein truncation, loss of a key domain, inactivation. In other words, mutations in exactly the same "important" parts of a gene do not always lead to a clinical autism phenotype [#658].
- Incomplete penetrance (неполная пенетрантность) — a key principle of modern neuropsychogenetics: even if a mutation destroys protein function, it does not guarantee the carrier develops a behavioural disorder or an ASD diagnosis. Between the genetic defect and the phenotype lies a space filled with modifying factors, listed as: (1) sex; (2) polygenic predisposition; (3) epigenetics (the author notes this deserves a separate discussion); (4) environment; (5) developmental conditions; (6) level of social support; (7) stress load [#658].
- Female Protective Effect: women are known to have a higher threshold for clinical manifestation of ASD — they need a more severe mutation, or its combination with other vulnerabilities, for a pronounced autistic phenotype to develop [#658].
- Behavioural compensation: the same biochemical damage can be compensated. A mutation that disrupts synapse development may be partly offset by increased activity of alternative cellular signalling pathways, by neuroplasticity (restructuring of synapse architecture), or by an adaptive cognitive environment. This explains why two people with identical mutations in the same protein region can develop differently — one with clinical autism, the other without — even though their genes are damaged similarly at the molecular level [#658].
- Conclusion: "Аутизм — не бинарная категория, а результат взаимодействия уязвимостей, компенсаций и среды" (Autism is not a binary category but the result of the interaction of vulnerabilities, compensations and environment). Predicting it therefore requires not only knowing the mutations in clinical interpretation but understanding the multi-layered regulation of the behavioural phenotype [#658].

#### Five critical limitations of the Z944 mouse study (checklist for reading "autism cure" headlines)
Context: a Stanford Medicine study published in Science Advances on the drug Z944 in autism triggered a wave of sensational headlines implying autism could now be fully cured. The channel's verdict in the title: "важная находка, но не "лечение одной дозой"" (an important finding, but not a "one-dose cure"). What the study actually did [#718]:
- It was conducted only on mice with the Cntnap2 mutation, which represents about 1% of ASD cases in humans.
- It found hyperactivity of the thalamic reticular nucleus (RT) and a link between it and autistic manifestations.
- Z944, a T-type calcium channel blocker, did increase social activity and reduce repetitive behaviour in the mice. Important: Z944 was originally developed as an epilepsy drug, not an autism drug.

The limitations, in the post's order [#718]:
1. Heterogeneity of ASD. ASD includes hundreds of different genetic variants. Results for one model (Cntnap2) cannot be extrapolated to the whole spectrum; what works for this variant may be ineffective or even harmful for other ASD variants.
2. Causality. It is unclear what comes first: RT dysfunction → autistic behaviour, or the reverse.
3. Interspecies differences. Mouse models do not reproduce the complexity of human social behaviour and neurodevelopment. Also, the study was conducted on males only.
4. Comorbid conditions. The study ignores the real complexity of autistic people. "Многие из нас" (many of us) have comorbidities (hEDS, MCAS; the source adds "и др.", without naming further ones) that could radically change the response to a drug. Example: with MCAS (САТК), mast cells may over-react to the drug, which could cause inflammation in the brain and worsen symptoms.
5. Ethical aspects. (a) Autistic children and people with high support needs cannot give informed consent, so "treatment" may be imposed on them; (b) there are no long-term safety data.

Bottom line: this is a typical situation in neuroscience — an interesting finding, but a huge gap between observed correlations, understanding of mechanisms and practical application. The study opens new questions rather than giving ready answers, let alone a "treatment". The post ends by asking readers what they think about the question of "treating" autism [#718].

#### Five omissions of the autism / vulnerable narcissism study (checklist for reading self-report comorbidity studies)
Context: after the study "Manifestation of narcissistic vulnerability in adults with autism without intellectual disability" came out, the author repeatedly met the opinion that autism and vulnerable narcissism are comorbid ("ведь есть исследование!" — *after all, there's a study!*). Having read it, she did not come to that view and wrote a breakdown. She reminds readers she is not a specialist and that one should always treat online information critically and re-check sources and facts [#725].

What the study did [#725]:
- Aim: assess levels of narcissism (grandiose and vulnerable) in autistic adults without intellectual disability.
- Autism was established with international tests and scales: DSM-5, ADOS-2, AQ, RAADS-R, EQ.
- Narcissism was measured with PNI-52, a modern instrument for separating grandiose and vulnerable narcissism.
- Participants: 87 autistic adults and 1,487 people from the general population (controls).

Main results as reported [#725]:
- Autistic people scored significantly higher on the overall narcissism scale, and the difference was specifically in vulnerable narcissism (hypersensitivity to criticism, avoidance, shame, guilt), not the grandiose type (exploitation, self-aggrandizement).
- 28.7% of the autistic participants exceeded the threshold for pathological narcissism.
- No critical differences between men and women, and no influence of age or intelligence, were found.
- The correlation between autistic people's social difficulties and the severity of vulnerable narcissism was significant.

What was missed — the five omissions [#725]:
1. No clinical assessment. All participants answered on their own, without an interview with a psychologist or psychiatrist. Autistic people may have interpreted questions literally or wrongly, which strongly affects reliability; difficulties with self-assessment and reflection are a very common factor in autism.
2. Questionnaires did not account for autistic traits. Some autistic traits may be misread as narcissistic traits.
3. Unaccounted comorbidities. Autistic people often have C-PTSD/PTSD symptoms and rejection-sensitive dysphoria. Their features (hypersensitivity to criticism, avoidance, shame, guilt) are easily confused with vulnerable-narcissism traits on tests, because tests often do not specify the *motivation* behind a behaviour. Accurate differentiation needs an in-depth clinical conversation.
4. Depression and anxiety not considered. A serious gap, since internal states can "mask" or amplify some traits.
5. Very different group sizes. Only 87 autistic people vs 1,487 controls — strongly unbalanced samples; such disproportions do not reflect the real picture and make results hard to interpret.

What can be said for certain (author's conclusion): autistic adults without intellectual disability show a high correlation with hypersensitivity to criticism, avoidance, shame and guilt, but this cannot be directly attributed to vulnerable narcissism, because no in-depth clinical interview differentiating other possible diagnoses/co-factors was done. Hence the results are insufficient to claim that autism and vulnerable narcissism are comorbid, and they cannot be transferred to the whole autistic population given the unbalanced samples. A clearer picture of the comorbidity of autism and covert narcissism requires more precise data including a full diagnostic interview and a comprehensive mental-health examination. She adds that autistic people are not immune from having a personality disorder (НРЛ) or vulnerable narcissism; the point is only that the data of *this particular study* cannot be read as unambiguous truth about comorbidity. She invites feedback on the breakdown format and ideas for future breakdowns [#725].

### Practical playbook

A reading checklist distilled strictly from the questions the channel asks of studies (each item is drawn from the breakdowns above) [#718, #725, #755, #317]:
- Who/what was studied? Mice vs humans, one genetic variant (e.g., ~1% of cases) vs the whole spectrum, males only [#718].
- Is causality established, or only correlation? Which comes first? [#718]
- Are comorbidities (hEDS, MCAS, C-PTSD, RSD, depression, anxiety) accounted for? [#718, #725]
- Was there a clinical interview, or only self-report questionnaires that may be read literally? [#725]
- Are the instruments adapted to autistic traits that may mimic the measured construct? [#725]
- Are the groups balanced and the sample large enough (cf. 87 vs 1,487; Wakefield's 12 children)? [#725, #755]
- Is there a conflict of interest (cf. Wakefield's law-firm ties)? [#755]
- Do group averages explain individual differences (cf. step 1 of the subtype study)? [#317]
- Are there long-term safety data and is informed consent possible for proposed "treatments"? [#718]

### Evidence and numbers

- ABIDE subtype study: 254 ASD vs 295 NT participants, resting-state fMRI, 3 dimensions, 4 subtypes [#317].
- At least 185 genes associated with autism (Nature); 36% of protein-truncating mutations in undiagnosed people fall in the same coding regions as in autistic patients [#658].
- Cntnap2 mutation ≈ 1% of human ASD cases; Z944 study in male mice only [#718].
- Narcissism study: 87 autistic adults vs 1,487 controls; 28.7% of autistic participants above the pathological narcissism threshold [#725].
- Wakefield vaccine study: 12 children, no significance [#755].
