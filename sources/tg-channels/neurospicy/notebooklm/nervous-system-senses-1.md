## Regulating the neurodivergent nervous system

### What this covers

This topic gathers everything the channel taught about how the neurodivergent (ND) nervous system works and how to regulate it: the anatomy of the nervous system, the sympathetic and parasympathetic states, the vagus nerve with its ventral and dorsal branches, vagal tone and heart rate variability, the window of tolerance with hyperarousal, hypoarousal and false regulation, dorsal vagal shutdown, an extensive toolbox of practices that lower activation (calming) or raise it (energising) — including bilateral stimulation through 8D audio and in-the-moment somatic techniques from the author's own therapy — and the author's own guide to insomnia in ND people (physiological causes and a sleep plan). Much of the material is translated from Neurodivergent Insights (clinical psychologist Megan Anna Neff). According to the source it matters because ND nervous systems are constantly activated and tend to get stuck in fight/flight or freeze, which undermines rest, burnout recovery, sleep and everyday coping — and because, in the author's words, our difficulties are not "all in our head" [#35, #578, article #34, article #35].

### Core ideas

- The ND nervous system is physiologically different, not imagined. The author likes the idea that "наша нейроотличность не означает, что все проблемы в нашей голове" (our neurodivergence does not mean all problems are in our head): many daily difficulties really are tied to how our nervous system works, and we deserve to know this and to be taken seriously by doctors [#35].
- ND nervous systems do not filter input and are constantly activated. In evolutionary terms this sensitivity was a great skill; in today's sensory-overloaded world it overstrains the system, so at the end of the day, when we can finally relax, we cannot [#34].
- ND systems are "rigid" and have a smaller window. Several studies (per the translated article) found low vagal tone in autistic and ADHD children and adults, meaning the system struggles to adapt to change; that is why ND people more often experience panic attacks, anxiety and depressive disorders and difficulty regulating emotions [#35, file #159].
- Regulation is the key to physical and emotional balance. The goal is a "golden middle" inside the window of tolerance where we feel comfortable and can handle daily tasks [#103].
- The window can be widened. There are natural baseline differences (including neurodivergence), but by observing one's own nervous system and doing regulating exercises one can, over time, build capacity to handle stress and change — slowly, one or two changes at a time [#159, msg #56, article #35].
- Each deliberate shift builds vagal tone. Every time we notice a stress state and move it in a more regulated direction, we increase vagal tone (nervous-system flexibility) and regain control over the body [#578].
- Rest without regulation may not restore. If the system stays stuck in fight/flight or freeze after the "danger" has passed, rest time does not deliver its full restorative effect — which harms burnout recovery [#578].
- Sleep is prepared during the day. In the author's insomnia guide, ND insomnia is "something more than just insomnia": it has nervous-system, demand-avoidance and physiological causes (low melatonin, serotonin, comorbid EDS, MCAS, MTHFR), so the main lever is reducing triggers and calming the nervous system through the day [#34].
- Regulated does not mean emotionless. In a regulated state the author still feels all the emotions, but without the sensation that the nervous system is doing "5000 оборотов в секунду" (5000 revolutions per second), so the emotions no longer overload her [#729].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Structure of the nervous system
The translated article starts by "refreshing" what "nervous system" means, then narrows down to the part that matters for regulation [#35]:
1. Central nervous system (ЦНС, CNS) — brain and spinal cord. Receives sensory signals, processes information and sends motor signals to the body; the "control centre". "Ощущение не существует до тех пор, пока сигнал не достигнет мозга." (A sensation does not exist until the signal reaches the brain.)
2. Peripheral nervous system (ПНС, PNS) — all nerves, neurons and ganglia outside the brain and spinal cord. Sends commands from the CNS to the body through motor neurons and receives information about the internal and external environment through sensory neurons; it plays a vital role in the mind–body connection. It has two parts:
   - Somatic nervous system — voluntary; controls all voluntary movements (opening and closing your hand, kicking a football).
   - Autonomic (вегетативная) nervous system — controls everything not under conscious control: digestion, heart rate, blood pressure, metabolism, breathing rate (in most cases); these happen automatically, like a sneeze. When we talk about trauma, anxiety and nervous-system regulation, this is "the most important player", so the rest of the article focuses on it.
3. The autonomic system's two branches — the sympathetic system (gas: activates and speeds up body systems, e.g. a rising pulse) and the parasympathetic system (brake: slows body systems, e.g. a slowing pulse). In a regulated (balanced) body they work together like gas and brake in a car [#35].

#### Sympathetic vs parasympathetic state checklists
Sympathetic / "fight or flight". Activated under stress to protect the body from threats by mobilising it for action; physical changes are accompanied by hormonal ones — a surge of adrenaline and cortisol that raises energy and further prepares the body for stressors. The full list of changes [#35]:
1. Fast and variable heart rate
2. Fast, shallow breathing
3. Raised blood pressure
4. Dilated pupils
5. High adrenaline and cortisol
6. Muscle tension
7. Increased energy
8. Heightened alertness
9. Narrow focus of attention
10. Slowed digestion
11. Poor immune function
12. Reduced sex drive

Why: all this helps us fight or flee stressors, perceive critical information about our surroundings and slow processes not needed for immediate safety (such as digestion). It happens unconsciously: whatever the body perceives as a threat triggers the response, however reasonable you think it is — being chased by an animal in a forest and your boss saying he needs to talk to you privately produce the same stress response. Because ND bodies are more sensitive to stimulation than the average neurotypical body, a loud sound or an unexpected touch can also trigger it [#35].

Parasympathetic / "rest and digest". Lets the body slow down; visible at the end of the day, during stretching or meditation. Just as sympathetic activation is critical for safety, parasympathetic activation is critical for recovery: it lets us digest food properly, heal, fight infections and lets the mind fall asleep. What happens in the body [#35]:
1. Slow, steady heart rate
2. Deep breathing
3. Lowered blood pressure
4. Relaxed pupils
5. Low adrenaline and cortisol
6. Relaxed muscles
7. Lowered energy or sleepiness
8. Reduced alertness
9. Broad awareness
10. Enhanced digestion
11. Increased immune function and bodily repair
12. Increased sexual arousal

This system is also fully unconscious: the body switches on rest-and-digest only after assessing the environment and recognising it as safe — "независимо от того, насколько расслабленным вы считаете, что должны быть" (no matter how relaxed you think you should be). For neurotypical people rest is relatively easy because their bodies take in less information; for ND or traumatised people it can be hard to convince the body it is safe [#35].

#### Car metaphor of the autonomic nervous system
The presentation asks the reader to imagine: the sympathetic nervous system is the gas pedal, the parasympathetic system is the brakes, and the vagus nerve is the driver who controls both. The vagus nerve is a cranial nerve governing many automatic physiological processes (heart rate, breathing, blood pressure, digestion, reproductive functions). It is part of the parasympathetic system: it activates it while suppressing the sympathetic system, slowing heart rate, breathing and pressure, enhancing digestion and relaxing the body [#159].

The translated article uses the same gas/brake image but names a different driver: the hypothalamus, the part of the brain responsible for homeostasis, controls both systems and "acts as the driver"; in a regulated body it smoothly uses both systems to keep us in nervous-system balance. (!) CONTRADICTION: the presentation calls the vagus nerve the driver, the article calls the hypothalamus the driver; the source does not reconcile the two [#35, file #159].

#### Polyvagal view of the vagus nerve (ventral vs dorsal)
- The vagus nerve. The tenth cranial nerve, the largest cranial nerve belonging to the parasympathetic system ("это очень важно знать!" — *this is very important to know!*). "Vagus" is Latin for "wandering"; the name comes from how it winds through many internal organs, which makes it the most important nerve of the gut–brain axis. It regulates digestion, heart rate, breathing rate, sexual arousal, reproductive function, and reflexes such as sneezing, coughing and swallowing, and connects the brain with most internal organs [#35].
- Two sides. Per Dr. Porges's polyvagal theory, the vagus has a ventral vagal and a dorsal vagal system [#35].
- Ventral vagal system — the front side of the vagus. When we feel stable, balanced and flexible, it is in charge: it helps the sympathetic and parasympathetic systems work in harmony so we stay in equilibrium — just the right amount of energy and mobilisation paired with needed rest. In this state we can rest, breathe, digest, engage socially, feel creative energy, process emotions and be resilient to stress. Going to sleep is enabled by the ventral vagus; this branch is critical for digestion, learning, immunity and all kinds of recovery [#35].
- Dorsal vagal system — in periods of extreme stress it often takes control. Its job is to protect us from having to experience pain and trauma by shutting systems down, causing numbness and dissociation. Because the vagus is parasympathetic, when it responds to stress it does so by pressing the brake. Example: you climb a mountain and suddenly fall off a cliff; the dorsal side quickly activates to shut you down and numb you. If the cliff is not very high it may make you lose consciousness for a moment until the body realises it is safe again; if it is really high it will most likely make you lose consciousness. Either way it works to prevent you from experiencing the pain, fear and trauma — "Она защищает вас!" (It protects you!). (!) UNCLEAR: the translation describes both the low and the high cliff as leading to loss of consciousness, so the intended contrast between the two cases is not clear [#35].

#### Vagal tone and heart rate variability
- Definition. "Vagal tone" describes the activity of the vagus nerve; high tone = high activity. With high tone the ventral vagal system is in control, so we feel more stable and recover quickly from stressful events. With low tone the vagus struggles to adapt flexibly between the two systems, which means more strain and stress on the nervous system [#35]. Elsewhere vagal tone is glossed as "гибкость нервной системы" (flexibility of the nervous system) [#578].
- Low tone in ND people. Several studies (per the article) found low vagal tone in autistic and ADHD children and adults — our nervous system struggles to adapt to change. The article author calls this a "жесткой нервной системой" (rigid nervous system), because the system does not adapt so flexibly [#35].
- Tone and the window. The higher a person's vagal tone, the wider their window of tolerance; widening the window means increasing vagal tone. The presentation's version: low tone → difficulty adapting to stress and a narrow window; high tone → easy adaptation and a wide window; "the higher the vagal tone, the better we cope with stress" [#35, file #159].
- HRV as the measure. One key factor in measuring vagal tone and the window is heart rate variability (ВСР): how much your heart rate varies over time and across changing situations. High vagal tone (a large window) = more variation and higher HRV; low vagal tone = low HRV [#35]. A separate video post states HRV is an indicator of the state of the nervous system; (!) - How to track. The article calls an Apple Watch or FitBit the best way. Record HRV every week, both before and after you start vagal-tone exercises, to watch your nervous system gain capacity [#35].

#### Window of tolerance
Purpose: a model of our capacity to self-regulate ("Наша способность саморегулироваться"), used to recognise what state we are in and choose a response [#159].

- Definition. The window of tolerance is the optimal state of the nervous system in which we are attentive, adaptive and engaged. In it we cope effectively with stress and change; the sympathetic ("fight or flight") and parasympathetic ("rest and digest") systems work in harmony; we adapt easily to our surroundings. It is also called a "regulated nervous system". It is the zone in which we can healthily handle strong emotions and stress, with a wide range of coping options and resilience to the demands of daily life. Characteristic feelings: calm, stable, engaged in the present. The window is "ваш уровень стрессоустойчивости" (your level of stress resilience) [#159]. The article adds: inside it our mind and body respond to stress easily and effectively, we are present and calm and recover quickly; we feel attentive, curious, open, engaged and present and manage emotions and stress effectively. "Большое окно терпимости равно здоровой, регулированной нервной системе." (A large window of tolerance equals a healthy, regulated nervous system.) [#35]
- Width matters. The wider the window, the more we can bear without harm to mental health, the more effectively we handle stress and the more easily we recover after overload. A small window means we quickly become dysregulated — hyperaroused (angry, anxious, irritable) or hypoaroused (frozen, dissociated, numb) [#159, msg #56, article #35]. The channel's framing post asks: do you struggle with anxiety or depression, often feel detached, sluggish and tired, or conversely regularly feel heightened anxiety, overexcitement and confusion, and find it hard to stay engaged and focused through the day? If yes, the cause may be a narrow window that limits your inner potential [#56].
- Leaving the window. The window has limits. When we face extreme stress we cannot recover from, or are continuously exposed to stressors without the support or time to handle them smoothly, we leave the window into hyper- or hypoarousal [#35]. Then either the sympathetic or the parasympathetic system takes control to protect us, "даже если это означает поведение, которое может быть нам не на пользу" (even if this means behaviour that may not be good for us) [#159].
- Hyperarousal (гиперактивация). The body mobilises to face danger: the sympathetic system is strongly activated and the parasympathetic strongly suppressed, so we get exaggerated sympathetic symptoms — not just energetic and alert, but very angry, out of control, anxious, hypervigilant, even paranoid [#35]. Presentation summary: sympathetic dominance; anxiety, aggression, fear; rapid heartbeat, fast breathing, raised blood pressure [#159]. #103 adds the states it causes: anxiety, irritability, restlessness, tension [#103].
  - *Why it exists:* against an immediate threat lasting a few minutes these traits protect us; in evolutionary terms hyperarousal was a smart, critical adaptation. Proper recovery required being able to "отряхнуться" (shake it off) after the stress was gone — safely discharging excess energy after leaving danger [#35].
  - *Why we get stuck:* Western society created many subtle daily stresses and prevented us from shaking off, so many of us are stuck in an uncompleted stress cycle. Because our bodies do not know the difference between public speaking, an argument, paying bills and being chased by a bear, they react the same way, and many of us live in hyperarousal [#35].
- Hypoarousal (гипоактивация). An alternative response to acute stress: a state of immobility and shutdown. If hyperarousal is sympathetic, hypoarousal is parasympathetic: the parasympathetic system is strongly activated and the dorsal vagal system dominates. Mind and body shut down to protect us from overload — pain and sensory signals are reduced (animals often enter this state just before a predator eats them; it prepares them for death and protects them from pain). Exaggerated parasympathetic symptoms: fatigue, dissociation, numbness, brain fog, immobility, depression, sometimes fainting — like a deer frozen in headlights or a possum playing dead [#35]. Presentation summary: parasympathetic dominance; fatigue, apathy, detachment; slowed heartbeat, slow breathing, digestive problems [#159]. #103 adds: low mood or depression, low energy, sleepiness, lack of motivation [#103].
  - *Why it exists:* if we cannot escape or repel trauma, the body freezes; it also helps us "play dead" — if a bear catches us and we pass out, the bear may think we are dead and leave us alone, and we survive [#35].
  - *Why we get stuck:* "в современности медведи заменены дедлайнами и Каренами" (in modern times bears have been replaced by deadlines and Karens). Dealing with acute and long-lasting stressors is not what our body evolved for; if the body cannot keep finding sources of safety we can get stuck in immobility, showing up as chronic fatigue, headaches, mild dissociation and illness [#35].
- False regulation (ложная регуляция). A person looks balanced to others but internally experiences stress and dysregulation: the nervous system is shut down in hypoarousal, so outwardly we seem calm and collected while inside we are frozen and numb. Autistic people are especially prone to it — which is why we can be in sensory shutdown while everyone else sees us as calm. It is especially true of highly masking autistic people "которые научились выживать, отключая свои тела" (who have learned to survive by shutting down their bodies) [#35].
- Up- vs down-regulation as the response. Raising activation helps get out of a depressed/shut-down state; lowering activation helps calm down when overexcited or anxious [#103].

#### Why the window is narrower for some people
Reasons a window can be small, per the article: PTSD, neurodivergence and anxiety disorders (the article says the list is not exhaustive and names no further causes); ND nervous systems tend to be more rigid than neurotypical ones, so our window tends to be smaller [#35]. The presentation's factors [#159]:
1. Traumatic experiences — people who grew up in a traumatic environment experience overload more often.
2. Neurodivergence — people with OCD, ADHD and autism are more sensitive to stress and have an initially less flexible nervous system. This means it is hard for us to adapt to changes and easier to lose control over emotions. ND people often have an amplified stress response: more stress hormones and harder recovery.

#### Two-step nervous-system mapping (worksheet)
Purpose: understand your own window before trying to widen it. The article: start by understanding the window — regularly check the state of your body (using infographics) to notice which state your nervous system is in at any moment; checking consistently over time reveals your nervous-system patterns and shows when you are in your window; once you have this information you can introduce exercises to widen it [#35]. The presentation: observe your nervous system — notice how you feel (breathing, tension, thoughts) and identify which state you are in (hypo-, hyperarousal or window); track your reactions — write down what causes you stress and understand how you react to it [#159].

1. Step 1 — Map your nervous system ("Картография НС"). The first step toward control over your body is becoming aware of the current state of your nervous system and being able to identify it at any moment. For each state, describe sensation, thoughts and behaviour. The worked example:
   - *Hyperarousal* — sensation: irritable, angry, anxious, agitated; thoughts: "Я просто хочу убраться отсюда" (I just want to get out of here), "пусть это прекратится" (make it stop); behaviour: likely to shout or react sharply.
   - *Window of tolerance* — sensation: energetic, creative, calm, happy, peaceful; thoughts: "Я справлюсь с этим" (I can handle this), lots of ideas; behaviour: more sociable, often starts a new creative project.
   - *Hypoarousal* — sensation: foggy mind, feeling disconnected, exhaustion, heaviness; thoughts: "Я хочу спать" (I want to sleep), "Я чувствую себя отстраненным" (I feel detached); behaviour: withdrawal, half-speech, disengagement, indifference [#159].
2. Step 2 — Map your triggers and helpers. Once you can tell where you "are", learn to identify your usual triggers (and the helpers that keep you inside your window), sorted as physical / emotional / interpersonal. The worked example:
   - *Hyperarousal triggers* — physical: heat, discomfort, being surrounded by irritating sounds; emotional: anger, irritation, shame; interpersonal: when I feel used or abandoned/rejected.
   - *Window helpers* — physical: calming, repetitive movements; sitting in a comfortable position, wearing comfortable clothes; interpersonal: talking with a friend at a deep level.
   - *Hypoarousal triggers* — physical: too much going on around me (too many people, sounds); emotional: sadness, overwhelm, shame; interpersonal: emotionally tense conversations — when people are angry [#159].

#### Ways to increase vagal tone and widen the window of tolerance (10 practices)
These appear both in the translated article ("Увеличение вагального тонуса") and on the presentation slides ("Увеличение вашего Окна толерантности"). Why bother, per the article: raising vagal tone increases stress tolerance, adaptability, relaxation, curiosity and the sense of safety, whereas living with a nervous system that is easily activated, on alert or shut down creates more fear, discomfort and dissatisfaction — "Каждый заслуживает жить в регулируемой нервной системе!" (Everyone deserves to live in a regulated nervous system!) [#35, file #159].

Pacing rule (before you start). Go slowly: these practices cannot all be introduced at once. Too many changes at once can overload the nervous system and make it resist the exercises; the article's "how to use" note says to try them one or two at a time, because too many routine changes at once can overload the body and do more harm than good. The goal is to create safety, which means moving slowly and mindfully: "Мы не можем исцелиться тем же стремлением, которое вызвало травму!" (We cannot heal with the same striving that caused the trauma!) [#35].

1. Breathing practices. "You've probably heard it a million times", but breathing exercises are one of the best regulation practices. Slow, deep breathing activates the vagus nerve and hence the parasympathetic system; it helps lower anxiety, focus the mind and strengthen the body / connection with the body. Conversely, fast, shallow breathing activates the sympathetic system and creates activation when the body is stuck in a frozen/immobile state — so breathing can be used in both directions [#35, file #159].
2. Cold exposure. Studies (per the source) show cold exposure increases vagal activity and HRV and reduces sympathetic responses. Options: adding 30 seconds of cold water at the end of a shower, immersing the face in a bowl of ice water, or putting ice on the back of the neck for 30 seconds to a minute. Cold on the neck was shown to be especially effective [#35, file #159].
3. Exercise and movement. Exercise was shown to increase HRV by stimulating and toning the vagus nerve. Vigorous movement (long walks, running), weightlifting and somatic movement are excellent ways to raise vagal tone. Caveat: because ND people often have hypermobility and joint problems, be careful with your body when choosing a type of movement. Weightlifting has the extra benefit of deep pressure on the joints, which many ND people find relaxing. Somatic movement is especially useful for treating trauma and relaxing the body mindfully. Dr. Sam Zoranovich is recommended as a source of ND-friendly somatic movement exercises [#35, file #159].
4. Mindfulness and meditation. Contemplative practices have proven mental-health benefits; meditation is linked to reduced brain inflammation, better memory and greater ability to absorb new information, and to increased HRV and vagal tone. Mindfulness in particular can activate the vagus nerve, especially when breathing exercises are included. Bonus (article): mindfulness can improve interoception — the ability to monitor internal states such as hunger, thirst and sleepiness — which many autistic and ADHD people struggle with [#35, file #159].
5. Probiotics and omega-3. Through the "gut–brain axis", studies (per the article) show a huge link between gut health and mental health. The vagus nerve is connected to organs such as the heart and is also closely tied to the gut — it is, after all, responsible for digestion. The gut and microbiome communicate with the brain via the vagus, so when the gut is stressed, the vagus is stressed too. Caring for the gut with probiotics and omega-3 in turn cares for the nervous system [#35, file #159].
6. Laughter. "Laughter really is the best medicine": studies (per the source) show laughter stimulates the vagus nerve by inducing diaphragmatic breathing; about ten minutes of laughter a day has been shown to bring many health benefits. Suggestion: add more humour to life or try intentional laughter meditations [#35, file #159].
7. Humming, singing and chanting. Mantra chanting is found in many cultures for good reason: humming, mantra chanting and singing (the slide also names vibration) are very powerful ways to activate the vagus nerve. One study reportedly showed that chanting "om" can deactivate the limbic centre of the brain responsible for threats and emotions. The vagus nerve is interconnected with the vocal cords and the muscles at the back of the throat, so using the vocal cords mindfully activates it directly [#35, file #159].
8. Massage. Human touch is very important and many of us suffer from a lack of contact. Mindful person-to-person contact both stimulates the vagus nerve and raises oxytocin, the bonding hormone. It can come from another person, but simple self-massage also works; a video by Suki Baker is cited as a great example [#35, file #159].
9. Havening. A form of self-soothing that creates safety through comforting, therapeutic touch — a special self-soothing touch that creates a refuge for oneself. The theory is that soothing touch raises serotonin, which has a calming effect. Specific movements: cupping the cheeks with the palms, holding a hand on the chest, or crossing the arms (as if hugging yourself) while stroking the shoulders and upper arms. This creates a sense of safety and well-being through your own touch. Note: many autistic people prefer deep pressure, so make sure to use a pressure that feels pleasant [#35, file #159].
10. Relaxation exercises. "Last but not least" — perhaps the most important tool in the regulation toolkit. They directly target the ventral vagal system (the slide says the vagus nerve) to calm the body out of dysregulation. Use them before sleep, to ground/strengthen yourself in the morning, to recover after stressful events, or (article) to take part in a conflict more mindfully. They include various breathing techniques, progressive muscle relaxation (PMR) and meditations [#35, file #159].

Conclusion of the article: in a regulated state the sympathetic and parasympathetic systems work together to adapt skilfully to stress and change; when our systems are rigid (small window) because of neurodivergence or trauma we easily fall into dysregulation, hyper- or hypoarousal. "Тем не менее, для нас всегда есть надежда." (Nevertheless, there is always hope for us.) By learning about our own vagal tone and window and then introducing vagal-tone exercises we can widen the window over time [#35].

#### Nervous-system state shifting (смена состояния нервной системы)
Purpose: make rest actually restorative and widen the window by switching the nervous system into a mode of safety. Source: the author's translation of Neurodivergent Insights material [#578, file #578].

- Definition. When we are in a stress state and can (A) identify it and (B) do something to lower or raise activation and enter a more regulated zone, we are doing nervous-system state shifting. It helps handle stress, and "как бонус" (as a bonus) each time we catch ourselves in stress and correct it to a regulated level, we increase vagal tone [#578].
- Step 1 — notice the state. Pay attention to which state your nervous system is in [#578].
- Step 2a — if mobilised (sympathetic dominance, too much excitation, need to calm down): do down-regulating activities, i.e. ones that reduce excitation and activation of the body. The post says excessive sympathetic activity must be balanced with relaxation methods [#578, msg #578].
- Step 2b — if immobilised (parasympathetic dominance — numbness, sluggishness): do up-regulating activities, i.e. ones that increase the body's energy and wakefulness. Up-regulation is useful when you need a surge of energy to stay awake for driving, an exam, work or other activities. The post names activating breathing, movement and sensory activities that "wake up" the body [#578, msg #578].
- Explore what works for you — the author stresses it is important to investigate what specifically helps you, using the activity lists below [#578].
- Why: "Каждый раз, когда вы намеренно выводите свою нервную систему из стрессовой реакции и возвращаете ее в состояние безопасности, вы увеличиваете вагусный тонус." (Every time you deliberately bring your nervous system out of a stress response and return it to a state of safety, you increase vagal tone.) [#578]

#### Down-regulating vs up-regulating activity lists
All from the attached PDF [#578].

Down-regulating (calming) core actions: breathing practices (slow, deep breathing), tapping, grounding, chanting mantras, or stimming to discharge excess energy. Named tools: progressive muscle relaxation; tapping; binaural beats and calming music; guided visualisation; breathing practices; yoga.

Up-regulating (energising) core actions: activities that mobilise the body for action — tapping, rocking, dancing, movement, pacing back and forth, creating pressure or friction (e.g. rubbing hands). Named tools: fast breathing techniques; listening to energetic music; lifting weights; doing a stimulating activity; brisk walking; exposing yourself to sunlight.

Contrasting qualities of experience (calming ↔ energising):
- familiar situations or activities ↔ new situations or activities
- slow pace ↔ fast pace
- slow, rhythmic, repetitive movements ↔ fast, rhythmic movements
- repetitive, calm music or sounds ↔ loud, fast, energetic music
- warm temperatures ↔ cold temperatures
- soft and few colours ↔ bright colours/variety
- weak stimuli (smells, tastes) ↔ strong or intense stimuli
- quiet setting ↔ loud setting
- dim lighting ↔ bright lighting
- repetition ↔ surprise
- stability ↔ irregularity/spontaneity
- deep pressure/weight ↔ movement

Calming (down-regulating) activities:
- rocking; rocking in a rocking chair
- wrapping up in a warm, heavy blanket
- taking a hot shower or bath
- focusing on calming scenes such as a sunset or a peaceful meadow
- using soft lighting to create a cosy atmosphere
- sipping a warm drink, e.g. soothing herbal tea
- listening to soft or slow music
- following slow, even rhythms
- enjoying background sounds or relaxing music
- using soothing or gentle scents (essential oils, lotions, candles)
- applying deep pressure through hugs or massage
- tapping with a bean bag
- humming, chanting mantras or singing
- practising yoga
- green therapy (time in nature)
- using soft materials or textures such as a cosy blanket or soft pillow

Energising (up-regulating) activities:
- holding ice cubes
- eating fruit ice
- sour or spicy sweets
- chewing a lemon slice
- frozen fruit smoothies
- dancing to fast music
- splashing the face with cold water
- sitting in a cool room
- looking at unexpected visual stimuli
- changing patterns of light and shadow
- invigorating nature sounds (birdsong)
- listening to fast music
- strong scents (essential oils, lotions, candles)
- light touch
- a brisk walk in fresh air
- touching rough or prickly textures
- taking a cold shower or bath
- crunching ice cubes
- rubbing menthol cream into the skin or using mint or eucalyptus essential oils
- standing in front of a fan
- doing a few star jumps
- running cold water over the wrists
- chewing gum/mints

#### Self-reflection exercise on "overreacting"
Purpose: counter shame when told you are "слишком бурно реагируете" (overreacting) or "чересчур чувствительны" (too sensitive). Premise: the autonomic nervous system works automatically, like sneezing — it fires without conscious control. If you recognise that your body easily goes into fight-or-flight (sympathetic mode), reflect on [#373]:
1. What thoughts and beliefs have you formed about these experiences?
2. Do these thoughts help you, or do they cause shame and increase stress?
3. How could you look at these situations anew to develop a kinder attitude toward yourself?
The exercise is taken from Megan Anna Neff's workbook "Нейроотличная нервная система" (The Neurodivergent Nervous System) [#373].

#### Bilateral stimulation via 8D audio
Question posed: is 8D audio "EMDR therapy, only in music format"? The author's answer: it is not real EMDR, but 8D music can have a similar calming effect, helping cope with stress and anxiety in the moment [#351].

- How it works. 8D audio uses equalisation, panning and effects to create the sensation of sound moving around the listener's head — an immersive experience as if you were in the centre of a concert hall with musicians moving around you. Headphones are required for the full effect. It works on the principle of bilateral stimulation, similar to EMDR, which may help "balance" the brain, giving relaxation and mental clarity [#351].
- What bilateral stimulation (BLS) is. A psychotherapeutic method based on alternating stimulation of the left and right hemispheres; it can be visual, auditory or tactile. BLS is a key component of EMDR therapy used for PTSD and other psychological trauma; research, per the post, shows BLS may help process traumatic memories by reducing their emotional intensity. Its mechanism is linked to the orienting reflex — the nervous system's natural reaction to new stimuli — which diverts attention from anxious thoughts and promotes relaxation [#351].
- Safety. BLS is considered safe for most people but may cause unexpected reactions in people with heightened sensitivity to sensory stimuli or certain neurological conditions [#351].
- Potential benefits: (1) better concentration and focus, especially for people with ADHD; (2) reduced anxiety and stress through the calming effect; (3) a sense of "clearing the brain" of intrusive thoughts. Important: these benefits are only subjective listener reports; there is not yet enough research proving this self-help tool effective [#351].
- Possible side effects: dizziness or nausea in some listeners; sensory overload, especially in autistic people [#351].
- Not recommended: listening for a long time; running an EMDR session on yourself using music or other variants of the bilateral stimulation used in EMDR [#351].
- Bottom line. 8D music does not replace professional therapy but can be a useful tool for managing stress; as with any new method, listen to your body and stop if discomfort appears [#351].

#### Preparing for sleep during the day (trigger audit and 8 sleep strategies)
From the author's own article "Нечто большее, чем просто бессоница" (Something more than just insomnia), written to collect advice in one place and explain the real causes of insomnia in ND people, with the emphasis on helping the nervous system stabilise to improve sleep and quality of life. She presents it as sharing experience and observations, not a substitute for a professional: if you have exhausting sleep problems, do not postpone seeing a specialist [#34, msg #34]. Some tips are also useful for organising the day better [#34].

Principle: "Готовиться ко сну нужно в течении дня." (You need to prepare for sleep during the day.) For that you must understand what exactly triggers your nervous system [#34].

Step 0 — trigger audit. Possible triggers listed by the author: sounds; smells; tactile sensations; masking/socialising; sudden changes or lack of stability; doing hard tasks such as driving somewhere or going to the shop; illness; too much or too little stimulation for the brain; forcing yourself to do something against your will; and physiological factors — the MTHFR mutation, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, diet and activity level. Many of these cause discomfort, but the difficulty is that we live like this for years, "get used to" our nervous system being in a state of shock and stop noticing it — and consequences are often not linked to the cause. The point of the audit is to find the cause [#34].

The 8 strategies [#34]:
1. Limit irritants/triggers during the day as much as possible. Practise unmasking, wear earplugs or noise-cancelling headphones, pay attention to "the moment" and scan your body to understand what is causing discomfort right now. Examples: leave a noisy party early, ask for the TV to be turned down. If sensory overload happens, try to recognise it and find a chance for a sensory break to unload the nervous system. *The author's own method — "сенсорный вакуум" (sensory vacuum):* in the middle of the day she goes to the bedroom, closes the curtains, uses earplugs, lies comfortably in bed, puts on a sleep mask and places a second pillow on top of her forehead, because that sense of protection calms her; a small weighted blanket for her hands to relax would be ideal.
2. Don't chase doing everything. "Не гонитесь за тем, чтобы все успеть и все сделать, иногда можно и забить." (Don't chase getting everything done; sometimes you can just let it go.)
3. Make the sleeping place comfortable. Bedding that feels pleasant on your skin; remove noisy chargers from the bedroom (some electronics are clearly audible, especially at night); put on calming music or rain sounds / brown or white noise in the background — it can reduce anxiety and acts as an anchor so the brain is not distracted. People with hypermobility can use pillows to stabilise joints during sleep.
4. Weighted blanket/throw. Can be used during the day or for sleep; helps with restless legs syndrome and calms the nervous system. "Важно: первый раз может быть шок." (Important: the first time may be a shock.)
5. Breathing and meditation. Diaphragmatic breathing (a couple of deep inhales/exhales several times a day) and mini-meditations help calm and "reboot" the nervous system very well; their effect on the vagus nerve is key for nervous-system recovery.
6. Diet. Where possible avoid gluten and sugar before sleep: they can cause hidden reactions in the body that lead mast cells to produce histamine, which in turn triggers the nervous system.
7. Circadian rhythms. Sleep in as dark a room as possible and limit screen time before sleep: "Мелатонин вырабатывается исключительно в полной темноте." (Melatonin is produced only in complete darkness.) If that is not possible, set the phone screen to a soft (yellow) mode or black-and-white; a sleep mask can also help if the body tolerates it.
8. Rule out apnoea. If possible, see a specialist to make sure you do not have sleep apnoea.

#### Over-the-counter and prescription options to ask a doctor about for sleep
The author stresses twice that even non-prescription products require a doctor's consultation — this is only an example of what you can ask your doctor about — and "Не эксперементируйте с вашим здоровьем!" (Don't experiment with your health!) [#34]:
1. Magnesium spray — use before sleep, apply to the feet.
2. Antihistamine — partly stabilises mast cells, reduces histamine in the body and thereby calms the nervous system; helps fall asleep if taken before bed.
3. Mast cell stabilisers (prescription) — more targeted stabilisation of mast cells; ketotifen ("Кетотифен") is named as an option to consider.
4. Methylfolate — the active form of vitamin B9, needed by the nervous system, especially if you have the MTHFR mutation. The author also advises using only methylated (active-form) vitamins.
5. Melatonin — anti-recommendation. The author strongly advises against over-the-counter melatonin because studies showed the dose can vary greatly from what the label states, sometimes exceeding it 100-fold.
6. Low Dose Naltrexone (LDN), from 1.5 mg (prescription) — described as an effective means of stabilising the nervous system that works well together with ketotifen.

The author notes these are all the tips she could recall and that she may extend the list later [#34].

### Detailed insights

#### Why the ND nervous system struggles to switch off
Our ND nervous system is built differently: we do not filter the information around us, and our nervous system is constantly activated. Some would call us "чрезмерно чувствительны" (excessively sensitive). From an evolutionary point of view this is a great ability: you can tell when it will rain because the air smells of rain, you smell keenly and can locate prey or danger, you hear sounds clearly — skills that were needed for survival. But today's world is overfilled with sensory information; under such conditions the nervous system works and overstrains constantly, so it is not strange that at the end of the day, when we can finally relax, we cannot [#34].

#### Pathological demand avoidance and sleep
PDA (ПИТ) affects our ability to "just make ourselves" go to bed, and persuasion is useless. An autistic adult may be unable to make themselves do something they understand is good for them if they perceive it as a demand; sleep can fall into the "demand" category, instantly triggering avoidance mechanisms. This can trigger a nervous system that is not ready to meet demands. The author asks readers to be kind and patient with themselves: you are not to blame, this is how your brain works, and "силой поменяться его не заставишь" (you can't force it to change). She planned a separate guide for people with PDA on sleep-related demand avoidance; meanwhile some of the strategies above can also help daily life [#34].

#### Physiological causes of poor sleep in ND people
- Neurochemistry. Per the author, ND people produce less melatonin than neurotypical people, have a serotonin shortage, and many have gastrointestinal problems — and, "as is known", 95% of serotonin is synthesised in the gut [#34].
- Common comorbidities. Comorbid conditions are common in ND people; among the widespread ones are MTHFR genetic mutations and the connective-tissue disorder Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (13 subtypes, genetic and inherited). Both can substantially affect how the body works and what we are capable of [#34].
- Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (EDS). Per the author it occurs in more than 50% of ND people. It affects a wide range of the body; the article focuses on pain: weak connective tissue can cause chronic joint and muscle pain, joint hypermobility and chronic fatigue. It can affect sleep directly: apnoea (breathing stops during sleep) occurs among people with EDS; severe joint and muscle pain makes it hard to fall and stay asleep; and people often complain of chronic fatigue after sleep, which may later reduce our ability to cope with sensory triggers [#34].
- Mast cell activation syndrome (MCAS). Often found together with EDS. Mast cells can activate for many reasons — viruses, stress, sensory overload, gluten/sugar. With mast cells, pain can be read as stress, and its presence can worsen the state of the nervous system. So the stress itself may already have passed while mast cells keep activating, creating a feeling of anxiety, aggravating inflammation, causing gastrointestinal problems, hormonal disruption, hives/psoriasis — all of which can disrupt not only sleep but daily functioning [#34].
- MTHFR and catecholamines. MTHFR is a genetic mutation impairing metabolism of folate, which is important for cell division and synthesis of neurotransmitters (catecholamines); MTHFR is also linked to raised homocysteine, which can harm the nervous system. Catecholamines are physiologically active substances acting as mediators (the source lists noradrenaline, dopamine, serotonin) and hormones (adrenaline, noradrenaline); they drive the fight-or-flight response. They are normally produced in fear, but because our bodies lack the resources to break these neurotransmitters down, we can feel fear without danger — i.e. be anxious for no particular reason. The familiar state of being very tired and wanting to sleep while the brain keeps thinking and won't let you drop off arises, per the author, precisely from a large build-up of catecholamines in the body [#34].
- Caveat from the author: "Пожалуйста, не ставьте себе диагноз на основе информации из статьи. Обязательно консультируйтесь с врачом." (Please don't diagnose yourself based on the article. Be sure to consult a doctor.) [#34]

#### Dorsal vagal shutdown (hypoarousal)
Dorsal vagal shutdown, also known as hypoarousal, is a type of "freeze response" that happens when facing overwhelming stress or trauma. It is a natural, normal reaction that lets the body save energy and protect itself when we cannot fight or flee; however, getting stuck in it can badly worsen quality of life [#60].
Symptoms may include: fatigue; dissociation; numbness; foggy mind; immobility; depression; fainting [#60].
It is important to understand that the state signals deeper psychological problems such as chronic stress or trauma, rather than being a standalone disorder: it arises as a symptom of ongoing issues (sustained stress, unresolved trauma or other mental-health problems), pointing to a deeper need for intervention. Treatment targets these underlying problems through therapy, medication and lifestyle changes aimed at stress management and self-care [#60].

#### Why regulation matters for rest and burnout recovery
The ND nervous system can get stuck in fight/flight or freeze in response to everyday stressors, unable to switch automatically into recovery and rest when the "danger" has passed. As a result, even when we set time aside to rest, we may not get its full restorative effect — which hurts burnout recovery, because rest time is a valuable resource we pin our hopes on. Techniques that switch the nervous system into safety mode make rest more effective and widen the window of tolerance (see *Nervous-system state shifting*) [#578].

#### Why understanding the nervous system matters (article framing)
The translated article opens: the autistic and ADHD nervous system is vulnerable and sensitive; ND people sometimes struggle to adapt to change, stress and conflict, and much more often than neurotypical people experience panic attacks, anxiety and depressive disorders and difficulty regulating emotions. The answers to why, and to what we can do to change this rigidity, lie in understanding the nervous system. The original author (a therapist who is herself autistic and ADHD) says part of her work with ND clients is helping them understand what happens in their nervous system: when we learn about it we can start reclaiming power — learn why our systems behave as they do so we can learn to influence them for our health and well-being. The channel author found the article very interesting and useful for understanding how our nervous system is built and how we can help ourselves, and hoped it would give readers self-help tools [#35, msg #35].

#### Author's case: somatic discharge of hyperarousal in therapy
Working on the nervous system was the first self-help accessible to the author before she received treatment for САТК (the abbreviation is not expanded in this post) and therapy for cPTSD (кПТСР), and it still helps her. Her psychotherapist is certified for cPTSD work, trained in nervous-system stabilisation, knows the window of tolerance, and takes into account the author's other diagnoses, since each adds its own nuances to how the nervous system works [#729].
- Technique 1 — free discharge movement. When the author's activation rose during a session, the therapist suggested standing up together and finding a body movement comfortable for her that would help "release" excess energy. The author swayed side to side, dangling her arms "как-будто у меня вместо рук рукава" (as if I had sleeves instead of arms). This released the energy that was flooding and overloading her [#729].
- Lesson on unmasking. Writing this, she realised this is how unmasking should happen: without the therapist's suggestion she would most likely have sat holding everything in, and a shutdown would have happened at home, as it had before [#729].
- Technique 2 — single-leg balance. Next, standing on one foot with the other knee bent and balancing (the therapist did it all alongside her). This switched the brain further, because it got busy with the important task "нам надо не упасть" (we must not fall). The author's opinion: such natural shifting of the brain's attention helps well in the moment and offloads the nervous system [#729].
- She files both as ways of moving the nervous system from hyperarousal to a regulated state [#729].

#### Author's experience with 8D audio
The author liked the first audio (The Tucker Zone — warning: it contains a knock on the door) because while listening she visualised everything happening, which felt like training in being present. As a person with severe dissociation she finds it a useful way to hook her attention. The sound is very realistic, so she advises not to set volume to maximum. The songs she likes, but they make her motion-sick because the movement is much faster, so she can listen to only one at a time. As a handy way to cope with stress she likes it a lot; she cannot say how effective it is, but it does switch her mind, as attention quickly latches onto the rhythm and the changing position of the sound [#351].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The insomnia article is explicitly the author's experience and observations, not a substitute for professional consultation; she warns against self-diagnosis from it and insists every medication option, even over-the-counter, needs a doctor's advice [#34].
- Several physiological claims in the insomnia article (less melatonin and serotonin in ND people, 95% of serotonin made in the gut, EDS in more than 50% of ND people, catecholamine build-up causing tired-but-wired states, OTC melatonin doses up to 100× the label) are stated without naming the studies or sources [#34].
- The catecholamine list in the insomnia article names serotonin among catecholamine mediators; the source gives no further explanation [#34].
- The 8D audio benefits are explicitly only subjective listener reports; there is not enough research proving effectiveness. Do not self-administer EMDR with music or other BLS; avoid long listening; stop at discomfort; autistic and sensory-sensitive people may get overload, dizziness or nausea [#351].
- Several research claims (low vagal tone in autistic/ADHD people, cold exposure raising HRV, the "om" chanting study, ten minutes of laughter, meditation and brain inflammation) are stated in the translated article and presentation without naming the studies [#35, file #159].
- (!) CONTRADICTION: the presentation names the vagus nerve as the "driver" of gas and brakes, the translated article names the hypothalamus [#159, article #35].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the article's cliff example says both a low and a high fall lead to loss of consciousness; the intended contrast is unclear [#35].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the presentation lists "ИСР" alongside vagal tone on the meditation slide; the parallel passage of the translated article reads ВСР (HRV), which suggests a typo, but the presentation itself does not say [#159, article #35].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: pages 23–26 of the presentation, the linked Teletype articles (#56, #60, #103) and all video posts (#445, #499, #500, #505, #527, #529) carry content not available in the export [#159, msg #56, #60, #103, #445, #499, #500, #505, #527, #529].
- Movement caveat: ND people often have hypermobility and joint problems, so choose exercise carefully; autistic people often prefer deep pressure, so adjust havening pressure to what feels pleasant [#35].
- Dorsal vagal shutdown is framed as a symptom of deeper problems, not a disorder in itself; treatment is aimed at the underlying stress or trauma [#60].
