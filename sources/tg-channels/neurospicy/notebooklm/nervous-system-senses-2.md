## Interoception, alexithymia and naming emotions

### What this covers

This topic gathers everything the channel Нейропряность taught about interoception — the sense of what is happening inside the body — and its consequences for autistic and ADHD people: trouble noticing hunger, thirst, heat, pain and breathing (homeostasis), trouble with self-regulation and emotion regulation, alexithymia (difficulty recognizing and naming emotions), empathy and relationships, emotional dysregulation, and the misreading of bodily arousal as emotions. It also collects the practical tools the channel offered: interoception exercises (mindful check-ins, body scan, interoception on the go, a heart-rate exercise), visual aids for naming feelings (feelings wheel, list of feelings, intensity indicator) and the idea of describing emotions in one's own non-standard terms [#20, #37, #38, #47, #49, #186, #284, #379, #799].

According to the source, this matters because many neurodivergent people only feel an emotion once it is already intense, cannot catch overload in time and so slide into meltdown or shutdown, neglect basic body needs, and are misjudged as cold or unempathetic, while understanding emotions is "key" to working through most traumas and difficulties [#47, #49, #284, #799].

### Core ideas

- Interoception is an internal GPS. It tells us we are hungry, cold, thirsty and also underpins emotional awareness; it has a physical and an emotional component (see *Two components of interoception*) [#20, #47, #186].
- Autistic and ADHD people often have interoceptive difficulties, which show up as forgetting to eat or drink, not noticing one is holding one's breath, or overheating; these break homeostasis and self-regulation (see *Six consequences of poor interoceptive awareness*) [#37, #186].
- Alexithymia is closely linked to interoception. Bodily sensations are tightly bound up with emotions, so improving interoception helps with recognizing emotions; Nastya recommends mastering interoceptive awareness first as the base for emotional awareness [#47, #49].
- Emotions are often felt only at high intensity (at least 8/10), so the stage of mild irritation is missed and one jumps straight to anger [#49].
- Regulate the nervous system first. Since overload is hard to notice, an early step in managing alexithymia is nervous-system regulation, including sensory regulation, to create safety and leave fight/flight/freeze [#47].
- Alexithymia is not absence of emotion or empathy; it is a feature of emotional awareness and of brain–body connection that becomes easier with understanding and support [#799].
- Visual aids and own-language descriptions help name feelings: the feelings wheel, list of feelings and intensity indicator; and allowing people to describe emotions as textures, colours, body sensations or energy rather than forcing "standard" terms [#49, #284, #379, #380, #381, #799].
- Arousal can be misattributed to the wrong cause (e.g. post-run heartbeat read as attraction), which is especially relevant to people who already find emotions hard to identify [#38].

- Naming an emotion goes from general to specific. Both handouts group feeling words under the same six core feelings: anger (Злость / Гнев), strength (Сила), sadness (Грусть), happiness (Счастье), fear (Страх) and calm (Спокойствие). You first pick the broad family and then a more precise word inside it (see *Feelings wheel*) [#383, #384].
- The wheel is a starting point, not a full catalogue. Its caption says it "не охватывает все возможные чувства" ("does not cover all possible feelings"). It is still an excellent starting point for identifying and describing your emotions [#384].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Two components of interoception
Source: the channel's presentation "Интероцепция и как ее улучшить" ("Interoception and how to improve it"), based on Megan Anna Neff's article "Interoception" (Neurodivergentinsights.com), shared after an online meeting on interoception [#186].
1. Definition. Interoception is the unique ability to feel what is happening inside our body; it is an internal GPS that helps us orient in physical and emotional space [#186].
2. Why it matters. It not only informs us about the body's current state but also lets us respond actively and caringly to its needs, which is essential for physical and emotional wellbeing [#186].
3. Two components. *Physical* interoception is feeling changes in the body's organs and systems; *emotional* interoception is the ability to be aware of and manage one's emotions [#186].
4. Link to self-care. Developing interoception skills makes it easier to perceive and respond to the body's needs, which promotes better self-care and "harmony with oneself" [#186].
5. Autistic specifics. For autistic people interoception can be especially hard; this causes problems recognizing and reacting to body signals, leading to stress, anxiety and difficulties with emotional regulation — so it is particularly important to develop this ability [#186].

#### Six consequences of poor interoceptive awareness
The presentation says autistic people face interoceptive difficulties more often than the general population, and that some traits usually attributed to autism itself are in fact the result of interoceptive difficulties: difficulty processing emotions (linked to increased anxiety); alexithymia (difficulty identifying and describing emotions); and empathy and perspective-taking (difficulty understanding another's viewpoint when we struggle to understand our own bodily experience). Interoceptive difficulties also make it hard to identify pain, hunger and thirst, which disrupts homeostasis, self-regulation and physical wellbeing and can leave an unclear sense of self and others, affecting health, relationships and emotion regulation [#186]. It then lists six consequences:

1. Difficulty maintaining homeostasis. Homeostasis is a system's striving to remain in balance; its hallmark is feedback loops, like a thermostat regulating room temperature, and the body runs thousands of them. Examples: glucose homeostasis regulated by insulin; thermoregulation through sweating and evaporation when overheated. The body responds automatically (signalling hunger or thirst), but interoceptive *awareness* — listening to those signals and acting on them — is also needed. Interoceptive signals mobilize us to act: thirst makes us drink (hydration and electrolyte balance), hunger makes us eat (blood sugar), cold makes us put on clothes (body temperature). Interoceptive difficulties make it harder to give the body what it needs [#186]. The channel's homeostasis article opens with the matching self-check questions: do you forget to eat or drink because you don't feel hunger or thirst; have you ever realised you weren't breathing and needed to take a breath; have you overheated because you didn't notice you were too hot — if so, "Возможно, у вас есть трудности с интероцепцией" ("Perhaps you have difficulties with interoception"), and the article links autism, ADHD and homeostasis through interoception [#37].
2. Self-regulation. Problems with homeostasis are directly tied to problems with self-regulation, described as "поход в горы без карты и компаса" ("a hike in the mountains without a map and compass"). Interoceptive awareness is the virtual map telling us what the body needs right now; without it, it is hard to recognize signals and therefore to care for and soothe the body. Interoceptive difficulties directly affect regulation of emotions, attention and behaviour, with knock-on problems in eating habits and physical wellbeing. Body signals are a foundation of self-care and self-protection; when awareness is poor, the true source of discomfort becomes unclear, so one cannot respond effectively [#186].
3. Emotion regulation. Defined as an important subset of self-regulation: the ability to be aware of one's emotions and intervene to calm the body; any activity that reduces the intensity of an emotion counts as emotion regulation. Its effectiveness depends on perceiving one's emotional states, and interoception supplies awareness of the physical signals that *precede* emotional reactions. Regulation skills work better when one can recognize early signs of rising intensity. Because interoceptive difficulty breaks the link between physical signals and emotions, the presentation concludes that resolving interoception problems is the first step in building effective emotion-regulation skills [#186].
4. Health and wellbeing. Health depends on responding adequately to body signals and judging their meaning. People with limited interoception may face: (a) *hydration* — not noticing thirst leads to drinking too little, causing headaches and digestive problems; (b) *temperature regulation* — misreading body signals leads to wrong responses to temperature change, raising the risk of frostbite or heat stroke; (c) *nutrition* — trouble sensing hunger leads to irregular meals and an uneven diet; (d) *pain and injury awareness* — weakened pain signals slow the reaction to physical problems and raise the risk of complications. Managing health therefore needs not only knowledge about the body but the ability to respond to its needs [#186].
5. Sense of self. Interoceptive awareness builds a coherent sense of one's own personality and the awareness that we are not just a mind but a body. Kelly Mahler is quoted: "Интероцепция придает нам ощущение того, что 'это я; это мое тело;" ("Interoception gives us the sense that 'this is me; this is my body; this is how I feel'"). Research cited in the presentation says the insula activates during self-reflection, such as looking at oneself in a mirror or at photographs, and damage to it can affect a person's ability to perceive themselves as an individual [#186].
6. Relationships (and empathy). Poor interoceptive awareness affects understanding oneself and therefore understanding others; emotional awareness arising from interoception is the foundation of empathy. Studies of insula damage link it with reduced perspective-taking and adaptation to social norms. On empathy: when we struggle to recognize our own emotions and body states we have less personal experience to draw on to understand others; the use of similar neural connections for empathy towards oneself and others is sometimes called the shared brain network of empathy. On perspective-taking: difficulty understanding and distinguishing one's own emotions leads to significant difficulty understanding others' emotions (Cook, Brewer, Shah and Bird, 2013), which can cause serious social misunderstandings [#186].

#### Four ways to improve interoception
The presentation stresses that interoception can be improved with practice and support; it is a process requiring regular practice and patience [#186].
1. Practise interoceptive-awareness activities — body scanning, deep breathing, progressive muscle relaxation, awareness of feelings and mindful check-ins. Regular practice helps you perceive body signals and understand and manage emotions better [#186].
2. Create conditions and adaptations for your needs — adapt your environment so it supports bodily needs and takes interoceptive difficulties into account. The concrete adaptation offered: timers as reminders to eat, drink/hydrate and take sensory breaks [#186].
3. Seek neurodivergence-affirming support — professionals or others who understand neurodivergent people can offer recommendations and strategies [#186].
4. Be patient and self-compassionate — improvement takes time; everyone's interoceptive experience is unique, and it is fine to go your own way at your own pace [#186].
General notes on the activities: research shows interoceptive awareness can be developed with practice; improvement can help emotion regulation and physical and emotional health; try the exercises one at a time, mastering them gradually. The three activities are *Mindful check-ins*, *Body scan* and *Interoception on the go* [#186].

#### Mindful check-ins
Goal: improve interoception through conscious attention to physical sensations. Characteristics: can be brief (10 seconds) or long; done without judgement or evaluation; aimed at identifying and observing sensations. Key point: do them regularly throughout the day to develop awareness of bodily and emotional experiences [#186].
- Variant a — with an external stimulus (a drink): (1) hold a hot or cold drink, focusing on the sensations; (2) let go and notice the changes; (3) repeat [#186].
- Variant b — with physical activity: (1) do an exercise (stretching, squats and similar); (2) note changes in the body (temperature, breathing); (3) stop moving and observe the sensations; (4) repeat the exercise [#186].

#### Body scan
Goal: improve interoceptive awareness through sequential observation of sensations in the body [#186].
1. Find a comfortable, quiet place.
2. Start at the feet and move gradually up to the head.
3. Focus on each body part, noting sensations without judging.
4. Use deep breathing to concentrate.
5. When distracted, gently return attention to the body.
Key points: use specific *descriptive* words instead of evaluative ones; avoid general terms and detail the sensation — examples of descriptions: soft, tense, hot, buzzing ("мягкий, напряженный, горячий, жужжащий"); practise regularly. Recommendations: be patient and gentle with yourself; start with a partial scan and move gradually to a full one. The exercise develops finer perception of bodily sensations and a better body–mind connection [#186].

#### Interoception on the go
For people short on time: quick check-ins during the day that build the "muscles" of awareness of body sensations. It has two steps, and the first must be mastered before moving to the second [#186].
1. Step one — quick interoceptive check, once or twice a day for a few moments. Two ways: (1) set 2 timers to go off during the day as "moments of interoceptive check"; or (2) attach the check to an activity you do several times a day, preferably one that creates sensations — washing hands, washing dishes, showering, after eating. During the check simply notice the sensations you have during that activity [#186].
2. Step two — interoceptive connectors. Once more experienced, start linking those sensations to specific body states and emotions. Over time you begin to map which sensations correspond to hunger, irritability, satisfaction and so on [#186].

#### Mindful nervous-system stabilization exercise (Kelly Mahler)
Taken, per the presentation, from Kelly Mahler's book "Восьмая сенсорная система" ("The eighth sensory system"). It increases body awareness and teaches lowering nervous-system activity (calming the nervous system). It splits into mini-exercises that grow harder, and gives informal feedback on the body's state without complex equipment. Goals: (1) increase awareness of heart rate; (2) practise strategies for lowering nervous-system activity. Equipment: any device that records pulse — smart watch, Fitbit or finger oximeter — or measuring with your finger and a watch [#186].
1. Estimate, then measure. Do 30–50 seconds of activity that raises the pulse (jumping, push-ups, running in place). First pay attention to your pulse and try to estimate your heart rate; then measure it and see how close you were. Track this over time to follow improvement in interoceptive awareness [#186].
2. Lower it in 60 seconds. After doing step one several times, add step two: measure your pulse (finger oximeter or smartphone) and see how low you can bring it within 60 seconds. Experiment with different things — slowing your breathing, focusing on something calming and so on — and keep recording results to follow progress over time [#186].
Caveat from the presentation: for autistic people, strategies to lower nervous-system activity do not always work as well, so it may take time and many experiments to find the strategy that suits you [#186].

#### Describing emotions in one's own terms
From a post forwarded from the channel Кораблик Леськи (Leska). Purpose: for people who cannot express their emotions in "standard" language [#284].

Why naming emotions is hard for most people — five reasons listed by the author:
1. There is a huge number of differing and sometimes mutually exclusive definitions of every emotion.
2. There are no high-precision "reference samples" to check against — the illustrations and examples used in psychology often feel fake and exaggerated.
3. The intensity and expression of emotional reactions varies from individual to individual.
4. Many literary works romanticize actual mental disorders that seriously reduce quality of life and call them a "healthy emotional norm".
5. The same literature, and the media, ascribe to emotions qualities they do not actually have in practice.
Hence fitting felt emotions into "accepted terms" is a task of the "Божество" level (Deity-level) — like explaining a subject we don't understand well ourselves in a language we haven't properly learned and which changes from region to region [#284].

The approach: the author's own inquiries among people who struggle greatly to understand their emotions in "standard" language showed that much of the problem can be solved by the opposite of standardization — allowing oneself and others to describe emotions through any convenient images, i.e. "позволяя изъясняться на привычном, понятном человеку языке" ("letting a person speak in a language that is familiar and clear to them"). Seven modalities people use [#284]:
1. Texture — an emotion felt as liquid, dense, solid, ridged/embossed, gaseous.
2. Colour — not necessarily synaesthesia; it can be a "mental" colour, not a literal spot in the eye.
3. Body sensations — "warmth in the chest", "cold in the upper part of the head", "itching around the solar plexus".
4. Facial expression — some identify their emotions precisely through their own facial expressions, which arise somewhat involuntarily.
5. Chemical reactions — for example a bubbling, toxic acid, or an "outflow of warmth".
6. Movement of energy — some body part (or the whole body) feels like moving electricity, tension.
7. Images/metaphors — "a little cloud in the chest", "a river flowing through the whole body".
Claimed benefit: in the long run this approach improves understanding of oneself, one's needs and reactions, and also helps establish contact with other people [#284]. The autismservicesgroup text translated in #799 echoes this: not everyone experiences feelings through words — some feel them in the body, through energy, in patterns and cycles, or in silence, and all these ways are normal and legitimate [#799].

#### Twelve ways alexithymia can show up
A card series by neurodivergent_lou, forwarded from Нейр∞тлично. Definition card: alexithymia is difficulty recognizing, observing and describing one's sensations and emotions; non-autistic people can have it too, but it is more common in autistic people — about 50% of autistic people are thought to experience it (Milosavijevic et al., 2016). "Алекситимия сама по себе не является" a diagnosis ("Alexithymia is not in itself a diagnosis") but a set of experiences [#604, #605]. Manifestations [#606, #607, #608, #609, #610, #611, #612]:
1. Knowing that an emotion is "bad/good" but not knowing which emotion it is [#606].
2. Imagining how another person might feel in a given situation, because we have no idea what we actually feel [#606].
3. No emotional reactions to situations where others expect them — for example opening presents or a bereavement [#607].
4. Feeling tension build up in the body but not understanding which emotion it is [#607].
5. Experiencing emotions as physical pain [#608].
6. Having physical sensations — racing heart, butterflies in the stomach — but no emotion [#608].
7. Only being able to feel emotions when they are really strong; being struck by a sudden overwhelming wave of negativity [#609].
8. Talking about difficult things easily, because the related emotions are not felt [#609].
9. A constant sense of anxiety or tension from not knowing what exactly we feel [#610].
10. Experiencing alexithymia is likened to "смотреть телевизор в черно-белом цвете" ("watching TV in black and white"), unable to make out the colours — the specific emotions [#610].
11. Finding it easier to describe decisions in terms of logic rather than emotion; often answering questions with "I don't know" because we really don't know what we feel [#611].
12. Not knowing what we feel towards people because we can't identify emotions — which can sometimes make us vulnerable; and, on the other side, staying composed in emergencies because we don't give in to emotions (such as stress) during the situation and switch to autopilot [#612].

#### Seven ways emotional dysregulation can look in autistic people
A second neurodivergent_lou card series forwarded from Нейр∞тлично, "Как может выглядеть эмоциональная дисрегуляция у аутичных людей?" ("What can emotional dysregulation look like in autistic people?") [#640]:
1. Trouble falling asleep because of the day's events. Strong emotions (anxiety, sadness, embarrassment) can prevent relaxing before sleep; we go over and over what we felt about a situation or how we think the day went, which hinders regulating the body and falling asleep [#641].
2. Trouble making decisions because of strong emotions. Example: in a restaurant, choosing from the menu can make us very anxious, which affects the ability to decide and can be overwhelming; executive-function difficulties and difficulty with change can also contribute [#642].
3. Trouble identifying specific emotions. Autistic people sometimes have alexithymia — difficulty noticing, recognizing and describing emotions; people with it may find it hard to distinguish emotional experiences from physical sensations. An autistic person may know they feel something very strong but not be able to say what, which causes confusion and overload [#643].
4. Physical symptoms from difficulty regulating emotions. Persistent physical symptoms such as gastrointestinal problems, general unexplained pain or exhaustion; and persistent signs of the body being in "fight or flight" — racing heart, dizziness, rapid breathing [#644].
5. Delayed emotional response. The author describes something significant happening while she feels complete numbness; the emotions may come only "спустя недели, месяцы или даже годы" ("weeks, months or even years later"). Conversely, something relatively minor may happen and she does not realise how much it affected her until weeks later her emotions suddenly spiral out of control [#645].
6. Internalizing emotional experiences as shame and guilt. Because we sometimes feel emotions very acutely, we may face judgement from others or end up in far-from-ideal situations; we then start internalizing — hiding inner tension and suffering — which sometimes leads to lower self-esteem and inner guilt and shame [#646].
7. Numbness. The opposite of intense emotions: total emotional numbness, where emotions are hard to feel at all and seem flat and monotonous; this is not easy to cope with [#647].

#### Alexithymia — what it is, what it is not, what helps
Text by autismservicesgroup, "Аутизм и алекситимия" ("Autism and alexithymia"), subtitle "when feelings are real but hard to name", translated by Nastya [#799].
- Definition: difficulty recognizing, understanding and describing one's emotions. "Это не отсутствие эмоций, а особенность эмоциональной осознанности." ("It is not an absence of emotions but a feature of emotional awareness.") [#799]
- How it may show up — it can be hard to: find words for feelings; understand what exactly you feel right now; distinguish similar emotions; explain your emotions to other people [#799].
- Why it is common in autism — autistic people often experience emotions differently because of: sensory-processing differences; interoception differences (sensing body signals from inside); a particular cognitive style; a habit of masking their emotions [#799].
- What it is not: lack of empathy; emotional coldness; indifference; a character flaw [#799].
- What it is: a feature of information processing; a feature of the brain–body connection; a full neurological experience; something that becomes easier with understanding and support [#799].
- What helps: emotion maps and "body maps" on which sensations can be marked; visual prompts and supports; enough time to let a feeling ripen (досозреть) and make sense of it; gentle, unintrusive questions instead of pressure and demands to say right away what you feel [#799].

#### Misattribution of arousal
From the channel's article "Раскрытие эмоций: Наука об ошибочной атрибуции в социальной психологии" ("Unpacking emotions: the science of misattribution in social psychology"). The misattribution of arousal theory describes how people can misinterpret physiological arousal — a state of heightened physiological alertness and activity — taking it for sexual attraction or other emotions. Example: with a racing heart and excitement after a run you meet someone you find attractive and attribute your arousal to that person rather than to the exercise you just finished [#38].

Four reasons the post gives for why this is especially important for people with ASD/ADHD [#38]:
1. Emotion recognition — people with ASD/ADHD often struggle to identify their own emotions, so understanding misattribution is critical for better self-understanding and emotional expression.
2. Social interaction — misattribution can complicate social interactions, adding barriers to communication and to understanding social cues.
3. Stress management — sensitivity to sensory overload and stress means one needs to understand how arousal can be misread in order to develop effective stress-reduction strategies.
4. Self-regulation — awareness of how physiological arousal can be misidentified helps develop strategies for better self-regulation and managing one's own behaviour.
Understanding these aspects, the post says, can improve quality of life and make social interactions easier [#38].

### Detailed insights

#### Interoception as internal GPS and its role in self-care
The channel's article "Аутизм и Интероцепция" frames interoception as a kind of internal GPS for the organism that helps understand one's physical and emotional state; it explores how neurodivergent people may have differences in interoceptive perception and how these affect daily life, argues that developing interoceptive skills can substantially raise the level of self-care, and links interoceptive difficulties to stress, anxiety and emotional regulation, offering methods to improve interoception [#20]. The fuller content of these ideas is in *Two components of interoception* and *Six consequences* above.

#### The autism–interoception paradox and the insula
The presentation describes a paradoxical picture: research shows that autistic people, despite *lower accuracy* of interoceptive data on objective tests (for example heartbeat-detection tests), often report *higher* interoceptive sensitivity and awareness of bodily sensations. The interpretation offered: autistic people may experience amplified signals while their ability to objectively measure and interpret those signals is less precise [#186].

The insula is called the "centre of interoception in the brain"; it is responsible for perceiving pain, emotions and awareness of body states. Research cited says people with high interoceptive awareness usually have a more developed insula, while those with weakened awareness show reduced grey matter and activity there. In autistic people the insula's response varies: some show hypoconnectivity (fewer connections) and reduced grey-matter volume, others hyperconnectivity, associated with heightened perception of interoceptive signals. The presentation cautions that autism manifests in diverse ways and brain-imaging results can be contradictory [#186].

#### Alexithymia and interoception: the link and the first step
Nastya's post describes alexithymia as a state in which identifying and describing feelings is hard: people may feel numb or experience many emotions at once, which makes understanding them difficult. Research, she writes, shows alexithymia is closely linked to interoception — "интероцепция — это наш внутренний GPS" ("interoception is our internal GPS") that tells us we are hungry, cold, thirsty. Since internal body sensations are closely tied to emotions, methods for improving interoception can help with being aware of and understanding emotions [#47].

Her own opinion: part of the difficulty of this state is that a person may struggle to prevent overload in time (because body sensations are impaired), and the overload can then lead to a meltdown or shutdown. Therefore one of the first pieces of advice for managing alexithymia is regulating the nervous system, including through sensory regulation. This is needed to create a condition of safety, which in turn helps the person switch from the "fight, flight, freeze" reaction to attending to sensations inside themselves [#47].

#### Why emotions are noticed late
In the feelings-wheel post Nastya writes that if you are confused when asked "how are you feeling?" you are not alone: many neurodivergent people struggle to identify emotions because "мы не чувствуем эмоцию до тех пор, пока ее интенсивность не станет хотя бы 8/10" ("we don't feel an emotion until its intensity reaches at least 8/10"). This is inconvenient because we can miss the moment we are slightly irritated and the next moment already feel anger and the urge to hit something. Identifying emotions is a process that requires patience and practice, and not everyone learns it right away; the author therefore recommends starting by mastering interoceptive awareness as an excellent base for emotional awareness. The article covers visual tools for identifying emotions and exercises for emotional awareness, with a "summary" column under each section and downloadable PDF tools [#49].

### Practical playbook

- Set timers to remind you to eat, drink and take sensory breaks; set two timers a day as interoceptive check moments [#186].
- Hook a 10-second check-in onto recurring sensory activities: handwashing, washing dishes, showering, after meals [#186].
- When scanning or checking in, use concrete descriptive words (soft, tense, hot, buzzing) instead of "good/bad" [#186].
- Before trying to name an emotion, calm the nervous system (including sensory regulation) so you move out of fight/flight/freeze [#47].
- To name a feeling: start at the wheel's centre (sadness, anger, fear, happiness, strength, calm), move outward to a more precise word, then rate its intensity 1–10 on the feelings indicator [#49, #379, #381].
- If standard words don't fit, describe the emotion by texture, colour, body sensation, facial expression, "chemistry", energy movement or an image [#284].
- When supporting someone with alexithymia: give time for the feeling to ripen, use visual aids and body maps, ask gently instead of demanding an immediate answer [#799].
- After physical exertion or stress, check whether arousal is being attributed to the wrong cause (e.g. a person rather than the run you just did) [#38].

### Evidence and numbers

- About 50% of autistic people are thought to experience alexithymia (Milosavijevic et al., 2016, cited on the neurodivergent_lou card) [#605].
- Cook, Brewer, Shah and Bird (2013), cited in the presentation: difficulty understanding and distinguishing one's own emotions leads to significant difficulty understanding others' emotions [#186].
- Research cited in the presentation (no authors given): autistic people show lower accuracy on heartbeat-detection tests but report higher interoceptive awareness; higher interoceptive awareness goes with a more developed insula; the insula activates during self-reflection (mirror, photos) [#186].
- Thresholds used in the methods: check-ins of 10 seconds or longer; 30–50 seconds of activity to raise the pulse; 60 seconds to lower it; 2 timers a day [#186].

## Meltdowns and shutdowns

### What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel shared about autistic meltdowns and shutdowns: what a meltdown is and what it can look and feel like, including the "internal" meltdown that is invisible from outside; why meltdowns often happen at home after a day of masking; why adults do not simply "outgrow" them; how meltdowns and overload get mistaken for panic attacks, and how a verbal shutdown differs from selective mutism. It also covers how others should respond (co-regulation instead of confrontation or coercion) and what autistic people do to recover afterwards. Almost all material is translated first-person content from autistic authors. According to the sources, it matters because misreading a meltdown (as aggression, a tantrum, disobedience, or "nothing happening") leads to responses that escalate it, and because people who understand their overload can stop blaming themselves and arrange their lives to need fewer meltdowns [#534, #638, #370].

### Core ideas

- A meltdown is a reaction to overload, not a behaviour choice. neurodivergent_lou defines an autistic meltdown as a reaction to overload during which the person temporarily loses control; it looks different in different autistic people (full list under *What can happen during a meltdown*) [#365].
- Not seeing meltdowns does not mean they are absent. Meltdowns can be held back all day and released at home, or happen internally with no visible sign; a person who looks fine may still be having them [#364, #366, #367, #370].
- Meltdowns persist into adulthood and change over time rather than being outgrown [#369].
- How you perceive a meltdown determines how you respond. Seen as aggression → confrontation; seen as a tantrum/disobedience → coercion; both escalate it. It should be seen as a state of extreme stress and overstimulation, which calls for co-regulation [#534].
- Overload is often mislabelled as panic or anxiety. Two authors describe having believed for years that their meltdowns/overload episodes were panic attacks [#482, msg #638].
- A verbal shutdown is not selective mutism. One is a temporary overload response anywhere; the other is an anxiety disorder with a stable safe/unsafe pattern [#736].
- Recovery is individual and non-linear; common supports are music, comfort objects, special interests and sleep [#262].
- Gentleness and self-regulation reduce meltdowns; pushing increases them, according to a parent of an autistic son [#771].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### What can happen during a meltdown (neurodivergent_lou)
Purpose: to recognise that a meltdown takes many forms. The card defines the meltdown as "реакция на перегрузку, когда аутичный человек временно теряет контроль" (a reaction to overload when an autistic person temporarily loses control) and stresses that meltdowns can manifest differently in different autistic people. Things that may happen during a meltdown:
1. Crying, screaming, shrieking ("Плач, крики, визги").
2. Running away from the situation.
3. Falling to the floor.
4. Strong emotions.
5. Possibly harming oneself or others — the card's example is hitting one's head.
6. Intense stimming, for example rocking or pacing back and forth.
7. Being unable to communicate.
8. A feeling that the world is slowing down or speeding up.
9. A rushing stream of thoughts ("Стремительный поток мыслей").
10. All senses becoming heightened ("Все чувства обостряются").
The first seven are mostly outwardly visible; the last three are inner experiences, which links to the internal meltdown below [#365].

#### Internal (hidden) meltdown model (neurodivergent_lou)
Purpose: to explain why someone can have meltdowns no one witnesses.
1. Why it develops. Autistic people may have internal meltdowns because they have subconsciously learned that they will be judged or suffer negative consequences in response to their "external" meltdowns [#367].
2. What it looks like from outside. It may seem that nothing special is happening [#367].
3. What it feels like inside. "невероятно интенсивно" (incredibly intense): a racing stream of thoughts, loss of control, strong emotions, dissociation, an urge to run away or to lash out, and a deep sense of dread ("глубокое чувство ужаса") [#367].
4. The reverse wish. Every autistic person is different: some have very visible outward meltdowns and wish their reactions were more hidden and less noticeable to others. This can be linked to the extremely negative, sometimes horrible reactions from other people they have faced during their meltdowns [#368].
5. The conclusion for observers. It is often assumed that if an autistic person is not seen in meltdown, or does not show it in a given setting, they are fine; this can be far from the truth. "Отсутствие видимых признаков мелтдауна не означает, что его нет." (The absence of visible signs of a meltdown does not mean there isn't one.) [#364, #370]

#### Delayed release: the "explosion at home"
Autistic people may mask and hold back their emotions all day, waiting until they get home, where they practically "explode" ("«взрываются»") because they can finally be safe enough to express what they really feel. This is described as an accumulation of stress and emotions over the day that is then released, sometimes in the form of a meltdown, once the person is home [#366]. Lesson: a calm person at school/work and a meltdown at home are the same day's overload, released where it is safe.

#### Perception determines response (neuroliminality)
Purpose: to explain the most common mistakes in handling meltdowns and why they escalate them. neuroliminality (translated by Anne Green Moon) opens by admitting that managing meltdowns is hard and that "некоторые аутичные люди могут проявлять агрессию и жестокое поведение во время этого состояния" (some autistic people may show aggression and violent behaviour during this state) [#534].
- Escalation is the key error. Escalating a meltdown is an easy mistake, often made unintentionally. In the author's personal view the most common mistake is treating meltdowns "как к агрессивному и провокационному акту неповиновения" (as an aggressive and provocative act of disobedience); this attitude most often makes meltdowns worse [#534].
- The mapping:
  1. See the meltdown as aggressive and provocative → you will most likely respond confrontationally.
  2. See it as a tantrum or disobedience → you will most likely respond with coercion.
  3. Instead, see it as a state in which the person is under incredible stress and overstimulation ("невероятного стресса и перевозбуждения") → respond with co-regulation.
- Why confrontation and coercion fail. When someone is overloaded and tense, confrontation or coercion can mirror the heightened state of irritation or put even more demands on the autistic person's mind [#534].
- The misunderstanding extends to the literature. Autism literature still labels meltdowns "проблематичное", "деструктивное" or "трудное" (problematic, destructive, difficult) behaviour, and many keep using "истерика" (tantrum) and "meltdown" interchangeably; the author cites Lewis & Stevens (2023) for this [#534].
- Prevention beats support. The author says that successfully supporting an autistic person during a meltdown ranks below the feeling of having managed to reduce and prevent the number of meltdowns they live through, "because I know what a drag it is to live through them" [#534].

#### Co-regulation during a meltdown (neuroliminality)
When trying to cope with or support someone during a meltdown, the supporter should:
1. Stay calm — so as not to mirror and amplify the person's irritation (the stated reason confrontation fails).
2. Provide a sense of physical and emotional safety.
3. Reduce the autistic person's cognitive load — because coercion piles yet more demands onto an already overloaded mind.
The term "ко-регуляция" is glossed in the post as "совместная" (joint) regulation during overstimulation and stress [#534].

#### Panic attack or overload? A re-reading (FINCHOFAUTISMLIFE, via #638)
Purpose: to help an adult who thinks they have panic attacks check whether it is actually sensory overload. Title: "Это были не панические атаки. Это была перегрузка." (It was not panic attacks. It was overload.), subtitle "My autistic brain was trying to tell me something." The author thought for years they had panic attacks, but it was stimulation overload in an autistic adult [#638].
1. What I used to think was happening (the symptoms): a racing heartbeat; tightness in the chest; an urge to run away; tears "out of nowhere". Naturally, the author concluded it was anxiety.
2. What didn't fit: these moments were not connected to anxiety or fear.
3. When they actually happened: in noisy places; in bright light; in crowds; during constant conversations; or when too many things had to be done at once.
4. The turning point: learning about sensory overload — "И всё стало понятно. Я не паниковал(а), я был(а) перегружен(а)." (And everything became clear. I wasn't panicking, I was overloaded.)
5. How overload feels: the brain seems to switch off; noise and light become unbearable; sudden irritability or "switching off"; a sudden feeling of cold or heat; an acute urge to escape or hide; sometimes even losing the ability to speak.
6. What helped me understand: being autistic, my nervous system perceives the world differently, which means "я могу перегружаться, даже если "ничего не случилось"" (I can get overloaded even if "nothing happened").
7. What I do now — instead of blaming myself: take breaks in quiet; allow myself to stim; avoid bright light and chaotic places; say "no" without guilt; plan rest time after socialising; use grounding techniques; use daytime naps.
8. Message to the reader: if this is familiar, you are not being dramatic, not broken and not "too sensitive"; you may be autistic, sensory-sensitive, or both — "Твоё тело просто просит покоя." (Your body is just asking for rest.) [#638]

(For a long time I thought my autistic meltdowns were panic attacks...) [#482]. (!) #### Selective mutism vs verbal shutdown (autisticguidetothegalaxy)
Purpose: both states can make speech impossible, but for different reasons; knowing which is which helps you explain your experience and helps others respond with patience instead of pressure. Selective mutism is an anxiety disorder with stable patterns; verbal shutdowns are temporary reactions to stress or overload [#736].

Selective mutism (СМ):
- It is an anxiety disorder.
- Speech is possible in some situations and impossible in others.
- The pattern is stable: safe spaces (e.g. home) vs unsafe spaces (e.g. school, work).
- Most often begins in childhood but can persist into adulthood; later onset is possible but rare.
- How it feels: the words are there but feel "blocked"; anxiety makes speech impossible.
- Potential triggers: social anxiety; fear of evaluation or embarrassment; talking with authority figures; new or unfamiliar situations [#736].

Verbal shutdown:
- A reaction to stress or overload.
- Can happen in any situation where demands, emotions or sensory stimuli become overwhelming.
- Speech returns once the nervous system returns to normal.
- Occurs in autism, ADHD, trauma and other conditions.
- How it feels: the brain and body are saving energy; the speech function is temporarily "switched off".
- Potential triggers: sensory overload (noise, light, smell, touch); emotional overload; stress, fatigue or burnout; too many demands at once [#736].

Key differences:
| Dimension | Selective mutism | Verbal shutdown |
|---|---|---|
| Duration | usually long-lasting and situational | usually short-term and temporary |
| Pattern | stable "safe vs unsafe" pattern | can happen anywhere at a peak of overload |
| Trigger | social anxiety and fear of speaking | overload, stress or exhaustion |

Both states need understanding, a safe environment and alternative ways of communicating. Clarification: selective mutism is an anxiety disorder, not a trait of autism or ADHD — yet an autistic or ADHD person can also have selective mutism. In short: "Селективный мутизм = симптом тревожного расстройства. Вербальный шатдаун = реакция на аутистическую перегрузку." (Selective mutism = a symptom of an anxiety disorder. Verbal shutdown = a reaction to autistic overload.) [#736]

Admin's observations: for selective mutism the channel admin thinks of Raj from *The Big Bang Theory*, who could not talk to women because of anxiety. For verbal shutdown the admin recalls being asked at home where something is and being unable to explain, because there is no energy to formulate words, so it is easier to get up and show where it is — what is felt most strongly is the absence of resource for talking [#736].

#### Recovery after a meltdown (childofautism)
Context: the author experiences both meltdowns and shutdowns but tends to go into shutdowns more often; a meltdown "высасывает все силы" (sucks out all one's strength). The things below are done in no particular order — "Я делаю то, что принесёт мне пользу" (I do whatever will benefit me) — and "Ваш опыт может отличаться" (your experience may differ) [#262].
1. Listen to music. Usually puts on headphones. Music is one of the author's special interests, so they listen to favourite songs they find calming after a meltdown, or put one particular song on repeat. Depending on the situation they may play the piano because it can calm them, though not every time.
2. Keep comfort objects close. The author loves having favourite plush toys and usually carries a comfort toy all the time; at home they like to hug a pillow and look at a jellyfish lamp.
3. Do something related to a special interest. Once calmer, they pick an activity linked to their special interests — bridges, sometimes Indian films, favourite shows/series; from time to time they look through the photos of bridges they have taken and enjoy comparing them. "Многие мои СИ могут успокоить меня" (many of my special interests can calm me).
4. Sleep. Sometimes they just sleep: sometimes a short nap and then waking up to do other helpful things; sometimes they feel so tired they can do nothing but sleep. Falling asleep after a meltdown, when possible, feels refreshing [#262].

### Detailed insights

#### Meltdowns do not disappear with age
It is sometimes assumed that autistic people "outgrow" ("«перерастают»") meltdowns and that autistic adults no longer have them. This is far from always true; meltdowns in autistic people can change over time [#369].

#### The lived experience of a meltdown from inside (neuroliminality and Lewis & Stevens)
neuroliminality says there is a big difference between how a meltdown looks from outside and how it is lived inside, and that they view meltdowns very differently from the allistic people around them. Lewis & Stevens (2023) are quoted: meltdowns have been widely studied through the lens of the parental gaze, but no research to date had provided a first-hand account of what living through a meltdown is like for the autistic person. From the author's experience, a meltdown is a state of overstimulation with a colossal inner struggle to regain control: on one side an urgent need for physical and emotional discharge (a "burst"), on the other a strong wish for the meltdown to stop and for as little harm as possible to be done. "Единственный выход из этого – пройти через мелтдаун." (The only way out of it is to go through the meltdown.) The author asks for a little sympathy, if not empathy, when you see an autistic person in meltdown: it is an inner crisis whose scale you cannot understand unless you have faced it yourself. "Наши мелтдауны не просто «неудобство» для других людей." (Our meltdowns are not just an "inconvenience" for other people.) [#534]

#### A parent's view: pushing causes regression and meltdowns
Mama Serenity Wolf, mother of an autistic son, writes that some people think she does not push him hard enough; they do not see how hard she tries and how careful she must be. If she pushes too hard, he actually regresses and goes backwards. If she is not extremely gentle, he has meltdowns, intense anxiety, insomnia and health problems. When he is allowed to regulate his nervous system in the ways that work for him, he blossoms and develops. If that takes more time than people think acceptable, that is fine — she will do what works best for her son. Lesson: demand pressure on an autistic child is described as a direct driver of meltdowns, and self-regulation on his own terms as the condition for growth [#771, img #771].
