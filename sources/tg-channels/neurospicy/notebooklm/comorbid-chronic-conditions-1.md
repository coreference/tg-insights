## The neurodivergent comorbidity cluster and self-help map

### What this covers

This topic collects the channel's posts on conditions that tend to come together with autism and ADHD. It covers comorbidity statistics for autism, the "triad" of hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (hEDS), mast cell activation syndrome (MCAS) and postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS), binocular vision dysfunction (BVD) and the MTHFR gene mutation. It also includes the author's visual self-help map for "neurocomplexity" and a forwarded awareness post on how stroke can look different in women. The source says these conditions matter because they strongly affect quality of life and daily functioning, are often misdiagnosed (BVD as "other conditions", MCAS as "psychosomatic"), and need a comprehensive approach, often with several specialists [#349, #779, #509, #767].

### Core ideas

- Autism rarely comes alone. A channel card lists 12 comorbidities with their rates. The highest are sleep disturbance (80%), ADHD (60%) and gastrointestinal problems (46%). Full list under *Autism comorbidity statistics card* [#64].
- hEDS, MCAS and POTS form a triad. These three conditions often occur with autism and ADHD and with each other. Diagnosing and treating them needs a comprehensive approach, often with several specialists [#779].
- Some conditions are hidden or misdiagnosed. A standard eye exam may miss BVD, and doctors often misdiagnose it as other conditions. MCAS is often labelled "psychosomatic" or "somatoform disorder", yet unlike those labels it can and should be treated [#349, #779].
- Self-help in neurocomplexity works on several fronts at once. The author's map has six parallel branches: pain control, supportive strategies, mast cell stabilisation, medication for depression/anxiety/ADHD, nervous-system support and psychotherapy [#509].
- Body signals should not be put down to stress. In women, stroke may look like confusion, a sudden headache or a "panic attack". These signs are often mistaken for stress or overwork, which is dangerous [#767].

#### The hEDS / MCAS / POTS triad
The author presents three conditions that are "часто коморбидна с аутизмом и СДВГ" (often comorbid with autism and ADHD). The post's image is a meme that captures how they cluster: "Почему, когда что-то случается, вы трое всегда оказываетесь рядом?" (Why is it that whenever something happens, you three are always around?), labelled САТК, СПОТ, гСЭД [#779, img #779].

1. Hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (гСЭД / hEDS).
   - *What it is:* a hereditary connective-tissue disease, which the author calls a variant of connective tissue dysplasia (ДСТ).
   - *Main features:* excessive joint mobility, moderately stretchy and fragile skin, and general weakness of connective tissue, "которые у нас есть во всем организме" (which we have throughout the whole body).
   - *Effects:* chronic pain and fatigue, frequent dislocations and subluxations, and "многие (!!!) другие осложнения" (many (!!!) other complications) in the body.
   - *Mechanism:* genetic mutations that affect collagen production, which is what makes the tissues fragile [#779].
2. Mast cell activation syndrome (САТК / MCAS).
   - *What mast cells normally do:* they are a natural and necessary part of the immune system that protects the body from allergens and pathogens. They are found everywhere in the body, in all connective tissues and even in the brain, and normally help the body cope with allergies, viruses and toxins.
   - *What goes wrong in MCAS:* the cells become overactive and react to everything as if it were an allergen or pathogen. They release large amounts of inflammatory mediators such as histamine, cytokines and prostaglandins.
   - *Symptoms:* when there are too many mediators, symptoms vary widely: allergic reactions, gastrointestinal problems, skin rashes, headaches, neurological and cognitive symptoms, "и многое другое" (and much more).
   - *Diagnosis:* MCAS is hard to diagnose because symptoms differ from person to person. Because few people know about it, it is often wrongly diagnosed as "психосоматика" (psychosomatics) or "соматоформное расстройство" (somatoform disorder). The author's point: "В отличие от этих неправильных диагнозов САТК можно и нужно лечить" (Unlike these wrong diagnoses, MCAS can and should be treated) [#779].
3. Postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (СПОТ / POTS).
   - *What it is:* a disorder of the autonomic nervous system. The heart rate rises abnormally when a person moves from lying down to upright.
   - *Symptoms:* dizziness, weakness, rapid heartbeat and fainting "(и многие др.)" (and many others).
   - *Links and impact:* POTS is often linked with hEDS and MCAS and can greatly affect quality of life [#779].

Overall conclusion: the three often occur together and can greatly affect daily life. Diagnosis and treatment need a comprehensive approach and often several specialists [#779]. The post links to other channel posts for the connective-tissue disease, MCAS symptoms and POTS symptoms. Those posts are not part of this topic [#779].

#### Binocular vision dysfunction (BVD)
Definition and mechanism. BVD is a condition in which the eyes cannot work together properly as one system. A small error in how the eyes synchronise means each eye sends the brain a slightly different image [#349].

Everyday difficulties people with BVD often have [#349]:
1. Trouble judging the distance to objects, which can affect driving or sports.
2. Inaccurate typing on a phone or computer, often missing the right keys.
3. Losing their place when reading, so they need a bookmark or a finger to track the text.
4. Poor eye-hand coordination, which makes things like threading beads or catching a ball hard.
5. Difficulty on stairs or uneven surfaces because depth perception is wrong.
6. Clumsiness when pouring liquids into containers or putting objects in a specific place.
7. Discomfort when watching 3D films or using VR devices.
8. A tendency to hold the head at an unusual angle to compensate for the vision problem.

Key features [#349]:
1. It affects up to 20% of the population.
2. It can greatly reduce quality of life.
3. It affects reading, computer work and everyday activities.
4. It is often misdiagnosed as other conditions.

Symptoms vary and can include [#349]:
1. Headaches and migraines.
2. Dizziness and balance problems.
3. Blurred or double vision.
4. Difficulty concentrating.
5. Anxiety, especially in large spaces.
6. Reading and learning problems.
7. Light sensitivity.
8. Nausea and motion sickness.

Causes: facial asymmetry, dysfunction of eye muscles or nerves, and brain injuries [#349].

Comorbidity: "Это состояние коморбидно и часто встречается среди людей с СДВГ, аутизмом и гипермобильным синдромом Элерса-Данлоса/ДСТ" (This condition is comorbid and is often found among people with ADHD, autism and hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome / connective tissue dysplasia) [#349].

Diagnosis and treatment: diagnosis needs a specialised examination, because a routine eye exam may not detect the problem. Treatment usually includes special prismatic lenses and vision therapy. People with such symptoms are advised to see an "офтальмологу, специализирующемуся на бинокулярном зрении" (ophthalmologist specialising in binocular vision) for an accurate diagnosis and effective treatment [#349].

Classic signs: numbness of the face and limbs, speech impairment and sudden weakness. Additional signs that may appear in women [#767]:

2. A severe headache with no cause.

4. Nausea and vomiting.
5. Sharp jumps in blood pressure.
6. A feeling like a "панической атаки" (panic attack).

## Ehlers-Danlos syndrome and hypermobility

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's educational material on Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (EDS) and hypermobility spectrum disorder (HSD): definitions, the 13 EDS subtypes and their genes, the full official hEDS diagnostic checklist (in Russian and English), the diagnostic pathway, a pre-appointment symptom questionnaire, the many comorbidities (pain, dislocations, digestive problems, dysautonomia, venous outflow from the brain, mast cell activation, anxiety), a candidate blood biomarker, and a patient-community overview of the situation in Russia. According to the source it matters because the condition affects many body systems at once, is largely invisible to others, takes on average about 12 years to diagnose, and is poorly known among Russian doctors [#79, #134, #222, #603].

### Core ideas

- EDS is a group of 13 inherited connective-tissue conditions; hypermobile EDS is by far the most common (90% of cases per #603) and the only major type whose gene is unknown [#204, #219, #603].
- HSD is diagnosed when hypermobility symptoms are not explained by another condition such as EDS — i.e. when someone has hypermobility and related symptoms but does not meet hEDS or other subtype criteria [#207, #221].
- Connective tissue is everywhere, so EDS/HSD is multisystem: pain, dislocations, GI, autonomic, sleep, mental-health, allergy and dental problems cluster together, which makes diagnosis hard and calls for multidisciplinary care [#79, #208].
- hEDS diagnosis is clinical: all three criteria (generalised hypermobility by Beighton score; two of features A/B/C; exclusion of other conditions) must be met at once [#375, #376].
- A blood biomarker may be coming: a 52 kDa fibronectin fragment was found in every hEDS/HSD participant of one study, but it is not yet validated [#222].
- hEDS and HSD may merge: the source reports the international "Road to 2026" project plans to treat them as one disease differing only in severity [#603].
- Much of it is invisible: people see someone smiling and working, not the pain, dislocations and dozens of hidden symptoms; the conditions also fluctuate day to day [#134, #209].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### 13 subtypes of EDS
The channel's comic states EDS is "группа из 13 наследственных заболеваний, которые влияют на соединительную ткань" (a group of 13 inherited diseases that affect connective tissue) [#204]. Post #219 defines EDS as a group of inherited connective-tissue diseases characterised by joint hypermobility, increased skin stretchiness, easy bruising, a tendency to form scars and other soft-tissue anomalies; severity ranges from minor joint instability to life-threatening complications. The 13 subtypes are classified by clinical presentation, genetic cause and inheritance pattern [#219]. The comic card lists all 13 types: arthrochalasia, brittle cornea syndrome, kyphoscoliotic, musculocontractural, spondylodysplastic, cardio-vascular, classical, classical-like, dermatosparaxis, hypermobile, myopathic, periodontal and vascular [#205]. Post #219 details the main ones:

1. Classical EDS (кСЭД / cEDS) — symptoms: skin hyperelasticity, atrophic scars, joint hypermobility. Gene: mutations in COL5A1 or COL5A2 [#219].
2. Hypermobile EDS (гСЭД / hEDS) — symptoms: joint hypermobility, frequent dislocations/subluxations, chronic pain. Gene: "Конкретная генетическая причина в настоящее время неизвестна" (the specific genetic cause is currently unknown) [#219].
3. Vascular EDS (сСЭД / vEDS) — thin translucent skin, extensive bruising, marked fragility of arteries, intestine and uterus. Gene: COL3A1 [#219].
4. Kyphoscoliotic EDS (ксСЭД) — congenital muscle hypotonia, scoliosis, eye fragility. Gene: PLOD1 [#219].
5. Arthrochalasia EDS (аСЭД) — severe joint hypermobility, congenital hip dislocation, skin hyperelasticity. Genes: COL1A1 or COL1A2 [#219].
6. Dermatosparaxis EDS (дСЭД) — marked skin fragility, sagging skin, easy bruising. Gene: ADAMTS2 [#219].
7. Cardio-vascular EDS (кв-СЭД) — severe progressive heart-valve disorders. Gene: COL1A2 [#219].
8. Classical-like EDS (кп-СЭД) — skin hyperelasticity, joint hypermobility and easy bruising but without atrophic scars. Gene: TNXB [#219].
9. Periodontal EDS (пСЭД), 10. brittle cornea syndrome (СХР), 11. spondylodysplastic EDS (сд-СЭД), 12. musculocontractural EDS (мк-СЭД), 13. myopathic EDS (мСЭД) — named only; the post says each has unique manifestations and genetic bases and promises to cover them later. (!) INCOMPLETE: symptoms and genes of these five are not given in the source [#219].

Although the types differ, the comic lists the most frequent shared signs: joint hypermobility and instability, skin hyperextensibility, tissue fragility (drawn as an arm in a sling and bandages), extreme fatigue, and chronic pain [#206].

#### Four forms of hypermobility spectrum disorder
HSD covers a range of conditions linked to joint hypermobility and is considered when a patient has hypermobility plus accompanying symptoms but does not meet the criteria for hEDS or another EDS subtype. It includes four forms [#221]:
1. Generalised joint hypermobility — Г-РСГ (G-HSD).
2. Peripheral hypermobility — П-РСГ (P-HSD).
3. Localised hypermobility — Л-РСГ (L-HSD).
4. Historical hypermobility — И-РСГ (H-HSD).
(!) INCOMPLETE: the source does not define how the four forms differ [#221].

#### Diagnostic pathway for EDS and HSD
Post #221 describes diagnosis as a combination of clinical assessment, genetic testing and differential diagnosis, in four key stages [#221]:
1. Clinical assessment. (a) History: a detailed history focused on joint dislocations/subluxations, skin problems and family history. (b) Physical exam: assessing joint hypermobility with the Beighton score, skin elasticity and other characteristic signs [#221].
2. Genetic testing. Identifying specific mutations linked to the different EDS subtypes by sequencing the relevant genes, for example COL5A1, COL5A2, COL3A1 (i.e. the classical and vascular genes — hEDS has no known gene, see *13 subtypes*) [#221].
3. Imaging. MRI or ultrasound to assess joint and soft-tissue abnormalities and potential vascular problems [#221].
4. Differential diagnosis. Excluding conditions with similar symptoms, such as Marfan syndrome, osteogenesis imperfecta and other connective-tissue diseases [#221].

#### 2017 diagnostic criteria for hypermobile EDS (checklist)
The channel shared the official checklist in English (v9, "for doctors across all disciplines to be able to diagnose EDS") and a Russian adaptation by the NGO «Не просто усталость», which states "Этот диагностический протокол предназначен для диагностики гСЭД врачами любых специальностей" (this diagnostic protocol is intended for diagnosing hEDS by doctors of any specialty) [#375, #376]. The form has fields for patient name, date of birth, evaluating doctor and date of visit. Core rule: "The clinical diagnosis of hypermobile EDS needs the simultaneous presence of all criteria, 1 and 2 and 3." [#376] — in Russian "необходимо одновременное наличие всех критериев: 1, 2 и 3" [#375].

Criterion 1 — Generalised joint hypermobility. Beighton score out of 9; one of the following must be selected [#375, #376]:
- ≥6 for pre-pubertal children and adolescents;
- ≥5 for pubertal men and women up to age 50;
- ≥4 for men and women over 50.

If the Beighton score is one point below the age- and sex-specific cut-off, two or more of these five questions must also be answered "yes" to meet the criterion [#375, #376]:
1. "Can you now (or could you ever) place your hands flat on the floor without bending your knees?"
2. "Can you now (or could you ever) bend your thumb to touch your forearm?"
3. "As a child, did you amuse your friends by contorting your body into strange shapes or could you do the splits?"
4. "As a child or teenager, did your shoulder or kneecap dislocate on more than one occasion?"
5. "Do you consider yourself “double jointed”?" (Russian: "Считаете ли вы себя сверхгибкими?" — *do you consider yourself super-flexible?*)

Criterion 2 — two or more of features A, B or C must be present [#375, #376].

*Feature A* — five or more of these 12 must be present (total scored /12) [#375, #376]:
1. Unusually soft or velvety skin.
2. Mild skin hyperextensibility.
3. Unexplained striae distensae or rubae (stretch marks) on the back, groins, thighs, breasts and/or abdomen in adolescents, men or pre-pubertal women, without a history of significant gain or loss of body fat or weight.
4. Bilateral piezogenic papules of the heel.
5. Recurrent or multiple abdominal hernia(s) (Russian version: umbilical, inguinal, femoral).
6. Atrophic scarring involving at least two sites, without the truly papyraceous ("cigarette-paper") and/or hemosideric scars seen in classical EDS.
7. Pelvic floor, rectal and/or uterine prolapse in children, men or nulliparous women without a history of morbid obesity or other known predisposing condition.
8. Dental crowding and a high or narrow palate.
9. Arachnodactyly, defined by one or more of: (i) positive wrist sign (Walker sign) on both sides, (ii) positive thumb sign (Steinberg sign) on both sides.
10. Arm span-to-height ratio ≥1.05.
11. Mitral valve prolapse, mild or greater, based on strict echocardiographic criteria.
12. Aortic root dilatation with Z-score >+2.

*Feature B* — positive family history: one or more first-degree relatives independently meeting the current hEDS criteria [#375, #376].

*Feature C* — at least one of [#375, #376]:
1. Musculoskeletal pain in two or more limbs, recurring daily for at least 3 months.
2. Chronic, widespread pain for ≥3 months.
3. Recurrent joint dislocations or frank joint instability, in the absence of trauma.

Criterion 3 — all of the following prerequisites must be met [#375, #376]:
1. Absence of unusual skin fragility — if present, other types of EDS should be considered.
2. Exclusion of other heritable and acquired connective-tissue disorders, including autoimmune rheumatologic conditions. In a patient with an acquired connective-tissue disease (e.g. lupus, rheumatoid arthritis), an additional hEDS diagnosis requires meeting both Features A and B of Criterion 2, and Feature C (chronic pain and/or instability) cannot be counted in that situation.
3. Exclusion of alternative diagnoses that may also include joint hypermobility through hypotonia and/or connective-tissue laxity: neuromuscular disorders (e.g. Bethlem myopathy), other hereditary connective-tissue disorders (other EDS types, Loeys-Dietz syndrome, Marfan syndrome) and skeletal dysplasias (e.g. osteogenesis imperfecta) — "include, but are not limited to" these. Exclusion may be based on history, physical examination and/or molecular genetic testing as indicated.

### Detailed insights

#### Comorbidities and their prevalence
Post #79 (by Настя) summarises the most common comorbidities of EDS/HSD with approximate prevalence and, where available, ethnic differences [#79]:
1. Chronic pain — persistent, often musculoskeletal; a hallmark of EDS and HSD, affecting about 90% [#79].
2. Joint dislocations and subluxations — common in hypermobile EDS and HSD, about 70–80% of patients [#79].
3. Gastrointestinal disorders — IBS, gastroparesis, reflux; about 70% of people with EDS report GI symptoms. IBS is more common in people of European descent, but GI problems are seen in all ethnic groups [#79].
4. Dysautonomia — autonomic nervous system dysfunction including POTS; affects 50–80% of people with hEDS. POTS is more common in people of European descent though diagnosed in all groups [#79].
5. Fatigue — chronic, often severe and disabling exhaustion in about 80% [#79].
6. Sleep disorders — insomnia, sleep apnea, unrefreshing sleep; about 70% [#79].
7. Anxiety and depression — anxiety about 70%, depression about 50% [#79].
8. Osteoarthritis — degenerative joint disease in about 50%, especially with joint hypermobility; rates are higher among people of European descent and some Asian populations [#79].
9. Cardiovascular problems — mitral valve prolapse, aortic root dilation, aneurysms; about 30% of people with vascular and classical EDS [#79].
10. Dental and orthodontic problems — high palate, crowded teeth, gum disease; a "significant share" (no figure) [#79].
11. Neurological problems — migraines, Chiari malformation, tethered cord syndrome; about 50% [#79].
12. Allergies and MCAS — about 50% report allergy or MCAS symptoms [#79].

The post's conclusion: these comorbidities can affect several body systems, which complicates diagnosis; reported prevalence varies with cultural, environmental and genetic factors; and a comprehensive multidisciplinary approach is needed to improve outcomes and quality of life [#79]. The comic lists the associated problems as headaches, GI problems, autonomic nervous system dysfunction, mast cell activation syndrome and anxiety disorders [#208].

#### Digestive system in hEDS
EDS is a group of hereditary conditions of connective tissue; the hypermobile type is the one most often linked to digestive problems. In hEDS the collagen-rich connective tissue becomes more "stretchy", which affects not only skin and joints but the whole digestive tract [#74]. Common symptoms: acid reflux; chronic indigestion; constipation or diarrhoea; abdominal pain and bloating; nausea and vomiting. Symptoms vary greatly between people and can involve both the upper and lower GI tract [#74]. The linked article (translated by the channel, "GI problems in hEDS and HSD") also explains how autonomic dysfunction such as POTS can contribute, and lists tests and treatment strategies doctors may offer. (!) INCOMPLETE: the tests and treatments themselves are only in the external article, not in the post [#74, img #74].

#### Dislocations and subluxations
Slides adapted from The Ehlers-Danlos Society, made because the author often got questions about what dislocations and subluxations are [#124]:
- Dislocation (вывих): "кости в суставе полностью отделяются друг от друга" (the bones in a joint fully separate from each other) [#125].
- Subluxation (подвывих): the bones shift relative to each other more than they normally should, but without full separation [#126].
- Where: can occur in various parts of the body; the most common sites include the temporomandibular joint, shoulder, thumb and kneecap [#127].
- Consequences: both can be painful and damage tissue in and around the joint; a dislocation can be extremely painful and distressing and requires urgent medical care [#128].
- Treatment: they are treatable, which lowers the risk of recurrence and reduces pain and injury. The most common and effective treatment is physical strengthening, supported by taping and supportive aids, under the guidance of a physiotherapist [#129].

#### Management of EDS/HSD
Symptom management aims to relieve symptoms and prevent complications, and may include: pain management, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, and support in education and employment [#210]. EDS and HSD can affect people of every age, race, gender and body type; understanding the symptoms, support and adaptations can be very helpful [#211]. The conditions fluctuate and affect a person day to day — the comic shows a hypermobile character curling small dumbbells while watching others lift heavy barbells, thinking "Как бы я хотела тоже так уметь..." (how I wish I could do that too...) [#209].

#### Invisibility of hypermobility

- pain; dislocations; osteoporosis; subluxations; sprains; osteoarthritis;

- constipation; diarrhoea; abdominal pain; bloating; reflux; nausea; vomiting; irritable bowel syndrome; motility disorder;
- organ rupture; prolapse; vascular compressions; vitamin deficiencies;
- mast cell activation syndrome; sleep disorders; chronic fatigue syndrome;
- anxiety; depression; temporomandibular disorders; postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome [#134].

#### Venous outflow from the brain in EDS
A recent study (reported by the channel) found that in people with connective-tissue disorders such as EDS, impaired venous outflow from the brain can present in a unique way: these patients have more severe symptoms and worse quality of life than "ordinary" patients with venous-outflow problems. Common symptoms include headaches, dizziness and drug allergies. The researchers stress that doctors need to be aware of these features to improve diagnosis and treatment [#135].

#### Candidate blood biomarker for hEDS/HSD
A study funded by The Ehlers-Danlos Society and published in the American Journal of Medical Genetics found potential blood biomarkers for hEDS and HSD. This matters because diagnosis has been hard for lack of established lab tests and molecular markers [#222].
- Design: blood samples from 466 adults, including 94 with hEDS and 80 with HSD [#222].
- Finding 1: a specific 52 kDa fibronectin fragment was present in the blood of every person with hEDS and HSD, and notably absent in healthy controls, people with other EDS types and people with various forms of arthritis [#222].
- Finding 2: its consistent presence in both hEDS and HSD suggests a shared pathophysiology, which questions the distinction between the two conditions [#222].
- Finding 3: a collagen I fragment was also found in everyone with hEDS and HSD, but it was present in other conditions as well (so it is less specific) [#222].
- Meaning: could lead to the first blood test for hEDS/HSD, a more reliable tool for clinicians, and shorter time to diagnosis, currently about 12 years on average [#222].
- Next steps: the authors stress that other researchers must confirm the results in further hEDS, HSD and control groups before diagnostic use. The Society is funding confirmation work. No diagnostic lab offers the test yet; if validated it could serve diagnosis, treatment trials and insight into causes [#222].

#### EDS and connective tissue dysplasia in Russia
A post forwarded from the Russian patient channel Невидимые зебры (Invisible zebras) [#603]:
- Scale (expert estimates voiced at the all-Russian congress «Человек и лекарство»): about 30,000 people in Russia with EDS of varying severity, and up to 30 million with undifferentiated connective tissue dysplasia (НДСТ) — "почти каждый 5-й человек" (almost every 5th person) has signs of connective tissue dysplasia. Experts stressed that connective-tissue disorders remain severely underestimated in Russian healthcare, a significant public-health problem given high prevalence and severe, sometimes life-threatening cases [#603, img #603].
- Prevalence facts: 90% of all EDS cases are the hypermobile type; 10 EDS types are ultra-rare (about 1 case per million); manifestations of hEDS often overlap with НДСТ signs [#603].
- Proposed merger: international experts in "Road to 2026" propose revising the boundary; according to "new international data" hEDS and HSD (equated in the post with НДСТ) are planned to be treated as one disease differing only in severity, which "может радикально изменить подход к диагностике, лечению и социальной поддержке" (could radically change the approach to diagnosis, treatment and social support) [#603].
- Awareness gap: many Russian medical workers are still unfamiliar even with the official 2017 EDS classification [#603].
- Why it matters — the problems listed [#603]:
  1. Clinical guidelines for EDS and connective tissue dysplasia need to be created in Russia.
  2. Low awareness of the prevalence and its many nuances: risks, complications, chronic pain.
  3. Difficulty getting qualified care, including free specialised rehabilitation, orthoses and assistive devices under ОМС, and access to a vertical (upright) MRI.
  4. No protocols for managing pregnancies and surgeries in EDS patients.
  5. No free genetic testing under ОМС when EDS is suspected after seeing a geneticist.
  6. Difficulty obtaining disability status and social support, because there is no clear grading of connective-tissue involvement severity.
  7. No specialised connective tissue dysplasia centres across the country with qualified doctors and systematic diagnosis and treatment.
  8. Low awareness of vascular and neurological risks, risk of sudden death and pregnancy complications.
  9. Underestimation of severity: connective tissue is key to the whole body, and in EDS its disorders can be systemic and affect many organ systems individually.
- Conclusion of the post: diagnosis and care must become a systemic healthcare task; specialised centres, clinical guidelines and better awareness among doctors are needed [#603].

## Mast cell activation syndrome (MCAS)

### What this covers

This topic gathers everything the channel Нейропряность posted about mast cells and mast cell activation syndrome (MCAS; in Russian САТК, «синдром активации тучных клеток»): what mast cells are and how they degranulate, how normal defensive activation turns into a self-sustaining pathological cycle, the very wide range of symptoms across body systems, the factors that contribute to it (infections, chronic stress, PTSD/cPTSD, early and medical trauma, genetics, hidden infections, toxic load, autoimmunity), and its connections to neurodivergence, hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, POTS, IBS, PMDD and Alzheimer's disease. It also covers treatment options discussed in the channel — antihistamines, quercetin, anti-inflammatory diet, stress and nervous-system regulation, low-dose naltrexone (LDN) and benzodiazepines — with study data and the author's personal LDN story. [#39, #291, #318, #353, #689, #690, #827]

According to the source, it matters because MCAS is more common than assumed, is frequently misread as "psychosomatic", is highly comorbid with neurodivergence, and substantially lowers quality of life; the author's stated goal is to spread information about MCAS and stress the importance of correct diagnosis and treatment «для улучшения качества жизни» (to improve quality of life) and to prevent repeated activations of mast cells. [#39, #291]

### Core ideas

- Mast cells are the body's first responders. They are innate-immune cells living in connective tissue all over the body; when activated they release stored mediators (histamine, cytokines, prostaglandins, proteases, heparin). Detail in *Mast cell activation cycle*. [#291, #695]
- MCAS is defence that has gone out of control. A trigger makes the protective response pathological: mediators are released abnormally and excessively, harmless stimuli are treated as dangers, and the state can become chronic and self-sustaining. [#291]
- Because mast cells are everywhere, MCAS is systemic. Symptoms range from anxiety, depression, brain fog and migraine to skin, gut, heart, reproductive and blood symptoms; see *MCAS symptom map by body system*. [#39, #291, #353]
- Stress and trauma are triggers, not just allergens. Stress releases CRH, which activates mast cells; trauma reshapes the HPA axis and autonomic nervous system, which in turn shape mast cell behaviour. See *Trauma and MCAS model*. [#318, #689]
- Histamine may drive PMDD. Elevated luteal-phase histamine is presented as a contributor to PMDD's mood, pain and cognitive symptoms; see *Histamine–PMDD mechanism model*. [#91, #92, #93, #94]
- Chronic stress + mast cells may feed Alzheimer's pathology via neuroinflammation and beta-amyloid build-up — a risk, not a destiny. See the *vicious circle* framework. [#318]
- Mast-cell-targeted therapies help neuropsychiatric symptoms. In a 553-patient survey, antihistamines, LDN and benzodiazepines were rated most helpful; see *Neuropsychiatric symptoms and treatment effectiveness*. [#690]

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Mast cell activation cycle (normal vs pathological activation)
Purpose: to understand what mast cells normally do and what goes wrong in MCAS. [#291]

1. Where mast cells are. Mast cells («тучные клетки») are a special type of cell of the innate immune system. They are present in the connective tissues of the whole body, especially in tissues of organs close to the external environment: the lungs, the gastrointestinal tract and the skin. The #695 post adds that they are a special kind of white blood cell living in connective tissue, with a key role in immune defence and allergic reactions; #39 notes that connective tissue is found practically everywhere in the body, which is why excess activation shows up in so many different ways. [#291, #695, #39]
2. Their normal functions. They act as the first line of defence against pathogens and other potentially dangerous substances, and thanks to their wide distribution can react quickly to various irritants and initiate an immune response. They take part in: (a) protecting the body from pathogens; (b) allergic reactions; (c) inflammatory processes; (d) wound healing. [#291]
3. Activation and degranulation. On activation mast cells release mediators — histamine, cytokines and prostaglandins — that regulate immune and inflammatory responses. #695 describes the process: on meeting an allergen the cell activates and starts degranulation (дегрануляция), the release of stored chemicals including histamine and heparin; these mediators are what cause redness, itching and swelling. Timelapse microframes (a video shared in #695, not transcribed) show the cell literally "exploding" its contents into the surrounding tissue. [#291, #695]
4. Normal ending. In a healthy body the activation cycle ends once the "danger" has passed. [#291]
5. Pathological activation = MCAS. MCAS is pathological activation of mast cells, characterised by abnormal and excessive release of mediators. It happens when the normal protective response gets out of control due to a particular trigger. As a result the immune system mistakenly perceives even harmless stimuli as dangerous and launches a protective reaction where normally none would happen. [#291]
6. Chronicity. The condition can become chronic and turn into a self-sustaining cycle. [#291]

#### MCAS contributing factors list
The #291 article lists factors that contribute to developing MCAS (six items): [#291]
1. Strong blows to the immune system (viral infections).
2. Chronic stress / PTSD / cPTSD.
3. Genetic factors.
4. Hidden chronic infections.
5. Toxic load.
6. Autoimmune diseases.

The earlier guide (#39) names everyday triggers explaining why MCAS is more common than we think: stress, herpes viruses, infections and toxic load. #689 widens the trigger list beyond allergens to hormones, infections, temperature changes and stress signals from the brain; the post stresses this list is not complete. [#39, #689]

#### MCAS symptom map by body system (15 groups)
Source: #353, "General symptoms of MCAS", based on a PMC review (PMC10672129). The post stresses there are many heterogeneous phenotypes that vary with where the mast cells are located, how many there are, and their ability to degranulate specific mediators. Symptoms can be constant or periodic and of varying severity. The full list as posted: [#353]

1. Systemic: fatigue; subjective feeling of raised and/or lowered body temperature; sweating; appetite change; weight gain/loss; sensitivity to chemicals and physical factors; slow healing. [#353]
2. Skin: urticaria (hives); itch; flushing; haemangiomas with itch/pain; various rashes; telangiectasias; stretch marks; skin papillomas (skin tags); folliculitis; ulcers; eczema; Quincke's oedema (angioedema); alopecia; onychodystrophy (nail dystrophy). [#353]
3. Ophthalmological: irritation / "dryness" of the eyes; difficulty focusing; blepharospasm. [#353]
4. Otological (ear/nose as grouped in the post): tinnitus; hearing loss; runny nose; rhinitis; nasal congestion; nosebleeds. [#353]
5. Oropharyngeal: pain; burning; leukoplakia; ulcers; angioneurotic oedema; dysgeusia (taste disturbance); inflammation/destruction of teeth and/or periodontium. [#353]
6. Lymphatic system: lymphadenopathy; rarely, splenomegaly. [#353]
7. Respiratory system: dry cough; shortness of breath (difficulty taking a deep breath); wheezing; obstructive sleep apnoea. [#353]
8. Cardiovascular system: pre-syncope (feeling faint); hypertension; labile blood pressure; palpitations; oedema; chest pain; Kounis syndrome (allergic angina). [#353]
9. Gastrointestinal tract: dyspepsia; gastro-oesophageal reflux; abdominal pain; nausea; vomiting; diarrhoea and/or constipation; gastroparesis; Quincke's oedema; dysphagia (usually proximal); bloating (after meals or spontaneous); malabsorption. [#353]

#### Trauma and MCAS model (stress signals, HPA axis, autonomic nervous system)
Source: #689, a translation of an Instagram post by @hyperresilient titled "Trauma can provoke mast cell activation syndrome". The model in six steps: [#689]
1. Mast cells respond not only to allergens. Triggers can be hormones, infections, temperature swings and stress signals from the brain. Chronic stress and trauma make mast cells more sensitive over time. [#689]
2. Stress and mast cells are connected. Stress makes the brain release CRH (corticotropin-releasing hormone). This activates mast cells, especially in the skin, gut and brain, causing release of histamine and inflammatory substances. [#689]
3. Trauma can change the nervous system. Early trauma, PTSD or chronic stress can alter the HPA (hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal) axis and the autonomic nervous system; both systems influence mast cell behaviour. [#689]
4. Many people with MCAS report past trauma, including: childhood trauma; medical trauma; chronic stress; complex PTSD. These patterns are often seen both in research and in real life. Caveat from the post: this does not mean trauma is the only cause, but it can be part of the overall picture. [#689]
5. That is why MCAS is often misunderstood. Symptoms can be triggered by "invisible" causes — emotions, memories or internal stress signals — not only allergens or external factors. [#689]
6. MCAS is often complex; recovery may include calming the nervous system, supporting immune regulation, and working with trauma if it is part of one's history. [#689]

#### Stress → CRH → mast cell → neuroinflammation → Alzheimer's vicious circle
Source: #318, the author's post "Chronic stress, PTSD and mast cell activation: potential risk factors for Alzheimer's disease", continuing an earlier post on cPTSD vs PTSD and citing a review in *Frontiers in Cellular Neuroscience* (linked via PMC5733004). [#318]

- Starting point (the review's claim): chronic stress can accelerate Alzheimer's disease (БА) pathogenesis in humans and animal models through increased inflammatory reactions, beta-amyloid accumulation, tau hyperphosphorylation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction and disturbed glucose metabolism. [#318]
- Step 1 — stress activates mast cells. Mast cells play an important role in the stress response: under stress, CRH is released and activates mast cells. [#318]
- Step 2 — mediators damage the barrier. Activated mast cells release pro-inflammatory mediators including histamine, cytokines and proteases. These can disrupt the integrity of the blood–brain barrier and promote neuroinflammation. [#318]
- Step 3 — mast cells start the cascade. Research quoted in the post says mast cells are the first cells to respond to brain injury because they can release pre-stored mediators — indicating a potential role in initiating the inflammatory cascade in brain trauma and stress. [#318]
- Step 4 — the vicious circle. Chronic stress creates a loop: increased mast cell activation → more neuroinflammation → which may promote beta-amyloid accumulation and AD development. [#318]
- Step 5 — microglia fail. Prolonged stress forms a closed cycle in which normal microglial function is disturbed. This lowers the brain's ability to clear harmful substances and increases beta-amyloid (Aβ) accumulation; inflammatory processes in nerve tissue intensify and neuron death speeds up. [#318]
- Conclusion: mast cell activation in brain trauma, stress and PTSD may accelerate the pathogenesis of neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative diseases including AD; stress management and regulation of mast cell activity could become new approaches to preventing and treating Alzheimer's. [#318]
- Caveat and prevention: this does not mean everyone with chronic stress or PTSD/cPTSD will inevitably develop AD — AD depends on many factors including genetics, lifestyle and environment. But the risk exists, and awareness can motivate prevention: stress management, a healthy lifestyle, mast cell stabilisation and cognitive stimulation. The author closes: early prevention and timely treatment can significantly reduce risks and improve quality of life. [#318]

#### Histamine–PMDD mechanism model
Source: the carousel #88–#95, forwarded from the author's "Neurospicy_dushnila" channel, titled «ПМС или ПМДР?» (PMS or PMDD?). [#88]

PMS vs PMDD distinction.
- PMS (premenstrual syndrome) is fairly common; the card says 90% of women experience it «в период менструации» (in the period of menstruation). Typical symptoms: headaches; fatigue; bloating; irritability. [#89]
- PMDD (premenstrual dysphoric disorder) is a serious mental condition in which women experience extreme mood swings and intense symptoms in the week before menstruation starts. Typical symptoms: sharp mood changes; depression; anxiety/tension; anger/rage. The card then asks what the cause might be. [#90]

Histamine as a cause. Modern research, per the card, indicates that elevated histamine may significantly influence PMDD. Histamines are chemicals produced by the immune system in response to allergens, injuries or stress; they play roles in many body functions including regulation of sleep, digestion and mood. In people with PMDD histamine levels tend to be higher in the luteal phase preceding menstruation. [#91]

Mechanisms (three bodily routes): [#92]
1. Inflammatory reactions — histamine, being a pro-inflammatory mediator, can worsen PMDD symptoms such as pain, swelling and irritability.
2. Effect on the hypothalamus — histamine can disturb the hypothalamus, the key regulator of the menstrual cycle, leading to hormonal imbalances.
3. Muscle spasms — high histamine can cause spasms of the uterine smooth muscle, intensifying pain during menstruation.

Brain route: histamines act as neurotransmitters in the brain, affecting mood, alertness and cognitive function. When histamine is elevated it can cause inflammation, especially in the brain; brain inflammation can disrupt neurotransmitter function and contribute to symptoms of depression and anxiety. Histamines can also influence other mood-regulating neurotransmitters such as serotonin and dopamine. [#93]

PMDD symptoms caused by histamine: [#94]
1. Anxiety and irritability — elevated histamine can increase anxiety and irritability during the luteal phase.
2. Mood swings — fluctuating histamine can contribute to sudden mood changes in people with PMDD.
3. Fatigue — histamine-induced brain inflammation can cause fatigue and lethargy.
4. Cognitive symptoms — because of histamine's effect on neurotransmitters, "head fog", poor concentration and memory problems can occur.

#### What to do about histamine-driven PMDD (4 measures)
From the final card (#95), «Что делать?» (What to do?): [#95]
1. Antihistamines may relieve some PMDD symptoms.
2. Histamine-lowering supplements — supplements such as quercetin may help reduce histamine levels.
3. Anti-inflammatory diet — avoid foods high in preservatives, alcohol and sugar; eat foods rich in omega-3 fatty acids and antioxidants.
4. Stress management through mindfulness, physical exercise and relaxation techniques, plus stabilising the nervous system.

Warning on the card (read from the image; the OCR captured only its end, «назначения любых добавок и препаратов»): always consult a doctor about prescribing any supplements and medications. [#95]

#### Neuropsychiatric symptoms and treatment effectiveness in MCAS (553-patient survey)
Source: #690, the channel's translation of an Instagram summary by @drtoddmaderis of a recently published study. [#690]

- Aim: find out how often neuropsychiatric symptoms occur in people with MCAS and which treatments work best. It compared the frequency of neurological and psychiatric symptoms in more than 500 MCAS patients versus healthy people, and examined how therapies commonly used in MCAS — antihistamines, low-dose naltrexone (LDN) and benzodiazepines — affect these symptoms. [#690]
- Method: researchers surveyed 553 MCAS patients and 558 controls with detailed questionnaires on (a) neurological symptoms — fatigue, clouded consciousness, migraines, neuropathy, fainting/pre-fainting, tinnitus, sensitivity to light/sound/smells, seizure states, insomnia, plus further unlisted items; (b) psychiatric symptoms — anxiety, depression, panic attacks, PTSD, OCD, mania/hypomania, psychosis/schizophrenia, hallucinations, plus further unlisted items. MCAS patients also rated how much specific therapies relieved these symptoms. [#690]
- Findings — burden: neuropsychiatric symptom burden was significantly higher in MCAS patients than in controls. Leading neurological symptoms (over 70% of MCAS patients): fatigue; cognitive impairment; neuropathy; migraine-like headaches. Leading psychiatric symptoms (40–66%): anxiety; depression; panic attacks; PTSD. [#690]
- Findings — treatments (patient ratings of relief, 0–10 scale): antihistamines 6.3; LDN 5.6; benzodiazepines 5.6. Neurological: LDN helped 29% with fatigue and cognitive impairment; benzodiazepines helped 51% with insomnia and 29% with restless legs syndrome. Psychiatric: benzodiazepines helped 70% with anxiety, 52% with panic attacks and 51% with agoraphobia; antihistamines and LDN were moderately effective for anxiety and depression. [#690]
- Why it matters (per the summary): it is one of the largest studies to date on neuropsychiatric symptoms in MCAS; the data suggest mast cell dysregulation plays a key role in brain inflammation and psychiatric manifestations; standard psychiatric approaches may not account for the mast cell link; MCAS-targeted therapies, especially antihistamines, LDN and benzodiazepines, may significantly improve quality of life. The study underlines the importance of considering an MCAS diagnosis in patients with unexplained fatigue, cognitive impairment and treatment-resistant psychiatric symptoms. [#690]

### Detailed insights

#### Prevalence, recognition and diagnostic status
- The #291 article opens with a common experience: coming to a doctor with many seemingly unrelated symptoms and complaints and hearing «Это всё психосоматика» ("It's all psychosomatic"). Such stories are often told by people diagnosed with MCAS. [#291]
- The article states MCAS occurs in 17% of the world's population and can show up as chronic disturbances in different body systems. [#291]

#### Personal case: LDN, MCAS and autoimmune thyroiditis (#827)
The author takes LDN to stabilise mast cells because of her MCAS, and reports it also helped with her autoimmune thyroiditis (AIT). [#827]
- 2024: an MRI of the neck showed "something wrong". Standard thyroid tests were normal, but P-TPO (thyroid antibodies) was 38 (upper limit 34), and ultrasound found AIT and a cyst, which was not biopsied (she says she fears such procedures and would probably have refused anyway). The endocrinologist told her simply to wait until the thyroid fails, since they were not going to treat it. [#827]
- End of 2024: she finally obtained medication to stabilise MCAS — LDN was prescribed. She explained to doctors that she did not want to lose her thyroid and, since they could suggest nothing else, wanted to try a treatment suitable both for MCAS and for autoimmune problems. [#827]
- October 2025: P-TPO had fallen to 28. Because the result was within the normal range, no repeat ultrasound was ordered, but she no longer feels such strong problems with the "lump" in her throat. [#827]
- Her conclusion: she sees this as confirming her hypothesis that mast cell stabilisation can reduce thyroid inflammation, and was glad she secured the drug in Norway, as it at least paused the process of thyroid failure. This is a single personal case, not a study. [#827]
