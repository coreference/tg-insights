## Anxiety and panic: CBT and body-based coping

### What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel shared about understanding and coping with panic attacks and anxiety. The core is a forwarded Russian translation of the Oxford Cognitive Therapy Centre booklet "Что такое паника" ("What is panic") by David Westbrook and Claudia Rauf, a complete cognitive-behavioural (CBT) self-help model: what panic is, why the body reacts as it does, why attacks start and recur, and how to prevent, ride out and ultimately dismantle them by changing thoughts and behaviour [#295]. Alongside it are a translated article by psychotherapist Rachel Pfeiffer arguing that anxiety is energy that must first be discharged through activating movement before calming practices can work [#728], and two short body-based pointers: a video on lowering anxiety and body tension [#502] and EFT tapping as a way to cope with anxiety [#533].

Why it matters according to the source: the forwarding post presents the booklet as explaining the nature of panic attacks, their causes, the main misconceptions and the behavioural strategies that start the panic mechanism, with recommendations for coping and for preventing future attacks, and suggests sharing it with loved ones or clients (for psychologists) who suffer from panic attacks [#295]. The booklet itself says its message is "Не отчаивайтесь!" ("Don't despair!"): many people suffer from panic attacks and many overcome them, but this requires serious effort following its advice [#295]. Pfeiffer's article frames anxiety as "не только проблема разума. Это телесный опыт" ("not only a problem of the mind. It is a bodily experience") [#728].

### Core ideas

- Panic is a normal fear response firing at the wrong time. The sensations of panic are an extreme form of the normal, instinctive fight-or-flight reaction; in panic the alarm goes off without real danger, like an oversensitive car alarm. It is unpleasant but not dangerous, and it ends by itself [#295].
- Thoughts are the key driver. The booklet's central claim is that the main factor that can cause a panic attack is one's thoughts — specifically catastrophic misinterpretation of normal anxiety sensations, which feeds a self-amplifying vicious circle [#295].
- What keeps panic going is avoidance, safety behaviours, anticipation and scanning. These keep the sense of danger alive and prevent learning that nothing terrible happens [#295].
- Short-term "first aid" vs long-term work. Relaxation, slow breathing and distraction are first aid that must be practised in calm times; the long-term solution is identifying and challenging panic thoughts and testing them in behavioural experiments [#295].
- Discharge before you calm (Pfeiffer). Forcing yourself to sit still and breathe while anxious is "the worst" thing; anxiety is energy that needs an exit, so activating practices come first and calming practices second [#728].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### What a panic attack is (three characteristics)
The booklet defines panic attacks by three main characteristics [#295]:
1. Strong fear and anxiety accompany them.
2. They arise suddenly.
3. The most intense feelings last a fairly short time — although the attack can feel long, and unpleasant sensations may persist for some time after the crisis has passed.

They are usually accompanied by a feeling that something terrible is about to happen: that you will die soon, go mad, make a fool of yourself, or something similar. Panic usually comes "как «гром среди ясного неба»" ("like a bolt from the blue") — the booklet says attacks seem completely unpredictable and not provoked by anything obvious, although people sometimes notice typical circumstances in which attacks occur. Panic attacks are very common and are not a sign of serious mental illness: roughly 10% of ordinary people have had at least one panic attack in their life, which the booklet equates to 5 million people in the UK [#295].

#### Symptoms of panic: body, thoughts, behaviour
Panic affects the body, thoughts and behaviour. The booklet lists the most common manifestations of each [#295].

Body — most common sensations:
1. Strong heartbeat — sometimes slowed, sometimes accelerated.
2. Heavy, fast breathing (sometimes called hyperventilation).
3. A feeling of not getting enough air.
4. Painful sensations in the chest, head or other parts of the body.
5. A "lump in the throat", a choking feeling.
6. A feeling of needing to go to the toilet.
7. Nausea.
8. Feeling weak, dizzy, or having "jelly legs" («ватных ног», literally "cotton legs").
9. Numbness or tingling, especially in the fingers, toes and lips.
10. Trembling in the body.
11. Sweating and feeling hot.
12. A sense of unreality — as if everything around is unreal or you are detaching from reality.

Caveat from the booklet: these are the most common sensations, but the most characteristic feature of panic is that it can produce completely unpredictable feelings; even without the listed sensations you can still suffer from panic attacks [#295].

Thoughts — most common fears during an attack:
1. I will have a heart attack.
2. I will collapse from exhaustion or faint.
3. I won't have enough air and will suffocate.
4. I won't be able to control my bladder and/or bowels.
5. It will be hard for me to breathe.
6. I will be sick (vomit).
7. I will lose control, go mad and end up in a psychiatric hospital.

Behaviour — most common responses to panic:
1. Escaping to a "safe place" such as home, or staying near people with whom one feels safe — most often people believe nothing will happen to them if they are in a safe place.
2. Avoiding situations in which panic happened before.
3. Taking specific actions that give a feeling of safety: lying down to rest if you fear a heart attack, holding on to something if you fear falling, opening a window if you fear suffocating.
4. Sometimes being so sure of danger that you call an ambulance or go to a doctor.

Booklet summary ("Основные мысли", key points): a panic attack is a sudden feeling of intense fear; it features pronounced unpleasant body symptoms such as heavy heartbeat, laboured breathing and sweating; it is accompanied by the feeling that something terrible will happen; people usually try to get out of the situation they are in or do something to feel safe [#295].

#### Fight-or-flight response: purpose and the 8 changes with their side effects
Purpose: to explain that panic sensations are not signs of illness but signs of a body preparing correctly for danger. The booklet calls the response «бой или полет» (literally "fight or flight") because in danger you either prepare to fight or try to "fly away" from it. The changes happen automatically and very quickly; they help you run or fight but have unpleasant side effects [#295]:
1. Faster breathing — as if taking in more oxygen for the muscles during fighting or running. Side effects: rapid breathing, which can bring dizziness, chest pain and a feeling that the breath is "cramped".
2. Faster heartbeat — so blood circulates faster, supplying the muscles with a surge of strength. Side effect: discomfort from the rapid heartbeat.
3. Blood flows to the leg and arm muscles (most needed for fighting), so the fingers, toes and skin get less blood. Side effects: numbness, tingling and coldness where blood is lacking.
4. Muscles tense to react quickly. Side effects: a feeling of tension that can lead to muscle pain or trembling.
5. Increased sweating, caused by running or fighting, turning into heat that must be shed. Side effect: unpleasant sensations of sweating.
6. Digestion and salivation slow down because their energy is needed for other tasks. Side effects: nausea, dry mouth, heaviness in the stomach.
7. Thoughts focus on the presumed danger to react in time. Side effects: heightened agitation, expecting danger, difficulty concentrating on other things.
8. Pupils dilate to catch maximum light and see danger clearly. Side effect: painful sensitivity to light or other visual stimuli.

Why this matters (the booklet's reasoning): all these changes are necessary when you meet real physical danger; it took millions of years to form them. If someone attacks you, they help you react or run. So the main sensations of panic serve not to show that something is wrong but to show that the body is ready for the correct reaction to danger. Similar reactions occur before important events — an athlete feels an adrenaline surge and these reactions just before a race, which helps him win. Problems arise only when such reactions occur at the wrong time or last longer than needed. In the modern world there is almost no physical danger; threats are mostly psychological (e.g. looking stupid), for which these physical reactions are less useful or useless — but the body does not feel the difference: any threat triggers fight-or-flight whether it is useful or not [#295].

Final note on duration: the danger response can persist for a while but cannot last long. After some time the body switches off the alarm and the reaction fades gradually — automatically, as it arose, but switching off takes longer than switching on. Panic attacks last on average from 5 minutes to an hour [#295]. Key points: fear is a normal automatic reaction needed for survival in real danger; it puts you in combat readiness; when danger disappears, fear gradually fades [#295].

#### Car-alarm (false alarm) model of panic
Purpose: to explain the role of fear in panic. In short, during a panic attack the fight-or-flight defence system fires when there is no real danger, i.e. at the wrong time [#295].
- Car alarms are meant to protect against thieves and should go off only when someone tries to steal the car — the right time, real danger.
- Sometimes alarms are too sensitive and react to passing cars or wind (the booklet adds "и т.п.", "and the like") — the wrong time, no real danger. Being oversensitive, the alarm responds to various signals as if they were dangerous.
- Panic attacks are like an alarm going off at the wrong time.

Lessons the booklet draws: human alarm systems formed over millions of years to protect people; if these reactions did serious harm, humanity would have died out generations ago. Panic is unpleasant but not dangerous. A car alarm going off at the wrong time is annoying but does not break the car; likewise people with panic attacks will not die from a panic attack and will not go mad. The body is built to cope with many demands, including overcoming fear; fear reactions exist to help, not harm. Panic attacks, like fear, are "самоуничтожаемы" ("self-extinguishing"): unpleasant, but after a while panic and fear dissipate by themselves [#295]. Key points: in panic, normal fear reactions fire at the wrong time; panic attacks do not harm you or drive you mad — they are part of a mechanism built to protect you; panic attacks do not last forever [#295].

#### Why panic attacks start: three models
The booklet notes that much more is known about how panic attacks run (chapters on thoughts and recurrence) than about why they start, but offers some models [#295]:
1. Health worry. Some people worry about their health more than others, expecting something terrible to happen to it. This can mean they interpret various bodily processes as frightening and are more likely to fall into the vicious circle of anxiety. For some, the first panic attack comes when they really do have a mild illness such as flu or a stomach ache, which makes them think something more serious will happen to their body.
2. Stress (stress-level vs panic-threshold model). Panic attacks can arise during stressful periods caused by work, relationships or other reasons. The booklet's diagram shows anxiety rising and falling in peaks against a fixed "panic level" line. At some level of anxiety anyone will experience panic. When stress is low, the panic level is unreachable — you simply don't panic. A period of stress shifts your baseline anxiety up to "high stress", so peaks of anxiety that previously were not enough to cause panic now exceed the panic level and can trigger an attack. Another weighty factor: for some people the panic level itself is lower, so even minor stresses can trigger an attack. (The diagram's labels: panic-attack episodes, panic level, high stress level, rising agitation, low stress level.)
3. Difficult feelings. Panic can be linked to difficulties in various areas of life where negative thoughts are hard to deal with. If troubles make you want to suppress feelings or not admit you have them, that does not make the feelings disappear; on the contrary, tension and agitation grow, which can lead to a panic attack. Some sufferers believe their panic occurred when life had difficulties — for example a quarrel with an important person or other relationship problems.

Key points: some people may be more prone to panic than others because of worry about health and safety; panic is often the result of stress or other painful feelings [#295].

#### Vicious circle of catastrophic misinterpretation (the panic cycle)
Purpose: the booklet's central model of how thoughts cause and maintain panic [#295].

The alarm's motto. The body's defensive alarm does not switch off when there might be real danger — it cannot waste precious time carefully checking whether a sabre-toothed tiger is really trying to eat you or you imagined it. It switches on even if you merely *think* real danger exists. Its motto: "Сначала реагируй, проверь это после!" ("React first, check it later!") [#295].

Worked example — the man on the dark street. A man walks home late at night; the street is dark and quiet, and he feels a bit uneasy because the setting is slightly menacing. Bang — a noise! His heart races, he is frightened and feels unsteady. He looks around quickly and sees a cat that accidentally knocked into a tin can — not a dangerous situation at all. Lessons: thoughts can cause strong sensations even without real danger; his alarm fired because he *thought* there was danger — he misinterpreted the cat's noise as the sound of real danger. When he found there was no danger, the panic subsided. Had he seen the cat before the noise, he would have interpreted it correctly and not been frightened; had he kept believing he was in danger, his fear would have grown into a panic attack. His reaction was based entirely on what he thought about the events [#295].

Application to panic. Panic arises in a person who thinks something terrible will happen to them, misinterpreting an ordinary event as a sign of catastrophe. In panic, the misinterpretations are usually not about an attack in the street (though they could be): people notice the body sensations typical of panic and then start thinking these are symptoms of something terrible. This is called catastrophic interpretation. The booklet's table of the most common examples [#295]:
- Rapid heartbeat, chest pain → «Со мной случится сердечный приступ!» ("I'm going to have a heart attack!")
- Difficulty breathing → «Я задохнусь!» ("I'll suffocate!")
- Dizziness, unsteadiness → «Я упаду в обморок!» ("I'll faint!")
- Sense of unreality, absent-mindedness, unusual sensations → «Я сойду с ума!» ("I'll go mad!")

The circle, step by step (from the booklet's diagram and text) [#295]:
1. Trigger (provocation) — anxious thoughts or frightening situations.
2. Danger is perceived.
3. Fear arises.
4. Physical symptoms appear, including rapid breathing.
5. Interpretation — the symptoms are read catastrophically: "Something terrible is going to happen!!!"
6. This raises fear further, producing stronger physical symptoms, which seem to confirm the fear: the booklet words this thought as "The symptoms are getting worse — so something really serious is happening to me!". This causes still more symptoms, and fear spirals up into a severe attack.

Why misinterpretation is the hinge: the booklet stresses that it is precisely the misinterpretation that makes feelings go round the circle, intensifying until panic. The initial symptoms mean agitation and nothing more; without misinterpretation you may feel worried and uncomfortable for a while but you will not panic, because nothing amplifies the feelings [#295].

Breathing's role in the circle. When agitated you breathe faster than the body needs (you are not fighting a sabre-toothed tiger), which further increases agitation. Over-breathing is not dangerous in itself but can produce other sensations such as dizziness and chest pain and so speed up the circle [#295].

Power of thoughts — two answers to "how can a mere thought cause such strong sensations; something must be wrong with my body":
1. Nobody says the sensations are unreal. Your heart really beats faster, you really sweat; these changes are real. The question is what causes them — heart problems or frightening thoughts about panic. The booklet's proposition is that the real cause of the physical sensations is your thoughts.
2. Everyday life shows how powerful thoughts are: a good film brings strong positive emotions, a horror film can frighten you, raise your heart rate and tense your muscles, a sad film can make you cry — although nothing is really happening; you see pictures on a screen and actors performing. Simply because you believe (even for a moment) that it is real, you get strong emotions and body sensations.

A further example: some people, just reading words like "panic attack" or "loss of consciousness" in this booklet, may feel more agitated. Reading cannot harm you, but it can trigger anxious thoughts that in turn cause real physical sensations. (This is why the booklet opens by advising readers who feel anxious while reading to put it down for a while and come back later.) Key points: the main factor that can cause panic is your thoughts; in panic you usually misinterpret physical sensations (especially anxiety symptoms) as a coming catastrophe such as a heart attack or fainting; thoughts can have a huge effect on body sensations [#295].

#### Six triggers of panic attacks
Why triggers seem absent: what starts the first signal (like the noise on the dark street)? Sufferers ask this because attacks often seem to come out of nowhere, which further convinces them something is really wrong. The booklet says triggers may be non-obvious, sometimes genuinely unclear, but careful analysis usually shows a trigger, even if faint. Since the core of the circle is misinterpretation of normal body sensations (especially those accompanying agitation or tension), panic can be triggered by anything that changes body sensations you notice (perhaps unconsciously) and start worrying about. After a first attack, you listen to your body even more closely, so even small changes can set off an attack. Even an unpleasant thought or image can be a trigger, sometimes so fast you don't notice — and because that small tension goes unnoticed consciously, the attack seems to come like a bolt from the blue. The listed triggers [#295]:
1. Even a small rise in agitation or tension for any reason — sometimes caused by constant worry that a panic attack might happen.
2. Other emotional states such as worry or anger, which can cause bodily reactions.
3. Physical exertion that changes heart rate and breathing.
4. Other changes in physical state caused, for example, by premenstrual syndrome, illness, hunger or fatigue.
5. Effects of drugs, including caffeine and alcohol.
6. Loneliness or lack of occupation, and therefore greater focus on one's health.

Rules attached: these factors can trigger attacks but are not dangerous in themselves and should not be avoided (except, of course, excessive alcohol and other drugs). Ignoring triggers is not the best way to solve the problem. While looking for triggers may help, try not to concentrate on it and don't worry if you sometimes can't find them; it is more useful to focus on learning to cope with attacks and understanding that panic attacks are not dangerous even when you don't know what triggers them [#295].

#### Why panic attacks recur: safety behaviours, anticipation, scanning
Some thoughts and actions work against you: they keep up the sense of real danger and so keep the fear going [#295].

A. Avoidance and other safety behaviours. The booklet's term for safety behaviour (поведение-защита) means any action you take to protect yourself from an imagined danger — heart attack, fainting and the like. They are many and hard to spot because they become habitual, but typical ones are [#295]:
1. Avoiding certain places — cramped rooms, supermarkets, hot rooms — and certain actions such as talking to certain people.
2. Going to some places only with people you feel safe with.
3. Always carrying tranquillisers or other pills in your bag "just in case".
4. Keeping complete rest or avoiding physical exertion to protect yourself from a heart attack.
5. Carrying a paper bag in case you are sick.
6. Holding on to something so as not to fall.
7. Opening windows and breathing often and deeply to protect yourself from lack of air.

Why they are harmful long-term: wanting to escape danger is normal, and when you fear something terrible you take steps to prevent it. But because the danger in panic is not real, these steps only make things worse — they stop you realising the danger is imaginary. In the dark-street example, if the man ran away without checking what made the noise, he would be even more convinced a killer tried to attack him; only by staying to see what really happened would he learn there was no danger. Likewise, if you act as though the danger (dying, going mad) is real and do something to prevent it, you never get the chance to learn you are not in danger. Supermarket example: if you've had attacks in supermarkets you may avoid them or hold on to the trolley so as not to fall; then you never discover you would not have fallen without the trolley. Instead of "Great, there's no real danger!" you think "Было так душно! Я бы упал, если бы не держался за тележку!" ("It was so stuffy! I would have fallen if I hadn't held on to the trolley!"). You keep believing the supermarket is dangerous instead of building confidence that you can cope on your own without extra aids. The booklet's illustration contrasts Situation 1, sitting down during panic ("Good thing I sat down — otherwise I'd have fallen!"), with Situation 2, staying standing ("Good thing I didn't fall, even though I thought a panic attack was starting!") [#295].

B. Anticipation (антиципация). Anticipating an attack means you start to feel fear when you get into a situation where you previously had an attack, because you fear another one. Another vicious circle: a previous attack → negative thoughts that another attack may happen → greater sensitivity to such situations → higher likelihood of a repeat attack (diagram: anticipation of an attack → negative thoughts → panic). Anticipation again means you are afraid to see what would happen if you went through the supposedly panic-inducing situation [#295].

C. Scanning (hypervigilance). You become oversensitive to acquiring new fears, especially about normal physical sensations. Everyone sometimes has odd body sensations — something aches, the heart beats unevenly — and these are ordinary and don't mean anything is wrong. But when hypervigilant you unconsciously search the body for these signals. Example: when choosing which car model to buy, suddenly the roads seem full of that model — not because owners started following you, but because the cars became important to you, so you notice them. The same with body sensations: they are always there but not always noticed; if you start monitoring them you will certainly find them; then fear of an attack or something serious appears, which can grow into panic. Like the car alarm triggered by passing cars, you can be oversensitive to signals that are not dangerous and misinterpret them negatively [#295].

Key points: every time you try to escape a panic attack you actually make life harder in the long run. When you flee during panic or take measures to avoid the horror, you (1) don't give yourself the chance to learn nothing serious will happen, (2) keep fearing it will happen again and again, (3) may become completely unsure you can cope and so avoid such situations. Anticipation and body scanning also maintain recurring attacks [#295].

#### Four prevention techniques ("first aid")
Context: understanding alone will not overcome panic. The approaches have helped many people, but it takes time to find which suits you; if nothing works in your situation, seek help from professionals. Because thoughts and beliefs are the key link in the panic model, coping requires dealing with thoughts first; the strategies in this chapter are "first aid", while long-term work on thoughts is needed. Their purpose is to stop panic early, before agitation becomes a full attack; they are especially useful in situations where you are more likely to be anxious or panic. Ideally practise them regularly when you are not anxious, so you know how to respond when panic approaches — the booklet states you will not manage to use these exercises in an emergency if you haven't practised them in calmer conditions [#295].

1. Relaxation. What: techniques that lower physical tension and thus the likelihood of an attack. When: early, before agitation turns into panic. Why: muscle tension is one of the fear symptoms in panic, so try to relax muscles every time you feel agitated. How: relaxation here does not mean the everyday sense of stretching out on the sofa in front of the TV; muscle relaxation is a skill that must be practised to be effective. There are many relaxing-music tapes and relaxation training programmes, many using alternating tensing and relaxing of muscles; yoga can also help. Try some of these to find what works for you. Pitfall: they work if used regularly, not only in moments of anxiety or panic [#295].
2. Controlled (slow) breathing. Why: in anxiety or panic your breathing speeds up to supply more oxygen and ready the body for action; rapid breathing is not dangerous in itself but can lead to more serious panic symptoms such as dizziness. Controlling breathing during panic can weaken the other symptoms and so break the vicious circle. Pitfall: it can be hard at first because of a paradox — the faster you breathe, the more you feel you lack air. Resist the urge to gulp more air and remind yourself to breathe slowly now; because of this paradox the technique works only if practised in advance. How (the booklet's instruction): fill your lungs with air; let your belly go free; you should feel some tightness because your muscles are tense; don't breathe in a restrained way from the upper chest; breathe through mouth and nose; breathe in to a count of "one and, two and, three and, four"; breathe out to "one and, two and, three and, four and, five and, six". Continue breathing like this until you calm down [#295].
3. Distraction (дистракция). What: switching attention away from anxiety-provoking thoughts to something else. It can be effective to focus on what's happening around you instead of on your body — away from your agitation, how your heart beats and how fast you breathe. Why: it helps break the vicious circle at an early stage. How — examples: count all the red objects you can see; repeat the words of a song to yourself; imagine an object such as a flower and think about it — its petals, colour, smell; think about something pleasant, funny or absorbing. Key condition: you must really concentrate on what you are thinking about, no matter what — "даже если это розовый мармеладный слон, который прыгает с облака на облако!" ("even if it's a pink jelly elephant jumping from cloud to cloud!"). Caveat: distraction can work in the short term but does not get rid of anxious thoughts or solve the problem; at some stage you need to fight the thoughts rather than just distract yourself (see *Challenging panic thoughts*) [#295].
4. What else might be bothering you? (solving problems instead of ignoring them). Panic can result from unresolved problems or other painful situations that would be worth resolving regardless of panic. Is something or someone bothering you? The booklet states panic attacks are less likely in people who solve problem situations as they arise [#295].

Key points: the techniques that can help prevent or ease an attack are relaxation, slowed breathing, distraction, and solving problem situations instead of ignoring them; practise them regularly outside anxiety or panic so they can be used to prevent an attack [#295].

#### Eight-point plan for coping during a panic attack
Context: even the best attempts to control panic don't succeed at once; mastering the techniques takes time and attacks may continue meanwhile. The booklet's plan for when attacks still happen [#295]:
1. Use the prevention techniques as early as possible if you have practised them: slow your breathing and try to distract yourself.
2. Paper bag for hyperventilation — if rapid breathing is your most pressing symptom, breathe into a paper bag for a while. The booklet's explanation: hyperventilation causes discomfort by disturbing the body's carbon-dioxide balance (the Russian translation misspells "carbon-dioxide" here); by rebreathing the air you just exhaled you can restore the balance and minimise unpleasant symptoms. How: take a paper (not plastic!) bag, hold it tightly around mouth and nose, breathe in and out slowly into it for some time; you should feel it relieve symptoms fairly quickly. (!) CONTRADICTION: the same booklet lists carrying a paper bag "in case you are sick" among safety behaviours that maintain panic; the bag-breathing advice is given for hyperventilation, a different purpose, and the booklet does not reconcile the two.
3. Remind yourself nothing terrible will happen. Panic is unpleasant, but nothing worse than that will happen. You survived panic last time and will survive it this time.
4. Adopt an attitude of waiting for it to pass. Don't complicate things by imagining how awful panic is; just wait for it to end. It will pass anyway even if you do nothing — like a painful visit to the dentist, you just have to wait until it is over.
5. Don't try to avoid the panic — you will discover it ends even if you make no effort.
6. Remind yourself what is really happening: you are not having a heart attack, not going mad, not losing consciousness; the body's defensive alarm has switched on to protect you. If you can stop "feeding" this system, it will switch off faster.
7. Become an observer. Imagine yourself watching what happens. This can be a unique opportunity to learn more about "your enemy" — the particular form your panic takes — and so be better prepared. Ask: what exactly am I feeling? Which thoughts make it worse? What can I learn from this?
8. Recall your realistic conclusions — if you have started working on anxious thoughts, remind yourself of the more realistic thoughts you arrived at.

(The booklet presents these as one bulleted plan of eight points, numbered here in order.)

#### Challenging panic thoughts (two phases)
Purpose: anxious thoughts about what might happen are the central problem of panic; distraction and other techniques work well as temporary measures, but what is really needed is to change these thoughts. The booklet calls this "борьба с беспокойными мыслями" ("fighting anxious thoughts") and describes two main phases [#295]:
- Phase 1 — formulate the thoughts: what are you afraid will happen to you? (The typical frightening thoughts listed under *Symptoms of panic* are a guide, but they are unique for each person.)
- Phase 2 — judge whether they are realistic or excessively frightening; if the facts are exaggerated, formulate more realistic thoughts.

Step 1: Identifying the thoughts. Either notice the thoughts directly during attacks, or recall a recent attack in full detail, moment by moment: what you felt and, above all, what conclusions you drew. What did you think was happening or about to happen? When you began to feel certain sensations, what did they mean to you? What thoughts ran through your mind? A useful question: «Что, по моему мнению, могло произойти самое ужасное?» ("What, in my view, was the most terrible thing that could happen?"). Pitfall: don't be "sensitive" (i.e. don't soften it) — the aim is to understand what you fear most, not to control the fears. If you most fear a heart attack, record that thought, not a milder version like "I'm afraid of looking stupid". Write the thoughts down rather than just thinking about them, as this is very helpful for identifying them [#295].

Step 2: Challenging the thoughts — the courtroom technique. Having written down the thoughts you believe cause your attacks, step back and reconsider them as if they were being tried in court. A court reaches the truth this way: one side presents all the evidence for the case; the other side presents all the information on its side; the judge or court weighs all the arguments and decides what is really true. You play both sides and the judge [#295]:
1. Find all the evidence that you are going to have a heart attack.
2. Find the evidence that this is not true — that no heart attack will happen and it is just your fear.
3. Weigh the arguments for and against as objectively as you can: what seems most true?

Rules of evidence: evidence must be weighty and fact-based, not mere feelings or intuitions — a court would not count those, so you shouldn't either. Avoid absolutes: almost nothing is certain; none of us can know for sure we won't have a heart attack or hundreds of other physical problems. The goal is not absolute certainty but understanding which outcome is most likely [#295].

Five guiding questions for weighing the evidence [#295]:
1. Does the evidence really support my panic thoughts? Look at your panic symptoms and ask whether they confirm what you'd expect if your fears were true. Core question: «Это больше похоже на опасность или на страх опасности?» ("Is this more like danger or like fear of danger?"). Ways to answer: ask "Are these really heart-attack symptoms or just something similar?" — most serious physical illnesses have symptoms unlike those of panic; do you have such symptoms too, or only panic-typical ones? Have you had medical tests, and do the results support a serious illness or just fear of one? Another way: ask whether what triggers the attack resembles what you fear. Some people start panicking just by thinking about what they fear and later notice the attack recedes when something distracts them (e.g. the phone rings). This shows fear arises from anxious thoughts — more anxious thoughts, more fear; fewer thoughts, less fear. But would you expect a heart attack just because you thought about it, or expect it not to happen because you stopped thinking about it? "Мысли, конечно, имеют определенную силу, но не такую же!" ("Thoughts certainly have a certain power, but not that much!"). Similarly, have you ever heard of someone going mad or having a heart attack just because they walked into a shop, for no other reason? Yet people can fear big shops; in such cases the model suggests the problem is fear and panic, not a real physical (or mental) threat.
2. Typical thinking errors. When frightened we think in standard ways that make things look worse: usually we overestimate the probability of the event and how serious the consequences would be. Example: fearing a panic attack while driving, you may think it very likely though it rarely happens; then you think if it did happen it would be a terrible catastrophe, though you have already experienced less serious consequences of such episodes; and you ignore the option of pulling over to the side of the road and waiting the attack out. So one way to assess evidence is to look at the facts more objectively.
3. What are the chances the feared event actually happens? Count how often you have feared something, then count how many times it actually happened (how many times you really had a heart attack or went mad). What does that tell you — are your thoughts plausible? Case: one man counted about 200 panic attacks driven by fear of dying — and he never died once; he concluded his thoughts were implausible. The booklet's analogy: who would bet on a horse that had never won 200 times in a row? So it is unreasonable to expect these thoughts to come true.
4. How serious would the consequences be if the feared thing did happen? Some things are objectively bad — nobody wants a heart attack or to die. But often what we fear is not as bad as we imagine; frightened, we don't see the real seriousness, and something merely unpleasant seems a catastrophe. Example: how serious would fainting be? What is the worst that could happen? Many people faint — does it ruin their lives? How long has it worried you — a year, five years? If it has happened even once, you know it was nothing terrible: unpleasant, but was it a catastrophe?
5. When you find evidence, write it down. As with identifying thoughts, it's easier to first write the evidence down and then evaluate it; it is easier to stay clear on paper. Later, with more practice, you can do the whole process in your head, but at first write. Practice is necessary; you will have to return to your thoughts again and again to see progress. If you come up with helpful thoughts for solving the problem, write them down too and repeat them during an attack, because if an attack has started and the thoughts are not yet formulated, you are unlikely to formulate them during it. An effective next step is testing the thoughts in practice — behavioural experiments [#295].

#### Useful facts about common fears
The booklet gives three fact sheets to use as evidence when challenging thoughts [#295]:

1. Fainting or dizziness. Some people do faint, but why? Practically, blood pressure drops so far that blood reaches the brain more slowly. People often faint after standing long in the heat, like soldiers on parade: standing means the heart must pump blood upward, and heat draws blood to the face to cool it, so the rest of the body lacks blood; the body then lowers blood pressure around the brain. The body responds by trying to prevent harm — the most obvious sign of fainting is falling, which puts the brain at heart level so the heart no longer has to pump upward; pressure rises, supply is restored and the person gets up with no sign of illness. Fainting is another example of how the body is adapted to protect, not harm, itself. During a panic attack, by contrast, we feel fear and the heart beats harder and faster, so blood pressure rises (the opposite of fainting). Hence it is practically impossible to lose consciousness during panic, because pressure rises rather than falls. Exception: people with blood-injury phobia — a specific group with strong fear at the sight of blood, wounds and surgery. For reasons not fully understood they react differently: their pressure drops when they face their fear, so they may faint at the sight of blood; other techniques are used for them to prevent fainting. If you don't have this specific problem, the chance of fainting during a panic attack is as low as in any other situation.

2. Oxygen and breathing. People who fear suffocating usually avoid small, stuffy or unventilated rooms, thinking there won't be enough air. In fact none of these situations can cause suffocation, because breathing uses a very small amount of oxygen and even a small room holds enough air. Example given: a room 2 sq. m in floor area and 2 m high (a very small room) has a volume of 8,000 litres of air; even sealed with no air going in or out, there is enough oxygen to live for many hours. In everyday life we don't use such cramped rooms, so oxygen arrives in far larger volumes. Room temperature does not affect the amount of oxygen either. Opening a window may feel more comfortable, but there is no real threat of suffocating in a small room. (!) UNCLEAR: by simple arithmetic 2 sq. m × 2 m = 4 cubic metres (4,000 litres), not 8,000; the 8,000 figure would fit a room 2 m × 2 m × 2 m; the source states it as given.

3. The difference between anxiety, panic and mental illness. For those worried about going mad: misunderstandings arise because people mean different things by "mental illness". If one defines it as any problem not purely physical (the Russian translation's wording here is garbled — it reads "связанная чаще с физическим состоянием", *"related more often to a physical state"*), then panic is more a mental disorder, since its nature is not exclusively physical. Don't confuse this with the definition of mental illness as a serious illness like schizophrenia or manic-depressive disorder — from that viewpoint panic is not a mental illness. All psychological disorders can be divided into two types: neuroses (panic, anxiety, most kinds of depression and a number of other common conditions) and psychoses (rarer illnesses such as schizophrenia). One type usually does not turn into the other: people prone to panic usually do not develop disorders of the second type. Analogy: you wouldn't assume a cold will probably turn into cancer; likewise, having panic attacks doesn't mean you will soon become schizophrenic. (!) #### Three-step behavioural experiment
Purpose: changing behaviour also helps overcome panic. Since avoidance and safety behaviours are fertile ground for continuing panic, an effective way to fight panic is to stop avoiding and drop the safety behaviours. Thinking the problem through is very important but may not be enough; the most effective way is to test your thoughts in practice — testing feared situations shows what really happens. Court analogy: if two witnesses disagree on whether the defendant could be seen from a window, instead of arguing endlessly you can run an experiment — put the defendant in the supposed place and look from the window [#295].

Steps [#295]:
1. Decide what to test (perhaps write the thought down). Example: your fear is that if you go to a shop alone you will faint unless you hold on to the trolley. The alternative "anxiety theory" predicts you will feel anxious but will not faint. The aim of the experiment is to establish which is true.
2. Run the experiment. In the example, go to the supermarket alone and don't hold on to the trolley. Ignore how uncomfortable you feel — what matters is to carry out the experiment.
3. Evaluate the results. Did you actually lose consciousness? Perhaps you worried or even panicked, but you didn't faint. The experiment showed there was no real danger of fainting — only anxious thoughts.

How to grade it: best to plan step by step and start with a small experiment. If you fear crowded places, run an experiment in a place a little more crowded than usual. Did something terrible happen — did you die, go mad? If not, go to a more crowded place for the next experiment, continuing step by step. Remember it will cause discomfort, but discomfort is not dangerous. The main purpose is to find out whether what you fear actually happens — do you die, faint, completely lose control? Experimenting on your fears builds confidence. Key points: identifying and fighting panic thoughts is the best long-term way to overcome panic attacks; try not to avoid anxious situations but run behavioural experiments to establish what will happen [#295].

#### Activation-then-calming sequence for anxiety (Rachel Pfeiffer)
Source: a channel translation of an article by psychotherapist Rachel Pfeiffer titled "Я бы никогда не стала пытаться дышать или медитировать, чтобы справиться с тревогой. Вот что я делаю вместо этого" ("I would never try to breathe or meditate to cope with anxiety. Here's what I do instead") [#728].

Thesis: when you feel anxious, the worst thing to do is force yourself to sit still and clear your mind, or tell yourself that it is enough to just calm down with gentle breathing [#728].

Mechanism (Pfeiffer's reasoning): "тревога — это энергия" ("anxiety is energy") — a surge in your nervous system that needs to go somewhere. It is like having one foot on the accelerator and the other on the brake at the same time: the whole body is full of arousal but has no outlet. The result: you feel stuck, restless, overloaded. "Решение — не подавление, а завершение" ("The solution is not suppression but completion") [#728].

Steps:
1. Discharge with activating practices (практики-активаторы). Use practices that channel the excess energy in the nervous system and give it a way out — shaking, dancing, humming or running. Pfeiffer says to think of this as a safety valve for your body [#728].
2. Then calm with calming practices (успокаивающие практики). Once the charge has been released, use slow breathing, meditation or gentle stretching — at that point these practices become effective. They help your system switch into the rest phase rather than trying to impose calm on a body that is still aroused [#728].
3. Differentiate your state. "Важно — различать" ("The important thing is to differentiate"): understand which state you are in and respond accordingly. Anxiety is not only a problem of the mind but a bodily experience; when you give this energy an outlet — up or down — you help your nervous system return to balance [#728].

(!) CONTRADICTION (between sources, not resolved by the channel): the Oxford booklet recommends slow breathing and relaxation as early "first aid" against panic and distraction during an attack [#295], whereas Pfeiffer calls forcing stillness and gentle breathing while anxious "the worst" thing and puts calming practices only after activating discharge [#728]. The booklet also stresses its breathing technique works only if practised in advance in calm conditions, which is partly compatible with Pfeiffer's "calm once discharged" ordering, but neither source addresses the other.

#### Reading about panic can itself trigger anxiety
The booklet warns at the outset that some people find reading about what troubles them can itself be a cause for worry. If you feel anxious while reading, put the booklet aside for a while and come back to it later. Its ideas are based on cognitive-behavioural therapy, and it is meant to help both people in therapy and those who decide to cope with panic on their own [#295].

#### Seeking professional help (UK context)
The booklet's chapter on professional help is written for the UK: if you want professional help and have already seen a mental health specialist, consult your GP ("терапевт") to determine the recommended type of help; for medication you need a psychiatrist; psychological treatments such as CBT can be offered by many mental health professionals; NHS resource shortages may mean a long waiting list; your GP can tell you what is available locally; you can also contact the British Association for Behavioural and Cognitive Psychotherapies (BABCP) directly and use its "find a therapist" function [#295].
