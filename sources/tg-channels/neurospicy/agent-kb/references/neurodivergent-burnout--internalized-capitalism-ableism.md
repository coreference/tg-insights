# Internalized capitalism, ableism and self-worth

## What this covers

This topic collects the channel's material on how values absorbed from a capitalist, ableist society — "you are worth what you produce", "be normal", "don't be a burden" — become internal, shape a neurodivergent person's self-esteem, push them into masking and eventually into adult burnout. It covers Lindsey Mackereth's life-stage model of internalized capitalism in late-identified autistic/ADHD/gifted people, two self-check lists (7 signs of internalized capitalism, 15 signs of internalized ableism), the critique of the "high-functioning" label, the inner critic in autistic burnout, and the alternative the channel proposes: worth independent of productivity, real support instead of forced positivity, and accommodations as something one deserves. According to the source, this matters because these attitudes "разрушать психическое здоровье и чувство собственной идентичности" (destroy mental health and the sense of one's own identity) [msg #615] and lead to burnout "из-за постоянных попыток соответствовать чужим ожиданиям" (because of constant attempts to meet other people's expectations) [msg #448].

## Core ideas

- **Internalized capitalism is a hidden driver of masking and low self-esteem in late-identified neurodivergent people.** Self-worth becomes built on the ability to hide one's traits and to be productive, so it is conditional and collapses into burnout in adulthood. Detailed in the *Life-stage model* below [msg #448, file #448].
- **Internalized ableism is the same mechanism turned on disability**: believing that only "normal" people are fully valuable, and treating one's own needs, symptoms and labels as shameful. Detailed in the *15 signs* [msg #615].
- **"High-functioning" measures usefulness to others, not wellbeing** — it rewards keeping the system running and not causing problems even at the cost of wearing oneself out [msg #687].
- **Self-criticism worsens autistic burnout**: the drive to meet social norms intensifies the inner critic, which deepens isolation and causes still more burnout [msg #23].
- **Forced positivity does not build resilience; support does** [msg #442].
- **Your worth is not defined by productivity or by meeting others' expectations** — you do not need to "contribute to society" to be valuable, and you deserve accommodations [msg #448, #602].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Internalized capitalism in hidden neurocomplexity — life-stage model (Lindsey Mackereth)
Source: a printable PDF by Lindsey Mackereth, translated by Нейропряность, titled "ИНТЕРНАЛИЗИРОВАННЫЙ КАПИТАЛИЗМ В СКРЫТОЙ НЕЙРОКОМПЛЕКСНОСТИ" with the subtitle "Влияние на выявление нейроотличий, маскинга и самооценку" (Internalized capitalism in hidden neurocomplexity: impact on identification of neurodivergence, masking and self-esteem). It traces how a neurodivergence that has not been identified develops through life stages — childhood, adolescence, early adulthood, middle adulthood — showing in parallel what happens to identification, masking and self-esteem. Use it to understand one's own history as a late-identified person and why burnout arrived when it did. (!) UNCLEAR: the PDF is a diagram and the transcription lost its layout, so the exact placement of some boxes on particular stages is reconstructed from reading order; the content of each box is complete [file #448].

**Overarching frame of the diagram.** It depicts the "Прямое и косвенное влияние капиталистических ценностей на человека в капиталистическом или близком к нему обществе" (direct and indirect influence of capitalist values on a person in a capitalist or near-capitalist society) [file #448].

**Stage 1 — Childhood: neurodivergence not yet identified.** The diagram lists why the neurodivergence stays unseen:
1. Less noticeable traits.
2. Denial of differences by the family of origin, because of lack of awareness and/or internalized ableism.
3. Early masking.
4. A general lack of awareness of how autism and/or ADHD present in groups other than white men — for example people assigned female at birth, BIPOC, transgender people, immigrants.
5. Giftedness: academic success or passing tests plus bias can cause autistic and/or ADHD traits to go unnoticed or be ignored.
[file #448]

**Self-esteem in childhood is probably unstable** because of factors tied to neurocomplexity:
1. Lack of validation from peers, family, teachers.
2. A feeling of being different inside, but without conscious understanding of why.
3. Parents who also heavily mask their traits and are themselves unidentified neurodivergent people, with or without internalized ableism.
4. Little or no access to accommodations at home or at school.
5. Social bullying and/or exclusion by peers, siblings or family (explicit or hidden).
Self-esteem may fall because the person internalizes differences in how their brain works as personal flaws and/or innate inferiority. Unconscious and/or conscious masking of neurodivergent traits begins, in order to avoid inner and outer conflicts by imitating neurotypicality [file #448].

**Stage 2 — Adolescence: neurodivergence ignored and/or denied.** Discomfort from masking and/or from an environment not adapted to neurocomplexity leads to a rise in secondary symptoms: anxiety, depression, substance use, eating disorders, financial difficulties from compulsive spending used as a coping strategy. Masking becomes entrenched and identity confusion arises [file #448].

**Stage 3 — Early adulthood: a period of relative "success".** A period begins of relative "success" in some academic or work environment that matches capitalist standards. For the first time in their life the person may experience:
1. Praise from authority figures (teachers, bosses, parents).
2. A sense of achievement and/or respect.
3. A sense of belonging to a community and/or acceptance by peers.
4. Consolidation of the masked version of oneself.
Self-esteem begins to rise thanks to capitalist successes at work or study — but it is based on the learned ability to hide one's traits and meet society's expectations. The person continues to pursue "success" within the capitalist system and builds a lifestyle around a growing income and the identity of a "хорошего работника/члена общества" (good worker / member of society). Self-esteem and self-image form around masking as neurotypical and around contribution to society/capitalism, and become dependent on them. Work and social usefulness turn into driving forces and daily stressors that regularly force the person beyond the limits of their masking capacity and the natural features of their nervous system [file #448].

**How internalized capitalism shows up at this stage:**
1. Excessive workload (overwork).
2. Guilt about rest and leisure.
3. A constant striving for more (can lead to consumerism or status-chasing).
4. Prioritizing personal success over the wellbeing of society.
[file #448]

**Stage 4 — Middle adulthood: late identification and burnout.** ADHD/autism is identified later in life, through professional diagnosis or self-diagnosis. Adult burnout: "Организм и психика требуют паузы после многих лет несоответствия потребностям" (body and psyche demand a pause after many years of their needs not being met). It may show up as:
1. Chronic fatigue.
2. Reduced tolerance to stimuli.
3. Difficulty with everyday tasks that used to come easily.
4. Problems with communication and/or concentration.
5. Social isolation.
6. Chronic pain not explained by illness.
[file #448]

**Way out — understanding and release from internalized capitalism through:**
1. Reclaiming the right to rest.
2. Exploring and rethinking personal values.
3. Developing a sense of community instead of competition.
4. Conscious consumption.
Then: explore and define your self-worth outside the frame of capitalism, taking your neurocomplexity into account; adapt your life to your sensory, social and cognitive needs/preferences; discover what really matters and what brings genuine joy. This may mean choosing a non-standard lifestyle that fits individual needs, which may be seen as "unsuccessful" by capitalist standards. The end point of the diagram is "Истинное соответствие собственной аутентичности и нейрокомплексности" (true alignment with one's own authenticity and neurocomplexity) [file #448].

### Six effects of internalized capitalism on neurodivergent people
From the channel's post introducing Mackereth's material. Definition first: internalized capitalism is the process by which people unconsciously absorb and accept capitalist values and ideology; in a capitalist society people are taught from childhood to value productivity, competition, material success and personal achievement, and these attitudes become deeply rooted in self-perception and daily behaviour — capitalist norms and expectations shape thinking, actions and personal goals. For late-identified neurodivergent people (autistic, ADHD or gifted) it "часто играет коварную роль в формировании стратегий маскировки и развитии самооценки" (often plays an insidious role in forming masking strategies and in the development of self-esteem) [msg #448]. Its effects:
1. It masks neurodivergence, forcing the person to adjust to "normality".
2. It lowers self-esteem because the person does not match imposed standards of success.
3. It leads to burnout through constant attempts to meet other people's expectations.
4. It hinders accepting and loving one's own traits.
5. It harms physical and mental health.
6. It makes developing an authentic personality harder.
[msg #448]

### Six steps to counter internalized capitalism
The post's list of what is important to resist this influence [msg #448]:
1. **Become aware** that internalized capitalism is present in your thinking — recognition is the starting point.
2. **Rethink your values and goals** — check which of them are actually yours rather than imposed standards of success.
3. **Accept your neuro-traits as part of a unique personality** rather than as defects to be hidden.
4. **Find a community of like-minded people** for support (the PDF frames this as community instead of competition).
5. **Work on raising self-esteem outside capitalist standards** — worth not measured by productivity or achievement.
6. **If needed, see a specialist** to work through these issues.
The post closes: "Помните, что ваша ценность не определяется продуктивностью или соответствием чужим ожиданиям. Каждый человек уникален и достоин любви и уважения просто за то, что он есть" (Remember that your worth is not determined by productivity or by meeting other people's expectations. Every person is unique and worthy of love and respect simply for being who they are) [msg #448].

### 15 signs of internalized ableism toward yourself (@soyouredisabled)
Purpose: a self-check, presented as "твой знак (и, возможно, даже знак для окружающих тебя) проверить как у тебя дела" (your sign — and perhaps a sign for those around you — to check how you are doing). The post's premise: "Знание - это уже половина дела" (knowing is already half the battle). Ableism is defined as discrimination and prejudice against people with disabilities, based on the idea that only healthy and "normal" people are valuable and complete; internalized ableism can destroy mental health and the sense of one's own identity [msg #615]. The signs:
1. **Feeling "not disabled enough"** — believing your experience doesn't count because it doesn't match a certain "look" or "idea" of disability.
2. **Feeling discomfort or being triggered when others talk about their experience of disability.**
3. **Using negative expressions about yourself** — not treating yourself kindly or as if you are worthy.
4. **Comparing yourself with other people.**
5. **Feeling relief, pride or joy when people "forget" that you are disabled.**
6. **Hyper-independence.**
7. **Feeling shame or awkwardness about your disability.**
8. **Hiding your symptoms** — because otherwise it seems you won't be taken seriously, or to fit in, or to avoid judgement.
9. **Minimizing or hiding your needs for accommodations and accessibility** — trying to be a "удобным инвалидом" (convenient disabled person) who causes no trouble.
10. **Telling your medical history in too much detail every time you ask for support.**
11. **Downplaying your difficulties and suppressing your needs and emotions.**
12. **Feeling uncomfortable taking up space (attention, room).**
13. **Avoiding labels like "disabled" or "disability" because you fear what others will think.**
14. **Disconnecting from your body.**
15. **Feeling guilty for wanting joy and connection while being disabled.**
[msg #615]

### Three strategies for quieting the inner critic in autistic burnout
Purpose: from the translated article "Самокритика при аутистическом выгорании" (Self-criticism in autistic burnout) — what to do once you notice self-critical thoughts and feelings that keep you in a threat state and block recovery (the background on why autistic people are prone to self-criticism is in *Self-criticism and the inner critic in autistic burnout* below) [article #23].

1. **Assess the reality of the situation ("Оцените реальность ситуации").** What: as soon as you notice self-critical thoughts and feelings, look at what you actually need to do and what the real consequences are of doing or not doing it — as opposed to the catastrophic consequences the critic threatens. Example 1 (teeth): the reality of not brushing your teeth today is not that they will fall out, "не сразу" (not immediately); but you may dislike how your mouth feels and how your breath smells, and you may want good oral hygiene in the long run — so you can decide to brush "из доброты к себе" (out of kindness to yourself), even if you dislike the sensation. Example 2 (school-party cake): the reality of not baking a cake for the school party may be that you won't get positive attention from the school and may not be seen as a pillar of the community — but you'll have more time to rest and recover before the children come home and demand more of your attention. By not baking and having time for yourself, you may actually be better able to care for your children the way you want [article #23].
2. **Try compassion instead of criticism ("Попробуйте проявлять сострадание вместо критики").** What: imagine treating yourself with compassion. The author clarifies that compassion here does not necessarily mean softness: "Я имею в виду мудрость, силу и доброту, учитывая реальность ситуации и ваши потребности" (I mean wisdom, strength and kindness, taking into account the reality of the situation and your needs). How: ask yourself "Что бы я сделал, если бы относился к себе с состраданием?" (What would I do if I treated myself with compassion?). Why: the same behaviour can be experienced completely differently inside. Example: "Я почищу зубы, потому что иначе они будут ужасными и выпадут" (I'll brush my teeth because otherwise they'll be awful and fall out) differs greatly in quality from "Я выбираю почистить зубы, потому что заслуживаю иметь здоровый рот" (I choose to brush my teeth because I deserve a healthy mouth). Likewise baking a cake for the school party can be driven by fear that not helping will make you look like a bad community member — or by loving to bake and wanting to give something to the community [article #23].
3. **Consider meeting your own needs ("Рассмотрите возможность удовлетворения своих потребностей").** What: look at your needs and ask how many you leave unmet at least partly because of self-criticism. Reflection questions: do you stop yourself from stimming, from engaging in special interests or from simply relaxing? How often do you do stressful activities out of fear rather than because they truly need doing? How: start examining where you direct your energy and whether it is really what you need to be doing if you treat yourself with compassion [article #23].

## Detailed insights

### "High-functioning" as a measure of usefulness
Lindsey Mackereth, quoted by the channel: "«Высокофункциональность» — это не о том, как ты себя чувствуешь или насколько хорошо справляешься внутренне. Это о том, насколько ты полезен для других." ("High-functioning" is not about how you feel or how well you cope inside. It is about how useful you are to others.) It is about meeting external expectations, keeping the system working and not creating problems — "даже если для этого приходится идти на износ и превышать свои пределы" (even if that means wearing yourself out and exceeding your limits). This links the functioning label directly to the internalized-capitalism logic of worth-as-usefulness and to the early-adulthood "success" stage of her model [msg #687].

### Self-criticism and the inner critic in autistic burnout
The channel's introduction to a translated article states that self-criticism can hit neurodivergent people especially hard, creating extra obstacles on the way to self-acceptance and to overcoming autistic burnout. The mechanism given: neurodivergence affects how we perceive ourselves and the world; the striving to meet social norms and expectations can intensify self-criticism, which in turn deepens the feeling of isolation and being misunderstood and leads to even more burnout — a self-reinforcing loop. The article covers what influences self-criticism and strategies to "усмирить нашего внутреннего критика" (tame our inner critic) [msg #23, img #23].

The article (translated for the channel by Настя from the English original on dralicenicholls.com, written in the first person by its original author) is addressed to people trying to recover from or prevent autistic burnout. It starts from the point that such people may already have noticed what makes their burnout better or worse — e.g. that masking played a big role, or that they tried to do too much, please too many people and ignore too many of their own needs — and may have taken steps to change these things. "Однако…" (However…): the author has noticed that when autistic people try to recover, there are many obstacles to lowering stress and increasing calming activities [article #23]. These obstacles are of two kinds:
- **External barriers:**
  1. A demanding job.
  2. Demanding friendships.
  3. Roles in the community.
  4. Lack of care.
- **Internal barriers:**
  1. Self-criticism.
  2. High standards.
  3. Shame.
  4. Internalized ableism ("Внутренний эйблизм").

The article focuses on self-criticism as an obstacle to recovery and is structured around three questions: what self-criticism is; why, as an autistic person, you may be more vulnerable to it; and what you can do about it [article #23].

**What self-criticism is.** Defined as "внутренний голос или ощущение, что мы делаем что-то не так, что мы не стараемся достаточно, что мы недостаточно хороши, что мы должны что-то сделать" (an inner voice or feeling that we are doing something wrong, that we aren't trying enough, that we aren't good enough, that we must do something). Often we internalize the voice of a critical parent or teacher — someone who told us we had to do something regardless of how it felt. The inner critic often shows up when you try to rest or do something pleasant: it may call you lazy or useless, or be subtler — "вы должны вместо этого убраться в доме" (you should clean the house instead), "преодолеть" (push through), "попытаться немного сильнее" (try a bit harder). The article invites a check of today: did you tell yourself you "нужно почистить зубы" (need to brush your teeth) or "нужно идти на сложную встречу" (need to go to a difficult meeting), that you "просто нужно взять и сделать" (just need to get on and do it) or "стиснуть зубы" (grit your teeth), or have very critical thoughts like "Я бесполезен" (I'm useless) or "Я недостаточно хорош" (I'm not good enough)? If this sounds familiar, the author stresses it is not your fault: "Пожалуйста, не критикуйте себя за то, что критикуете себя." (Please don't criticize yourself for criticizing yourself.) [article #23]

**Why autistic people are at higher risk of self-criticism.** Self-criticism is a very common human experience, but the article gives several reasons it is heavier for autistic people [article #23]:
1. **More criticism received while growing up.** A neurodivergent person probably faced more critical remarks than neurotypical people: being told not to do the things that made them happy and regulated them, that they looked strange, did things wrong, misread social cues, should be faster, less clumsy and generally more socially acceptable.
2. **More experiences of not fitting in.** More experiences of feeling one is doing something wrong, not feeling accepted, isolation and loneliness — even without open criticism, one felt criticized and rejected.
3. **The evolutionary threat system.** Humans are pack animals, so any sign — however subtle — that we don't fit in is wired to activate our threat systems (fight, flight, freeze, fawn — "борьба, бегство, замирание, угодничество"), because for ancestors moving in hunter-gatherer groups, isolation from the group made us extremely vulnerable.
4. **Masking out of fear of rejection.** Many mask from fear of being rejected or excluded. This fear is real: if we don't behave in certain ways in certain situations we can lose access to valuable resources — work, benefits, medical care, valuable relationships. But many mask constantly even when there is no serious threat to wellbeing, as an after-effect of "cave-person days" when acceptance by the whole tribe was vital; we may also have internalized the mask through years of conditioning.
5. **Threat used as a motivator.** Threat is a powerful motivator, and parents, teachers and peers used it. The article's examples:
   - "Если ты не будешь чистить зубы, они выпадут" (If you don't brush your teeth, they'll fall out)
   - "Если ты не сделаешь домашнее задание, тебя оставят после уроков" (If you don't do your homework, you'll be kept after school)
   - "Если ты не будешь делиться, тогда ты эгоист и никто тебя не полюбит" (If you don't share, you're selfish and nobody will love you)
   - "Если ты не будешь смотреть в глаза, люди подумают, что ты странный" (If you don't make eye contact, people will think you're strange)
   - "Если ты издаешь такие звуки, ты не заведешь друзей" (If you make those noises, you won't make friends)
6. **Threat turned inward.** We may have learned to motivate ourselves with threat too:
   - "Если я не получу 100% на тесте, я буду неудачником" (If I don't get 100% on the test, I'll be a failure)
   - "Если у меня не чистый дом, то я отвратителен" (If my house isn't clean, I'm disgusting)
   - "Если я не буду хорошо смотреть в глаза, я не получу работу" (If I don't make good eye contact, I won't get the job)
   - "Если я не приложу дополнительных усилий, чтобы компенсировать свою странность, меня не полюбят" (If I don't make extra effort to compensate for my strangeness, I won't be loved)

**Mechanism — why this blocks recovery.** Harsh self-criticism keeps us in a "threat" state most of the day; the threat system activates cortisol production and reduces our ability to relax. We hold ourselves to neurotypical standards, find faults in ourselves, and instead of resting and recovering keep acting out of fear — that we are not good enough, that "это не так уж и сложно" (it's not that hard), that we "должны работать усерднее" (should work harder). The article's reflection questions: How much of your behaviour is motivated by threat — fear of consequences if you don't do something? How often do you get the chance to relax or engage in your special interests, only to find you feel very anxious doing it? [article #23]

The channel author then shared her own experience in a "Личный опыт" (personal experience) article; see *Personal case: taming the inner critic by talking to yourself as to a friend* below [msg #33, article #33].

### Personal case: taming the inner critic by talking to yourself as to a friend
While translating the self-criticism article, the channel author (Настя) realized she had to some extent managed to tame her own inner critic — "Теперь он хотя бы не токсичит…" (Now at least it isn't toxic…). She explicitly disclaims expertise ("Не скажу, что я супер мега ниндзя гуру" — I won't say I'm a super mega ninja guru) and invites readers to share their own thoughts and experience [msg #33, article #33].

**Background — how the critic was built.** She lived by the principle of "прыгать выше собственной головы" (jumping higher than one's own head, i.e. overreaching). From childhood many things went badly for her and she always strove to be better — her parents demanded it because it was a matter of status — trying ever harder, especially at work and in relationships. She studied people and how to be a charismatic person so that everyone would like her and she would fit in. Without noticing, she ended up in depression, because "быть нейроотличным человеком, который старается жить по принципам нейротипичного мира не плоходит безследно" [sic] (being a neurodivergent person trying to live by the principles of the neurotypical world doesn't pass without a trace). At 28 she was diagnosed with ADHD and depression; at the time it felt like a sentence, though it explained a lot. It was a shock to learn that not everyone suffers like this and not everyone has to put maximum effort into even the simplest tasks. She fell into "очень темную яму, которую вырыла собственноручно" (a very dark pit that I dug with my own hands) and couldn't climb out because she had only herself [article #33].

**Roots in not being cared for.** It was always easier for her to care for others than for herself; she did not even think of caring for herself and did not know the feeling, because nobody had cared for her since childhood. Her mother called her an ideal child, because she could be left in her cot for many hours without crying, playing alone with shiny spoons. She "just existed", thinking it didn't matter and she'd cope anyway — experience showed she wouldn't [article #33].

**The trigger.** Lying down scrolling TikTok, she came across a video of a man saying we should treat ourselves the way we would treat our closest friend when they need support. She immediately imagined what that could be like and felt "безумно грустно и тепло одновременно" (insanely sad and warm at the same time) [article #33].

**The method as she practised it:**
1. **Replace criticism with a supportive conversation.** When passing a mirror or making mistakes, instead of just criticizing herself she began to talk to herself as if she had to support a friend.
2. **Repeat supportive statements methodically, several times every day.** Her examples: that her weight does not reflect her essence; that failing at something does not make her a failure; that she is simply in a very, very hard period of life, she has only herself, and her task is just to get through it; that she has gained and lost weight more than once and will get back the shape she wants in a healthy way, "а не голодом и измором" (not through starvation and attrition).
3. **Choose one priority task.** She told herself the priority now was to get out of the pit — slowly and without rushing.
4. **Use humour to put things in proportion.** Half-jokingly she explained to herself that if the end of the world came, it wouldn't matter what she weighed or that she couldn't run a successful business because of depression.
5. **Keep going even without believing it.** She did not believe herself at first and did not immediately feel her own care — it was unfamiliar and seemed pointless. She simply decided to keep doing it. With no psychologists, friends or partner to support her at the time, she had to "довериться процессу и будь что будет" (trust the process, come what may).
[article #33]

**Where she is now — the lesson.** She still has autistic burnout; she couldn't avoid it because she didn't have enough knowledge about it. She sometimes has too little energy to do even what she wants; on some days she must choose one thing — e.g. going to the shop or going to the doctor — and that may be the only thing she can do that day. She has severe sensory overloads after which she recovers for hours. What saves her as far as possible (since she can't live in a vacuum without overloads) is self-care. She has accepted "что сейчас так" (that this is how it is now). She hasn't given up: she keeps looking for information on how to help herself, but in small steps, always holding a conversation with herself about what is realistic in her current situation and that she cannot do everything at once. She credits the daily self-talk of that earlier period with helping her accept herself now, and says the video changed her views on demands on herself and self-criticism [article #33].

### Beyond toxic positivity (Adam Grant)
The channel translated a note by Adam Grant ("Beyond toxic positivity", 11 Feb 2024). Its three points: in hard times, imposed positive thinking does not make people stronger — it only denies the reality the person faces ("In hard times, urging people to be positive doesn't boost their resilience. It denies their reality."). People who are suffering need not only moral support but a reliable footing to withstand life's upheavals ("People in pain don't need good vibes only. They need a hand to stay steady through all the vibrations."). Strength does not come from forced smiles; it comes when you feel genuinely supported ("Strength doesn't come from forced smiles. It comes from feeling supported.") [msg #442, img #442].

### Worth independent of contribution; what autistic people deserve
An affirmation translated from Tracy Rodriguez, addressed to autistic friends: you are valuable, important and worthy of support; "Вам не нужно «вносить вклад в общество», чтобы быть ценными" (You don't need to "contribute to society" to be valuable); you are valuable regardless of what you do or don't do; "we are in this together", and here there is support and safety. It names three things autistic people deserve:
1. Sensory accommodations.
2. Flexible ways of communicating.
3. An environment that supports your needs.
This is the direct counter-statement to the "contribution to society/capitalism" basis of self-worth described in Mackereth's model [msg #602].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- (!) UNCLEAR: the Mackereth PDF is a diagram whose layout was lost in transcription; the assignment of some boxes (e.g. the self-esteem boxes and the "non-standard lifestyle" box) to specific life stages is reconstructed from reading order [file #448].
- The channel author frames her inner-critic experience as personal, not expert ("супер мега ниндзя гуру"); it is a single self-report, and she notes self-talk did not prevent her current autistic burnout — it helped her accept herself [msg #33, article #33].
- The self-criticism article's evolutionary explanation (pack animals, hunter-gatherer groups, "cave-person days") and the cortisol mechanism are the original author's claims, given without cited studies [article #23].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the Telegraph page for #23 lists "Настя" as author, while the text is a translation of an article on dralicenicholls.com; the original author is not named in the text beyond the URL [article #23].
- The 15 signs of internalized ableism use the language of disability; the post applies it to the channel's neurodivergent audience without separately discussing whether autism is a disability [msg #615].
