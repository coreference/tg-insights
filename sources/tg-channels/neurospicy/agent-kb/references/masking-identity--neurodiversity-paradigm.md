# What autism is: the neurodiversity paradigm

## What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's foundational material on what autism (and, more broadly, neurodivergence) *is*: a full translated definition of autism by Nick Walker written outside the pathology paradigm, a glossary of the four core neurodiversity terms, five scientific models for thinking about autism and ADHD as spectra, the double empathy problem as a counter-theory to "mind-blindness", and a children's book that flips the usual perspective by describing a neurotypical boy through an autistic child's eyes. The channel frames this as important because how autism is defined decides where responsibility for social failures is placed, who gets support, and whether difference is treated as a deficit or as a variant [msg #65, #75, #76, #397].

## Core ideas

- **Autism is a genetically based neurological variant, not a disorder by scientific fact.** Per Nick Walker, calling autism a disorder is a value judgement; the neurodiversity model treats it as part of natural human biodiversity (see *Nick Walker's definition of autism*) [msg #76].
- **Social difficulties are a by-product, not the core.** Walker argues that autistic social struggles stem from intense, chaotic sensory and cognitive experience that eats attention and energy, plus social rejection — not from inherent "social and communication deficits" [msg #76].
- **Communication breakdown is two-way.** The double empathy problem (Milton, 2012) says NT–autistic misunderstanding is mutual; autistic people communicate and empathise well with other autistic people (see *Double empathy problem*) [msg #75].
- **Autism and ADHD are spectra of many traits with no sharp line between norm and pathology.** Several models compete to describe that spectrum; the practical consequence is to focus support on real needs rather than on whether diagnostic criteria are met (see *Five models of the neurodiversity spectrum*) [msg #396, #397].
- **Perspective-taking reveals that "typical" is also strange from the outside.** The channel author values imagining oneself fully in another's place, which makes any choice look understandable and enables constructive response instead of condemnation (see *Perspective reversal*) [msg #65, #66].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Four requirements for a good introductory text on autism
Nick Walker opens his definition by stating why he wrote it: the world needed a good basic introductory text "What is autism?" that would meet four criteria [msg #76]:
1. **Consistent with current scientific data** — «соответствовал современным научным данным» (*consistent with current scientific data*). The definition must reflect what research currently shows rather than outdated models [msg #76].
2. **Not based on the pathology paradigm** — «не основывался на парадигме патологии» (*not based on the pathology paradigm*). It must not frame autism as a disease or defect to be fixed [msg #76].
3. **Short, simple and accessible** — so that anyone can read and understand it [msg #76].
4. **Formal enough for professional and academic use** — so it can be cited and used by specialists, not only by the community [msg #76].

### Nick Walker's definition of autism (non-pathology paradigm)
Purpose: a concise, citable, scientifically grounded description of autism that does not use deficit language. The channel posted it as a translation of Walker's text from Neuroqueer Heresies. Its components, in the order of the text [msg #76]:

1. **What autism is.** «Аутизм - это генетически обусловленный неврологический вариант человека» (*Autism is a genetically based human neurological variant*). The complex set of interrelated characteristics that distinguish autistic from non-autistic neurology is not yet fully understood, but current data indicate that the main difference is an especially high level of synaptic connectivity and responsiveness of the autistic brain [msg #76].
2. **What this does to experience.** High connectivity tends to make the autistic person's subjective experience more intense and chaotic than that of non-autistic people, on both the sensorimotor and the cognitive level: the autistic mind tends to register more information, and the impact of each bit of information tends to be stronger and less predictable [msg #76].
3. **Autism is developmental.** It begins in the womb and has a pervasive influence on development at many levels throughout life. It produces distinctive, atypical ways of thinking, moving, interacting, and sensory and cognitive processing. A common analogy is that autistic people have a different neurological «операционная система» (*operating system*) from non-autistic people [msg #76].
4. **Prevalence and the rise in diagnoses.** Current estimates put one to two percent of the world's population as autistic. Although the number of people diagnosed has risen steadily over recent decades, the evidence indicates the increase reflects greater public and professional awareness rather than an actual increase in the prevalence of autism [msg #76].
5. **Heterogeneity and disability by context.** Despite shared neurological features, autistic people differ greatly from one another; some show exceptional cognitive talents. But in a society built around the sensory, cognitive, developmental and social needs of non-autistic people, autistic people are almost always disabled («ограничены в возможностях», *limited in their capabilities*) to some degree — sometimes very obviously, sometimes more subtly. In this framing, the limitation arises from the fit between the person and a society designed for others [msg #76].
6. **Why social interaction is hard (the mechanism).** Social interaction is one context in which autistic people tend to struggle consistently. The causal chain Walker gives: (a) an autistic child's sensory experience is more intense and chaotic than a non-autistic child's; (b) the ongoing task of navigating and integrating that experience takes more of the child's attention and energy; (c) so there is less attention and energy left to focus on the subtleties of social interaction; (d) difficulty meeting non-autistic social expectations often leads to social rejection; (e) rejection further compounds social difficulties and impedes social development [msg #76].
7. **The misreading this produces.** Because of that chain, autism is often misconstrued as essentially a set of "social and communication deficits" by people who do not realise that the social challenges are «лишь побочными продуктами интенсивного и хаотичного характера аутистического сенсорного и когнитивного опыта» (*merely by-products of the intense and chaotic nature of autistic sensory and cognitive experience*) [msg #76].
8. **Disorder vs. neurodiversity.** Autism is still widely regarded as a "disorder", but in recent years this view has been challenged by proponents of the neurodiversity model, which holds that autism and other neurocognitive variants are simply part of the natural spectrum of human biodiversity, like variations in ethnicity or sexual orientation — which were also pathologised in the past. Walker's conclusion: «описание аутизма как расстройства представляет собой ценностное суждение, а не научный факт» (*describing autism as a disorder represents a value judgement, not a scientific fact*) [msg #76].

### Core neurodiversity vocabulary
The channel's glossary post ("Let's sort out the terms") defines four terms, giving the Russian and English forms [msg #616]:
1. **Нейротип (Neurotype)** — the way a person's brain works, often matching the person's diagnosis or its absence. Both "autistic" and "neurotypical" are neurotypes; i.e. neurotypical is itself a neurotype, not the absence of one [msg #616].
2. **Нейроразнообразие (Neurodiversity)** — describes a *group* of people with different neurotypes. The word "neurodiverse" can be used for a group that includes autistic people, neurotypical people, people with ADHD and people with РЛ; the author presents these as examples of an open-ended list. So neurodiversity is a property of a group, not of an individual [msg #616].
3. **Нейроотличный (Neurodivergent)** — a person whose neurology or brain structure differs substantially from the norm. The term is usually used for people with developmental and learning disorders [msg #616].
4. **Нейротипичный (Neurotypical)** — a term that arose in the autistic community, referring to a person with typical neurology, someone without developmental or learning disorders. The abbreviation НТ (NT) is used in the channel's other posts [msg #616, #66].

### Five models of the neurodiversity spectrum
Source: a post by Yana ("Dzhumka") shared by the channel, "Neurodiversity: spectrum, trait overlaps and diagnostic problems". Context: the modern world is gradually moving away from treating neurodivergences — especially autism and ADHD — as strictly separate diagnoses and increasingly sees them as **spectra**: complex, multidimensional structures reflecting the diversity of human behaviour and cognition. This highlights the richness of variation but also the difficulty of diagnosing and supporting people at the intersection of normal and pathological traits [msg #397].

**What a "spectrum" means.** It can be understood more "strictly" — including only possible combinations of traits from the approved diagnostic criteria — or more "loosely", allowing that every person on the planet has some neurodivergent traits to some degree, i.e. is «немного аутичен» (*a little autistic*). How exactly to define the spectrum so as to effectively help the neurodivergent people who need it is the main subject of debate among current researchers (psychiatrists and geneticists) [msg #397].

**What is already certain**, per Yana: autism and ADHD should be viewed as variations of many traits, in particular attention problems, sensory sensitivity, impulsivity and difficulties with social interaction. These traits may be barely noticeable or so pronounced that they start to limit daily life. Scientists and neurodiversity advocates stress that **there is no clear boundary between norm and pathology**, which shifts diagnosis and support toward individual needs [msg #397].

The five models proposed so far:
1. **Simple Spectrum (Модель простого спектра).** Autism and ADHD form one continuous continuum. *All* people have traits associated with these conditions, but only in some do they reach a **clinically significant level**. Analogy: like blood pressure or cholesterol — small deviations can be normal, large ones may require intervention [msg #397].
2. **Far End (Модель крайнего выражения).** Autism and ADHD are the extreme points of a shared continuum. A person may be "a little autistic", but only those at the very "edge" of the spectrum receive a diagnosis. The model focuses attention on the most pronounced cases [msg #397].
3. **Network Continua (Модель сети континуумов).** Traits associated with autism and ADHD are treated as **interconnected nodes**. Example: sensory processing problems can amplify attention difficulties, and impulsivity can worsen mood-regulation problems. Each node sits on its own continuum — in one person one trait is stronger, in another person a different one [msg #397].
4. **Complex Spectrum (Модель сложного спектра).** Autism and ADHD are a multidimensional space where traits intersect not only with each other but also with features of other conditions, such as anxiety disorders. This helps account for the complexity of trait interactions and the uniqueness of each person [msg #397].
5. **Emergent Far End (Модель "порога возникновения", "threshold of emergence").** Autism or ADHD manifests only when a certain threshold concentration of traits is reached. Example: if difficulties in sensory processing, attention and social adaptation intensify to a critical mass, a clinical picture forms [msg #397].

**Four themes the post raises**, as listed by the channel when sharing it; the channel describes Yana's post as the result of reading a huge number of studies and articles and singling out the most relevant points [msg #396]:
1. **From categories to a continuum.** The move from strict diagnostic categories to understanding neurodivergence as a spectrum or continuum, which allows a wider range of presentations and individual features to be taken into account rather than forcing people into or out of a box [msg #396].
2. **Overlapping traits.** The complexity and multidimensionality of neurodiversity: different traits can overlap and reinforce each other, creating a unique combination in each person [msg #396].
3. **Norm vs. pathology.** The problem of drawing the line between "norm" and "pathology" in the context of neurodiversity — a line that directly affects who gets diagnosed and how help is provided [msg #396].
4. **Support without a diagnosis.** The need to support people whose features make life difficult even if they do not meet all the criteria for an official diagnosis, focusing on the person's real needs and not only on diagnostic criteria [msg #396].

### Double empathy problem (Damian Milton) versus mind-blindness theory (Simon Baron-Cohen)
This is Part 1 (introduction) of the channel author's own article series, which she says she compiled over a month from many sources with her own translation of the theory [msg #75]. Guiding questions: is it only autistic people who struggle to understand neurotypicals, and can empathy across the spectrum boundary be a *mutual* problem?

1. **The mind-blindness theory.** In the mid-1980s the prominent researcher Simon Baron-Cohen claimed that autistic people have «умственной слепотой» (*mind-blindness*) — a fundamentally impaired theory of mind. Theory of mind is defined as the ability to represent other people's beliefs, emotions, desires and intentions, and to understand and predict their behaviour [msg #75].
2. **Its reach.** The concept is still taught in many psychology textbooks and is supported by more than 75% of the top scholarly articles on theory of mind and autism in Google Scholar. It also guides work in sociology, philosophy, economics, anthropology and robotics. Through this lens Baron-Cohen and followers, until recent years, explained autistic "impairments" in empathy, verbal and non-verbal communication and communication in general — even though the very concept of autism, its components and diagnostic criteria changed enormously over those 40 years [msg #75].
3. **Autistic pushback.** Since the 1990s autistic activists such as Jim Sinclair argued that their ways of communicating do not mesh easily with neurotypical ones [msg #75].
4. **Milton's theory (2012).** Autistic researcher Damian Milton published the essay "On the ontological status of autism: the 'double empathy problem'", proposing that communication breakdowns between NT and autistic people are a two-sided problem — a difficulty of *mutual* understanding («взаимо_понимания») [msg #75].
5. **What later research showed.** This became a turning point. Subsequent work showed that most autistic people fit into groups without problems, communicate effectively, show social reciprocity, and empathise sensitively and accurately — with other autistic people. Understanding and empathising was difficult only between members of different groups (on and off the spectrum) [msg #75].
6. **Implications.** These findings shake the usual foundation and remove the *sole* responsibility for unsuccessful social contact from autistic people. They also echo the neurodiversity principle that autistic people generally have a different approach to information at every stage — gathering, processing, storing and exchanging it [msg #75].
7. **Insider grounding.** Milton stresses that the theory corresponds to «аутистической феноменологии, полученной из инсайдерских источников» (*autistic phenomenology obtained from insider sources*) — it reflects the personal experience and perception of autistic people [msg #75].
8. **Status of the evidence.** Scientific support is growing but not yet stable, and as of 2024 the leading classifications, ICD and DSM, still follow the earlier reference points in their criteria. M. Lerner (Stony Brook University) is quoted: «Не все исследователи настроены на это новое направление» (*Not all researchers are on board with this new direction*) and the double empathy problem is, empirically, a younger theory [msg #75].

### Perspective reversal: "Why Johnny Doesn't Flap: NT is OK!"
Purpose: a perspective-taking exercise. The channel author says she likes the idea of relativity — how much one's view can change if one imagines oneself as precisely as possible in another person's place, with their experience, ideas, values and thinking algorithms. She believes this approach makes any choice or action of that person appear understandable and justified, and thus lets one do something constructive with it instead of blindly condemning it or considering it "bad", "unpredictably dangerous" or "alien" («чужаком») [msg #65].

The tool for this is the 2015 children's book by Clay and Gail Morton (illustrated by Alex Merry), whose title card reads "Why Johnny Doesn't Flap: NT is OK!". The author notes that some sketches may be dated but most still seem valuable, and she shared a Russian translation she found online [msg #65, img #65].

The book is narrated by an autistic child describing his neurotypical friend Johnny. Mum explains that Johnny is NT — he doesn't have autism, so his brain works differently — and that this is fine. Each vignette describes ordinary neurotypical behaviour as an autistic person might perceive it, ending with the refrain "and that's okay" [msg #66]:
1. **Punctuality.** Johnny is due at 4:00 but sometimes arrives at 3:58 or 4:03. The narrator gave him a watch for his birthday, yet "he still has this problem"; despite his "terrible punctuality" they have fun [msg #66].
2. **Order of activities.** Sometimes Johnny wants checkers then cards, sometimes cards then checkers — «Он не умеет сохранять одинаковый порядок каждый раз» (*He can't keep the same order every time*) [msg #66].
3. **Eye contact.** Johnny looks you straight in the eyes when talking, which can be very uncomfortable — but he doesn't do it to spite you, he's just like that [msg #66].
4. **Scripting.** Johnny watches the same cartoons but never repeats the intro word for word («никогда не повторят [sic] вступление дословно»); the narrator isn't sure Johnny even memorised it — "he pays attention to strange things" [msg #66].
5. **Expressing joy.** When something wonderful happens, Johnny doesn't flap his hands or jump; he just stretches the corners of his mouth and opens his eyes a little wider. "Maybe he doesn't know how to show emotions" [msg #66].
6. **Special interests.** Johnny has no topic he knows everything about (World War II, dinosaurs, trams); he seems uninterested in detailed information the narrator shares, and is unlikely to become an expert in anything — but he's a good guy [msg #66].
7. **Uneven knowledge.** Johnny functions well at school and follows rules, but asked simple facts — which company made the lift, the computer or the cameras — he stares blankly. He understands some things well and struggles with others; "but that's true of everyone" [msg #66].
8. **Emotional reactions.** Johnny never has a meltdown when something terrible happens, like a fire alarm or a cancelled art lesson. He is afraid of what others will think; the narrator thinks he locks his feelings inside, "but that's just how he copes" [msg #66].
9. **Literal language.** Johnny "has communication problems": he says the maths test was «проще пареной репы» (*simpler than a steamed turnip* — an idiom for "very easy") when he means it was easy, and says «Учитель метал гром и молнии» (*The teacher was throwing thunder and lightning*). The narrator tries to explain that turnips have nothing to do with a maths test, but Johnny doesn't seem to understand that one should say what one means; eventually the narrator decodes his strange phrases [msg #66].
10. **Constant socialising.** At recess Johnny always wants to play with other kids and never withdraws into his own world; the narrator wonders whether he ever has time to sit and think about his favourite advert or metro announcement — "maybe he's too fixated on socialising" [msg #66].
11. **Conclusion.** Being friends with an NT child is interesting; he has many quirks that can be annoying until you get used to them. Mum says everyone's brain is built differently and these differences are not bad. «Я думаю, что быть НТ – это нормально» (*I think being NT is okay*) [msg #66].

The lesson: every trait that clinical language describes as an autistic deficit (lack of eye contact, literal speech, rigid routines, intense interests, flapping) has a mirror image; from an autistic baseline, neurotypical norms (approximate timing, idioms, constant socialising, muted joy, suppressing distress for others' opinion) look equally odd — differences, not defects [msg #65, #66].

## Detailed insights

### "Everyone has ADHD/autism now"
The channel shared a meme (via the "Autistic translations" channel, by @elliemidds) that flips a common complaint. The crossed-out line «У всех нынче СДВГ и/или аутизм.» (*Everyone has ADHD and/or autism nowadays*) is replaced by «Как мы могли подвести так много людей?» (*How could we have failed so many people?*); the image reads "Oh my god. Everyone has ADHD/autism now. How did we let so many people down?" The point: the growing number of diagnoses is reframed not as a fad but as evidence of how many people previously went unrecognised and unsupported — consistent with Walker's claim that rising diagnoses reflect awareness, not true prevalence growth (see *Nick Walker's definition of autism*, component 4) [msg #333, img #333].
