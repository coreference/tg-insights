# Autistic social communication and processing

## What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel Нейропряность shared (mostly forwarded from Нейр∞тлично and translated from the autistic author neurodivergent_lou) about how autistic people experience, process and conduct social interaction: the hidden days of processing after any interaction, delayed emotional processing, voice volume and tone, auditory processing difficulties, ready-made self-advocacy scripts, and the myth that autism is the opposite of empathy. It also includes a bot for checking whether messages sound toxic, a quote on honesty that is direct yet kind, and a quote distinguishing limerence from healthy love. The source's stated reason this matters: much of the cost of socialising for autistic people is invisible to others ("Это часть жизни аутистов, которую люди не видят" — "this is a part of autistic life people do not see"), so autistic people are misread as rude, bored, stubborn or unempathetic when they are actually processing differently [msg #411, #454, #742, #285].

## Core ideas

- **Socialising continues after it ends.** For an autistic person each social interaction is said to require 3–5 days of processing afterwards; masking during it adds a need for days of rest without expectations (see *Post-interaction processing*) [msg #410, #411, #413].
- **Emotions may be processed with a delay** of days to years, so an autistic person may feel an emotion without knowing its cause or even which emotion it is [msg #416].
- **Voice differences are misread.** Volume regulation, unwritten volume rules, monotone or unusual stress, and over-imitation while masking can be read as boredom, disinterest, rudeness or sarcasm; autistic people attend more to words, neurotypicals more to tone (see *Autism and tone of voice*) [msg #451–#457].
- **Auditory processing difficulties** show in six recognisable ways, from saying "What?" to buy time to misheard words (see *Six signs*) [msg #738–#744].
- **Self-advocacy can be scripted.** Sixteen ready phrases let an autistic person ask for processing time, written information, exit signals, email instead of calls, and explain stimming, eye contact, burnout and unmasking [msg #627–#635].
- **"Autism is the antonym of empathy" is a harmful generalisation.** Empathy has at least three types — cognitive, affective and compassionate — and people mean very different things by the word [msg #285].
- **Honesty does not require cruelty**, and **healthy love does not consume your functioning** the way limerence does [msg #802, #723].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Post-interaction processing in autism (neurodivergent_lou)
Purpose: to explain to non-autistic people (and to autistic readers themselves) why socialising costs far more than the visible event. The carousel opens with the headline claim "Для аутичного человека каждое социальное взаимодействие требует обработки в течение 3-5 дней..." ("For an autistic person every social interaction requires processing over 3–5 days…") [msg #410]. Its components, in the order of the cards:

1. **Socialising = the event + the following days of processing.** The author believes people do not realise that for autistic people social interaction includes not only the actual social part but also processing of the interaction in the following days. This is a part of autistic life others do not see; if you socialise, you are forced to deal with the days of processing that follow [msg #411].
2. **"Looking fine" hides real difficulty.** Autistic people may seem to be coping normally while internally experiencing real difficulties. Everyday actions such as communication take an "incredible" amount of energy, and little time may remain for other things because so much time goes into making sense of what happens every day [msg #412].
3. **Masking during interaction and its recovery cost.** The author constantly monitors how she behaves in social interactions, compares her behaviour with others' and imitates them. Autistic masking can be completely exhausting; after masking for a long time she needs several days of "отдохнуть без ожиданий" ("rest without expectations") — defined as a state in which you are not required to perform any tasks, be productive or meet any standards. Otherwise she risks driving herself into burnout [msg #413].
4. **Overthinking and re-reading what was said.** After communicating she tends to think too much and re-interpret what she said; sometimes it simply occurs to her that something she said 5 days ago could actually have been misinterpreted. Her explanation: autistic people communicate differently from the majority from childhood and are often misunderstood, which creates negative experiences — so the re-checking is learned from a history of being misread [msg #414].
5. **Learning not to trust one's own perceptions.** She also believes autistic people learn not to trust their own sensations, including sensations about how they communicate, which can make them truly doubt themselves and how they behave in society [msg #415].
6. **Delayed emotional processing.** Autistic people sometimes have a delay in processing emotions: they may not process emotions right after socialising, and it can take several days, weeks, months or even years to realise and process what they feel in response to certain situations. It can show up as feeling an emotion without knowing what caused it, or not even knowing exactly which emotion it is [msg #416].

### Autism and tone of voice (neurodivergent_lou)
Purpose: to explain voice-related differences that cause misunderstandings in both directions. The carousel is titled "Аутизм и тон голоса" ("Autism and tone of voice") [msg #451]. Components:

1. **Regulating volume is hard.** Autistic people sometimes find it hard to regulate their voice volume — it can be difficult to tell how loudly they are speaking and therefore to control it. Sensory overload is a second cause: when ambient sounds feel amplified, they may speak louder to drown out the sensory noise around them [msg #452].
2. **Unwritten volume rules are hard to read.** Likewise, social rules about voice volume in particular situations can be hard to understand. Her example: there is an unspoken rule that you must not speak loudly in a library, but if you are playing football you are expected to shout — she adds the hedge "по крайней мере, так мне кажется" ("at least, that is how it seems to me") [msg #453].
3. **Autistic tone is misread by others.** Because an autistic person's tone differs from what others are used to — for example monotone or stress on particular sounds — it is perceived as boredom, disinterest or even rudeness. Autistic people may pay more attention to what exactly is said than to the tone it is said in, whereas neurotypical people may, conversely, give more weight to tone than to the words themselves [msg #454].
4. **Masking by imitating speech can backfire.** As part of masking, an autistic person may try to imitate the speech of non-autistic people, having realised that their autistic traits do not help them get what they need. For the author this sometimes made her voice excessively expressive and sound sarcastic, because she tried too hard to imitate other people's manner of speaking — "Получается обратный эффект." ("The opposite effect results.") [msg #455].
5. **Neurotypical tone is hard to interpret.** Autistic people can find it hard to interpret neurotypical people's tone of voice; tone may contain hidden social hints that are difficult to catch. They often need such cues to be expressed clearly and directly [msg #456].
6. **Special interests change the voice.** When talking about her interests, she usually starts speaking fast with emphasis on many words, and her pitch goes higher and lower because she is experiencing joy from her special interest [msg #457].

### Six signs of auditory processing difficulties (neurodivergent_lou)
Purpose: to list "Варианты проявления трудностей с обработкой аудиальной информации..." ("ways auditory processing difficulties can show up…") so that behaviours are not misread [msg #738].

1. **Saying "What?" even when you heard exactly what was said.** Processing what she hears takes her quite a long time, so to give herself more time she sometimes says "что?" even when she did hear; this buys time to process and think before answering. Pitfall: she sometimes confuses people by answering before they have finished repeating — she simply needed more time [msg #739].
2. **Using subtitles for TV or video.** She relies on subtitles to take in information more easily and not miss anything, because she reads faster than she processes sound. Subtitles can also serve as visual reinforcement of what is heard [msg #740].
3. **Difficulty with phone calls.** Autistic people can find phone calls hard; one possible reason is auditory processing difficulty. On a call you rely on sound alone, without, for example, the lip-reading available face to face, which makes taking in information harder [msg #741].
4. **Difficulty with verbal instructions.** People with auditory processing problems may struggle to follow directions and instructions given orally — for example directions for getting somewhere, or oral instructions at school or work. This complicates everyday life. Others sometimes assume the person is deliberately making trouble or being stubborn, when in fact they are struggling to process the instructions [msg #742].
5. **Difficulty holding a conversation in noisy places.** In noisy places it is especially hard to take in what others say. Autistic people may find it hard to tell whether this comes from autism-typical sensory overload or from auditory processing problems that prevent perceiving speech in a noisy environment [msg #743].
6. **Frequently mishearing words or sounds.** Words or sounds may be misheard, especially similar-sounding ones: it can seem one word was said when it was actually a completely different similar-sounding word, which can make communication confusing [msg #744].

### Self-advocacy scripts for autistic people (neurodivergent_lou)
Purpose: phrases the author relies on as an autistic person ("Скрипты самоадвокации на которые я полагаюсь как аутичный человек…" — "self-advocacy scripts I rely on as an autistic person…") to state needs and pre-empt misreadings [msg #627]. Each script is given with the need it addresses (the scripts are short; the "why" is the need they name):

1. **Asking for processing time:** "Sometimes it's hard for me to process information; could you give me time to think about it?" [msg #628]
2. **Explaining headphones and stim toys:** "I rely on my headphones and stim toys as disability aids. I'm still listening. These aids help me take in information more easily." — "Я все еще слушаю." ("I'm still listening.") pre-empts the assumption that using them means inattention [msg #628].
3. **Naming worry about what to say:** "I usually worry about what to say to strangers, especially in new situations. What should I say to that person?" — asking for a prompt rather than freezing [msg #629].
4. **Checking emotions directly:** "It's hard for me to interpret body language and facial expressions — are you upset?" [msg #629]
5. **Asking for an end time:** "Could you tell me roughly when this will end? It's not because I don't want to spend time with you; it just helps me prepare." [msg #630]
6. **Agreeing on an exit signal:** "Can we agree on a sign or word I can use so you know I'm overwhelmed and need to leave the situation?" [msg #630]
7. **Clarifying what kind of support is wanted:** "Do you want me to give you advice, or would you prefer I just listen to what you want to say?" [msg #631]
8. **Disclosing burnout and limited energy:** "Right now I'm experiencing autistic burnout and my energy is very limited." [msg #631]
9. **Pre-arranging meltdown help:** "If I have a meltdown, could you help me move to a quiet place, find noise-cancelling headphones, and not touch me?" [msg #632]
10. **Explaining unmasking:** "I'm experimenting with unmasking my autistic traits. This means I may look more autistic, because I no longer suppress my traits." [msg #632]
11. **Protecting routine:** "I rely on routine that helps me cope and stay calm, even if my routine isn't quite clear to you. Please don't interrupt my activities." [msg #633]
12. **Flagging a high pain threshold:** "I have a high pain threshold, which means I may not realise that I'm in pain or that something is wrong." [msg #633]
13. **Asking for written information:** "It's hard for me to remember information — could you write it down for me, or let me write it down?" [msg #634]
14. **Explaining lack of eye contact:** "I don't make eye contact because it is hard for me. I'm not being rude, and we can still communicate as usual." [msg #634]
15. **Asking for orientation in a new situation:** "I'm struggling in a new situation. Could you tell me what is happening and what I should do?" [msg #635]
16. **Asking for email instead of calls:** "I have trouble with phone calls because I can't understand what is being said to me; could we communicate by email?" (this links to sign 3 of auditory processing) [msg #635]

The carousel has 8 cards with two phrases each, i.e. 16 scripts in total [msg #628, #629, #630, #631, #632, #633, #634, #635].

### Three types of empathy (Aleksei Sannikov)
Purpose: psychologist Алексей Санников argues against common generalisations — that people with BPD (borderline personality disorder) lack empathy, that "РАС это антоним эмпатии" ("ASD is the antonym of empathy"), and that empathy means feeling the same as the other person. His opinion: "Я считаю, что даже на бытовом уровне, делать таких обобщений не стоит." ("I believe that even at an everyday level such generalisations should not be made.") His reasoning: people put completely different meanings into the word "empathy", and when asked to clarify they get flustered and cannot always give a clear definition of what they mean in that specific case — while the term is multifaceted. Etymology: from Greek ἐν "in" + πάθος "passion, suffering, compassion, feeling, sympathy". He quotes the Wikipedia definition: "Осознанное сопереживание текущему эмоциональному состоянию другого человека без потери ощущения происхождения этого переживания." ("Conscious co-experiencing of another person's current emotional state without losing the sense of the origin of that experience."); an empath is a person with a developed capacity for empathy. Empathy is currently divided into several types [msg #285]:

1. **Rational / intellectual (cognitive) empathy.** The other's state is primarily analysed and studied: we compare the person's behaviour with their actions and with knowledge of how people act when experiencing a given emotion. It rests on one's own experience of similar feelings; facial expressions, gestures, gaze and voice matter greatly for determining what the other is feeling [msg #285].
2. **Emotional / affective empathy.** Based on the mechanism of psychological contagion: a person feels another's emotions but cannot (or not always) name them. Such people, he says, have a special nervous system and can experience others' pain as their own personal tragedy — unsurprisingly, they experience significant emotional and nervous overload. He invites the reader to notice the "huge difference" between types 1 and 2 [msg #285].
3. **Compassionate empathy (empathic concern).** The wish to help and support a person. A curious effect: at that moment there may be no understanding of what is happening with them, nor co-feeling; yet the heaviness of the other's emotion can feel painful to someone with this form developed, and it prompts them to help [msg #285].

### Compassionate-empathy offer of help (Aleksei Sannikov)
What to do when compassionate empathy makes you want to help [msg #285]:
1. **Offer solutions or ask directly** — "Чем тебе помочь?" ("How can I help you?").
2. **If the other person cannot answer, ask yourself:** "Что помогло мне в подобной ситуации?" ("What helped me in a similar situation?") or "Что могло бы мне помочь?" ("What could have helped me?").
3. **Turn the answers into concrete offers**, for example: (a) I can just be next to you; (b) I can listen to you; (c) I can do something (e.g. give an umbrella in a downpour). The source ends the list with "... и т.п." ("…and so on"), i.e. the options are open-ended [msg #285].

## Detailed insights

### Checking whether a message sounds toxic
A forwarded post recommends a bot that helps determine how toxic messages are — both ones you have received and ones you want to send; the forwarding channel calls it "Очень полезная штука" ("a very useful thing") and thanks its creators. It exists in Telegram (@autentik_toximeter_bot) and WhatsApp [msg #260]. Example output shown in the screenshots, for a message (apparently to a partner) expressing disappointment:
- Detected characteristics: emotional pressure; imposing expectations. Result: "Может быть ТОКСИЧНЫМ" ("May be TOXIC") [img #260].
- Assessment: the text is an emotional statement that may be perceived as toxic depending on the relationship context. The author expresses disappointment and a sense of rejection, which is not toxic in itself, but the way it is expressed may create emotional pressure on the partner [img #260].
- Continuation: the author seems to impose their expectations and ideas about how the relationship should develop without fully taking into account the partner's needs and boundaries. Mentioning features related to autism spectrum disorder (РАС) and the partner's stress shows some understanding of the situation, but the overall tone may be perceived as pressure [img #261].
The lesson carried by the example: the bot separates the legitimacy of the feeling (disappointment is not toxic) from the delivery (pressure, imposed expectations) [img #260, #261].

### Honest versus brutal
A Tumblr quote by ryan-sometimes (translated by Anne Green Moon, forwarded from Нейропанк under the tags "neuropunk reminders" / "cripplepunk reminders"): the author hates people who call themselves "предельно честными" ("brutally honest") when in reality they are just mean. You do not need to be cruel to be honest; you can deliver the truth "прямым способом, но при этом по-доброму" ("in a direct way, yet kindly"). If you use the truth only to hurt other people, that is not sincerity — you are just behaving like jerks [msg #802]. In context of this topic, it complements the autistic preference for clear, direct communication (see *Autism and tone of voice*, component 5) with the point that directness and kindness are compatible.

### Limerence versus healthy love
The channel posted a quote by Lindsey Mackereth (from her Substack article "Why You Can't Stop Thinking About Them (And It's Not Love"): limerence often disrupts sleep, nutrition, work and hobbies because your brain is looping on this person; healthy love coexists with self-care and life balance and does not consume your functioning [msg #723]. The usable test: if attachment to someone is eating your sleep, eating, work and hobbies, the quote classifies it as limerence rather than healthy love [msg #723].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The "3–5 days" figure for processing each social interaction is a claim from an autistic author's personal post, not a study; elsewhere in the same carousel she says emotional processing can take days to years, so the 3–5 days is not an upper bound [msg #410, #416].
- Most of the material is first-person (neurodivergent_lou) and hedged ("иногда", "может", "мне кажется" — "sometimes", "may", "it seems to me"); it describes possibilities, not universal autistic traits [msg #453, #454].
- (!) UNCLEAR: Sannikov's claim that people with affective empathy "have a special nervous system" is stated without evidence or explanation [msg #285].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the image in #285 has no text detected; the empathy post's list of help offers ends with "и т.п." ("and so on") in the source itself [msg #285].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the toxicity-bot screenshots show only the analysis, not the original message that was analysed [img #260, #261].
- Noisy-place difficulty may be sensory overload or auditory processing — the source says autistic people may be unable to tell which [msg #743].
