# Sensory processing, overload and sensory regulation

## What this covers

This topic collects the channel's material on sensory overload in adults with ADHD and autism: what overload is and the eight sensory systems; over- and under-reactivity; the signs and symptoms of overload in adults; ten less visible ways sensory differences affect daily functioning in ADHD; eleven practical strategies for adults; a guide to rethinking sensory problems (grieving and acknowledging limits, building a sensory profile, a sensory-friendly environment, tools, sensory rest and self-care); the definitions of sensory regulation, dysregulation and self-regulation; the channel's central thesis that sensory regulation is the foundation on which emotional, behavioural and executive-function regulation rest; sensory-friendly activities; a first-person account of overload being mistaken for panic attacks; a comparison of stimming in ADHD and autism; and a short note on visual snow. Two of the core texts are translations (by Настя and Рина) of longreads by clinical psychologist Dr. Megan Anna Neff [article #25, #41]. According to the source, sensory overload "может значительно оказывать влияние на нашу жизнь, доставляя дискомфорт и вызывая постоянные циклы выгорания" (*can significantly affect our life, causing discomfort and triggering constant cycles of burnout*), so addressing it can significantly improve quality of life and help break the endless burnout cycle [msg #41].

## Core ideas

- **Overload = more input than the senses can process.** It varies from mild to severe depending on the person and the environment, and it looks different in each person (see *What sensory overload is*) [article #25, #41].
- **Sensory overload is common in both ADHD and autism, but the stated cause differs.** In ADHD the presentation names difficulty *processing* sensory information (the ADHD brain struggles to block certain inputs); in autism, difficulty *regulating* sensory input (see *Sensory overload in ADHD vs autism*) [msg #171, #172] [article #25].
- **Sensory differences reach far beyond "loud noise is unpleasant".** They affect time management, motor planning, organisation, communication, focus, transitions, emotion regulation and sleep (see *10 hidden ways*) [article #25].
- **Overload cannot be fully avoided — but it can be managed and lived with more harmoniously.** The adult path is acknowledgement (including grief), learning one's sensory profile, and deliberate accommodations plus scheduled sensory rest (see *Rethinking sensory problems in adults*) [article #25, #41].
- **Sensory regulation is the foundation of self-regulation.** When the sensory system is out of balance, emotion regulation, focus and attention, control of actions and behaviour, and executive functions all suffer (see *Sensory regulation as the foundation of self-regulation*) [msg #46, #174, #175].
- **Sensory regulation should be prioritised.** Traditional approaches that target emotions or behaviour directly are, in the channel's presentation, ineffective when sensory dysregulation is present (see *Prioritising sensory regulation*) [msg #182, #183].
- **Sensory overload is stress, and it can masquerade as panic.** The presentation states "Сенсорная перегрузка = стресс" (*sensory overload = stress*) [msg #184]; a translated first-person post describes years of believing one had panic attacks that were in fact overload (see *Overload mistaken for panic attacks*) [msg #638].
- **Stimming is regulation and communication, not bad behaviour.** In ADHD it relates more to attention and releasing energy; in autism to self-regulation and sensory balance; both deserve respect (see *ADHD vs autism stimming comparison*) [msg #733] [article #25].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### What sensory overload is
Definition (both Neff articles): sensory overload happens when our sensory systems are clogged with more incoming information than they can process effectively. People with sensory processing differences are more prone to it [article #25, #41].

**The restaurant example.** Entering a restaurant, we land in a busy place full of people's chatter, the clink of dishes and a mix of smells, all against flickering lights and playing music. The brain faces an excessive flood of sensory data, which causes discomfort and stress. The intensity ranges from mild to severe depending on the individual and the environment; even one loud sound or bright light can tip the scales, leaving us stressed and uncomfortable — from noisy restaurants to crowded grocery stores [article #25, #41].

**It shows up differently in each person.** Some feel heightened sensitivity and are overwhelmed even by weak stimuli; others become irritable or agitated in crowded places. The ability to concentrate can drop sharply under a sensory flood, which hampers decision-making and organisational skills [article #25, #41].

**Common triggers** listed in the ADHD article: crowded spaces; noisy environments; scratchy or rough fabrics; too much cognitive information at once; bright lights; strong smells [article #25].

**Where it can lead.** When we are in noisy places, competing stimuli can pile up into sensory over-strain and lead to a so-called "сенсорному кризису или сенсорной блокаде" (*sensory crisis or sensory shutdown*). Overload can affect anyone occasionally but is more characteristic of people with ADHD (the ADHD article) and becomes a frequent phenomenon for adults with ADHD and autism (the adult guide); continuous sensory bombardment makes ordinary tasks exhausting, difficult and frightening [article #25, #41]. Shutdowns and crises look different in each person, so it is important to be attuned to our own specific signs in order to know how to intervene and help ourselves [article #25, #41].

### The eight sensory systems ("five senses plus three hidden")
The ADHD article lists eight main sensory systems (an image card titled *the eight sensory systems* shows: touch, vision, taste, hearing, smell, interoception, vestibular apparatus, proprioception) [article #25]:
1. **Visual system** — the eyes; perceives and interprets light and colour; lets us see and recognise objects, shapes and movement.
2. **Auditory system** — the ears; perceives and interprets sounds; lets us hear and distinguish pitch, loudness and timbre.
3. **Olfactory system** — the nose; perceives and interprets smells; lets us detect and recognise different aromas.
4. **Gustatory system** — taste receptors on the tongue; lets us distinguish sweet, sour, bitter and salty.
5. **Tactile system** — the skin; perceives touch and pressure; lets us feel textures, temperatures and levels of pressure applied to the body.
6. **Vestibular system** — the inner ear; perceives body position, balance and movement in space; supports balance and coordination.
7. **Proprioceptive system** — receptors in muscles, tendons and joints; perceives body position, movement and force; tells us where our body parts are relative to each other and how much effort an action needs.
8. **Interoceptive system** — receptors in the internal organs; perceives internal sensations such as hunger, thirst, pain, temperature and emotions; lets us recognise and respond to physiological needs and emotional states.
[article #25]

### Sensory over-reactivity vs under-reactivity
Many adults with ADHD have differences in how the brain perceives sensory information; with too much input this can lead to overload. The article's explanation: the ADHD brain processes sensory information differently and often has difficulty blocking certain sensory inputs, which produces atypical sensory profiles [article #25].
- **Over-reactivity** (чрезмерная сенсорная реактивность) — heightened perception of sensory information. The author is over-reactive in smell, hearing and touch: smells others barely notice can give her an instant migraine. She attributes the intensity of her olfactory sensitivity more to her autism than to ADHD, but notes such experiences also occur in people with ADHD. Overload is common for people with hypersensitive systems [article #25].
- **Under-reactivity** (сенсорная недостаточная реактивность) — the brain needs more signal to register a sensory input. Such people tend to seek more intense sensations and need more input to become aware of their sensory surroundings [article #25].

### 10 signs of sensory overload in adults (ADHD article)
Offered for readers wondering whether they have sensory sensitivity [article #25]:
1. Heightened sensitivity to certain sounds — loud noises, background noise or specific frequencies; this makes it hard to focus because of excessive load and trouble filtering out background input.
2. Discomfort or unease in bright or flickering light.
3. Difficulty focusing or concentrating when many stimuli or distractions are present.
4. Feeling overwhelmed or irritated in crowded or noisy places; anxiety or stress can lead to an extreme reaction called a sensory crisis.
5. Avoiding or disliking certain textures or fabrics in clothing or touch.
6. Irritation or unease from certain smells or sharp scents (perfume, cleaning products, specific foods).
7. Physical discomfort or mild over-strain from certain tastes or food textures.
8. Constant exhaustion or fatigue from continuously processing sensory information; fatigue can compound, because overload can lead to insomnia and restless sleep.
9. Increased irritability, restlessness, agitation or anxiety in an overloaded sensory environment; emotional reactions range from irritation and frustration to emotional exhaustion or feeling crushed.
10. Physical symptoms — headache, fatigue, muscle tension, dizziness or nausea — in response to sensory-rich environments.
[article #25]

An image card in the same article ("Признаки сенсорной перегрузки" — *signs of sensory overload*, Dr. Neff) gives six felt signs: (1) feeling as if a wave of fatigue washes over you; (2) a feeling of tension and the need to stim; (3) restlessness and discomfort; (4) a feeling of panic and the desire to escape; (5) strong irritability; (6) the need to block incoming sensory signals (covering ears, closing eyes) [article #25].

Why ADHD and overload go together, per the article: with ADHD it does not take much to trigger overload, which leads to overwhelm, restlessness or mild over-arousal. Being overloaded adds a layer of stress to an already strained sensory perception and to executive-function problems — "Сенсорное переполнение усиливает нашу существующую проблему" (*sensory overflow amplifies our existing problem*). The constant barrage can easily overload an already heightened system, causing trouble concentrating, increased distractibility and restlessness. In adults with ADHD overload can show as irritability, anxiety and a rapidly arriving sense of overwhelm in sensory-rich settings [article #25].

### 10 hidden ways sensory differences affect adults with ADHD
Purpose: obvious signs (irritation at loud sounds, certain textures) are noticed, but sensory experience touches almost every aspect of daily life — from getting up to fetch a glass of water to commuting or talking with a friend — and its intersection with task switching, organisation and changes of routine often goes unnoticed [article #25]. The ten ways:

1. **Environmental sensitivity and difficulty filtering sensory information.** Being highly sensitive, loud sounds, bright light or strong smells easily over-stimulate us, which makes everyday activities hard — moving through noisy grocery stores, classrooms with various smells, crowded spaces. It also makes it harder to *start* tasks in stimulating environments. Task switching, already hard in ADHD, gets harder when the transition involves unpleasant sensations. The author's example: switching out of the shower is especially hard for her — the sensory pleasure of warm water abruptly gives way to cold air, with a row of getting-ready tasks ahead, which can feel overwhelming. Filtering difficulties also raise distractibility: adults with ADHD find it hard to filter out irrelevant stimuli, which can prevent entering a flow state and can make focusing in conversations hard, leading to misunderstandings or difficulty taking full part in discussions [article #25].
2. **Sensory aspects of time management.** "Time blindness" in ADHD is well known, but the role of sensory processing in it is less recognised. During overload the brain's ability to measure time accurately can be disrupted — overwhelming input pulls attention away and distorts the perception of time passing, making schedules hard to keep. Overload can also push us to seek sensory stimulation or sensory-rich activities as a coping mechanism; hyperfocusing on something like videos or video games, we lose track of time and may neglect other duties [article #25].
3. **Motor planning.** Planning movements in space — sequencing steps, coordinating balance and steps — relies on several sensory systems, mainly proprioception and the vestibular sense. Difficulties there make motor planning harder: even moving a computer to another spot, or picking up two glasses and walking to the sink to fill a cup, can become complicated. Executive-function problems additionally complicate breaking tasks into steps; the two overlap and interact so that planning even simple motor actions can feel daunting [article #25].
4. **Seeking sensory stimulation (stimming).** To regulate input we often look for opportunities to stim — hand manipulation, tapping, constant movement. Stimming provides repetitive input that is under our control. People know adults with ADHD fidget a lot but often don't understand why: besides releasing pent-up energy, we regulate our sensory input by stimming and by deliberately creating input through fidgeting. The problem: where stillness is expected (e.g. work meetings) it is harder to stay regulated — exactly when we are trying hardest to keep focus and a little stimming would help. The translators add: "может быть скрытый стимминг, например, повтор песни про себя, покусывание щеки изнутри, ерзанье языком по зубам" (*there can be hidden stimming, e.g. replaying a song to yourself, biting the inside of the cheek, running the tongue over the teeth*) [article #25].
5. **Organisation and planning problems.** ADHD already makes organising and planning hard; overload further complicates decisions, planning and organisation, adding cognitive distraction. Example: trying to make important decisions in a noisy or chaotic setting makes clear thinking and objective weighing of options hard, which can lead to emotional overload and to indecision or, in some cases, impulsivity. When organising information (planning tasks, setting goals), overload can disrupt logical structuring of thoughts, breaking complex tasks into steps and prioritising [article #25].
6. **Sensitivity in communication.** Communication problems are a less known aspect of ADHD: we get distracted in conversations and may miss subtle social cues, which can cause misunderstandings in relationships. Trouble filtering background noise or being pulled away by visual or audio stimuli makes it harder to follow the conversation or hear everything. People unfamiliar with ADHD may misread this as disrespect or lack of interest, adding obstacles to social interaction [article #25].
7. **Maintaining focus and attention.** We already struggle with regulating attention; overload makes it much harder to keep mental focus during studying, reading or meetings, as the constant flow of impressions causes frequent distractions [article #25].
8. **Transitions and changes.** When already overloaded, adapting to new conditions or routines that involve different stimuli can be overwhelming. New smells, textures, sounds or routines add sensory load, so we may feel more overloaded during periods of change and need more time to adapt and find balance [article #25].
9. **Emotion regulation.** Emotion dysregulation is often a core ADHD symptom (the article attributes it to differences in brain connectivity). Overload amplifies emotional reactions and makes regulating feelings harder. Moreover, the sensory side of calming techniques can be difficult: deep breathing can be hard because interoception problems make it hard to perceive one's breath — so we may need alternative strategies that fit our sensory needs [article #25].
10. **Sleep and relaxation problems.** People with ADHD often sleep poorly for several reasons — flattened melatonin curves, circadian rhythm disruption, sleep apnoea (the article adds that there are further reasons without naming them). Sensory over-stimulation from noise or light adds to difficulty relaxing and preparing for sleep; after a day of overload it is hard to discharge accumulated energy and fall asleep. Strategies for managing input and a calming evening routine that helps regulate the nervous system are presented as a substantial help for sleep quality [article #25].

An "at a glance" summary card (Dr. Megan Anna Neff) restates the common sensory difficulties among adults with ADHD in compact form: sensitivity to stimuli (loud sounds, bright light, sharp smells) easily leading to over-stimulation and emotional dysregulation; sensory seeking such as fidgeting, tapping or constant movement to regulate input; difficulty with transitions and changes; difficulty with organisation and planning (overload during decisions or organising information); difficulty filtering non-essential information, causing distractibility; sensitivity to sensory aspects of communication (background noise or visual stimuli during conversations); sleep and/or relaxation problems from over-stimulation or sensitivity to noise or light; difficulty sustaining concentration in tasks requiring continuous mental effort such as studying, reading or meetings [article #25].

### 11 strategies for managing sensory overload in adulthood
Framing: managing sensory problems as an adult with ADHD brings unique difficulties and opportunities compared with children; it involves a mindful approach, awareness of one's sensory limits and consciously taking action to create sensory safety. "Сенсорную перегрузку нельзя полностью избежать" (*sensory overload cannot be fully avoided*), but strategies can help manage it and reduce its impact [article #25]:
1. **Identify triggers.** Learn which environments, situations or stimuli tend to overload you; knowing your specific triggers lets you build strategies to manage them. The article suggests starting by going through a list of sensory triggers.
2. **Plan ahead.** When possible, prepare for potentially overloading situations — e.g. earplugs or noise-cancelling headphones in crowded places, or having a quiet place to retreat to.
3. **Take breaks.** Take regular breaks through the day to rest and recover; the author likes to call these breaks "сенсорным детоксом" (*sensory detox*).
4. **Use sensory accommodations.** Use tools or techniques that reduce input or help regulate perception — weighted blankets, sensory toys, noise-cancelling headphones. Look up or create a list of sensory tools for ideas.
5. **Practise relaxation techniques** such as deep breathing exercises or other exercises that lower nervous-system activity and help calm it.
6. **Set boundaries.** Communicate your needs and limits to others, set boundaries and ask for support when needed; understanding your sensory limits and creating boundaries that respect them lets you take better care of yourself.
7. **Create a sensory-pleasant environment** — minimise clutter, use soft lighting, listen to calming music; consider starting by identifying your sensory preferences.
8. **Use sensory diets.** A sensory diet — the author prefers the term "план сенсорной безопасности" (*sensory safety plan*) — is an individual plan that includes specific sensory activities through the day to regulate and balance sensory input.
9. **ADHD coaching.** Executive-function problems, ADHD symptoms and sensory-processing differences interact in complex ways: "Когда мы перегружены когнитивно, это усиливает сенсорную перегрузку!" (*when we are cognitively overloaded, it amplifies sensory overload!*). An ADHD coach can help build systems and strategies for ADHD-related overload, which also helps with sensory-processing problems.
10. **Play with sensations.** Play — sensory toys or experiments with new sensory experiences (kinetic sand, fidgets). Satisfying sensory experiences you find can become powerful tools for managing sensory experiences.
11. **Consider an occupational therapist specialising in sensory integration.** An OT may recommend sensory integration therapy to improve how the brain processes sensory signals, help you explore strategies for your sensory profile, and give information on treating sensory overload and sensory processing disorder.
Finding what works may take trial and error, so play and be patient. "Обучение управлению нашей сенсорной регуляцией играет ключевую роль в регуляции наших эмоций!" (*Learning to manage our sensory regulation plays a key role in regulating our emotions!*) The conclusion: managing sensory problems in adults is a path of self-knowledge and purposeful action; by acknowledging our sensory difficulties and limits we can face overload with more resilience and autonomy [article #25].

### Symptoms of sensory overload in adults (adult guide)
The guide (by Neff, who describes herself as an autistic person with ADHD — AuDHDer) says symptoms vary greatly and depend on individual sensory profiles. Seven general symptoms [article #41]:
1. Feeling overwhelmed, anxious or stressed in sensory-rich environments; difficulty focusing, concentrating or sustaining attention because of excessive input in the foreground of attention and difficulty filtering background input.
2. Physical manifestations — headaches, fatigue, muscle tension or heightened sensitivity to stimuli.
3. Emotional reactions ranging from irritability and frustration to emotional exhaustion or feeling crushed.
4. Coping mechanisms such as avoiding social interaction, seeking solitude or repetitive behaviour for self-regulation (stimming).
5. Insomnia and restless sleep.
6. Feeling restless, irritable and having more difficulty managing emotions.
7. Shutting down — mild dissociation and a feeling of fogginess or numbness.
Since overload shows differently in everyone, it is important to know your own specific signs to know how to intervene [article #41].

### 11 signs of sensory overload in adults (adult guide)
General symptoms give an overview, but studying your *own* signs is "vital" for managing overload. Eleven specific signs to watch for — this list ties each sign to its everyday consequences [article #41]:
1. Heightened sensitivity to sounds, especially loud or sudden noises typical of home or workplaces.
2. Discomfort in brightly lit or fluorescent-lit environments such as offices or shops.
3. Difficulty concentrating in settings with multiple stimuli, affecting work performance or daily tasks.
4. Feeling overwhelmed in crowded places — public transport, shopping centres, social events.
5. Aversion to certain textures or fabrics, complicating the choice of clothing or bedding.
6. Distress from strong or pervasive smells, affecting the ability to be in certain public places or use common household products.
7. Physical discomfort or aversion to certain food textures and tastes, complicating cooking and visiting restaurants.
8. Constant fatigue from processing excess sensory information, draining energy for personal and professional commitments.
9. Heightened irritability or anxiety in overloaded environments, affecting relationships and social interactions.
10. Physical symptoms such as headaches or nausea in sensory-rich environments, together with difficulties such as finding the demands of raising small children or managing adult responsibilities hard because of sensory sensitivities.
11. Difficulties with aspects of intimacy and sex because of sensory aversions.
[article #41]

### Rethinking sensory problems in adults: the guide's path to sensory safety
Purpose: a sequence for adults (especially those diagnosed late) to move from struggling through overload to a life with less constant overload and fewer burnout cycles. The guide's three themes are the signs and consequences of overload in adults with ADHD and autistic adults, how to manage sensory problems effectively, and how to develop sensory safety [article #41].

**Step 1 — Recognise what the discovery means and meet it with kindness.** Realising as an adult that you have a sensory processing disorder can be a revelation: discovering that not everyone experiences the world as an intense sensory assault brings a new level of understanding and a whirl of emotions — surprise, embarrassment and mourning for years spent struggling without this knowledge. The author says that once she accepted her sensitivities, the world's loudness and overload hit her harder, bringing sadness, disappointment and even despair. Adults face challenges different from children's; managing them requires a mindful approach, acknowledging sensory limits and deliberate steps toward a sensory-friendly environment. It is "very important" to accept our sensory experiences with kindness and patience; acknowledging how sensory challenges shaped our lives lets us take active measures to protect our wellbeing and opens the door to self-affirmation and resilience [article #41].

**Step 2 — Shift perspective: from control to living with it.** To the common question of how to take control of sensory problems, the author offers a nuanced view: "хотя полностью контролировать эти проблемы может быть невозможно, мы определенно можем научиться жить с ними более гармонично" (*although fully controlling these problems may be impossible, we can definitely learn to live with them more harmoniously*). Acceptance is key: it lets us embrace our sensitivities and find joy in experiences we used to avoid. Feeling well involves continuous self-awareness and measures tailored to our needs — examples: a quiet sensory-friendly space at home, noise-cancelling headphones in noisy settings, unwinding at the end of a sensory-rich day under a weighted blanket [article #41].

**Step 3 — Acknowledge your sensory limits (and grieve).** For the late-diagnosed, acknowledging the emotions tied to sensory difficulties is an important step toward self-awareness and kindness. Many learned to push past their limits to meet the world's demands; but acknowledging and respecting sensory boundaries is crucial for daily wellbeing and for preventing the endless burnout cycle. The author's story: her grief over what sensory problems disrupted — missed joys at family gatherings, school events and holidays — was deep; realising that moments she wanted to remember were clouded by sensitivities was painful, and understanding that not everyone lives these events in overload brought both clarity and sadness. Yet this acknowledgement paved the way to setting necessary boundaries; the process began with mourning the reality of her limits, and only through that grief could she truly understand and advocate for her needs, finding comfort in quieter, sensory-pleasant settings. The lesson: acknowledging limits — and the grief that may come with it — enables self-compassion and a more effective defence of one's own interests; it is the first step to meaningful change. "Это баланс между оплакиванием того, чего быть не может, и принятием стратегий, которые позволяют нам более комфортно ориентироваться в нашем мире." (*It is a balance between mourning what cannot be and adopting strategies that let us navigate our world more comfortably.*) [article #41]

**Step 4 — Learn your sensory profile** (see *Sensory profile* below). Once limits are acknowledged and emotions processed, the first step to sensory safety is deeply understanding your sensory profile — developing a "sensory perspective" to identify preferences, triggers and soothers — which lays the foundation for a personalised sensory safety plan [article #41].

**Step 5 — Integrate sensory accommodations into your life.** With a clearer profile, bring sensory-friendly elements into personal and professional spaces and use effective tools and strategies — described as an emancipating act that makes environments harmonise with your preferences, triggers and soothers and improves overall quality of life [article #41]:
- *Create a sensory-friendly environment*: think carefully about your preferences and triggers; adjust lighting to reduce glare, reduce background noise for auditory comfort, tidy and declutter for visual calm, introduce comforting textures and scents; possibly assemble a sensory soothing kit. Tailoring the environment creates a "sensory refuge" that matches your profile and becomes a place of shelter and comfort.
- *Use sensory tools and strategies* (see *Four sensory tools and strategies*).

**Step 6 — Make time for sensory rest.** Sensory rest gives the sensory system a chance to rest and recover and is the basis for wellbeing and avoiding overload traps. Deliberately taking breaks from over-stimulating environments, activities or inputs lets the nervous system recalibrate and restore balance; this pause is vital so the sensory-processing mechanisms can reset and stabilise. The author calls these periods "сенсорной детоксикации" (*sensory detox*) and says integrating them into the daily routine is not just useful but necessary — scheduled moments of calm protect against the continuous cycle of sensory burnout [article #41].

**Step 7 — Build self-care and relaxation practices.** Activities that calm and lower nervous-system activity regulate input and reduce stress — deep breathing exercises, gentle stretching, soothing baths, vagus-nerve stimulators such as Sensate, quiet creative projects. Caveat: "традиционные методы самоухода, такие как медитация и осознанность, могут не всегда найти отклик у всех, особенно у тех из нас, кто страдает СДВГ" (*traditional self-care methods such as meditation and mindfulness may not resonate with everyone, especially those of us with ADHD*), so explore self-care that actually works for you even if it departs from traditional ideas of what self-care looks like [article #41].

Conclusion of the guide: managing adult sensory problems is a complex process of self-exploration, self-compassion and purposeful steps; recognising overload, identifying triggers and comforters, integrating accommodations and ensuring regular sensory rest create a sensory-friendly living environment and strengthen self-regulation and resilience [article #41].

### Sensory profile: preferences, triggers, soothers
Why it is harder than it looks: people who spent years wading through overload may have become disconnected from their bodies, or the constant stream of impressions may have made it hard to tell what is genuinely pleasant from what overloads [article #41]. Three components, in order:
1. **Sensory preferences** — take time to explore which sensory experiences bring comfort, joy or calm; by tuning into your senses and noticing what feels pleasant or soothing, identify specific sights, sounds, textures, tastes and smells that resonate with you; this is the foundation for a supportive sensory environment.
2. **Sensory triggers** — pinpoint the specific experiences that cause discomfort, stress or overload. Method: observe the moments when you feel most disorganised or overloaded and look for patterns and types of stimuli (sights, sounds, smells, textures, other inputs). Knowing triggers lets you manage environments and approach potentially overwhelming situations with a new sense of control and confidence.
3. **Sensory soothers** — "Сенсорные успокоители - это деятельность, стратегии и ресурсы, которые возвращают нас к равновесию, когда мы чувствуем себя перегруженными или дезорганизованными." (*Sensory soothers are the activities, strategies and resources that bring us back to balance when we feel overloaded or disorganised.*) They differ subtly from preferences: they are the go-to inputs for recalibrating and finding your centre again. Examples: rhythmic movement, calming melodies, deep-pressure touch, retreating into a calm comfortable space. Knowing them lets you weave them into routines deliberately.
[article #41]

### Four sensory tools and strategies
From the adult guide, to add to daily rituals alongside a sensory-friendly environment [article #41]:
1. **Noise-cancelling headphones** — reduce distractions, allowing more focus and calm in noisy conditions.
2. **Fidget toys or stress balls** — provide tactile stimulation that can redirect nervous energy and improve concentration.
3. **Deep breathing exercises or mindfulness techniques** — key for regulating emotions and reducing stress, helping modulate sensory perception (compare the caveat in Step 7 and the interoception caveat in *10 hidden ways*, item 9).
4. **Physical exercise or repetitive movement** — walking, rocking or using a weighted blanket provide proprioceptive input that helps ground and calm the body.
[article #41]

### What sensory regulation is and what it looks like
Definition (presentation card): "Сенсорная регуляция - это процесс обработки сенсорной информации и управления уровнем возбуждения" (*Sensory regulation is the process of processing sensory information and managing the level of arousal*). It influences our ability to self-regulate and to adapt to the environment, and it allows us to filter, interpret and adapt to sensory stimuli [img #168].

The six markers of working sensory regulation ("Как выглядит сенсорная регуляция?" — *what does sensory regulation look like?*) [img #169]:
1. **Focused attention** — attention can be held on what matters.
2. **Optimal response to sensory stimuli** — reactions are proportionate, neither excessive nor absent.
3. **Smooth transitions between activities** — switching from one activity to another is not disruptive.
4. **Comfortable interaction with sensory experiences** — sensory input is engaged with without distress.
5. **Emotional balance** — emotions stay in equilibrium.
6. **Enjoyment of sensory experiences** — sensory input can be a source of pleasure.
[img #169]

### What sensory dysregulation is and what it leads to
Definition: "Сенсорная дирегуляция [sic] - это состояние, при котором наша система обработки сенсорной информации не справляется с сенсорными стимулами" (*Sensory dysregulation is a state in which our sensory processing system cannot cope with sensory stimuli*). It can lead to six outcomes [img #170]:
1. **Overload** (перегрузка) — too much input.
2. **Underload** (недогрузка) — too little input; the source thus frames dysregulation as two-directional, not only "too much".
3. **Increased or decreased sensitivity to sensory stimuli** — hyper- or hypo-sensitivity.
4. **Difficulty concentrating.**
5. **Emotional problems.**
6. **Reduced functioning** (снижение функциональности).
[img #170]

The concluding card adds that sensory dysregulation can deeply affect emotional experience in two opposite ways — "усиливая чувствительность или приводя к отключению" (*heightening sensitivity or leading to shutdown*) [img #185].

### Sensory overload in ADHD vs autism
The presentation devotes one card to each condition, with the same structure: cause → manifestations → what is important.

**ADHD** [img #171]:
- Statement: people with ADHD often experience sensory overload.
- Cause: difficulties *processing* sensory information.
- Manifestations: (1) emotional outbursts; (2) difficulty concentrating.
- What matters: (1) recognising the influence of sensory overload; (2) developing strategies to manage it.

**Autism** [img #172]:
- Statement: sensory overload is also common among autistic people (the card's OCR reads "артимом" [sic], i.e. autism).
- Cause: difficulties *regulating* sensory input.
- Manifestations: (1) overwhelming reactions to sensory stimuli; (2) heightened sensitivity to sensory input.
- What matters: (1) understanding the impact of sensory overload; (2) developing strategies for navigating and coping with the sensory environment.

The channel's post introducing the ADHD article frames it this way: in today's world, with an endless flow of information, stimuli and irritants, people with ADHD may feel overload especially acutely, and everyday sensory impressions can turn into unbearable load; the article aims to help readers adapt to and understand their own sensory needs [msg #25]. The article itself notes that most research and tools target children — the author's own online search found only images of little boys with stress toys and weighted vests, useful to her as a parent but not as an adult — and that it focuses on ADHD, with autistic adults to be covered separately [article #25].

### Self-regulation and its three components
Definition: self-regulation is the ability to manage thoughts, emotions and behaviour. It helps cope with difficulties, make decisions, manage emotions and maintain balance [img #173]. It includes:
1. **Self-awareness** (самосознание) — recognising and understanding our emotions, thoughts and physical sensations.
2. **Self-control** (самоконтроль) — the ability to manage impulses and regulate our behaviour even in difficult situations.
3. **Self-monitoring** (самонаблюдение) — tracking our own progress, reflecting on our actions and making adjustments as needed.
[img #173]

### Sensory regulation as the foundation of self-regulation
The channel's central model: sensory regulation is the base layer; emotional, behavioural and executive regulation sit on top of it. The original article post lists four areas affected when the sensory system is in imbalance [msg #46]:
1. **Emotional regulation** — sensory dysregulation makes it harder to control our emotions.
2. **Focus and attention** — our ability to concentrate suffers when the sensory system is in imbalance.
3. **Management of actions and behaviour** — sensory dysregulation prevents us from effectively controlling our actions and reactions.
4. **Executive function problems** — sensory dysregulation can overload the brain, and as a result executive function suffers.
The article states that sensory regulation "служит основой для различных других **систем саморегуляции**" (*serves as the basis for various other self-regulation systems*), including emotions, executive functions and behaviour, and that different sensory regulation strategies can improve life [msg #46].

The presentation spells the link out [img #174, #175]:
- Sensory regulation is the foundation for self-regulation; it influences emotions, behaviour, attention and relationships; dysregulation interferes with self-regulation [img #174].
- Impact of sensory overload: it makes managing emotions harder; weakens attention and executive functions; reduces control over actions; interferes with regulating relationships [img #174].
- Improved sensory regulation supports self-regulation, creates a balanced sensory environment and helps cope with overload [img #175].
- Conclusion: sensory regulation is the basis of self-regulation; strengthening sensory regulation improves self-regulation; developing both systems allows one to function successfully in a world of sensory stimuli [img #175].

**Layer 1 — Sensory regulation and emotion regulation** [img #176, #177]:
- Impact of overload on emotions: it disrupts emotional well-being; causes heightened emotional reactions; leads to irritation, anxiety and nervous breakdowns [img #176].
- Emotion regulation is defined as the ability to understand, manage and respond to emotions, and as a set of skills for recognising and managing emotions [img #176].
- Link: sensory regulation provides the basis for emotion regulation. "Хорошая сенсорная регуляция = более стабильное эмоциональное состояние" (*Good sensory regulation = a more stable emotional state*); problems with sensory regulation = difficulty managing emotions [img #177].
- Conclusion: meeting sensory regulation needs = more successful emotion regulation [img #177].

**Layer 2 — Sensory regulation and behaviour regulation** [img #178, #179]:
- Sensory dysregulation leads to overload; disrupts attention, focus and emotions; overloads both the cognitive and the emotional system; and weakens three abilities: (a) concentrating on tasks, (b) filtering out non-essential stimuli, (c) taking part in tasks and activities [img #178].
- Behaviour regulation is the ability to manage and modulate actions, reactions and behaviour. It includes: (1) self-control; (2) impulse management; (3) making considered decisions; (4) adapting to change and new environments; (5) maintaining socially acceptable behaviour [img #178].
- Mechanism: problems with sensory processing (overload / high sensitivity) affect behaviour regulation because sensory difficulties trigger stress reactions, and stress makes it harder to modify actions and behaviour [img #179].
- Conclusion: sensory regulation and behaviour regulation are interconnected; sensory dysregulation can negatively affect behaviour regulation [img #179].

**Layer 3 — Sensory regulation and executive functions** [img #180, #181]:
- Executive functions are cognitive processes for: planning; organisation; concentration; impulse regulation; achieving goals. They include working memory, flexible thinking, self-control and problem solving. They matter for goal-directed behaviour, decision making and task management [img #180].
- Interrelation: sensory problems overload the brain and affect executive functions; sensory regulation supports executive functions and improves regulation of attention and concentration. Conclusion: the two are closely linked; supporting sensory regulation raises effectiveness and improves cognitive functions [img #181].

### Prioritising sensory regulation: building the foundation for effective regulation
Purpose: argues for the order of intervention — sensory first [img #182, #183].
- **Traditional approaches**: focus on regulating emotions or behaviour; do not take sensory regulation into account; are ineffective when sensory dysregulation is present [img #182].
- **Sensory regulation**: the foundation for effective self-regulation; it provides stability for regulating emotions and behaviour [img #182].
- **Three ways to remove sensory dysregulation**: (1) creating a sensory-friendly environment; (2) providing sensory breaks; (3) using sensory-oriented interventions and therapy [img #182].
- **Result**: a solid base for self-regulation is built, and the effectiveness of emotion and behaviour regulation increases [img #183].
- **Comprehensive approach**: takes into account the interconnections between sensory, emotional and behavioural regulation and paves the way to successful self-regulation [img #183].

The concluding card summarises the method: by prioritising sensory regulation — integrating and adjusting sensory input, managing levels of arousal, attention and emotional reactions — we create an environment that improves emotional resilience and regulation skills, contributing to overall well-being. "Признание важности сенсорной безопасности раскрывает силу саморегуляции" (*Recognising the importance of sensory safety unlocks the power of self-regulation*). Sensory regulation forms the basis for navigating the world with more comfort and well-being; whether you are a neurodivergent person or a caregiver, implementing sensory regulation strategies creates a supportive environment and strengthens self-regulation skills [img #185].

### Three sensory regulation strategies
Goal: support sensory regulation and improve well-being [img #184].
1. **Sensory safety plan (план сенсорной безопасности).**
   - Identify the actions/experiences that give you regulated sensory input — the card's examples: calming sensory activities, music, sensory toys.
   - Draw up a plan for managing sensory triggers and sensory-saturated environments (i.e. decide in advance what you will do when you meet a trigger or must be in a high-stimulation place).
2. **Environment modification.**
   - Adjust the lighting and reduce noise.
   - Use visual supports and organisation systems.
3. **Mindfulness and self-care (Сознательность и самоуход).**
   - Practise mindfulness techniques: deep breathing, meditation, mindful (body) scanning.
   - Prioritise self-care: sensory breaks and releasing excess energy.
Reminders on the card: "Сенсорная перегрузка = стресс" (*sensory overload = stress*) and "Обучение расслаблению - важный инструмент" (*learning to relax is an important tool*) [img #184].

### Overload mistaken for panic attacks
A translated first-person post (source: FINCHOFAUTISMLIFE) titled "Это были не панические атаки. Это была перегрузка." (*These were not panic attacks. It was overload.*), subtitled "Мой аутичный мозг пытался мне что-то сказать" (*My autistic brain was trying to tell me something*). The author spent years believing they had panic attacks, but it was stimulation overload in an autistic adult [msg #638].

1. **Symptoms that looked like panic**: rapid heartbeat; tightness in the chest; the urge to run away; tears out of nowhere. "Naturally, I thought: this is anxiety." [msg #638]
2. **The mismatch that gave it away**: the episodes were not connected with anxiety or fear. They happened in specific conditions — noisy places; bright light; crowds; constant conversations; or having to do too many things at once [msg #638].
3. **Turning point**: learning about sensory overload made everything clear: "Я не паниковал(а), я был(а) перегружен(а)" (*I wasn't panicking, I was overloaded*) [msg #638].
4. **How overload feels** (distinguishing signs): the brain seems to switch off; noise and light become unbearable; sudden irritability or "shutdown"; a sudden feeling of cold or heat; an acute urge to run away or hide; sometimes even losing the ability to speak [msg #638].
5. **Understanding**: being autistic, their nervous system perceives the world differently, which means they can get overloaded even when "nothing happened" [msg #638].
6. **What they do now instead of blaming themselves**: take breaks in silence; allow themselves to stim; avoid bright light and chaotic places; say "no" without guilt; plan rest time after socialising; use grounding techniques; use daytime naps [msg #638].
7. **Message to the reader**: if this is familiar, you are not being dramatic, not broken and not "too sensitive"; you may be autistic, sensory-sensitive or both. "Твоё тело просто просит покоя" (*Your body is simply asking for rest/peace*) [msg #638].

### ADHD vs autism stimming comparison
Translated post (source: ashahaidelhi). Stimming (self-stimulatory behaviour) is often misunderstood, yet it is one of the most important ways children (and adults) with autism and ADHD regulate their inner world. The comparison runs along five dimensions [msg #733]:

1. **When stimming appears**
   - ADHD: often during boredom or low concentration; helps regulate attention and relieve tension; usually decreases with full engagement.
   - Autism: often during sensory overload or strong emotions; gives a sense of comfort and predictability; may intensify with stress, emotional arousal or in new places.
2. **Purpose**
   - ADHD: relieving restlessness; staying alert in boring situations; works as a way to "wake up" the brain.
   - Autism: calms the nervous system; helps cope with excess sensory information; provides self-soothing and emotional regulation.
3. **Typical kinds**
   - ADHD: nail biting; chewing pencils/clothes; tapping or drawing; bouncing or jiggling a leg.
   - Autism: rocking back and forth; hand flapping or spinning; lining objects up in a row; repeating words, phrases or sounds.
4. **Impact on daily life**
   - ADHD: perceived as "fidgeting" in class; can genuinely improve concentration when allowed; often wrongly perceived as a lack of discipline.
   - Autism: may look unusual to others; sometimes misread as "strange behaviour"; extremely important for managing emotions and sensory needs.
5. **Adults' reactions**
   - ADHD: adults often say "sit still" or "stop it"; support includes stress toys, movement and chewing aids.
   - Autism: sometimes considered "inappropriate"; support means safe conditions for stimming and spaces that take sensory needs into account.

Key thought: stimming in ADHD is more connected with attention and releasing energy, in autism with self-regulation and sensory balance; both kinds are natural and deserve respect. Instead of trying to stop stimming, offer safe options — stress toys, chewing accessories, sensory pauses or calm spaces; these small supports help children feel understood. Remember: stimming is not "bad behaviour" but a way of communicating [msg #733].

## Detailed insights

### The adult experience of discovering sensory differences
Both Neff articles open with the same personal account: the author reinterpreted her sensory experiences after learning of her neurodivergence at age 37. Before looking at the world through a sensory lens, she assumed everyone felt overwhelmed in restaurants, grocery stores, cinemas or crowded rooms. Researching online, she found only pictures of little boys with fidgets and weighted vests — helpful for parenting her children but not for an adult. Her point: although sensory processing disorder affects both children and adults, most research and tools target children, which is why she writes guidance for adults, drawing on both her lived experience and her work as a clinical psychologist [article #25, #41].

A forwarded post (from the channel Аутичные переводы) defines visual snow as a constant visual symptom in which people see millions of flickering particles before their eyes around the clock, like television static when the TV is mistuned. The forwarding author learned about it only at 18 and was very surprised that not everyone sees this way, and asks readers whether they have the syndrome [msg #137].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The ADHD/autism cause distinction (processing vs regulating sensory input) is stated on slides without further explanation or evidence [msg #171, #172]. The ADHD article attributes its author's own olfactory hypersensitivity more to autism than ADHD, while saying it can occur in ADHD too [article #25].
- (!) CONTRADICTION (tension between sources): the presentation and the adult guide recommend mindfulness, meditation and deep breathing [msg #184] [article #41], while the same guide warns that meditation and mindfulness may not resonate with people with ADHD and the ADHD article notes deep breathing can be hard when interoception is impaired [article #25, #41]. The sources present these as individual differences rather than resolving them.
- The claim that traditional emotion/behaviour-focused approaches are ineffective under sensory dysregulation is the presentation's position; no studies are cited [msg #182]. The Neff articles likewise rest on the author's personal and clinical experience; no studies are cited [article #25, #41].
- The panic-vs-overload account is a single person's translated experience, not a diagnostic criterion; it does not claim panic attacks never occur in autistic people [msg #638].
- The stimming comparison is a generalised translated infographic-style post; its categories (e.g. typical stims per condition) are tendencies ("часто", "обычно" — *often, usually*), not rules [msg #733].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the full text of the teletype article in #46, the activities list in #54 and the video in #450 were not available [msg #46, #54, #450].
- Several slide transcriptions contain OCR typos ("дирегуляция", "артимом", "звоции"); the meanings (dysregulation, autism, emotions) are clear from context [msg #170, #172, #176].
