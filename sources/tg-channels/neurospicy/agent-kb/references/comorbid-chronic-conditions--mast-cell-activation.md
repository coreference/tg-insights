# Mast cell activation syndrome (MCAS)

## What this covers

This topic gathers everything the channel Нейропряность posted about mast cells and mast cell activation syndrome (MCAS; in Russian САТК, «синдром активации тучных клеток»): what mast cells are and how they degranulate, how normal defensive activation turns into a self-sustaining pathological cycle, the very wide range of symptoms across body systems, the factors that contribute to it (infections, chronic stress, PTSD/cPTSD, early and medical trauma, genetics, hidden infections, toxic load, autoimmunity), and its connections to neurodivergence, hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, POTS, IBS, PMDD and Alzheimer's disease. It also covers treatment options discussed in the channel — antihistamines, quercetin, anti-inflammatory diet, stress and nervous-system regulation, low-dose naltrexone (LDN) and benzodiazepines — with study data and the author's personal LDN story. [msg #39, #291, #318, #353, #689, #690, #827]

According to the source, it matters because MCAS is more common than assumed, is frequently misread as "psychosomatic", is highly comorbid with neurodivergence, and substantially lowers quality of life; the author's stated goal is to spread information about MCAS and stress the importance of correct diagnosis and treatment «для улучшения качества жизни» (to improve quality of life) and to prevent repeated activations of mast cells. [msg #39, #291]

## Core ideas

- **Mast cells are the body's first responders.** They are innate-immune cells living in connective tissue all over the body; when activated they release stored mediators (histamine, cytokines, prostaglandins, proteases, heparin). Detail in *Mast cell activation cycle*. [msg #291, #695]
- **MCAS is defence that has gone out of control.** A trigger makes the protective response pathological: mediators are released abnormally and excessively, harmless stimuli are treated as dangers, and the state can become chronic and self-sustaining. [msg #291]
- **Because mast cells are everywhere, MCAS is systemic.** Symptoms range from anxiety, depression, brain fog and migraine to skin, gut, heart, reproductive and blood symptoms; see *MCAS symptom map by body system*. [msg #39, #291, #353]
- **Stress and trauma are triggers, not just allergens.** Stress releases CRH, which activates mast cells; trauma reshapes the HPA axis and autonomic nervous system, which in turn shape mast cell behaviour. See *Trauma and MCAS model*. [msg #318, #689]
- **Histamine may drive PMDD.** Elevated luteal-phase histamine is presented as a contributor to PMDD's mood, pain and cognitive symptoms; see *Histamine–PMDD mechanism model*. [msg #91, #92, #93, #94]
- **Chronic stress + mast cells may feed Alzheimer's pathology** via neuroinflammation and beta-amyloid build-up — a risk, not a destiny. See the *vicious circle* framework. [msg #318]
- **Mast-cell-targeted therapies help neuropsychiatric symptoms.** In a 553-patient survey, antihistamines, LDN and benzodiazepines were rated most helpful; see *Neuropsychiatric symptoms and treatment effectiveness*. [msg #690]

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Mast cell activation cycle (normal vs pathological activation)
Purpose: to understand what mast cells normally do and what goes wrong in MCAS. [msg #291]

1. **Where mast cells are.** Mast cells («тучные клетки») are a special type of cell of the innate immune system. They are present in the connective tissues of the whole body, especially in tissues of organs close to the external environment: the lungs, the gastrointestinal tract and the skin. The #695 post adds that they are a special kind of white blood cell living in connective tissue, with a key role in immune defence and allergic reactions; #39 notes that connective tissue is found practically everywhere in the body, which is why excess activation shows up in so many different ways. [msg #291, #695, #39]
2. **Their normal functions.** They act as the first line of defence against pathogens and other potentially dangerous substances, and thanks to their wide distribution can react quickly to various irritants and initiate an immune response. They take part in: (a) protecting the body from pathogens; (b) allergic reactions; (c) inflammatory processes; (d) wound healing. [msg #291]
3. **Activation and degranulation.** On activation mast cells release mediators — histamine, cytokines and prostaglandins — that regulate immune and inflammatory responses. #695 describes the process: on meeting an allergen the cell activates and starts degranulation (дегрануляция), the release of stored chemicals including histamine and heparin; these mediators are what cause redness, itching and swelling. Timelapse microframes (a video shared in #695, not transcribed) show the cell literally "exploding" its contents into the surrounding tissue. [msg #291, #695]
4. **Normal ending.** In a healthy body the activation cycle ends once the "danger" has passed. [msg #291]
5. **Pathological activation = MCAS.** MCAS is pathological activation of mast cells, characterised by abnormal and excessive release of mediators. It happens when the normal protective response gets out of control due to a particular trigger. As a result the immune system mistakenly perceives even harmless stimuli as dangerous and launches a protective reaction where normally none would happen. [msg #291]
6. **Chronicity.** The condition can become chronic and turn into a self-sustaining cycle. [msg #291]

### MCAS contributing factors list
The #291 article lists factors that contribute to developing MCAS (six items): [msg #291]
1. Strong blows to the immune system (viral infections).
2. Chronic stress / PTSD / cPTSD.
3. Genetic factors.
4. Hidden chronic infections.
5. Toxic load.
6. Autoimmune diseases.

The earlier guide (#39) names everyday triggers explaining why MCAS is more common than we think: stress, herpes viruses, infections and toxic load. #689 widens the trigger list beyond allergens to hormones, infections, temperature changes and stress signals from the brain; the post stresses this list is not complete. [msg #39, #689]

### MCAS symptom map by body system (15 groups)
Source: #353, "General symptoms of MCAS", based on a PMC review (PMC10672129). The post stresses there are many heterogeneous phenotypes that vary with where the mast cells are located, how many there are, and their ability to degranulate specific mediators. Symptoms can be constant or periodic and of varying severity. The full list as posted: [msg #353]

1. **Systemic:** fatigue; subjective feeling of raised and/or lowered body temperature; sweating; appetite change; weight gain/loss; sensitivity to chemicals and physical factors; slow healing. [msg #353]
2. **Skin:** urticaria (hives); itch; flushing; haemangiomas with itch/pain; various rashes; telangiectasias; stretch marks; skin papillomas (skin tags); folliculitis; ulcers; eczema; Quincke's oedema (angioedema); alopecia; onychodystrophy (nail dystrophy). [msg #353]
3. **Ophthalmological:** irritation / "dryness" of the eyes; difficulty focusing; blepharospasm. [msg #353]
4. **Otological (ear/nose as grouped in the post):** tinnitus; hearing loss; runny nose; rhinitis; nasal congestion; nosebleeds. [msg #353]
5. **Oropharyngeal:** pain; burning; leukoplakia; ulcers; angioneurotic oedema; dysgeusia (taste disturbance); inflammation/destruction of teeth and/or periodontium. [msg #353]
6. **Lymphatic system:** lymphadenopathy; rarely, splenomegaly. [msg #353]
7. **Respiratory system:** dry cough; shortness of breath (difficulty taking a deep breath); wheezing; obstructive sleep apnoea. [msg #353]
8. **Cardiovascular system:** pre-syncope (feeling faint); hypertension; labile blood pressure; palpitations; oedema; chest pain; Kounis syndrome (allergic angina). [msg #353]
9. **Gastrointestinal tract:** dyspepsia; gastro-oesophageal reflux; abdominal pain; nausea; vomiting; diarrhoea and/or constipation; gastroparesis; Quincke's oedema; dysphagia (usually proximal); bloating (after meals or spontaneous); malabsorption. [msg #353]
10. **Genitourinary system:** menorrhagia; pelvic pain; endometriosis; vulvodynia; vaginitis; dysmenorrhoea; miscarriages; infertility; dysuria. [msg #353]
11. **Musculoskeletal system:** myalgias; migrating bone/joint pain; osteopenia/osteoporosis. [msg #353]
12. **Neurological:** headache; migraine; sensory neuropathies; dysautonomia; episodic weakness; seizure disorders; non-epileptic seizures; cognitive dysfunction; insomnia; hypersomnia; restless legs syndrome. [msg #353]
13. **Psychiatric:** depression; anger/irritability; mood lability; anxiety; panic; obsessive-compulsive disorders; attention deficit/hyperactivity syndrome (ADHD). [msg #353]
14. **Haematological:** tendency to bruise; polycythaemia; anaemia. [msg #353]
15. **Immunological:** hypersensitivity reactions; increased risk of malignant neoplasms and autoimmune diseases; impaired healing; increased susceptibility to infections. [msg #353]

### Trauma and MCAS model (stress signals, HPA axis, autonomic nervous system)
Source: #689, a translation of an Instagram post by @hyperresilient titled "Trauma can provoke mast cell activation syndrome". The model in six steps: [msg #689]
1. **Mast cells respond not only to allergens.** Triggers can be hormones, infections, temperature swings and stress signals from the brain. Chronic stress and trauma make mast cells more sensitive over time. [msg #689]
2. **Stress and mast cells are connected.** Stress makes the brain release CRH (corticotropin-releasing hormone). This activates mast cells, especially in the skin, gut and brain, causing release of histamine and inflammatory substances. [msg #689]
3. **Trauma can change the nervous system.** Early trauma, PTSD or chronic stress can alter the HPA (hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal) axis and the autonomic nervous system; both systems influence mast cell behaviour. [msg #689]
4. **Many people with MCAS report past trauma**, including: childhood trauma; medical trauma; chronic stress; complex PTSD. These patterns are often seen both in research and in real life. Caveat from the post: this does not mean trauma is the only cause, but it can be part of the overall picture. [msg #689]
5. **That is why MCAS is often misunderstood.** Symptoms can be triggered by "invisible" causes — emotions, memories or internal stress signals — not only allergens or external factors. [msg #689]
6. **MCAS is often complex; recovery may include** calming the nervous system, supporting immune regulation, and working with trauma if it is part of one's history. [msg #689]

### Stress → CRH → mast cell → neuroinflammation → Alzheimer's vicious circle
Source: #318, the author's post "Chronic stress, PTSD and mast cell activation: potential risk factors for Alzheimer's disease", continuing an earlier post on cPTSD vs PTSD and citing a review in *Frontiers in Cellular Neuroscience* (linked via PMC5733004). [msg #318]

- **Starting point (the review's claim):** chronic stress can accelerate Alzheimer's disease (БА) pathogenesis in humans and animal models through increased inflammatory reactions, beta-amyloid accumulation, tau hyperphosphorylation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction and disturbed glucose metabolism. [msg #318]
- **Step 1 — stress activates mast cells.** Mast cells play an important role in the stress response: under stress, CRH is released and activates mast cells. [msg #318]
- **Step 2 — mediators damage the barrier.** Activated mast cells release pro-inflammatory mediators including histamine, cytokines and proteases. These can disrupt the integrity of the blood–brain barrier and promote neuroinflammation. [msg #318]
- **Step 3 — mast cells start the cascade.** Research quoted in the post says mast cells are the first cells to respond to brain injury because they can release pre-stored mediators — indicating a potential role in initiating the inflammatory cascade in brain trauma and stress. [msg #318]
- **Step 4 — the vicious circle.** Chronic stress creates a loop: increased mast cell activation → more neuroinflammation → which may promote beta-amyloid accumulation and AD development. [msg #318]
- **Step 5 — microglia fail.** Prolonged stress forms a closed cycle in which normal microglial function is disturbed. This lowers the brain's ability to clear harmful substances and increases beta-amyloid (Aβ) accumulation; inflammatory processes in nerve tissue intensify and neuron death speeds up. [msg #318]
- **Conclusion:** mast cell activation in brain trauma, stress and PTSD may accelerate the pathogenesis of neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative diseases including AD; stress management and regulation of mast cell activity could become new approaches to preventing and treating Alzheimer's. [msg #318]
- **Caveat and prevention:** this does not mean everyone with chronic stress or PTSD/cPTSD will inevitably develop AD — AD depends on many factors including genetics, lifestyle and environment. But the risk exists, and awareness can motivate prevention: stress management, a healthy lifestyle, mast cell stabilisation and cognitive stimulation. The author closes: early prevention and timely treatment can significantly reduce risks and improve quality of life. [msg #318]

### Histamine–PMDD mechanism model
Source: the carousel #88–#95, forwarded from the author's "Neurospicy_dushnila" channel, titled «ПМС или ПМДР?» (PMS or PMDD?). [msg #88]

**PMS vs PMDD distinction.**
- PMS (premenstrual syndrome) is fairly common; the card says 90% of women experience it «в период менструации» (in the period of menstruation). Typical symptoms: headaches; fatigue; bloating; irritability. [msg #89]
- PMDD (premenstrual dysphoric disorder) is a serious mental condition in which women experience extreme mood swings and intense symptoms in the week before menstruation starts. Typical symptoms: sharp mood changes; depression; anxiety/tension; anger/rage. The card then asks what the cause might be. [msg #90]

**Histamine as a cause.** Modern research, per the card, indicates that elevated histamine may significantly influence PMDD. Histamines are chemicals produced by the immune system in response to allergens, injuries or stress; they play roles in many body functions including regulation of sleep, digestion and mood. In people with PMDD histamine levels tend to be higher in the luteal phase preceding menstruation. [msg #91]

**Mechanisms (three bodily routes):** [msg #92]
1. **Inflammatory reactions** — histamine, being a pro-inflammatory mediator, can worsen PMDD symptoms such as pain, swelling and irritability.
2. **Effect on the hypothalamus** — histamine can disturb the hypothalamus, the key regulator of the menstrual cycle, leading to hormonal imbalances.
3. **Muscle spasms** — high histamine can cause spasms of the uterine smooth muscle, intensifying pain during menstruation.

**Brain route:** histamines act as neurotransmitters in the brain, affecting mood, alertness and cognitive function. When histamine is elevated it can cause inflammation, especially in the brain; brain inflammation can disrupt neurotransmitter function and contribute to symptoms of depression and anxiety. Histamines can also influence other mood-regulating neurotransmitters such as serotonin and dopamine. [msg #93]

**PMDD symptoms caused by histamine:** [msg #94]
1. Anxiety and irritability — elevated histamine can increase anxiety and irritability during the luteal phase.
2. Mood swings — fluctuating histamine can contribute to sudden mood changes in people with PMDD.
3. Fatigue — histamine-induced brain inflammation can cause fatigue and lethargy.
4. Cognitive symptoms — because of histamine's effect on neurotransmitters, "head fog", poor concentration and memory problems can occur.

### What to do about histamine-driven PMDD (4 measures)
From the final card (#95), «Что делать?» (What to do?): [msg #95]
1. **Antihistamines** may relieve some PMDD symptoms.
2. **Histamine-lowering supplements** — supplements such as quercetin may help reduce histamine levels.
3. **Anti-inflammatory diet** — avoid foods high in preservatives, alcohol and sugar; eat foods rich in omega-3 fatty acids and antioxidants.
4. **Stress management** through mindfulness, physical exercise and relaxation techniques, plus stabilising the nervous system.

Warning on the card (read from the image; the OCR captured only its end, «назначения любых добавок и препаратов»): always consult a doctor about prescribing any supplements and medications. [img #95]

### Neuropsychiatric symptoms and treatment effectiveness in MCAS (553-patient survey)
Source: #690, the channel's translation of an Instagram summary by @drtoddmaderis of a recently published study. [msg #690]

- **Aim:** find out how often neuropsychiatric symptoms occur in people with MCAS and which treatments work best. It compared the frequency of neurological and psychiatric symptoms in more than 500 MCAS patients versus healthy people, and examined how therapies commonly used in MCAS — antihistamines, low-dose naltrexone (LDN) and benzodiazepines — affect these symptoms. [msg #690]
- **Method:** researchers surveyed 553 MCAS patients and 558 controls with detailed questionnaires on (a) neurological symptoms — fatigue, clouded consciousness, migraines, neuropathy, fainting/pre-fainting, tinnitus, sensitivity to light/sound/smells, seizure states, insomnia, plus further unlisted items; (b) psychiatric symptoms — anxiety, depression, panic attacks, PTSD, OCD, mania/hypomania, psychosis/schizophrenia, hallucinations, plus further unlisted items. MCAS patients also rated how much specific therapies relieved these symptoms. [msg #690]
- **Findings — burden:** neuropsychiatric symptom burden was significantly higher in MCAS patients than in controls. Leading neurological symptoms (over 70% of MCAS patients): fatigue; cognitive impairment; neuropathy; migraine-like headaches. Leading psychiatric symptoms (40–66%): anxiety; depression; panic attacks; PTSD. [msg #690]
- **Findings — treatments (patient ratings of relief, 0–10 scale):** antihistamines 6.3; LDN 5.6; benzodiazepines 5.6. Neurological: LDN helped 29% with fatigue and cognitive impairment; benzodiazepines helped 51% with insomnia and 29% with restless legs syndrome. Psychiatric: benzodiazepines helped 70% with anxiety, 52% with panic attacks and 51% with agoraphobia; antihistamines and LDN were moderately effective for anxiety and depression. [msg #690]
- **Why it matters (per the summary):** it is one of the largest studies to date on neuropsychiatric symptoms in MCAS; the data suggest mast cell dysregulation plays a key role in brain inflammation and psychiatric manifestations; standard psychiatric approaches may not account for the mast cell link; MCAS-targeted therapies, especially antihistamines, LDN and benzodiazepines, may significantly improve quality of life. The study underlines the importance of considering an MCAS diagnosis in patients with unexplained fatigue, cognitive impairment and treatment-resistant psychiatric symptoms. [msg #690]

## Detailed insights

### Prevalence, recognition and diagnostic status
- The #291 article opens with a common experience: coming to a doctor with many seemingly unrelated symptoms and complaints and hearing «Это всё психосоматика» ("It's all psychosomatic"). Such stories are often told by people diagnosed with MCAS. [msg #291]
- The article states MCAS occurs in 17% of the world's population and can show up as chronic disturbances in different body systems. [msg #291]
- MCAS is a relatively new diagnostic term: it appeared in the international ICD-10 edition only in 2016, under code D89.40. At the time of the post (November 2024) MCAS had not yet been officially introduced into clinical practice in Russia. [msg #291]
- The #39 guide says MCAS is more common than we think because of various triggers met in everyday life. [msg #39]

### Comorbidity with neurodivergence and connective-tissue conditions
- MCAS has high comorbidity with autism and with hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, which is itself highly comorbid with neurodivergence (author's clarification in #318). [msg #318]
- MCAS can worsen POTS, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome and IBS, which are common in neurodivergent people. [msg #39]
- The #291 article lists, as topics for future posts, the influence of viruses on mast cell activation, MCAS comorbidity with connective tissue dysplasia, and how diagnosis of this difficult condition proceeds. ### Personal case: LDN, MCAS and autoimmune thyroiditis (#827)
The author takes LDN to stabilise mast cells because of her MCAS, and reports it also helped with her autoimmune thyroiditis (AIT). [msg #827]
- **2024:** an MRI of the neck showed "something wrong". Standard thyroid tests were normal, but P-TPO (thyroid antibodies) was 38 (upper limit 34), and ultrasound found AIT and a cyst, which was not biopsied (she says she fears such procedures and would probably have refused anyway). The endocrinologist told her simply to wait until the thyroid fails, since they were not going to treat it. [msg #827]
- **End of 2024:** she finally obtained medication to stabilise MCAS — LDN was prescribed. She explained to doctors that she did not want to lose her thyroid and, since they could suggest nothing else, wanted to try a treatment suitable both for MCAS and for autoimmune problems. [msg #827]
- **October 2025:** P-TPO had fallen to 28. Because the result was within the normal range, no repeat ultrasound was ordered, but she no longer feels such strong problems with the "lump" in her throat. [msg #827]
- **Her conclusion:** she sees this as confirming her hypothesis that mast cell stabilisation can reduce thyroid inflammation, and was glad she secured the drug in Norway, as it at least paused the process of thyroid failure. This is a single personal case, not a study. [msg #827]

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- (!) INCOMPLETE: the full MCAS guide (#39) and the diagnostic-process material are in an external article not included; diagnostic criteria and the guide's diet/supplement protocol are not available. [msg #39]
- (!) UNCLEAR: the PMDD carousel uses inconsistent abbreviations — ПМДР, «ПДМР» [sic] and «ПМР» [sic] — all apparently meaning PMDD; it also says PMS affects 90% of women «в период менструации» although PMS is premenstrual. The cards cite "modern research" without naming studies. [msg #89, #91]
- The 17% prevalence figure (#291) is stated without a source in the post. [msg #291]
- The 553-patient study's treatment results are patient self-ratings from questionnaires, relayed via an Instagram summary; the study itself is not named. Benzodiazepines appear among "effective" therapies; the source adds no caveats about them. [msg #690]
- The Alzheimer's link is framed as a potential risk factor: the author explicitly says chronic stress or PTSD/cPTSD does not mean one will inevitably get AD. [msg #318]
- Trauma is described as a possible contributor, not the only cause of MCAS. [msg #689]
- The LDN–thyroid result is one personal case; no repeat ultrasound was done after the antibodies normalised. [msg #827]
