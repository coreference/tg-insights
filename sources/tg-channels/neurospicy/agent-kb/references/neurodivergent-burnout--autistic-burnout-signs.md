# Autistic burnout: definition, causes and warning signs

## What this covers

This topic gathers everything the channel posted on what autistic (and, broadly, neurodivergent — autistic and ADHD) burnout is, how researchers define and provisionally diagnose it, what causes it, what it looks and feels like from the inside, how it differs from ordinary burnout, depression-like illness and "laziness", and which checklists and scales a person can use to track it. According to the source, it matters because the state is "deeply exhausting", is still treated by society as an inconvenience rather than a debilitating struggle, is easily missed or pushed through, and does not respond to the ordinary neurotypical advice to "rest more" [msg #28, #149, #150, #388].

## Core ideas

- **Autistic burnout is a distinct syndrome, not ordinary burnout.** It arises from chronic life stress plus a mismatch between expectations and abilities without adequate support; it is pervasive, long-lasting (usually more than 3 months) and involves exhaustion, loss of functioning and reduced tolerance to stimuli (see *Raymaker definition*) [msg #392, #28].
- **The cause is the environment, not autism itself.** Burnout develops because autistic needs are not met in a neurotypical society: masking, minimising needs, lack of support, social, sensory and daily-life demands exceeding capacity [msg #432, #388].
- **The neurodivergent nervous system carries a higher baseline stress load** and recovers less easily, so stress accumulates day after day until the system shuts down (see *The neurodivergent stress day*) [msg #446].
- **Hallmark signs** are extreme exhaustion that sleep does not fix, sensory intolerance, executive-function collapse, skill regression, social withdrawal and loss of the ability to mask [msg #147, #388, #392, #398].
- **Simple rest does not cure it**: the nervous system gets stuck in hyper- or hypoarousal and has to be helped manually back to safety before rest and activity can help [msg #388, #446].
- **Tracking helps**: a concrete list of signs helps a person not miss burnout or try to push through it, and the ABSC checklist gives a score to compare over time [msg #150, #31].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Raymaker et al. (2020) definition of autistic burnout
The channel gives this research definition: autistic burnout is a syndrome caused by chronic life stress and a mismatch between expectations and abilities in the absence of adequate support. It is characterised by three features:
1. **Pervasive, long-term exhaustion** — "долгосрочным (обычно более 3 месяцев)" (*long-term (usually more than 3 months)*).
2. **Loss of functioning.**
3. **Reduced tolerance to stimuli.**
[msg #392]

The channel's earlier introduction (#28) frames it in similar words: a unique and deeply exhausting state that primarily affects people with autism (РАС) and ADHD, arising either from prolonged stress or from an acute stressful experience, especially one tied to the need to adapt to the surrounding neurotypical world. It contrasts it with the general phenomenon of burnout in a world where the constant pursuit of efficiency and productivity has become the norm. The accompanying article (Telegraph) is announced as covering: what autistic burnout is, its main symptoms, what can trigger it, whether it is a medical diagnosis, and how it relates to depression and to neurotypical burnout [msg #28].

### Higgins et al. (2021) provisional diagnostic criteria
Purpose: provisional criteria for recognising autistic burnout, translated by the channel from an Embrace Autism infographic. All of 1 and 2 plus at least one item from 3 must be present [msg #392]:
1. **Significant mental and physical exhaustion** ("Значительное умственное и физическое истощение"; the card shows it as a battery at 1%) [msg #392].
2. **Interpersonal withdrawal** ("Межличностная отстранённость"; the infographic card renders it as increased social withdrawal) [msg #392].
3. **One or more of the following:**
   - A. **Significant reduction in functioning** in social, occupational, educational, academic, behavioural or other important areas of life.
   - B. **Confusion, difficulties with executive functions and dissociative states** (card: "more problems with executive functions").
   - C. **Intensification of autistic traits and reduced ability to mask or hide autistic characteristics** (card: "reduced ability to camouflage").
[msg #392]

### Autistic Burnout Symptom Checklist (ABSC), Russian translation
What it is: a tracking tool for autistic adults — explicitly including self-diagnosed people and those who strongly suspect they are autistic. The channel author translated it from Embrace Autism (embrace-autism.com) [msg #31, #386].
How to use it:
1. Tick every symptom that has bothered you **more than usual over the last two weeks** [msg #31, #386].
2. Add up the ticks to get a total score [msg #31].
3. Repeat later and compare the total with your future results, to track changes over time — the channel later nudges readers: "Давно ли вы проверяли сколько баллов у вас по чек-листу аутичного выгорания?" (*How long ago did you check your score on the autistic burnout checklist?*) [msg #31, #386].

Caveats stated with the tool: it does not replace medical advice; if you are worried about your physical or mental health, consult your doctor; it is **not yet standardised**, so it must not be used for diagnostic purposes [msg #31, #386].

### Core and additional characteristics of neurodivergent burnout (with causes)
The channel author's own overview post. Definition: burnout in people with autism and ADHD is characterised by pervasive, long-term exhaustion, loss of functionality and increased difficulty managing everyday tasks. It is largely the result of neurodivergent people living in a world not adapted to them; lack of awareness of neurodivergence, social expectations and demands, and the need to mask lead to an accumulation of life stressors [msg #388].

**Core characteristics** [msg #388]:
1. **Chronic exhaustion** — constant, deep tiredness that does not improve with rest.
2. **Reduced tolerance to stimuli** — heightened sensitivity to sensory input such as light, sound and touch.
3. **Intensified executive-function problems** — greater difficulty planning, organising, remembering and managing daily tasks.
4. **Loss of skills** — deterioration of executive functioning, including difficulty thinking, remembering, planning, doing basic self-care and managing daily activities.

**Additional characteristics** [msg #388]:
1. **Emotional dysregulation** — more difficulty managing emotions, leading to outbursts or intense anxiety.
2. **Intensification of neurodivergent traits** — increased repetitive behaviour, heightened sensory sensitivity, greater difficulty adapting to change.
3. **Cognitive impairment** — more trouble with memory and concentration, making daily tasks harder.
4. **Inability to mask** — people often lose or reduce the ability to mask their traits; the author notes this is one reason burnout is often the time when adults get identified as neurodivergent.
5. **Problems perceiving time** — greater difficulty sensing the passage of time, leading to procrastination or last-minute stress.
6. **Increased impulsivity** — which may lead to risky decisions.

**Causes** [msg #388]:
1. Masking.
2. Minimising one's needs.
3. Lack of appropriate support and accommodations.
4. Depletion of executive functions.
5. Overall load exceeding capacities and support.
6. Hyperfocus.

**Why simple rest does not help (author's explanation)** [msg #388]:
- Advice to "rest more, stress less" is built for a neurotypical nervous system, which can switch easily between calm/rest and activity/stress; when that system feels "safe" it can launch the body's recovery mechanisms.
- For neurodivergent people this does not work, partly because they only realise they are in neurodivergent burnout after they have already spent all their resources on constant attempts to match the expectations and pace of the neurotypical world.
- As a result the already inflexible nervous system gets stuck in hyperactivation (fight/flight) or hypoactivation (freeze). So, the author says, skills of **manually switching the nervous system** are needed to help it return to balance, where rest can actually be felt as beneficial.
- "Для нас отдых - это целый осознанный процесс" (*For us rest is a whole conscious process*) that the nervous system has to be taught — but rest alone will not restore a person from neurodivergent burnout. The author's position: "нейроотличное выгорание - очень комплексное состояние, которое требует комплексного подхода" (*neurodivergent burnout is a very complex state that requires a complex approach*).

### Three-level personal fatigue signs list (ФНР channel author)
A forwarded post from the author of a channel about functional neurological disorder (ФНР), inspired by another person's list, mapping their own fatigue signs into three levels. Rule: as fatigue increases, items from the previous level carry over into the next. Readers are invited to compile their own list [msg #203].

**Level 1 — medium fatigue (🟡)** [msg #203]:
1. Recalling sad stories; philosophical questions become more troubling.
2. Harder to control tics and FND symptoms.
3. The world feels too bright and loud.
4. Much more stimming.
5. Headache (often with physical fatigue).
6. Hard to sit still.

**Level 2 — high fatigue (🟣)** [msg #203]:
1. Walking in circles with poor awareness of it.
2. Becoming very hot.
3. Harder to control what one says to people — oversharing.
4. Sad thoughts appear; saying "I want to die" while understanding in the moment that it is not true.
5. More echolalia — speaking in templates and quotes, repeating after others.
6. Wrong word order and wrong word endings.
7. Hard to formulate thoughts; not understanding English / able to speak only in a language the brain picks at random.
8. Sometimes, instead of sleepiness, being unable to relax — a feeling of inner trembling.
9. Unable to start tasks or to stop them (in particular, scrolling the phone).
10. Unable to complete all the steps of the routine.
11. Irritable and tearful (in "chronic fatigue" may cry over small things all day).
12. Hard to show empathy.

**Level 3 — full exhaustion (🔵; several days in the medium and high modes)** [msg #203]:
1. On waking, staring at the wall and not grasping the urgency of tasks.
2. Hard to communicate out loud.
3. Food becomes tasteless.
4. Wanting to LIE DOWN.
5. Body aches.

### Five domains of skill regression (Lindsay M)
From the channel's translation of a longread by Lindsay M on skill regression in autism and/or ADHD. **Definition:** skill regression is the loss of previously acquired abilities, which can happen for various reasons including environmental changes, stress or mental-health problems; it is not uncommon in autistic and ADHD people at different stages of life. It can show up as a decline in communication (written or spoken), social or daily-living skills, typically during stressful periods — major life changes, increased demands, or overload. It can be especially distressing because it may feel like losing part of one's identity or independence [msg #398].

Examples by domain [msg #398]:
1. **Communication skills**
   - Verbal/written regression: someone who used to speak or write in full sentences may struggle to, preferring single words or gestures.
   - Social cues: difficulty recognising or responding to social cues that were previously understood, such as eye contact or turn-taking in conversation.
2. **Social skills**
   - Withdrawal from peers: someone who used to enjoy socialising may start avoiding group and even one-on-one activities, leading to isolation.
   - Difficulty initiating conversations: someone who could start conversations or keep up small talk may become more passive, waiting for others to involve them.
3. **Daily-living skills**
   - Self-care: someone who could maintain personal hygiene, choose clothes and dress may find they cannot manage these tasks at their usual pace.
   - Cooking: someone who used to help with cooking, grocery shopping and menu planning may lose interest and stop taking part, relying entirely on partners or food delivery.
4. **Executive-functioning skills**
   - Time management: someone who managed their schedule well may start missing deadlines or forgetting important dates.
   - Planning: someone who could plan a project effectively may struggle to identify the steps or gather the materials needed to finish it.
5. **Emotional regulation**
   - More meltdowns: someone who coped well with frustration may have emotional outbursts more often in response to minor setbacks.
   - Difficulty adapting to change: someone who adapted well to changes in routine may struggle even with minor adjustments, leading to anxiety and/or shutdown, and may start avoiding routines to prevent this dysregulation.

**Causes of skill regression:** Lindsay M says that across years of research and clinical work she has met many individual contributing factors to acute and chronic regression in neurodivergent people, which seem to group into two main, often interconnected causes: **burnout** and **unmasking** [msg #398].
The channel author adds a third, previously discussed mechanism: **state-dependent memory** — skills are used while in a special state of consciousness during masking; when burnout hits there is no resource left for masking, and therefore no access to the skills learned in that state [msg #398].

### Causes of autistic burnout — explaining it to friends and family (part 1)
Part 1 of a three-part handout for explaining autistic burnout to loved ones. It describes autistic burnout as "медицинское состояние" (*a medical condition*) caused by prolonged time in stressful conditions where an autistic person cannot cope with the demands placed on them. It stresses that autistic burnout differs from occupational (work) burnout: unlike work burnout, it can be caused by many factors in the social environment [msg #432].
Causes that "research shows" [msg #432]:
1. **Social demands:** too many social events; the need to conform to social norms; a relationship with someone who does not understand autism.
2. **Sensory demands:** an environment that does not take sensory needs into account.
3. **Demands of daily life:** an excessive number of errands and administrative tasks.
4. **Insufficient support:** at work, at school and/or at home.
Key message for the family: burnout is not caused by autism itself — "Оно возникает из-за того, что аутичные потребности не удовлетворяются в нейротипичном обществе" (*It arises because autistic needs are not met in a neurotypical society*) [msg #432].

### Consequences of autistic burnout — first-person checklist for family (part 2)
A fill-in list written in the first person, so an autistic person can show loved ones how burnout affects them [msg #433]:
1. My autism may become more visible.
2. It is harder for me to mask my autistic traits.
3. I have greater difficulties with verbal communication.
4. I experience stronger emotional dysregulation (e.g. more frequent meltdowns or shutdowns).
5. My mental health worsens (depression and anxiety increase).
6. Suicidal thoughts increase (e.g. thinking about or planning suicide).
7. I often or constantly feel physical and cognitive exhaustion and overload.
8. I become more forgetful, find it harder to concentrate, have "brain fog".
9. I cannot cope with work; I need more time to process information and complete tasks.
10. I have problems organising time.
11. I react more sharply to changes in my daily routine and to unexpected events.
12. My sensory sensitivities increase.
13. Other — a blank line to add individual manifestations.

**Important note for the family:** the state can change from day to day. On some days there may be more energy and the person may feel closer to their usual self; this does not necessarily mean they have fully recovered, nor that they exaggerated the severity of their state on other days [msg #433].

### The neurodivergent stress day and the overtrained-muscle analogy
From the PDF text version of the author's video (posted at readers' request so it can be printed). Purpose: to explain why burnout develops in neurodivergent people and why common advice fails [msg #446].

1. **The myth.** There is a mistaken belief that stress strengthens us and helps adaptation. For a neurodivergent person, the author says, stress is destructive and can cause PTSD and complex PTSD [file #446].
2. **Why the baseline load is higher.** The author claims neurodivergent people are physiologically different: a differently built brain, a sensitive nervous system, and a body that produces more cortisol which takes longer to clear, affecting many biological processes. Things that are not stressful for a neurotypical person can be stressful for them, so the stress load is higher at baseline. Neurodivergence also often comes with co-occurring conditions that affect or depend on the nervous system, so stress can quickly have physical health consequences [file #446].
3. **The analogy.** Neurodivergent burnout is the consequence of prolonged time in stressful conditions. Imagine someone training 8–12 hours every day (the author flags these numbers as "чисто мои представления" — *purely my own notion*), constantly stressing the muscles without letting them recover: instead of strengthening, the fibres get injured and break down until the body simply refuses to move. The nervous system is the same: living every day trying to meet neurotypical standards only spends the resources of an already hypersensitive nervous system [file #446].
4. **The worked example — one day.** A stressful situation both neurotypes share: being late for an important meeting (the taxi is late, everything is snowed in). For a neurotypical person the stress may end there and the nervous system returns to normal. For the hypothetical neurodivergent person stress starts in the morning: standing up from lying down causes tachycardia due to comorbid POTS (СПОТ); then overload from the noisy shower and the feel of cream/cosmetics on the face; no appetite for breakfast, so low blood sugar may trigger mast-cell activation with panic-attack-like reactions; noisy family/children add sensory overload; then the taxi is noisy and stuffy and the driver talks non-stop; at the meeting they must explain the delay without it sounding like an excuse, because any explanation becomes an "excuse" in neurotypical eyes; they must talk, and attempts to clarify also bring negative consequences; all the while they hide the need to stim, because they must not show they are neurodivergent — and hiding it also costs energy. After the meeting they must go to a crowded shop or go hungry: noise, light, crowds, constant violation of personal space, children running and shouting; at the checkout, exhausted by heat and sensory overload, they must strain to hear the cashier and answer loudly; carrying heavy bags causes joint subluxations due to hypermobility; at home there are family interactions, chores and noise until late evening [file #446].
5. **The lesson.** Every single situation that day overloaded the nervous system — "Каждая." (*Every one.*). Day after day, with more or less stress, there is never a chance to recover properly, because a nervous system in survival mode cannot really switch to rest. The outcome is a shutdown of the nervous system because it can no longer cope. The author notes this is a hypothetical example that many will recognise, and some face even heavier loads [file #446].
6. **What follows for recovery.** Once the nervous system has shut down or is stuck in hyper- or hypoarousal, exposing it even to moderate stress is critical, because, like a cornered animal, it reacts to everything as danger — "ДАЖЕ ЕСЛИ ВЫ ГОЛОВОЙ ПОНИМАЕТЕ ЧТО ЭТО НЕ ТАК И ОПАСНОСТИ НЕТ" (*EVEN IF YOU UNDERSTAND WITH YOUR HEAD THAT IT IS NOT SO AND THERE IS NO DANGER*). We cannot decide how the autonomic nervous system reacts, but we can create conditions in which it first calms down and gains a sense of safety, and only then start doing things; otherwise there is an uncomfortable swing between "заставить себя" (*forcing yourself*) and "лежать умирать" (*lying there dying*) [file #446].
7. **Why common advice fails.** Advice like "do sports, go for a walk, have fun, talk to friends" can sound maximally superficial and disrespectful. The author's own case: if constant stress has led to, for example, fibromyalgia and chronic fatigue syndrome (СХУ), physical exercise will not help, because the body reacts to exertion as danger, activity tolerance will not grow and stamina will not increase; she says some recent studies have already refuted the theory that people with ME/CFS need to exercise to expand activity tolerance. What matters more is finding a way to stabilise the nervous system so it feels safe, and only then starting measured activity — a process that takes much longer and requires much more attentiveness to oneself, from oneself and from specialists, but which she calls foundational for her recovery [file #446].

## Detailed insights

### Lou's first-person account: what autistic burnout feels like
A translated series by the autistic creator neurodivergent_lou (via the channel Нейр∞тлично):
- **The signature sign:** "Я знаю, что у меня аутичное выгорание, когда я чувствую, что могу проспать целый год, и этого будет недостаточно..." (*I know I'm in autistic burnout when I feel I could sleep for a whole year and it wouldn't be enough...*) [img #146].
- **What it can include:** it is different for everyone, but may include exhaustion; reduced ability to cope with sensory information; losing the ability to do things one used to do. **Mechanism:** autistic burnout happens when, over time, an autistic person's energy is spent faster than they can replenish it. Some people see it as a survival mechanism — the body shuts down to cope with the constant exhaustion of living in a non-autistic world [img #147].
- **Recognition and self-neglect:** autistic burnout was accepted as a concept only relatively recently, although autistic people have described it for a long time; Lou still thinks society does not understand how dangerous and debilitating it can be. Lou admits not taking her own burnout seriously — expecting to cope when she actually needed to rest and care for herself — which is hard given society's implicit message that to be worthy we must always be productive [img #148].
- **Not an inconvenience, and not the same as "burnout":** autistic burnout is still discussed as an "inconvenience" rather than the debilitating struggle it often is; autistic burnout and what the neurotypical world calls "burnout" are different things, which complicates the discussion further [img #149].
- **A concrete list of signs helps:** having a specific list of autistic burnout signs helped Lou not to miss it or try to push through it. Her list includes feeling she could sleep for years and still be tired; during earlier burnouts she dreamed of sleeping for years and waking up many years later still tired — "Как будто мой мозг подсознательно просил об отдыхе" (*As if my brain was subconsciously asking for rest*) [img #150].
- **The level of exhaustion:** she cannot really describe it — as if she could sleep for years; she cannot do anything even when she desperately wants to. The exhaustion autistic people feel in autistic burnout often differs from the exhaustion described as burnout in non-autistic society, and she considers this important to remember [img #151].
- **It is not laziness:** autistic burnout has for too long been misread as "laziness" and met with phrases like "we all get a bit tired", "you just need to try and push through", "we all need a bit of time" — which ignore how debilitating and draining it is. "Это не лень." (*It is not laziness.*) [img #152].
- **Burnout vs illness confusion:** Lou sincerely thought she was ill and it turned out she was showing all the signs of autistic burnout and had badly overstrained; at other times she really was ill and mistook it for autistic burnout. She wonders how this affects autistic people's health — whether they may not realise they are actually ill and not seek help because they wrongly assume it is "just burnout" [img #153].

### Dual masking, double burnout (AuDHD)
A translation of a text by The Ashling about people who are both autistic and ADHD (РАСДВГ / AuDHD): "Двойной маскинг означает, что отдых редко ощущается настоящим." (*Dual masking means rest rarely feels real.*) What dual masking involves [msg #726]:
- Autistic traits are smoothed over to seem "polite"; ADHD traits are hidden to look "organised".
- Speech is slowed down so as not to seem too emotional, then speeds up when emotional excitement arrives — followed by guilt.
- Eye contact takes effort; meltdowns/shutdowns are postponed; feelings are processed before being expressed.
- Every movement is rehearsed, every moment replayed; every aspect of oneself is adjusted for others' convenience — "но не для твоего" (*but not for yours*).
- The text insists this is not overthinking or drama but a matter of survival, and states that research confirms masking autism and ADHD increases burnout, fatigue and emotional strain: "Цена реальна. Ноша тяжела." (*The cost is real. The burden is heavy.*) and the world does not see it [msg #726].

### Executive dysfunction as nervous-system resistance (author's personal observation)
The author shares a thought from personal experience: sometimes executive dysfunction arises from the **resistance of the nervous system** to a task that feels unsafe. Her example: she hates unloading and putting away dishes from the dishwasher — she dislikes touching freshly washed ceramic/glass, but above all cannot stand the sound of plates and glasses being stacked. Even when someone else does it she leaves the room, because the sound is unbearable, and if she stays long she is overloaded for the rest of the day. Before she had noise-cancelling headphones she resisted the task as much as possible, because it meant sensory torture for her nervous system. The problem almost disappeared once she used headphones and felt that putting dishes away was now safe: she no longer has to force herself, knowing a solution makes the process safe, starting is much easier, and she spends less energy on the task. She adds that executive dysfunction shows even more strongly during burnout, when the nervous system becomes hypersensitive to sensory stimuli, and asks readers whether creating sensory safety improves their executive function [msg #822].

### Eating during heavy burnout
The author notes that during severe burnout a "safe food" can become something like macaroni and cheese that one fixates on — which brings nutritional deficits, weight gain and guilt about not eating something healthier [msg #707].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- **Not a formal diagnosis tool:** the ABSC is explicitly not standardised and not for diagnosis; the Higgins et al. criteria are labelled "provisional" [msg #31, #392].
- (!) CONTRADICTION / UNCLEAR on medical status: the family handout calls autistic burnout "медицинское состояние" (*a medical condition*) [msg #432], while the channel's other posts present it as a syndrome with only provisional criteria and the #28 article raises "является ли оно медицинским диагнозом" (*whether it is a medical diagnosis*) as an open question whose answer is not in the input [msg #28, #392].
- **Who is affected:** #28 and #388 extend the concept to ADHD ("people with autism and ADHD"), while Raymaker/Higgins criteria and the ABSC are framed for autistic adults [msg #28, #388, #392, #31].
- **Acute vs chronic onset:** #28 says it can arise from prolonged stress or from an acute stressful experience, whereas the Raymaker definition stresses chronic stress and duration usually over 3 months [msg #28, #392].
- **Burnout vs illness:** Lou warns that autistic people may mistake real illness for burnout and not seek help, and vice versa [img #153].
- **Hedged physiological claims:** the cortisol, POTS, mast-cell and exercise/ME-CFS claims in the stress-day essay are the author's own explanations; she cites "некоторые последние исследования" (*some recent studies*) without naming them, and flags the 8–12 hours figure as her own notion and the day as a hypothetical example [file #446].
- **Highly individual:** "Аутичное выгорание у всех разное" (*autistic burnout is different for everyone*); "Ваш опыт может отличаться" (*your experience may differ*); the state may fluctuate day to day [img #147, img #372, msg #433].
- **Safety note:** both the personal fatigue list and the family checklist mention suicidal or death-wish thoughts as burnout manifestations [msg #203, #433].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: several knowledge pieces sit in linked materials not included in the input (the #28 article, Lindsay M's full longread, the webinar recordings, part 3 of the family series) [msg #28, #398, #388, #432].
