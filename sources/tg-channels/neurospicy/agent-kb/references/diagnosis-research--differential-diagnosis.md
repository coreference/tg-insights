# Differential diagnosis: autism, ADHD and look-alike conditions

## What this covers

This topic gathers everything the channel posted on telling autism and ADHD apart from conditions that resemble them, and from each other: PTSD and complex PTSD (cPTSD), borderline personality disorder (BPD), bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, schizoid personality disorder, OCD, depression, generalized anxiety disorder, panic attacks and selective mutism. Most of the material is translated: Venn diagrams from Neurodivergent Insights, a self-reflection checklist by Lindsey Mackereth, comparison tables from a psychologist's channel, and research summaries from a psychiatry channel [msg #43, #387, #544, #592].

The channel's reason for caring: these conditions overlap, so a professional may miss autism when PTSD is present, and "от этого зависит успешность терапевтических вмешательств" (*the success of therapeutic interventions depends on this*). The same point is made about bipolar disorder, where treatment success, including medication, depends on telling the overlapping traits apart [msg #43, #44]. A recurring message is that two conditions can be present at once ("Или оба?", *Or both?*), so the question is often "which, or both" rather than "which one" [msg #387, #544].

## Core ideas

- **Overlap is real, so misdiagnosis is common.** Autism and ADHD share symptoms with many psychiatric conditions, and they often co-occur with them. Clinicians must screen for both and ask what *causes* a trait, not just whether it is present (see *Why ADHD and autism are hard to tell apart*) [msg #58, #544].
- **The same behavior can have different causes.** The Mackereth checklist and the Venn diagrams repeatedly separate traits by motive. Avoiding eye contact because it distracts you points to autism; avoiding it out of distrust points to cPTSD. Repetitive behavior that soothes points to autism; compulsions tied to a specific fear point to OCD [msg #387, #601].
- **Onset and course distinguish conditions.** Autism and ADHD are present from childhood or early on. PTSD follows a traumatic event. Bipolar disorder often develops in late adolescence or youth. Schizophrenia symptoms appear in adolescence [msg #592, #594, #598, #600].
- **Co-occurrence is the rule, not the exception.** Autism often comes with at least one other condition, bipolar disorder among them. BPD and autism frequently co-occur, and autistic people are more vulnerable to developing BPD [msg #44, #544].
- **Some states look alike but are not disorders.** Verbal shutdowns are reactions to overload, not selective mutism. Autistic meltdowns may be mistaken for panic attacks. Autistic burnout is not the same as depression [msg #736, #482, #595].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Lindsey Mackereth's checklist "Autism or cPTSD?"
**What it is and who made it.** A checklist that helps sort out the differences between autism and complex PTSD. Its author is Lindsey Mackereth, a licensed mental-health specialist and expert in autism assessment, who built it from ten years of work with neurodivergent people. The channel translated it into Russian as a PDF titled "Аутизм или кПТСР? Чек-лист", subtitled "Инструмент для самопознания, помощи терапевтам и поддержки союзников" (*a tool for self-knowledge, for helping therapists and for supporting allies*) [msg #387, file #387].

**When to use it.** The post lists four uses: (1) self-exploration; (2) understanding loved ones better; (3) as supplementary material for therapy or coaching; (4) when preparing for an autism or mental-health assessment. It can also help therapists, coaches, assessors, medical workers and allies of neurodiverse people and trauma survivors. The post stresses: "это не диагностический инструмент, а скорее помощник для самоанализа" (*it is not a diagnostic tool but rather an aid to self-analysis*) [msg #387].

**Background page: Autism vs cPTSD.** Page 1 contrasts the two conditions on four points [file #387]:
- *Autism.* A neurodevelopmental difference, defined as a disorder (autism spectrum disorder) in DSM-5-TR and ICD-11. It is an innate neurodivergence, characterized by five things: (1) social differences; (2) heightened or lowered sensitivity; (3) repetitive movements; (4) specific, focused areas of interest; (5) difficulty with change and disrupted routine. It lasts a lifetime. Difficulties linked to autistic traits can be eased when a person's sensory, communicative, social and psychological needs are understood and accommodated.
- *Complex PTSD.* Not currently in DSM-5-TR but identified in ICD-11. Many symptoms overlap with PTSD. It is acquired through repeated exposure to eight kinds of experience: (1) neglect or trauma, including emotional trauma; (2) bullying; (3) name-calling, being ignored or criticized by mentors or loved ones; (4) discrimination; (5) prolonged crisis situations; (6) persecution; (7) gaslighting or other forms of manipulation; (8) other forms of abuse. In many cases it is treatable: symptoms can often be treated or managed with empirically supported trauma-focused and skill-building psychotherapy.

**How to fill it in.** For each trait you *currently and most often* identify with, put a tick in the left (lilac) column. Then tick the matching cells in the Autism (green) and/or cPTSD (coral) columns, but only the cells that are colored and contain a checkbox. At the end, count the ticks in each column [file #387].

**Group A: traits that count for both autism and cPTSD** (both cells colored; page 2) [file #387]:
1. Routine helps me feel the world is safer and more predictable.
2. I have chronic sleep problems.
3. I spend a lot of time alone compared with others.
4. I often feel I am not like everyone else.
5. It is hard for me to make and/or keep friendships.
6. I like to control events in my life.
7. I experience dissociation (feeling detached from myself, other people or surroundings).
8. I have repetitive body movements or behavioral habits I perform regularly: skin picking, rocking, pacing, making sounds, repeating words, listening to songs on repeat, nail biting, fidgeting.
9. I use repetitive movements or behavior to cope with high stress and/or emotional overload.
10. I tend to experience overload more often than others.
11. I experience my emotions very intensely and/or find it hard to identify them.
12. It is hard for me to share my emotions with others.

**Group B: traits that count for autism only** (only the green cell is colored; the last item of page 2, all of page 3, the first five items of page 4) [file #387]:
13. I have particular areas of interest that I spend a lot of time studying, engaging with or discussing, or that I focus on intensely.
14. I rely on important routines to feel like myself.
15. If my routine is disrupted, I quickly become irritable and disorganized, even if I hide it.
16. I prefer to avoid eye contact because it distracts me when I am trying to talk and/or listen.
17. Time alone helps me cope with the sensory overload I experience in public places.
18. I often feel I am missing something when I try to understand what others think or feel.
19. It is hard for me to understand people when they don't say what they mean.
20. I would like more relationships but don't understand how to create and maintain them.
21. I don't feel much motivation for social situations and usually don't get much benefit from them.
22. I use repetitive movements or behavior to express happiness, excitement or joy.
23. I use repetitive movements or behavior even when I am calm.
24. I don't share my emotions because I often can't work out how to explain what I feel.
25. My overload is usually linked to overstimulation from sensory input (crowds, light, sounds, clothing, other sensations).
26. I often avoid situations for sensory reasons (too loud, noisy, bright, cold/hot, smells).
27. I reached at least one developmental milestone late: started talking, walking or tying shoelaces late, learned to drive late, became independent from parents late.
28. I feel a lot of empathy for animals and/or objects.
29. I can lose the ability to speak when I am very tense.
30. I often find small talk hard to understand and hard to keep up my part of it.
31. I like to control things because it prevents sensory overload.

**Group C: traits that count for cPTSD only** (only the coral cell is colored; the rest of page 4 and all of page 5) [file #387]:
32. I avoid sharing my emotions because I don't trust others or feel strong shame and guilt about my emotions.
33. People seem to like me, but I usually feel lonely because I rarely feel an emotional connection with anyone for long.
34. I often find myself skeptical and/or pessimistic about what others think or feel.
35. It is hard for me to trust others.
36. I often become moody or have mood swings in relationships with others.
37. I usually perceive the world as a place I cannot trust.
38. I need to control things because otherwise I don't trust them.
39. When stressed, I tend to get angry at myself and/or others.
40. I isolate myself to avoid pain from others.
41. I prefer to avoid eye contact because I don't trust others enough to allow that closeness.
42. I feel safer when I am alone.
43. I have intrusive thoughts, flashbacks or nightmares.
44. I feel constantly on guard, expecting danger.
45. I struggle with chronic symptoms of depression and/or suicidal thoughts.
46. I don't rely on routines, but they often help me avoid triggers of hypervigilance or irritability.
47. I often have disturbing dreams that leave me uneasy.
48. I often avoid situations that remind me of difficult events or trauma.
49. I usually feel shame or guilt about myself or feel like a failure.
50. It is hard for me to feel positive emotions.
51. My overload is usually linked to emotional triggers or flashbacks of past interactions with other people.

**The pattern behind the items.** Several items come in matched pairs that differ only in motive: eye contact avoided because it distracts (16, autism) vs out of distrust (41, cPTSD); control that prevents sensory overload (31) vs control because of distrust (38); emotions not shared because they are hard to explain (24) vs because of distrust or shame (32); overload from sensory input (25) vs from emotional triggers or flashbacks (51); routines relied on to feel like oneself (14) vs routines not relied on but useful for avoiding hypervigilance triggers (46); time alone for sensory recovery (17) vs feeling safer alone and isolating to avoid pain (40, 42) [file #387].

**How to read the result.** Comparing the columns is for self-analysis only. It is meant to give some idea of whether your symptoms and traits fit autism, complex PTSD or a combination. It is not a diagnostic tool, and there are no "баллов" (*points / scores*) that lead to a diagnosis or identification. Bring any questions, concerns or insights from it to your next session with a therapist or coach. The author also calls it a great tool to take to an autism or mental-health assessment. The content does not replace professional medical consultation, examination, diagnosis or treatment. Always consult a doctor or other qualified specialist before starting, changing or stopping any health-related treatment [file #387].

### C-PTSD vs BPD table
A two-card comparison from the channel Домик психолога Ани [img #297, img #298].

**Similarities between C-PTSD and BPD** [img #297]:
1. Impulsivity.
2. Emotional instability and outbursts of anger.
3. Dissociation and flashbacks.
4. Fear of rejection.
5. Maladaptive ways of coping with emotions and stress.

**Differences** (C-PTSD on the left, BPD on the right) [img #298]:
1. *Relationships.* C-PTSD: a pull toward solitude, difficulty getting close and building relationships, and a marked level of avoidance. BPD: unstable relationships with people and a strong sense of emptiness and loneliness.
2. *Under stress.* C-PTSD: dissociation, depersonalization, irritability and substance use. BPD: hysterics, suicide attempts, risky behavior and self-harm.
3. *Self-esteem.* C-PTSD: stably negative self-esteem. BPD: unstable self-esteem that swings from idealization to devaluation and self-criticism.

### C-PTSD vs PTSD table
Same source; the post title is "Таблицы сходств и различий К-ПТСР с ПРЛ и ПТСР" (*tables of similarities and differences of C-PTSD with BPD and PTSD*) [msg #300].

**Similarities between C-PTSD and PTSD** [img #299]:
1. Flashbacks, intrusive memories, nightmares and insomnia.
2. Memory problems and reduced concentration.
3. Strong fears, a sense of danger, numbness and feeling cut off from others.
4. Guilt and shame, irritability and avoidance.
5. Hypervigilance, an exaggerated startle response and panic attacks.

**Symptoms present in C-PTSD (what sets it apart)** [img #300]:
1. Behavioral disturbances: self-destructive behavior and outbursts of anger.
2. Difficulty maintaining relationships, and a change in one's former personality.
3. Persistent, long-lasting disturbances in the affective sphere: dissociative states and heightened emotional reactivity.
4. Origin: PTSD arises from a single traumatic event. C-PTSD arises from prolonged, repeated traumatic events that continue for months or even years.

### BPD vs autism Venn diagram ("Misdiagnosis Monday")
**Context.** This diagram comes from Neurodivergent Insights' "Понедельник неправильных диагнозов" (*Misdiagnosis Monday*) series, translated by the channel, with an article by dr. Megan Anna Neff. Neff's argument: BPD is often misdiagnosed in autistic women and genderqueer people. It is further complicated because BPD and autism often co-occur and autistic people are more vulnerable to developing BPD, so a person can have both. If the underlying autism is missed, the borderline symptoms usually intensify. Many specialists do not consider autism when diagnosing BPD, so she says autism should always be on the differential list when BPD is suspected, precisely because the autistic neurotype makes people more prone to developing it [msg #544].

The diagram matches up four themes, then lists the overlap and each side's distinguishing traits [img #544]:

1. **Identity.** BPD: *identity instability*, a persistent pattern of instability in relationships, self-image and mood. Autism: *masked identity*; autistic people who actively mask often have a blurred sense of self and form their identity through interaction with other people.
2. **Heightened emotions.** BPD: emotional distress caused by attachment trauma, feelings of abandonment, conflicts and reminders of past trauma. Autism: emotional distress caused by unexpected changes, social misunderstandings, and cognitive and sensory overload.
3. **Inconsistency in relationships.** BPD: intense relationships often accompanied by idealization and devaluation of the partner. Autism: difficulty maintaining friendships, or a tendency to have one intense friendship at a time.
4. **Emptiness vs special interests.** BPD: *constant emptiness*, a persistent feeling of emptiness, uselessness and lack of purpose. Autism: special interests make up a large part of identity, and without them the person may feel empty and lost.

**Overlap (both):** (1) dissociation; (2) a blurred sense of self; (3) impaired self-esteem; (4) relationship difficulties; (5) a tendency to systematize and categorize; (6) heightened risk of victimization, suicidality and self-harm; (7) higher rates of eating disorders and substance abuse; (8) difficulty controlling impulses; (9) difficulty regulating intense emotions; (10) paranoid thoughts; (11) rejection sensitivity [img #544].

**Distinguishing features of BPD:** (1) a pervasive fear of being abandoned; (2) destructive impulsivity that arises when the person feels abandoned; (3) projection, often used as a form of communication [img #544].

**Distinguishing features of autism:** (1) a monotropic thinking style; (2) self-soothing through repetition or keeping to routine; (3) unconsciously missing hidden social signals [img #544].

Bottom line of the card: "У человека могут быть оба состояния одновременно" (*a person can have both conditions at the same time*). Its note says the diagram reflects common experiences, individual experience may vary, and it is not meant for diagnosis [img #544].

### PTSD vs autism Venn diagram
Translated by Аутичные переводы from Neurodivergent Insights [img #592].
- **PTSD only (5):** (1) hypervigilance; (2) avoidant behavior; (3) unwanted memories and flashbacks; (4) nightmares; (5) arises after a traumatic event.
- **Both (10):** (1) difficulty forming close relationships; (2) heightened risk of becoming a victim of violence; (3) difficulty controlling impulses and intense emotions; (4) high sensitivity to incoming sensory information; (5) high risk of self-harm, suicide, alcohol or drug abuse, and developing depression; (6) negative beliefs about oneself and the world; (7) hypervigilance, nervous-system reactivity and an exaggerated startle response; (8) sleep problems; (9) dissociation; (10) stimming.

The channel's own PTSD vs autism mini-article frames the issue: the two are separate clinical conditions that share features, which makes them hard to differentiate. A professional may miss autism when PTSD is present, so it is crucial to know where the two overlap and where they differ [msg #43].

### Depression vs autistic burnout Venn diagram
[img #595]
- **Depression only (7):** (1) an intense sense of one's own worthlessness; (2) reduced or lost ability to feel pleasure; (3) suicidal tendencies; (4) sleep disturbances; (5) negative thoughts about oneself, the world and the future, which respond well to CBT correction; (6) usually a good response to behavioral activation; (7) symptoms last more than two weeks.
- **Both (9):** (1) loss of motivation; (2) appetite change; (3) difficulty concentrating; (4) social withdrawal; (5) executive-function problems; (6) reduced or slowed physical activity; (7) tearfulness, getting upset easily; (8) fatigue; (9) a feeling of emptiness.
- **Autistic burnout only (6):** (1) a worse response to behavioral activation; (2) heightened sensory sensitivity; (3) attempts to reframe negative thoughts can increase shame; (4) usually sets in after a period of activity with high sensory and social demands; (5) a chronic feeling of exhaustion; (6) loss of skills.

The card defines behavioral activation as a psychological therapy that encourages a person to develop or return to activities that matter to them. The practical point is that two standard depression tools, behavioral activation and CBT reframing, help in depression but may work worse in autistic burnout, or even increase shame [img #595].

[img #597]

### Bipolar disorder vs ADHD Venn diagram
[img #598]
- **Bipolar only (6):** (1) usually a good response to mood stabilizers; (2) often develops in late adolescence or youth; (3) psychotic symptoms may be present; (4) mood is clearly divided into episodes (mania and depression); (5) mood changes are sudden or cyclical; (6) SSRIs can trigger mania.
- **Both (14):** (1) hyperfocus; (2) hyperactivity; (3) sleep problems; (4) sensory sensitivity; (5) difficulty with impulse control; (6) heightened risk of becoming a victim of violence; (7) rejection sensitivity; (8) problems with attention, concentration and memory; (9) difficulty switching between tasks; (10) high risks of eating disorders, OCD, suicidal behavior and alcohol or drug dependence; (11) difficulty with emotional regulation; (12) executive-function difficulties; (13) irritability and tension; (14) dangerous behavior.
- **ADHD only (6):** (1) a good response to psychostimulants; (2) present in childhood; (3) mood changes are situational; (4) difficulty understanding object permanence; (5) an interest-based nervous system; (6) time blindness.

The channel's own bipolar vs autism mini-article adds that autism often comes with at least one other mental or health condition, bipolar disorder among them. Current research suggests bipolar disorder and autism may share genetic links and often co-exist, which makes diagnosis hard because the traits overlap. Telling them apart matters because the success of therapy, including medication, may depend on it [msg #44].

### ADHD vs autism Venn diagram
[img #599]
- **ADHD only (5):** (1) hyperactivity and impulsivity; (2) attention problems and a higher risk of substance abuse and mental disorders; (3) difficulty understanding social signals because of concentration problems; (4) craving for novelty and new experiences; (5) difficulty inhibiting responses.
- **Both (16):** (1) stimming; (2) special interests; (3) social differences; (4) impulsive behavior; (5) rejection sensitivity; (6) difficulty with emotional regulation; (7) difficulty with interoception (the ability to perceive one's body); (8) interwoven, interconnected thinking patterns; (9) difficulty switching from one task to another; (10) difficulty identifying a pattern of communication (information processing tied to behavior, and difficulty finding common interests and connecting ideas); (11) executive-functioning difficulties; (12) an interest-based nervous system; (13) differences in eye contact; (14) heightened risk of victimization (becoming a victim in relationships); (15) strong focus; (16) sensory problems.
- **Autism only (4):** (1) soothes oneself through repetitive behavior; (2) strict adherence to rules and rituals; (3) craves familiarity and routine in life, not particularly seeking new experiences; (4) difficulty intuitively perceiving all social signals.

The key contrasts: ADHD seeks novelty while autism craves the familiar, and social signals are missed in ADHD because of attention but in autism because of intuitive perception [img #599].

### Schizophrenia vs autism Venn diagram
[img #600]
- **Schizophrenia only (5):** (1) hallucinations; (2) disorganized thinking; (3) paranoid and delusional thinking patterns; (4) symptoms appear in adolescence; (5) psychosis.
- **Both (14):** (1) dissociation; (2) depression and anxiety; (3) social isolation; (4) social difficulties; (5) an inward orientation; (6) heightened suicide risk; (7) executive-function difficulties; (8) widespread substance dependence; (9) difficulty with eye contact; (10) widespread self-harming behavior; (11) sensory sensitivity; (12) perceptual disturbance; (13) flattened affect; (14) disorganized thinking.
- **Autism only (5):** (1) echolalia; (2) repetitive behavior and routine; (3) difficulty with social reciprocity; (4) symptoms appear at an early age; (5) special interests.

The translator's note explains that "perceptual disturbance" in the overlap means flashes of light, or the sensation of being touched, while staying aware that it isn't really happening [img #600].

### Selective mutism vs verbal shutdown
**Purpose.** Both states can make speech impossible, but for different reasons. Selective mutism is an anxiety disorder with stable patterns. Verbal shutdowns are temporary reactions to stress or overload. Translated for the channel from autisticguidetothegalaxy [msg #736].

**Selective mutism, five features:** (1) it is an anxiety disorder; (2) speech is possible in some situations but not others; (3) the pattern is stable, safe spaces (such as home) versus unsafe spaces (such as school or work); (4) it most often starts in childhood but can persist into adulthood; (5) later onset is possible but rare [msg #736].

**Verbal shutdown, four features:** (1) a reaction to stress or overload; (2) it can happen in any situation where demands, emotions or sensory stimuli overwhelm; (3) speech returns once the nervous system settles; (4) it occurs in autism, ADHD, trauma and other conditions [msg #736].

**How each feels.** In selective mutism, the words are there but feel "заблокированными" (*blocked*); anxiety makes speech impossible. In a verbal shutdown, the brain and body are saving energy and the speech function temporarily "отключается" (*switches off*) [msg #736].

**Potential triggers of selective mutism:** (1) social anxiety; (2) fear of evaluation or embarrassment; (3) talking with authority figures; (4) new or unfamiliar situations [msg #736].

**Potential triggers of a verbal shutdown:** (1) sensory overload (noise, light, smell, touch); (2) emotional overload; (3) stress, fatigue or burnout; (4) too many demands at once [msg #736].

**Key differences:**
1. *Duration.* Selective mutism is usually long-lasting and situational; verbal shutdowns are usually short and temporary.
2. *Pattern.* Selective mutism follows a stable "safe vs unsafe" pattern; shutdowns can happen anywhere at peak overload.
3. *Trigger.* Selective mutism comes from social anxiety and fear of speaking; shutdowns come from overload, stress or exhaustion [msg #736].

**Why it matters.** Knowing the difference helps you explain your experience and helps others respond with patience rather than pressure. Both states need understanding, a safe environment and alternative ways of communicating. Selective mutism is an anxiety disorder, not a trait of autism or ADHD, but an autistic person or a person with ADHD can also have it. In short: "Селективный мутизм = симптом тревожного расстройства. Вербальный шатдаун = реакция на аутистическую перегрузку." (*Selective mutism = a symptom of an anxiety disorder. Verbal shutdown = a reaction to autistic overload.*) [msg #736]

**Admin's illustrations.** The channel admin gives two examples. For selective mutism she thinks of Raj from *The Big Bang Theory*, who could not talk to women because of anxiety. For a verbal shutdown she describes being asked at home where something is and being unable to explain, because there is no energy to form words, so it is easier to get up and show where it is. What she feels is specifically a lack of resource for talking. This also shows a shutdown can happen in a safe place such as home, unlike selective mutism [msg #736].

## Detailed insights

### Why ADHD and autism are hard to tell apart
Many neurodivergent people struggle to get an accurate diagnosis, especially with ADHD and ASD. Research shows the two overlap substantially in symptoms and in genetic and neurobiological basis, and many people with ADHD show autistic traits and vice versa [msg #58].

The post names these points that make them hard to separate:
1. Similar problems with social interaction, communication and emotion regulation.
2. Overlapping difficulties with executive functions, attention and sensory processing.
3. Repetitive behaviors and interests in both groups.
The post ends the list with "и др..." (*and the like*) without naming further items [msg #58].

ADHD and ASD also often occur together in one person, which complicates the picture further. The author's conclusion: when assessing either condition, specialists should screen for signs of both and ask the right questions to find out what causes a trait. In her view, only a comprehensive diagnosis can establish a person's precise neurodivergence profile and build the most effective support plan. A longer article by clinical psychologist Anna Neff, "СДВГ vs. РАС", was translated for the channel [msg #58].

### Schizophrenia and autism: shared history
Their history is tangled: for many years autism was considered part of the schizophrenia spectrum. In the first edition of the DSM, children with autistic symptoms were labeled as having childhood schizophrenia, and only in 1980 did autism get its own category, in DSM-III. The channel's mini-article covers a brief history of autism, a definition of schizophrenia, the overlapping traits and the differences, stressing that telling them apart matters for accurate diagnosis [msg #50].

### Bipolar I and schizophrenia: one clinical axis (research summary)
Source: the channel Идентификация с дуркой, summarizing a study published on nature.com (article s41398-025-03403-6) [msg #663].
- **The problem.** Schizophrenia and bipolar I share symptoms such as hallucinations and delusions, and overlapping phenomenology and neurobiology. When bipolar I has psychotic features, it is often misdiagnosed as schizophrenia. That delays correct treatment and leads to a worse course of illness [msg #663].
- **Schizophrenia is heterogeneous.** Patients can have completely different symptoms, from auditory hallucinations and paranoia to marked apathy and detachment, so a single universal profile is almost impossible [msg #663].
- **Method.** The researchers used the machine-learning method U-MAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) to simplify and visualize complex data and show how symptoms group into clusters, "превратить хаос множества психиатрических описаний в наглядную карту" (*to turn the chaos of many psychiatric descriptions into a clear map*). They added network analysis to find which symptoms are central, or dominant, in a disorder's overall picture. The goal was to look at the structure of the disorders through real symptom patterns rather than formal diagnoses [msg #663].
- **Finding 1: schizophrenia.** Cluster analysis cleanly separated the negative symptoms (avolition, anhedonia, mutism) from the others (delusions, hallucinations, disorganization, affective disturbances). The negative symptoms correlate strongly with each other and weakly with the rest, suggesting they are a relatively autonomous syndrome block in the pathogenesis of schizophrenia [msg #663].
- **Finding 2: bipolar I.** Two clusters emerged: one where patients had almost no psychotic symptoms, and one where most did. This matches the DSM-5 distinction between bipolar I with and without psychotic features. Unlike schizophrenia, though, "ни один отдельный симптом не позволял чётко различить эти две группы" (*no single symptom clearly distinguished these two groups*). The split rested on a weak, blurry combination of features with no clear "anchor" symptoms. Sub-clustering showed that patients without psychosis also had almost no anxiety symptoms, while anxiety was more common in the psychosis group. Overall, the bipolar I symptom picture was diffuse, with no stable patterns [msg #663].
- **Finding 3: central symptoms.** In bipolar I, network analysis found positive symptoms central, especially paranoid delusions. In schizophrenia there was a single patient cluster, and network analysis confirmed negative symptoms are central, which Eugen Bleuler had begun to describe in the 19th–20th centuries. The more negative symptoms a patient had, the more other psychotic manifestations they had too, suggesting negative symptoms may mark a severe course of schizophrenia [msg #663].

### Hallucinations in BPD (research summary, part 1)
Source: the channel Идентификация с дуркой [msg #734].
- **Why clinicians call them "pseudohallucinations".** In clinical practice, hallucinations in BPD are often labeled "псевдогаллюцинации" (*pseudohallucinations*), signaling doubt that they are real hallucinations. One reason may be that DSM-5 says that in BPD, hallucinations occur "лишь у некоторых людей в периоды стресса" (*only in some people during periods of stress*). So clinicians may hesitate to call every positive perceptual disturbance in this group a hallucination [msg #734].
- **What research shows.** Research over the past decade shows genuine hallucinations in BPD are not rare, with prevalence of 26–54%. They span several senses: 21–59% are auditory, 30–33% visual, 10–30% olfactory and 13% tactile [msg #734].
- **Chronic, not transient.** Auditory hallucinations, the most common, included demeaning and threatening voices lasting for years (on average more than 15) and causing marked distress. This points to a chronic symptom, sharply contrasting with the view of "stress-induced" or "temporary" hallucinations in BPD [msg #734].
- **Definitions.** A *pseudohallucination* is when a person hears, sees or feels something but is aware that the experience is subjective ("я слышу голоса, но понимаю, что они в моей голове", *I hear voices but understand they are in my head*); full conviction that it comes from outside is missing. A *hallucination in the strict sense* is perceived as existing objectively in the outside world ("голос исходит из соседней комнаты", *the voice is coming from the next room*). The author concludes that in content, form and psychological impact, BPD hallucinations are close to those in schizophrenia. BPD patients often partly doubt the source, yet still experience the voices as real enough to count as hallucinations [msg #734].
- **Typical features of the voices.** They occurred at least once an hour, lasted several minutes, were perceived as inside or near the head, and were about as loud as the patient's own voice [msg #734].
- **Known voices, trauma content.** More than half (61%) heard the voices of people they knew, often relatives, abusive figures or significant people from the past, which the author reads as an **internalization of traumatic experience**. The content was almost always sharply negative: direct insults, accusations, attacks on self-esteem and mockery. Some patients reported voices commenting on their actions in real time, which resembles the "running commentary" in schizophrenia [msg #734].
- **Place on a continuum.** Hallucinations occur in 3–6% of the general population and 75% of people with schizophrenia, so BPD sits in an intermediate position on this nosological continuum. By the phenomenology of the hallucinations, BPD patients appear to belong at the severe end of the spectrum [msg #734].

### OCD as a bee: how OCD works and how to talk back to it
A translated text by mylovelyocd that explains OCD's mechanism through metaphors and offers replacement phrases [msg #626].
- **Mechanism.** OCD is like a bee: it buzzes constantly and keeps the brain on high alert. The more you swat at it, the angrier it gets. The more compulsions you perform, the harsher OCD becomes; the more you argue with it, the more confidently it demands. OCD is also like a thief, stealing moments, pleasure and calm when they cross its path [msg #626].
- **Reframe 1.** Instead of "никто больше не думает об этом!" (*nobody else thinks about this!*), try "я не знаю, что происходит в головах других людей. Все, что я могу, это жить свою жизнь в соответствии с моими ценностями" (*I don't know what goes on in other people's heads. All I can do is live my life according to my values*) [msg #626].
- **Reframe 2.** Instead of "у меня все иначе! Я ужасен!" (*it's different for me! I'm terrible!*), try "может, у меня все иначе, кто знает. В любом случае, я в безопасности" (*maybe it is different for me, who knows. Either way, I am safe*) [msg #626].
- **What OCD uses against you.** Your past, your thoughts, your diagnoses, and also your lack of diagnoses [msg #626].
- **Content.** OCD is complex; obsessions often involve contamination, violence, sexual themes and other things [msg #626].
- **Stance.** OCD should not take away life's pleasant moments. It is a liar that will say anything to convince you, but you, not OCD, are responsible for your life, and you deserve happiness [msg #626].
