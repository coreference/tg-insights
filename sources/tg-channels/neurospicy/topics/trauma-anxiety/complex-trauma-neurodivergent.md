# Complex trauma (C-PTSD) in neurodivergent lives

## What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel taught about complex post-traumatic stress disorder (C-PTSD): how it differs from single-event PTSD, what emotional flashbacks are, what trauma looks like in everyday behaviour (people-pleasing, hypervigilance, extreme independence, suppressed needs), and why it is so common among autistic, ADHD and gifted people, especially those diagnosed late. It also covers trauma-related memory loss, the way autistic children's pain goes unbelieved, why neurodivergent women are especially exposed to abuse and gaslighting (with a full practical guide to recognising and countering gaslighting), and a therapist's statement of what C-PTSD therapy aims at [msg #301, #305, #466, #528, #648, #650, #785, #787].

According to the source, naming C-PTSD correctly matters because it shows that these patterns are trauma responses rather than character traits, and that you are not "overdoing it", not dramatising and not a failure [msg #648]. The channel adds that both PTSD and C-PTSD can substantially lower quality of life, so professional help is strongly advised when symptoms are noticed [msg #305].

## Core ideas

- **C-PTSD comes from duration and repetition, not one event.** PTSD follows one event that broke an otherwise normal life; C-PTSD comes from prolonged, systematically repeated trauma that piles up over time. It has the PTSD symptoms plus problems with emotion regulation, self-image and relationships (see *PTSD vs C-PTSD comparison*) [msg #301, #648].
- **Emotional flashbacks are re-lived feelings, not remembered pictures.** The person feels the old fear, shame or despair as if it were happening now, often without any image or clear memory, and puts the blame on the present situation (see *Emotional flashback recognition*) [msg #305].
- **Late-diagnosed neurodivergent people often carry C-PTSD.** Being punished for traits they could not control, masking, and growing up misunderstood are repeated emotional injuries. Survival strategies such as masking, people-pleasing, over-effort or withdrawal do not disappear after diagnosis [msg #648].
- **Survival adaptations are not defects.** The channel's message: "Вы не слишком чувствительны. Вы выживали." (*You are not too sensitive. You were surviving.*) Healing means gradually unlearning seeing yourself as the problem and rebuilding safety inside yourself [msg #648].
- **Trauma can erase memory, and this is protection, not lying.** Forgetting dates, events or whole years can be how the mind coped with unbearable pain (see *Eight principles for treating people whose memory was damaged by trauma*) [msg #650].
- **Neurodivergent women face double vulnerability to abuse.** Gaslighting, which destroys trust in one's own perception, can prepare the ground for harsher violence (see the gaslighting frameworks) [msg #785].
- **C-PTSD therapy aims at quality of life, not productivity.** This is a therapist's reframe quoted by the author [msg #787].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### PTSD vs C-PTSD comparison
Purpose: to tell the two conditions apart and to recognise C-PTSD's wider set of symptoms and everyday manifestations. The channel author presented this as a summary of a longer article on C-PTSD [msg #301].

**Symptoms shared by PTSD and C-PTSD.** Both involve psychological and behavioural stress symptoms:
1. Flashbacks («флэшбеки»).
2. Hypervigilance («гипервигилантность»).
3. Attempts to avoid unpleasant reminders of the traumatic event(s) [msg #301].

**The core difference.**
- *PTSD:* "При ПТСР все было в целом нормально, пока не произошло травмирующее событие, которое изменило ситуацию." (*With PTSD, everything was broadly normal until a traumatic event happened that changed the situation.*) [msg #301]
- *C-PTSD:* what matters most is the duration and systematic repetition of traumatic events, which accumulate or become more complex over time. C-PTSD includes some PTSD symptoms plus additional ones [msg #301].

**Additional C-PTSD symptoms (all ten in the source):**
1. Difficulty controlling emotions.
2. Strong anger at, or distrust of, the world.
3. A constant sense of emptiness or hopelessness.
4. Feeling permanently "broken" or useless.
5. Feeling completely different from other people.
6. Feeling that no one can understand what happened to you.
7. Avoiding friendships/relationships, or struggling to keep them going.
8. Frequent dissociative symptoms (depersonalisation/derealisation).
9. Physical symptoms: headaches, dizziness, chest and stomach pain.
10. Regular suicidal thoughts [msg #301].

**Manifestations of trauma («Проявления Травмы»), five everyday behaviours:**
1. Crying when expressing your true emotions.
2. Holding back the truth to avoid conflict.
3. Extreme independence.
4. Inaction or indecision out of fear of punishment.
5. People-pleasing («угодничество (пипл плизинг)») in order to get love [msg #301].

**Three further features of C-PTSD:**
- *C-PTSD and pessimism.* People with C-PTSD often cannot keep up optimism about the future. This can lead to a lack of motivation to do anything in the present, even when they want to on a cognitive level [msg #301].
- *The core dilemma of C-PTSD.* "стремление к отношениям прямо противоречит воспоминаниям, которые говорят о том, что отношения небезопасны" (*the longing for relationships directly contradicts memories that say relationships are unsafe*) [msg #301].
- *Suppression of needs.* People with C-PTSD often hold back from expressing their needs. They believe it is easier to bear the disappointment of an unmet need they never voiced than of one they voiced and that still went unmet [msg #301].

### Emotional flashback recognition
Purpose: to recognise when an overwhelming present-day reaction is actually re-lived old trauma. The post is an excerpt from the channel's long-read on C-PTSD vs PTSD [msg #305].

**What it is.** People with C-PTSD can be especially prone to "emotional flashbacks". In these they feel the intense emotions they had during the trauma, such as fear, shame, sadness or despair. They react to a present event as if it caused those feelings and do not realise it is a flashback. Unlike ordinary remembering, the person does not just recall the past; they re-live it as happening here and now. And unlike PTSD flashbacks, emotional flashbacks may come without clear visual images or memories of a specific traumatic event [msg #305].

**The sudden flood of emotions can include (all seven in the source):**
1. Fear or panic.
2. Helplessness or despair.
3. Intense shame or guilt.
4. Anger or irritability out of proportion to the current situation.
5. Feeling detached from reality (derealisation) or from oneself (depersonalisation).
6. Deep sadness.
7. A sense of one's own worthlessness or defectiveness [msg #305].

**Thoughts during a flashback.** The person may feel hypersensitive or think something is wrong with them. Self-deprecating thoughts may come up, for example "Я ничтожество" (*I am a nobody*) or "Я ущербный и уродливый" (*I am defective and ugly*) [msg #305].

**Triggers.** Flashbacks can be set off by situations, people or circumstances that somehow resemble the original trauma: smells, places or similar experiences. Some triggers are easy to identify; some are harder. Often the person does not see the link between the trigger and the emotional reaction, especially when the trigger is tied to memories they are not aware of [msg #305].

**Worked example.** A person feels intense shame and embarrassment over a mistake at work. The intensity may come from re-living the harsh criticism and judgment they got from caregivers in childhood. If they do not realise it is an emotional flashback, they will think the shame comes only from the work mistake, and as a result feel inadequate, useless and stupid. The lesson: misreading the flashback adds a second layer of self-blame [msg #305].

**Author's personal note (alexithymia).** The channel author says that, as a person with alexithymia, it was especially hard to learn to recognise that "сейчас происходит эмоциональный флэшбэк и поэтому я себя так плохо чувствую" (*an emotional flashback is happening now, and that is why I feel so bad*) [msg #305].

**Caveat from the post.** Both PTSD and C-PTSD can substantially reduce quality of life. If you notice such symptoms in yourself or loved ones, the post says it is crucial to seek professional help: a qualified specialist can build an individual treatment plan and support recovery [msg #305].

### C-PTSD after late neurodivergent diagnosis
Purpose: to explain why pain persists after an adult autism/ADHD diagnosis, and to give a frame for grief and healing. Source: Neurotribe [msg #648].

**The situation.** Realising you are neurodivergent as an adult can be both a revelation and a blow to the gut ("одновременно озарением и ударом под дых"). Your whole life story starts to rearrange itself. The shame, the burnout and the thoughts of "what is wrong with me?" begin to make sense. Alongside that clarity a quieter question often comes up: why does it still hurt? For many, the post says, the answer is not just fatigue or sensitivity but C-PTSD. Unlike PTSD, which is often linked to one event, C-PTSD develops over time, especially in environments where your needs went unnoticed or were constantly dismissed [msg #648].

**Examples of prolonged, repeated emotional injuries that cause it (the source says the list is not exhaustive):**
1. Being punished for traits you could not control.
2. Hiding your true self in order to fit in.
3. Being raised in an atmosphere of misunderstanding, isolation or blame.
4. Living in a constant state of "пожалуйста, любите меня, пожалуйста, не злитесь" (*please love me, please don't be angry*) [msg #648].

**Why late-diagnosed people carry it silently.** This is why so many late-diagnosed people, especially autistic or ADHD people, carry it in silence for years. Before diagnosis they learned to survive through masking, people-pleasing, excessive effort or complete withdrawal into themselves, and these patterns do not vanish by magic after diagnosis [msg #648].

**Signs listed (four):**
1. Flashbacks without clear pictures of the past.
2. Hypervigilance even in safe places.
3. An automatic urge to please everyone.
4. Deep-rooted guilt that is hard to shake off.
The post's verdict: "Это не черты характера, а реакции на травму." (*These are not character traits but reactions to trauma.*) [msg #648]

**Why it is more common in neurodivergent people.** According to the post, research shows that neurodivergent people, especially autistic or ADHD, face trauma disorders including C-PTSD significantly more often. The reason given is not weakness but having to live in systems that were never built for them. The post does not name the studies [msg #648].

**Grief after diagnosis.** A diagnosis can bring relief, but it also opens the door to grief. There is grief for the years you thought something was wrong with you, and grief for missed support, misunderstood actions, and the version of yourself you had to hide [msg #648].

**Why name it.** Calling C-PTSD by its name means you are not just "overdoing it", not dramatising, not a failure. You adapted to survive, often very skilfully, but survival mode does not have to be your permanent state [msg #648].

**What healing means.** Healing from C-PTSD does not mean pretending the past did not happen. It means "постепенно разучиваться считать себя проблемой" (*gradually unlearning seeing yourself as the problem*), and rebuilding a sense of safety not only outside but also inside yourself. The post closes: "Теперь вы можете делать больше, чем просто выживать" (*Now you can do more than just survive*) [msg #648].

### Eight principles for treating people whose memory was damaged by trauma
Purpose: guidance for people around someone with trauma-related memory loss. The original is by Caleb Mulenga [msg #650].
1. **They are not lying or dodging responsibility.** Their mind went through so much pain that it had to "erase" some memories in order to survive [msg #650].
2. **Trauma is rarely loud.** Sometimes it sounds quiet: "Я не помню этот день" (*I don't remember that day*), "Блин, весь тот год как в тумане" (*Damn, that whole year is like a fog*) [msg #650].
3. **Don't rush to judge** someone who forgets dates, events or moments that matter to you. Their psyche may simply have been protecting them when everything around them felt unbearable [msg #650].
4. **Memory loss is not always about age or absent-mindedness.** Often it follows from having lived too long with inner pain [msg #650].
5. **They are not ignoring you or dismissing your feelings.** Their brain had to switch into survival mode to cope with what happened [msg #650].
6. **Healing is not always getting the memories back.** Sometimes it is accepting the empty places inside and learning to live with them [msg #650].
7. **Don't confuse forgetfulness with indifference.** "Иногда забыть — единственный способ выжить." (*Sometimes forgetting is the only way to survive.*) [msg #650]
8. **Be gentler.** Many strong people you know lost parts of their past against their will. Their mind did it so they could keep living [msg #650].

### Four reasons neurodivergent people are more vulnerable to abuse
Purpose: to explain the "double vulnerability" of neurodivergent women. The material is by the ADHD project «Где моё внимание?», written for the campaign "16 days of activism against gender-based violence" [msg #785].
1. **Difficulty recognising abuse.** Abuse can take many non-obvious forms that are much harder to spot than physical abuse: emotional, psychological, economic, reproductive. Many neurodivergent people find it harder to recognise "normal" boundaries and subtle social signals, and to notice "red flags" [msg #785].
2. **Masking to avoid conflict.** From childhood "we" (the authors write as neurodivergent people) get used to suppressing discomfort to fit in. We may agree to things we dislike so as not to stand out or seem "strange". This makes it harder to set boundaries, say no and protect ourselves. Sometimes we simply repeat social patterns without fully understanding what they mean, which raises the risk [msg #785].
3. **Desensitisation to mistreatment.** Neurodivergent people face bullying, rejection and a constant feeling of being misunderstood for many years. As a result they "get used to" abusive treatment, blame themselves for it, or start to think they are overreacting [msg #785].
4. **Tendency to take things literally.** Many people with ADHD and autism struggle to read hints, sarcasm and other subtle social cues. Example: one might take the invitation "поехали ко мне, посмотрим фильм" (*come over to my place, we'll watch a film*) as meaning only watching a film, although it is often a euphemism for an offer of sex. This can create more dangerous situations in itself, and it also makes neurodivergent people more vulnerable to gaslighting and manipulation (the post links a study for this claim) [msg #785].

**Why gaslighting is especially dangerous.** Gaslighting is manipulation that makes a person doubt their own reality, and "Он может становиться инструментом подготовки к более жёстким формам насилия" (*it can become a tool for preparing harsher forms of violence*). It destroys the ability to trust yourself and your perceptions. When a victim doubts her own memory, emotions and right to boundaries, she becomes much more vulnerable to other kinds of violence, such as physical and sexual violence [msg #785].

### Gaslighting: definition and six signs
From the PDF guide titled, in translation, *Gaslighting: how to recognise and resist it. A practical guide for neurodivergent people* [file #785].

**Definition.** The guide defines gaslighting as one kind of covert abuse, a form of psychological or emotional pressure that builds gradually and unnoticed and often disguises itself as care or logic. It is manipulation that makes a person doubt reality and their own sanity. The gaslighter does everything to convince the victim that the problem is not his actions but her inadequate reaction, distorting what really happened. Over time this lowers self-esteem, makes the person doubt their own sanity, increases anxiety and makes them more vulnerable to future abuse [file #785].

**Why neurodivergent people miss it.** According to the guide, it can be especially hard for neurodivergent people to recognise gaslighting. The reasons given are how they were socialised, emotional sensitivity, a learned distrust of their own perceptions, and frequent past abuse, especially in childhood [file #785].

**Signs to watch for (six):**
1. The person's words and actions do not match each other.
2. The person denies their guilt and shifts responsibility for their behaviour onto you.
3. The person confuses you by mixing insults with caring remarks.
4. The person undermines your motivation to change and resists your growth.
5. Even when faced with facts, the person keeps denying what they did.
6. You are told that you are "making it up", have "gone mad", or "got it all wrong" [file #785].

### Five classic gaslighting phrases, decoded, with ready-made answers
**Disclaimer from the guide:** every phrase must be read in context. Hearing one of them does not automatically mean the speaker is a gaslighter who wants to harm you, but these expressions deserve attention, especially from neurodivergent people. In the scripts the speaker is female (e.g. «задетой», «не обязана») because the guide is addressed to women [file #785].

1. **"I was just joking. You're overreacting."**
   - *How it sounds:* your reaction is excessive; I didn't mean anything bad.
   - *What is really happening:* the gaslighter devalues your feelings and shifts the blame onto you. It is a classic tactic: your feelings are not taken into account and you are made to doubt your own judgment.
   - *Why it is manipulation:* the "joke" caused unpleasant emotions; instead of an apology the blame is shifted; you are denied the right to be upset.
   - *How to respond:* "I feel hurt, and that matters." / "I'm not comfortable when people joke with me like that." / "I deserve to have my feelings respected." [file #785]
2. **"I didn't lie. I just didn't tell you."**
   - *How it sounds:* you can't be angry, because technically there was no lie.
   - *What is really happening:* the gaslighter deliberately withheld information to manipulate the situation. Lying by omission is a form of deception. Hiding the truth while knowing it will affect your decisions or feelings is still a breach of trust.
   - *Why it is manipulation:* if the truth would have changed nothing, you would have been told it; the person knew hiding it would hurt you and deliberately left themselves the loophole "I had nothing to do with it".
   - *How to respond:* "When an important part is hidden, it feels like a lie." / "For me, honesty means both telling the truth and not hiding what matters." / "I need more transparency, otherwise I lose trust." / "If information is significant, its absence also has an effect." [file #785]
3. **"I only did it because I love you."**
   - *How it sounds:* his problematic behaviour is justified because it is driven by love and care.
   - *What is really happening:* the gaslighter uses love as an excuse for control and mistreatment. Love cannot justify harm. This manipulation makes the victim doubt what is happening even more and makes her feel guilty and ungrateful.
   - *Why it is manipulation:* love must never be a lever for guilt or coercion; if his actions really came from love, you would not feel controlled or unprotected.
   - *How to respond:* "Love doesn't justify causing pain." / "I feel hurt, and that matters." / "It's important to me that care doesn't violate my boundaries." / "I don't have to put up with behaviour that hurts me." [file #785]
4. **"How am I supposed to not shout when you don't listen to me?"**
   - *How it sounds:* it is your own fault you were shouted at.
   - *What is really happening:* the gaslighter blames you for his own anger-control problems. This normalises mistreatment by making you feel responsible for it. The guide gives the healthy alternative reaction: "I was upset, but that doesn't justify shouting. I'll work on it."
   - *Why it is manipulation:* he justifies his problematic behaviour by blaming you for it; everything is presented as if you caused the shouting, rather than his own decision to raise his voice.
   - *How to respond:* "Shouting doesn't make me more attentive, it only scares me." / "It's important to me that people speak to me calmly." / "I will listen when the conversation is safe for me." [file #785]
5. **"No, you remember it all wrong."**
   - *How it sounds:* I know better; you can't rely on your memory.
   - *What is really happening:* your reality is being distorted. This is a form of psychological control that makes you doubt yourself. Over time it can lead to loss of self-confidence and growing dependence on the gaslighter's version of events.
   - *Why it is manipulation:* he undermines your confidence in your own convictions; he reshapes events the way he needs in order to control the situation.
   - *How to respond:* "Я помню это по-другому, и мои ощущения важны" (*I remember it differently, and my perceptions matter*) / "I trust my perceptions, they are real." / "We may remember things differently, but my memories are no less real." [file #785]

### Gaslighting self-check questions
Purpose: to judge a relationship by the overall pattern, not by single phrases [file #785].

**Look not only at the words but at the overall picture:**
1. Does the person often explain hurtful or problematic behaviour with supposedly convincing justifications?
2. Does it turn out that he is always right and you are not?
3. If you had acted the same way for the same reasons, would it seem right to you?
4. Would this person be upset if you treated them the same way?

**Pay attention to your own sensations:**
5. Do the person's explanations make you feel that you "shouldn't be upset"?
6. Do you feel that someone is trying to mislead you?
7. Do you sense that real events are being distorted?

**Scoring:** the more "yes" answers you can give, the more likely it is that you are facing gaslighting. The guide gives no numeric threshold [file #785].

### Six steps for what to do when facing gaslighting
**Safety disclaimer from the guide:** do not set boundaries harshly if you are in a situation of physical violence, as this can be unsafe [file #785].
1. **Trust your perceptions.** Tell yourself: "I know what I experienced/heard/saw." The aim is to reclaim your right to your own reality [file #785].
2. **Record the facts.** Write down events, screenshot messages, save dates. This reduces doubt and helps you check against reality [file #785].
3. **Don't justify yourself.** Gaslighting "feeds" on your explanations. Short phrases work better: "I have a different version of events", "I see the situation differently" [file #785].
4. **Mark your boundaries.** For example: "I don't like it when my words or feelings are devalued", "This way of talking is unacceptable to me" [file #785].
5. **Take distance, physical and emotional.** If possible, take a pause, leave the room and regulate your state. Gaslighting intensifies in long dialogues [file #785].
6. **Seek support and help.** Talking with someone you trust helps restore clarity and the sense that you have not gone mad [file #785].

## Detailed insights

### Unrecognised pain in autistic childhood as a source of trauma
This is a first-person account by an autistic author (status-quo-hater on tumblr, translated by Anne Green Moon), shared as an example of how autistic people may react to pain differently. The author gets ill, gets injured and feels pain, and dislikes it. But most of the time you would not notice by looking at them. They almost never cry or make any sounds that signal pain. When ill they almost never behave "like a sick person": they behave normally, just go to bed earlier, and often wear a mask and wash their hands when others are around [msg #466].

This holds even for severe pain and illness that would confine another person to bed and make them moan, such as broken ribs or flu. During both, according to their roommate, they simply carried on "как ни в чем не бывало" (*as if nothing had happened*) [msg #466].

The author names this as one of the main reasons they were deprived of medical care as a child. It never looked as if they were in enough pain to be taken seriously, and when they tried to act out pain it looked like a terrible imitation. They had to walk a mile home with a dislocated kneecap because the adults did not believe they were injured. They still have problems with that knee, and no doctor ever saw it, because their parents did not want to notice and did not believe them, since they were not crying [msg #466].

This happened so often that they stopped going to their parents when ill or injured. They started going to their teachers instead, which led to social services being called. That achieved nothing except making the parents angry for "lying" to them, so the author stopped asking teachers for help too. They learned to ignore every injury and illness until they moved out. The lesson as the author puts it: "Я не был в состоянии выразить свой крик о помощи, поэтому никогда ее не получал." (*I was not able to express my cry for help, so I never got it.*) [msg #466]

The channel adds the caveat "Ваш опыт может отличаться." (*Your experience may differ.*) and asks readers whether their pain is visible from outside and whether they were believed when ill or injured [msg #466].

### Neurodivergent statistics on domestic violence
These are the figures given in the gaslighting post, with the sources it links.
- General statistics (from a UK domestic-abuse statistics page, ncdv.org.uk): one in four women and one in seven men experience domestic violence. About 2.3 million people aged 16 and over were victims of domestic violence between March 2023 and March 2024, of whom 1.6 million were women and girls [msg #785].
- Neurodivergent adults are 2–3 times more exposed to domestic violence (linked to a paper in *Autism Research*, Wiley) [msg #785].
- "Many meta-analyses" show that 84% of neurodivergent people reported experiencing domestic violence (no specific source is given for this figure) [msg #785].
- 60–90% of neurodivergent women reported experiencing serious sexualised or domestic violence (linked to a *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience* article) [msg #785].
- Conclusion drawn by the authors: women and neurodivergent people are both more exposed to violence, so "нейроотличные женщины сталкиваются с двойной уязвимостью" (*neurodivergent women face a double vulnerability*) [msg #785].

### What C-PTSD therapy is for
The channel author shares a phrase from her psychotherapist that calmed her: "Терапия кПТСР не нацелена на то, чтобы сделать тебя работоспособной. Наша цель улучшить твое качество жизни" (*C-PTSD therapy is not aimed at making you able to work. Our goal is to improve your quality of life.*) The author thinks this may be important to hear for people in a similar situation, "кто забывает выбирать себя" (*who forget to choose themselves*) [msg #787].
