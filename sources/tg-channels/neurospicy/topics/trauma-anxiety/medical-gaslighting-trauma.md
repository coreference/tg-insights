# Medical gaslighting and medical trauma

## What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel taught about medical gaslighting and medical trauma: how being repeatedly dismissed by clinicians ("it's psychosomatic", "you invented it, it's actually anxiety", "go to a psychiatrist") produces genuine psychological trauma in people with hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (hEDS), hypermobility spectrum disorder (HSD), functional neurological disorder (FND) and other invisible conditions. It covers the research the channel cites (an interview study naming "clinically associated traumatization", PTSD rates in hEDS), the channel author's first-person account of how years of gaslighting taught her to distrust her own body, an FND patient's explanation of why such patients appear "unpleasant", and a translated list of eleven ways autism collides with chronic illness. According to the source it matters because fear of doctors makes people ignore important symptoms and delay treatment, and because gaslighting leaves people believing they "made it all up" [msg #106, #378, #766, #780].

## Core ideas

- **Dismissive care is itself a source of trauma.** Repeated hostile or uninterested encounters accumulate into lost trust in healthcare and acute anxiety about seeking help; researchers call this "clinically associated traumatization" (see *Clinically associated traumatization*) [msg #106].
- **PTSD is markedly more common in EDS/HSD than in the general population**, linked to physical injuries, severe medical experiences or both, and partly preventable (see *Evidence and numbers* and *What needs to change*) [msg #666].
- **Medical gaslighting teaches people to distrust their own bodies**, feeding dissociation, interoception problems and worsening alexithymia [msg #378].
- **"Difficult" patients are broken and exhausted, not difficult by personality** — the FND patient's argument (see *Why FND patients seem "unpleasant"*) [msg #766].
- **Autism and chronic illness pull in opposite directions** on eleven dimensions, and the combination, reinforced by medical gaslighting, leads to traumatization (see *Autism vs chronic illness*) [msg #780].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Clinically associated traumatization (three findings of the hEDS interview study)
Purpose: a named model of how medical care becomes traumatic for hEDS patients. The forwarded post (from Neurospicy_dushnila, by Настя) describes a new study (pubmed 37426705) in which researchers conducted 26 in-depth interviews with patients with hypermobile EDS. The results showed a three-step chain [msg #106]:
1. **Hostility and lack of interest.** Patients often meet hostility and disinterest from healthcare workers — the starting point of the chain [msg #106].
2. **Cumulative effect → loss of trust.** It is not one bad visit: the accumulated effect of such encounters leads to loss of trust in the healthcare system as a whole [msg #106].
3. **Acute anxiety about seeking care.** Patients develop acute anxiety at the very thought of having to seek medical help [msg #106].

The phenomenon was named "**клинически ассоциированная травматизация**" (clinically associated traumatization). Why it matters, per the post: it worsens quality of life and can harm health, because fear of doctors can make people ignore important symptoms and postpone needed treatment. The post's conclusion: the study underlines the need to raise awareness of hEDS among medical workers and improve approaches to treating these patients — "Только так мы сможем разорвать порочный круг недоверия и страха" (*Only this way can we break the vicious circle of distrust and fear*) between patients and doctors; it calls for more understanding, empathetic and effective care for all patients regardless of diagnosis [msg #106].

### What needs to change — four measures against PTSD in EDS/HSD
The Ehlers-Danlos Society infographic (translated by the channel, citing Halverson et al., 2023) states that PTSD in EDS and HSD is a reality and "в некоторых случаях это можно предотвратить" (*in some cases it can be prevented*). What is needed [msg #666, img #666]:
1. **Trauma-informed medical care** [msg #666].
2. **Acknowledgement of the emotional consequences of misdiagnosis and of being ignored** [msg #666].
3. **Earlier diagnosis and access to mental-health support** [msg #666].
4. **Expanded physician education on EDS and HSD** [msg #666].

### Script of a helpful FND diagnosis conversation
Purpose: what the FND patient-author (forwarded from an FND channel) says would have "helped a lot" instead of the silent page-flipping and referral to a psychiatrist. The proposed doctor's speech contains these components, each answering a gap the author experienced [msg #766]:
1. **A mechanism-based explanation of the diagnosis**: "ФНР — это заболевание, при котором от стресса разрушаются функциональные связи между отделами мозга, и поэтому появляются проблемы с движением" (*FND is a disease in which stress disrupts the functional connections between brain regions, and this is why movement problems appear*). This replaces a diagnosis that sounds like "blah-blah-blah" [msg #766].
2. **A treatment recommendation with its reasons**: psychotherapy and antidepressants, "because they reduce stress; because there are studies that for some people with FND this helps get rid of the movement problems; because nothing better has been devised". Giving the "why" is what keeps a psychiatric referral from sounding like "you are not sane" [msg #766].
3. **Honesty about disability status**: disability cannot be obtained for this diagnosis "потому что это большое упущение нашего здравоохранения, а не пустяковая болезнь" (*because this is a big omission of our healthcare system, not a trivial illness*) — framing the gap as a system failure rather than a verdict on the patient's suffering [msg #766].
4. **Rehabilitation, with realism and contacts**: rehabilitation may be useful, but very few specialists can work with this diagnosis — "Вот контакты:...." (*Here are the contacts:....*). The author stresses this because when she asked a neurologist to help her find a specialist, she usually got silence [msg #766].

### Autism vs chronic illness — eleven collisions
A list by spooniesaga, translated by the channel, under the title "When autism and chronic illness collide". Each pair sets an autistic need against what chronic illness demands [msg #780]:
1. **Processing time vs fast decisions.** Autism means needing more time to gather and process information and make decisions; chronic illness requires deciding quickly, reacting to sudden symptoms and talking to doctors, often with very limited information [msg #780].
2. **Predictability vs unpredictability.** Autism brings a strong need for predictability, routines and schedules; with chronic illness it is impossible to plan, keep a stable regime, or predict how you will feel even an hour from now [msg #780].
3. **Communication difficulty vs self-advocacy demand.** Autism involves difficulty communicating, explaining one's symptoms and defending one's needs; chronic illness requires communicating clearly and advocating for oneself in the hardest states, when one has the least strength [msg #780].
4. **Sensory sensitivity vs medical sensory load.** Autism brings heightened sensitivity to sounds, smells, light, touch and textures; chronic illness forces tolerating intensified sensory difficulties for medical procedures — hospitals, devices, side effects, compression garments, which are rarely sensory-comfortable [msg #780].
5. **Recovery time vs endless admin.** Autism requires a lot of time to recover in a calm environment; chronic illness brings endless administrative tasks — managing treatment, booking appointments, following instructions, prescriptions, insurance, and constant mental load [msg #780].
6. **Need for movement vs limited mobility.** Autistic people need movement to manage emotions and support the nervous system; chronic illness brings mobility limits and severe energy shortage — the needed movement cannot be had without risking deterioration [msg #780].
7. **Masking cost vs double masking.** Autism already costs much energy on "masking"; chronic illness leads to total exhaustion when one must mask both the autism and the illness at the same time [msg #780].
8. **Emotional regulation load vs added emotional burden.** Autism already strains emotional regulation; chronic illness adds a constant sense of loss, irritation, fear for the future and of doctor visits [msg #780].
9. **Restricted food choice vs illness-driven food limits.** Sensory sensitivity in autism strongly limits food choice; chronic illness adds nausea, pain, allergies, hypersensitivity, special dietary needs and unpredictable appetite — sometimes foods that are sensory-safe are not suitable symptom-wise [msg #780].
10. **Control and autonomy vs dependence.** Autism means needing control and predictability over environment and schedule and the independence to do things according to one's nervous system's needs; chronic illness means a severe loss of independence and dependence on others and external circumstances that dictate what one can and cannot do [msg #780].
11. **Direct communication vs vagueness.** Autism needs direct communication — honesty, clear instructions, lots of data; chronic illness brings vague answers, dismissal (обесценивание), contradictory medical opinions, and lack of clarity both accidental and deliberate [msg #780].

All eleven pairs in the source are reproduced above [msg #780].

**Channel author's addition**: she adds that autism is a predisposition to C-PTSD (кптср), and chronic illness is very often the reason "we" meet medical gaslighting, plus life in one's own limited body. Altogether this leads to traumatization, especially when people try for a long time to live as if they did not have these traits — a stance reinforced by medical gaslighting — thereby harming themselves without realizing the consequences, "а потом остаемся в этом теле..." (*and then we remain in this body...*) [msg #780].

## Detailed insights

### How gaslighting erodes trust in the body — a first-person account
The channel author (with a trigger warning) explains that she still gets strongly triggered by phrases such as "это все психосоматика" (*it's all psychosomatics*), "ты себе придумала, на самом деле это тревожность" (*you made it up, it's actually anxiety*) and "а ты знаешь что такое соматоформное расстройство?" (*do you know what somatoform disorder is?*). Notably, these phrases evoke a "fight" ("бей") response rather than "freeze" ("замри"), which she experiences as a sharp contrast, since she is usually in chronic disconnection from her body [msg #378].

The trigger was a passage from a book about PTSD, which she quotes: many trauma survivors delay seeing a doctor or dentist or do not seek help at all until seriously ill or injured; they may fear physical contact or distrust doctors and nurses; the examination itself can be a trigger reminding of past trauma, or they may have been mocked when describing their condition. People with traumatic pasts often have many physical symptoms with no clear physical cause; someone may have told them their problems are "in their head" and psychological, which can make them doubt whether they can trust their body's signals. The passage is headed "Вам не нужно справляться со всем в одиночку - существует помощь" (*You don't have to cope with everything alone — help exists*) [msg #378].

Her reaction: "то есть где-то существует помощь...в которой мне отказывали все эти годы...?" (*so somewhere help exists... which I was denied all these years...?*), followed by the bitter realization that years of medical gaslighting had taught her not to trust her body and herself. She could not sleep all night due to an emotional flashback. She felt in one moment how each such experience gradually distanced her from herself, contributing to dissociation, interoception problems and worsening alexithymia. She learned to distrust her feelings and body signals, ignore her needs and simply endure pain — and looking back, was horrified that by believing the doctors she had systematically traumatized herself without suspecting where it would lead [msg #378].

She notes that writing the full story brought emotional flashbacks in an unusually intense and prolonged stream. Her broader claim: medical gaslighting is a relatively new term, and many people do not realize this is what is happening to them — or they feel the trauma but are afraid to say so, because few people truly understand the pain and its impact on life. She hopes her story helps others validate their experience of traumatization, since gaslighting often plants the thought that the person invented everything themselves. Her closing message: "Прошу вас, доверяйте себе" (*Please, trust yourselves*) [msg #378].

### Why FND patients seem "unpleasant" — the patient's side
The FND patient-author answers the question "why are FND patients so unpleasant" from the inside [msg #766]:
- **The symptoms are frightening.** Yesterday you were healthy; today you lie crying from pain and fear because your body cramps and shakes violently, or you can no longer walk, or you suddenly lose sensation in body parts. Whether you go to a doctor yourself or are hospitalized urgently, the outcome is the same: you feel terrible but all the tests are perfect [msg #766].
- **Most doctors don't know FND.** Patients very rarely reach a doctor who knows what FND is and how to work with it on the first try, so the appointment looks like quiet flipping through test results, awkward silence and a recommendation to see a psychiatrist — which the patient hears as "you are not sane". The author says that even she, with experience of psychiatrists and psychoeducation, heard it that way; the issue is not psychiatric stigma but that "нет ничего очевидного в том, что ты жалуешься на руки, а тебя отправляют лечить голову" (*there is nothing obvious about complaining about your hands and being sent to treat your head*) [msg #766].
- **Doctor-shopping is survival, not whim.** The patient leaves with no diagnosis that doesn't sound like "blah-blah-blah", no treatment, no recommendations about the movement problem. So they go to a "better" doctor, again and again — not out of joy but because living like this is impossible, so they choose to risk it [msg #766].
- **Exhaustion and defensiveness.** You get tired of the illness and of doctors, lose trust in them, lose confidence that you are normal and can be helped, and start defending yourself before the doctor even speaks [msg #766].
- **No legitimizing "excuse".** The illness took work and hobbies, but there is no disability status, treatment or understanding of the diagnosis; a wheelchair is at your own expense. Lack of support makes you feel you are either ill with "nonsense" or don't deserve help; the hundredth referral to a psychiatrist and psychotherapy sounds like mockery [msg #766].
- **Mental-health professionals often don't know FND either**, and when the patient asks the neurologist for help finding a specialist, they usually get silence [msg #766].
- Conclusion: "мы неприятно себя ведем не потому что у нас неприятная структура личности. А потому что мы сломлены и очень устали" (*we behave unpleasantly not because we have an unpleasant personality structure, but because we are broken and very tired*), with a plea to help patients feel they are not alone in this pain [msg #766].

### Biological vulnerability to trauma in hypermobility
Research cited in the Ehlers-Danlos Society material (Baeza-Velasco et al., 2011) indicates that people with hypermobility may be more prone to anxiety disorders and also more sensitive to bodily sensations such as temperature or pain, which can feel intense and cause stress; this heightened sensitivity may increase their vulnerability to trauma. The elevated PTSD rate may stem from physical injuries, severe medical experiences, or both (Halverson et al., 2023) [msg #666].

## Evidence and numbers

- **Halverson et al., 2023 (PMID 37426705)**, adults with hypermobile EDS, as reported by the Ehlers-Danlos Society [msg #666]:
  - 85% had a traumatic experience within the healthcare system.
  - 33% had a PTSD diagnosis.
  - 68% avoided mentioning their EDS diagnosis for fear of stigma and bias.
  - 85% said they additionally avoided medical care altogether.
- The same PMID (37426705) is the study described in the forwarded post as 26 in-depth interviews with hEDS patients (see *Clinically associated traumatization*) [msg #106, #666].
- People with EDS and HSD face PTSD more often than the general population (Halverson et al., 2023) [msg #666].

- The hypermobility-anxiety-sensitivity link is hedged in the source ("могут быть более склонны" — *may be more prone*) [msg #666].
