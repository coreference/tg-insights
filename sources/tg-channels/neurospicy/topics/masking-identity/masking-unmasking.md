# Masking, camouflaging and unmasking

## What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel Нейропряность (Neurospicy) published on autistic masking and camouflaging: the research definition (Hull's three-part model), why camouflaging is costly in energy and mental health and leads to autistic burnout, how masking hides autism so well that it is missed by others, by diagnostic questionnaires and by the "gifted child" label, a full self-assessment workbook for spotting one's own masking strategies, and two reflections on unmasking that warn against simply "tearing off the mask" and describe it instead as slow, safe, values-based emergence of a self. According to the source, masking is for autistic people "не просто способ вписаться, это выживание в преимущественно нейротипичном обществе" (*not just a way to fit in, it is survival in a predominantly neurotypical society*), and its price is autistic burnout [msg #24].

## Core ideas

- **Masking is survival, and it has a price.** In a world that expects conformity, masking autistic traits and imitating neurotypical behaviour is an everyday survival practice for autistic people, and the cost is autistic burnout. The channel's own introductory article set out to explain the essence of masking, its consequences, and strategies that reduce the *need* to mask, making communication and finding connection easier "в мире, где быть "своим" часто кажется роскошью" (*in a world where being "one of us" often feels like a luxury*) [msg #24, img #24].
- **Camouflaging is the umbrella; masking is one part of it.** Camouflaging = compensation + masking + assimilation (Hull et al.); in everyday speech "masking" is used as a synonym, but clinically masking is a subset (see *Social camouflaging model*) [msg #156].
- **Camouflaging costs energy and harms mental health.** It requires time, effort and energy, is linked to burnout, depression and anxiety, and raises the risk of autistic burnout starting or worsening [msg #156, #696].
- **Good masking produces disbelief and lack of support.** People who camouflage well are often not believed when they disclose autism, so they receive poor support, and exhaustion and burnout become frequent [msg #156, #403, #404, #519].
- **Masking keeps people safe — so unmasking requires safety.** Unmasking without a sense of self, values, or a safe environment can lead to social punishment and new internalised messages of being "bad" (see *Unmasking as excavation*) [msg #73, #518].
- **Unmasking is emergence, not return.** For people who masked from early childhood, a fully formed self may never have had a chance to develop; unmasking lets it appear for the first time, non-linearly and in the body (see *Unmasking as emergence*) [msg #660].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Social camouflaging model (Hull et al., 2018)
Purpose: a research model, translated by the channel from an autistic burnout guide, describing how some autistic people use a set of actions and strategies to navigate the neurotypical social world better, fit in, build connections, prevent rejection and social ostracism, or gain acceptance from others (Hull et al., 2017; 2018). The three forms:

1. **Компенсация (Compensation)** — strategies used to actively compensate for difficulties in social situations. Examples given: copying body language and facial expressions; learning social cues from films and books; using pre-prepared social scripts in different contexts [msg #156].
2. **Маскирование (Masking)** — strategies used to hide autistic traits or create a neurotypical image. Examples: adjusting face and body to look confident and/or relaxed; forcing eye contact [msg #156].
3. **Ассимиляция (Assimilation)** — strategies used to try to fit into social situations. Examples: playing a role; avoiding or forcing oneself into interaction with others; feigned interest; exaggerating expressions and enthusiasm for other people's sake [msg #156].

Terminology note from the source: "В разговорной речи маскирование часто используется как синоним камуфлирования. Однако с клинической точки зрения маскирование считается подмножеством камуфлирования" (*In everyday speech masking is often used as a synonym for camouflaging. Clinically, however, masking is considered a subset of camouflaging*) (Hull et al., 2018) [msg #156].

Why it costs so much: autistic people have to set aside time to rehearse social interactions, and acting and suppressing one's true behaviour require more effort/energy than authentic behaviour. This harms overall mental health (Alaghband-Rad et al., 2023; Cook et al., 2021), further increasing the risk of autistic burnout appearing or worsening [msg #156].

### Camouflaging self-assessment workbook — Part A (Lindsey Mackereth)
Purpose: for people who suspect they mask their neurodivergent traits but do not know *how*. The checklist and practical guide, created by Lindsey Mackereth and translated by the channel ("Исследование камуфлирования [+ Другие нейрокомплексные социальные различия]" — *Exploring camouflaging [+ other neurocomplex social differences]*), aims to deepen awareness of masking and help take next steps in integrating that new information. It contains screening items, assessment questions and data on common neurodivergent social traits and common signs of masking, plus bonus application questions to work through alone or with a psychotherapist, coach, partner or friend; the post suggests printing it and taking it with you (for example to a session) [msg #496].

How to use: Part A lists common camouflaging/masking strategies found in autistic people. They can also occur in other neurocomplex people, so the instructions encourage everyone to look through it even if they do not identify as autistic. Put a ✔ next to every item that resonates to identify areas of possible social deviation from neurotypical norms. Each section ends with an "Other:" line for your own items [file #496].

**Compensation strategies / signs** [file #496]:
1. Creating scripts for social situations — relying on pre-prepared scripts or rehearsing conversations in advance to make interactions go smoothly.
2. Repeating phrases exactly as others said them — imitating and using the exact words and phrases other people use.
3. Using behaviour learned from watching other people's interactions — often observing and adopting others' behaviour to fit in socially.
4. Practising facial expressions and body language — regularly rehearsing and refining expressions, gestures and body language to match allistic (non-autistic) people.
5. Researching social skills to improve one's own — actively seeking resources and information on social skills to improve interactions and seem more allistic.
6. Learning and using social skills seen on TV and in films — taking inspiration from social skills shown in media and building them into one's own interactions.
7. Deliberately copying someone's behaviour, body language or expressions during interactions — consciously imitating and mirroring others to blend in.
8. Watching others to understand social skills — spending considerable time observing and studying how others navigate social situations in order to learn and imitate their skills.

**Masking strategies / signs** [file #496]:
1. Constant preoccupation with how others perceive you and a drive to meet their expectations.
2. Carefully adjusting body language to seem interested and engaged in conversations.
3. Heightened awareness of the impression you make on others, leading to shyness/self-consciousness.
4. Constant monitoring and adapting of body language and facial expressions to fit social norms.
5. Excessive focus on managing your own facial expressions and body language in social situations.
6. Feeling you must maintain eye contact even when it is uncomfortable or overwhelming.
7. Constant vigilance in tracking and adjusting body language to project relaxed behaviour.

**Assimilation strategies / signs** [file #496]:
1. Conversations feel unnatural and do not flow smoothly.
2. You often catch yourself pretending to be "normal" in social situations.
3. You rely on the support or presence of others to navigate social interactions.
4. You believe you cannot fully be yourself around others.
5. You feel the need to put on a mask or façade to navigate social situations.
6. You try to avoid or minimise interactions with others in social settings.
7. Social situations feel like performances rather than opportunities for genuine connection.
8. Interacting with others requires conscious effort and feels forced.

**Part A exercise — 5 reflection questions** [file #496]:
1. Choose one trait from the camouflaging sections that matches your current social strategy.
2. What messages did you receive over your life that reinforced this behaviour? From whom? When?
3. How has this behaviour helped you socially in the past?
4. What has been the personal cost of this behaviour?
5. Have there been times you felt you did not need this behaviour in a social setting? Where? When? Who were you with?

The sequence moves from naming a strategy, to its origin (who taught it), to acknowledging its protective function, to its cost, and finally to locating situations where it was unnecessary — i.e. evidence of safe contexts [file #496].

### Neurodivergent social differences checklist — Part B (Lindsey Mackereth)
Purpose: Part B lists common social differences of neurocomplex people (the source lists autistic people, people with ADHD, gifted people "и т.д.") found in autistic people. Again tick ✔ every item that resonates, to identify areas of possible social deviation from neurotypical norms. Part B draws on the adult ADHD diagnostic interview DIVA and the autism interview guidelines MIGDAS-2 (see *Tools*). The "more common neurodivergent social traits/differences" [file #496]:

1. You prefer one-to-one conversations.
2. You have a small circle of friends.
3. You are an independent thinker and do not always agree with others.
4. You tend to have difficulties with authority.
5. You are less comfortable in unstructured social situations (e.g. free mingling) than structured ones (e.g. doing an activity together).
6. You need depth and complexity in conversations and friendships.
7. You are easily distracted in conversation when other conversations are going on in the same space.
8. You are easily distracted by your own thoughts while talking with others.
9. You are easily distracted by external stimuli while talking with others.
10. You find it hard to speak at a socially acceptable volume; you often talk too loudly or too quietly.
11. You prefer to talk during activities meant to be enjoyed quietly (e.g. watching films).
12. You often need a lot of words to say something.
13. You often do not give others a chance to get a word in.
14. You find it hard to start and/or end a conversation.
15. You often finish other people's sentences.
16. You have an opinion on everything and voice it immediately.
17. You quickly tire of relationships and friendships.
18. You often take on other people's tasks without being asked.
19. You often get irritated when the pace of conversation feels too slow.
20. You often get irritated when you perceive communication as inefficient.
21. You have less motivation/need for social interaction than other people you know.
22. You prefer not to be touched by other people.
23. You get upset when people around you do not care about larger systemic problems.
24. You need more time alone than most people you have met.
25. You need a little more time to think before answering in conversation.
26. You prefer to talk about your special interests rather than other topics (this item appears twice in the translated list).
27. You get irritated when others interrupt your routine tasks or activities.
28. You enjoy only certain kinds of humour, often not those popular with the majority.
29. You find it hard to keep a conversation going on abstract/small-talk topics ("поддерживать течение разговора на отвлеченные темы").
Plus an "Other:" line [file #496].

**Part B exercise — 4 reflection questions** (answer as you see fit; "Доверьтесь своим ощущениям" — *trust your feelings*) [file #496]:
1. Which social difference you ticked in Part B has, in your view, caused the most social or communicative discomfort/conflict in important areas of your life?
2. How do you currently handle this difference when it shows up in front of other people?
3. How do you currently handle it internally when it arises — what do you tell yourself?
4. How could you change your inner dialogue in such situations to show even more respect for the particularities of your own thinking, while still respecting the particularities of the thinking of the people around you?

### Unmasking as excavation, not disclosure (Настя's reframe)
Purpose: a first-person account by Настя of why her first unmasking failed and how values-based integration training shifted her approach "от Раскрытия к Раскопкам" (*from Disclosure/Uncovering to Excavation*) [msg #73, img #73].

1. **The failed model — unmasking as tearing off a mask.** She takes words very literally, so she first imagined unmasking as removing a mask lying over the "real me": if she could just peel off the surface layer, a whole unmasked autistic person would magically appear underneath. In practice she thought this meant letting herself stim or look odd or awkward (which she often did anyway despite her efforts), or completely ignoring social rituals and conventions [msg #73].
2. **The mistake — focusing on outward behaviour.** She treated unmasking as "обратному тренингу социальных навыков" (*reverse social-skills training*): getting rid of "fake" behaviour without asking how she actually felt about it [msg #73].
3. **Why it was dangerous.** She still did not know who she was behind the mask, so removing her neurotypical "costume" was useless and dangerous. "Наши маски, какими бы разрушительными они ни были, формируются для нашей защиты" (*Our masks, however destructive they are, are formed for our protection*). She ended up socially punished, and without any sense of self or understanding of her values that punishment was devastating. With no armour left, she created new social messages that she was "bad" or "wrong" to replace the ones she had been trying to get rid of [msg #73].
4. **The shift — finding values in one's stories.** She was hesitant to return to unmasking at all after it "ended very badly"; the only reason she joined Values-Based Integration training was that she had read about the process of finding values in one's own stories, which resonated enough to overcome her fears. The direction implied by the title is excavation: digging for who one is and what one values before changing outward behaviour [msg #73].

### Unmasking as emergence of an authentic self (Lindsey Mackereth)
Purpose: a therapist's description, translated by Настя, of what unmasking looks like over time and why the metaphor itself can mislead [msg #660].

1. **The question and the grief.** In therapy the question often arises: «Кто я, если я не играю роль?» (*Who am I if I am not playing a role?*). It rarely has quick answers. It is usually followed by a period of quiet mourning — for the years spent adapting, for effort that went unnoticed, and for the self that was never given a chance to develop [msg #660].
2. **Something forms in the silence.** Preferences start to reappear. Boundaries become clearer. Autonomy stops feeling like a risk and is experienced as a right. A client may notice that silence nourishes more than conversation, or that a simple "no" without explanation brings unexpected relief. Stimming returns. The joy of solitude is rediscovered. Pleasure in choosing for oneself [msg #660].
3. **Why small changes matter.** These changes are subtle but very significant: each act of self-respect signals safety to the nervous system, and each moment of authenticity strengthens new neural connections — neuroplasticity working "не ради выживания, а ради целостности" (*not for survival but for wholeness*) [msg #660].
4. **The metaphor's limit.** "Unmasking" implies a fully formed self was simply hidden and waiting to be found; but for many autistic adults, especially those who masked from an early age, that self never had a chance to fully develop. Hence: "Анмаскинг — это не возвращение к зарытой истине. Это позволение истине впервые проявиться." (*Unmasking is not a return to a buried truth. It is allowing the truth to appear for the first time.*) [msg #660].
5. **What the process is like.** The emergence is rarely linear; it is embodied, repetitive and often uncomfortable. It requires painstaking work of retraining a nervous system that has long been on guard, and it requires trust — not only in relationships but in one's own instincts, needs and perception [msg #660].

## Detailed insights

### What masking feels like from the inside
Lindsey Mackereth: "Masking isn't just "acting normal" or "playing along." It's an emotional marathon demanding hyper-awareness of one's behavior and others' reactions, constant editing of natural impulses, and a relentless internal commentary at full speed." The three components are: hyper-awareness of one's own behaviour and of others' reactions; constant suppression/editing of natural impulses; and a non-stop internal monologue running at full speed. The quote comes from her Substack piece titled "Leadership Coaching's Dirty Secret: Burning Out Highly Masked Autistic Adults" [msg #696, img #696].

### Masked traits are invisible to others but not to the person (Lou, @neurodivergent_lou)
- Autistic people can mask their traits so well that others do not notice them, even when those traits significantly affect the person's life; this is why the inner experience of autism must be understood, not only its outward appearance [img #403].
- Lou's own example: in some situations she masks so thoroughly that her "постоянные трудности с социальным общением и взаимодействием" (*persistent difficulties with social communication and interaction* — the diagnostic wording) are invisible to others, while inside she is carefully controlling her communication and imitating other people. This leads to exhaustion and harms her mental health and her sense of self [img #404].
- Difficulty with change and routine can be missed because it doesn't match the textbook description. For a long time Lou thought she had no such difficulties, because she did not recognise herself in how the criterion "ограниченные и повторяющиеся модели поведения, деятельности или интересов" (*restricted and repetitive patterns of behaviour, activities or interests*) was described. Her actual difficulties were: losing the ability to do anything if the time of a meeting or plan changes; constant vague anxiety about the future; difficulties when the usual route has to change [img #405].
- Other ways difficulty with change can show up: inability to switch between tasks (e.g. extreme difficulty getting out of bed); when packaging or a food recipe changes, "we" may never eat that food again; difficulty buying or using new things; feeling overwhelmed when the arrangement of objects in a room changes; difficulty finishing or starting a new series or book [img #406].
- Screening questionnaires can miss autism. Some autism-scale questions seem random — e.g. preferring the library to a party is treated as a possible indicator of autism — but those asking do not consider the hidden reasons for such a choice. Autistic people can enjoy parties and be very social. Questions based only on stereotypes can confuse and prevent autism from being noticed [img #407].

### The cost of passing: "high-functioning" and misread signs
- Erin Ekins (@QueerlyAutistic): autistic people whom society considers "high-functioning" tend to have a hugely disproportionate level of mental illness and trauma, to the point of it effectively limiting and threatening their lives. "То, что мы МОЖЕМ притворяться, не значит, что это нас не убивает." (*The fact that we CAN pretend does not mean it isn't killing us.*) The channel tagged it #Жиза (*so relatable*) [msg #519, img #519].
- Rebecca Murter's card reframes misattributed experiences: it was not clinical depression — it was burnout; not panic attacks — meltdowns; not always anxiety — sensory overload. "Это был аутизм" (*It was autism*) [img #525].
- Pete Wharmby's card (forwarded from Нейропанк): "MASKING KEEPS AUTISTIC PEOPLE SAFE... ....DON'T TELL US WE CAN UNMASK IF YOU HAVEN'T MADE IT SAFE." The lesson: masking is a safety strategy, so the responsibility for making unmasking possible lies with creating safe conditions, not with telling autistic people to stop [msg #518, img #518].

### The "gifted child" label as a mask for neurodivergence (MtfoxX3 thread)
- Claim: if you were a "gifted child" at school and were never diagnosed with anxiety, depression, ADHD or autism, you should probably get checked [img #520].
- The author's impression is that "в 9 случаях из 10" (*in 9 cases out of 10*) the "gifted child" label appears because the child: (1) fears failure and puts too much pressure on themselves; (2) prefers studying to socialising; (3) has excessively high expectations of themselves; (4) hyperfocuses on studies [img #521]; (5) neglects self-care if they have "not earned" it; (6) bases self-esteem on how well they do at school; (7) often befriends teachers/adults rather than peers, because a destructive need to be "adult" robs them of childhood — "and many other unhealthy patterns" [img #522].
- Mechanism: in academic settings it is much easier to call such children "gifted" than to investigate the underlying reasons for their behaviour. The result is often that the correct diagnosis and treatment come only after finishing school, when the brain has little time left to develop [img #523].
- Caveat by the author: not all "gifted children" will have any of this; the point is that the "gifted child" label leads many people not to get the diagnosis and support they need [img #524].

### Masked autistic people are disbelieved
Livingston et al. (2019) report that autistic people who camouflage their traits are often not believed when they disclose their autism and are therefore poorly supported because of their neurotypical presentation; this lack of support meant exhaustion and burnout were frequent [msg #156].
