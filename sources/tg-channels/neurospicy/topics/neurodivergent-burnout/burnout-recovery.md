# Recovering from neurodivergent burnout

## What this covers

This part covers one post. In it the channel shares a Russian translation (by Настя) of an article by Dr. Megan Anna Neff, "Восстановление от аутистического выгорания: Как создать план восстановления" (*Recovering from autistic burnout: how to create a recovery plan*). Neff is an autistic psychologist. The article explains what autistic burnout is and what causes it. It describes two phases of recovery and gives two lists of recovery supports, one drawn from Raymaker's research and one from Neff herself. It then shows how to build a written recovery plan from "pillars", how to prevent the next burnout, and the deeper psychological work of unmasking, internalized ableism and grief [msg #40, article #40].

According to the source this matters because autistic burnout "может быть абсолютно изнурительным и подавляющим" (*can be absolutely exhausting and overwhelming*). It also usually builds up over years, so it cannot be fixed quickly, although "надежда на исцеление есть" (*there is hope for healing*) [msg #40]. Neff speaks from both sides: as an autistic person she knows burnout from experience, and as a psychologist she knows the damage it can do to mental health and well-being [article #40].

## Core ideas

- **Burnout builds up over years, so recovery is slow.** Burnout usually follows several years of overwork, overstraining the body and trying to adapt to a neurotypical world. Recovery therefore "не происходит за одну ночь" (*does not happen overnight*), and patience with yourself and with the process is one of the hardest parts. Full detail is in *No quick fix* [msg #40, article #40].
- **Recovery has two phases.** Phase 1 is immediate recovery from the current episode. Phase 2 is sustainable (deep) recovery, which means building a life that lowers the risk of future episodes. See *Two phases of recovery* [msg #40, article #40].
- **You usually cannot just go back to "normal".** For many autistic people, the demands of their ordinary life are what caused the burnout. Recovery therefore means rethinking that life, not returning to it. See *Rethinking "normality"* [article #40].
- **A personal recovery plan is key.** Recovery should be planned around your own pillars, such as sensory needs, sleep, rest and demand reduction, and turned into small, actionable goals. Neff advises picking only the ideas that appeal to you, because "иногда меньше значит больше" (*sometimes less is more*) [article #40].
- **Prevention is the best strategy.** Self-knowledge about your triggers and early signs lets you step out of situations in time [article #40].
- **For people who mask heavily, rest and supports may not be enough.** Recovery may also require unmasking, rebuilding your identity to include your neurodivergence, overcoming internalized ableism and grieving your limitations [article #40].
- **The guide applies to ADHD too, in the channel author's view.** The article is about autistic burnout. The channel author, who has both ADHD and autism, says it resonates strongly and thinks it is useful for neurodivergent people with either condition [msg #40].

- **Autistic burnout is more than tiredness.** It is a long-lasting state of global exhaustion, loss of skills and heightened sensitivity to the environment, growing out of ongoing stress — especially in highly masking autistic people pressured to meet social expectations that do not fit their needs (see *Three main characteristics of autistic burnout*) [file #167].
- **Pick, don't do everything.** The guide explicitly warns that trying every strategy will quickly overload you; different strategies work for different people, so try the ones that interest you with the goal of finding one or two that work, and do not get upset if some don't [file #167].
- **Self-care is a key part of recovery, not a luxury.** Recognising your individual sensory and emotional needs and meeting them with activities adapted to you is called the main step on the path to healing and recovery [file #167].
- **Burnout keeps the body stuck in stress.** The body often gets stuck in a stress state and needs extra help to complete the stress cycle — through breathing, movement, stimming, crying, laughing, hugging, creativity and other activities (see *Stress cycle*) [file #167].
- **Knowing your sensory profile protects you from deeper burnout.** Knowing what works and what does not is a way of caring for yourself and protecting yourself from even more burnout; it lets you build your own spaces of sensory safety [file #167].
- **Track energy in and out.** Understanding what gives you energy (sparks) and what takes it (drains) is presented as essential while coping with burnout [file #167].
- **Reduce decisions when executive function is overloaded.** Pre-made lists — safe food, playlists, a sensory kit — spare the brain from having to make decisions in moments when that is hard [file #167].
- **Recovery is planned and paced.** Boundaries, a gradual return to activities broken into small steps, and goals matched to your energy level form the recovery-planning part [file #167].
- **Self-advocacy = self-disclosure + request.** Lack of access to accommodations and support affects burnout; self-advocacy is how to obtain them, and the guide gives a formula, a disclosure scale and ready scripts [file #167].

- **Recovery needs withdrawal and fewer demands, and it takes a long time.** It needs pulling back from people and work, solitude, a break from speaking, time for special interests and, if needed, a safety plan. The channel warns it may take months or even years [msg #434].
- **Prevention means changing the environment.** The channel says autistic burnout is caused by environmental factors. Preventing a repeat therefore means significant life changes, such as a different way of working, more support, accommodations, a sensory-safe space and an autistic community. Recovering and then going back to the same life is not enough [msg #434].
- **The old limits were not real capacities.** Before burnout the person may have masked all day, agreed to everything and pushed through overload. Those limits were built on masking and survival. After burnout this is no longer possible, and the body asks for a life that actually fits the person [msg #613].
- **Slowing down shows what you need.** Burnout forces a person to slow down. In that slower pace they notice what brings pleasure, what drains them, and what they need to feel safe and balanced. Relearning boundaries builds on those observations [msg #613].
- **Restorative activities are individual and often sensory.** The forwarded workbook says neurodivergent people should find self-regulation methods that fit them personally. These are often linked to the senses, sensations, movement and interests. Used regularly, they help both to cope with burnout and to prevent it [msg #532].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Three defining features of autistic burnout
Neff defines autistic burnout as "состояние физического, эмоционального и умственного истощения" (*a state of physical, emotional and mental exhaustion*). She says it differs from other forms of burnout and is marked by three features [article #40]:
1. **Chronic exhaustion.** A pervasive physical, cognitive and social tiredness [article #40].
2. **Reduced tolerance to stimuli.** Increased sensitivity to sensory input and to stimuli in general [article #40].
3. **Loss of skills.** Many executive-function skills get worse during burnout. People may struggle with verbal ability (speech), attention regulation and other cognitive functions [article #40].

In essence, burnout happens when we feel drained, overloaded and crushed. It can be very hard to cope with, it affects day-to-day functioning, and it can damage mental and physical health. The triggers she lists are stress, sensory overload, social isolation and difficulty "navigating" social interactions [article #40].

### Five common causes of autistic burnout
Neff says many factors can contribute. Burnout is often caused by a combination of them, and the mix differs from person to person. She advises identifying *your* specific causes so you can design strategies to manage the current burnout and prevent future ones [article #40].
1. **Stress.** Autistic people often experience everyday activities as more stressful than neurotypical people do. Disrupted routines, socializing in allistic (non-autistic) situations and the demands of work and school add up to daily stress that is exhausting for many [article #40].
2. **Sensory overload.** Autistic people are often more sensitive to stimuli such as loud sounds or bright light. Long periods of sensory overload strain the nervous system, reduce capacity and make burnout more likely [article #40].
3. **Executive-function overload.** Executive functions are the mental processes responsible for planning, decision-making and behavioral control. Overloading them for a long time can lead to burnout and to losing executive-function skills [article #40].
4. **Difficulty adapting to allistic social or educational systems.** Autistic people may find it hard to decode and follow social rules in allistic spaces, or may manage it only at a high cognitive cost, for example through masking. Advocating for their needs at school or work can also be hard, which leads to frustration and exhaustion [article #40].
5. **Difficulty getting support and accommodations, or no support at all.** Many autistic people face barriers to getting a formal diagnosis and accommodations. The resulting lack of support feeds overload and burnout [article #40].

The channel post summarizes the causes in one line: years of overwork, overstraining the body and trying to fit a neurotypical world [msg #40].

### Two phases of recovery from autistic burnout
Purpose: ending the current crisis and preventing the next one are separate jobs, and they need different work. The channel post names them as "Восстановление после непосредственного эпизода выгорания" (*recovering from the immediate burnout episode*) and "Создание жизни, снижающей риск будущих эпизодов выгорания" (*creating a life that reduces the risk of future burnout episodes*) [msg #40]. The article says that for most people effective recovery requires this two-stage process [article #40].

1. **Immediate recovery (немедленное восстановление).** This phase deals with the causes and consequences of the current burnout. It consists of practices that restore energy within the burnout cycle you are in now: rest, reducing sensory input, self-care, setting healthy boundaries and supportive practices. Recovery can take very different forms. For some people it means a lot of rest and sleep. For others it means eating the same foods. For others it means spending time with their special interests [article #40].
2. **Sustainable / long-term ("deep") recovery.** This phase means restructuring your life and creating a more sustainable lifestyle. You re-evaluate your values and identify and address the underlying factors that led to the burnout. Neff calls this phase "глубоким восстановлением" (*deep recovery*). It takes a more systemic, structural view of your life and deals with problems and parts of ourselves that are more deeply rooted. It often involves structural and personal changes. Her examples are giving up constant people-pleasing and rethinking your relationship with food, alcohol, health, movement, sleep, work and your sensory environment. Creating a sustainable life often means re-evaluating the concept of "normality" in order to create a new normal. Building a life that suits you will probably take creativity and commitment, and it does not happen overnight [article #40].

### Raymaker's recovery supports
Neff reports these themes from Raymaker's study of burnout in autistic people. They are what autistic adults interviewed in the study named as helpful in their own recovery [article #40]:
1. **Acceptance and access to social support.** Individual and community support, peer support, attention to autistic needs and time spent without masking. It also includes advocating for your rights and getting reasonable accommodations [article #40].
2. **Leaning on autistic strengths.** Using your autistic strengths and special interests [article #40].
3. **Formal support.** Mental-health support, time off, breaks and the possibility of social withdrawal [article #40].
4. **Reducing load.** Cutting down activities, setting boundaries and asking for help [article #40].
5. **Self-advocacy and health.** Focusing on a healthy lifestyle and getting an autism diagnosis [article #40].
6. **Self-knowledge.** Understanding your patterns and triggers and making strategic decisions to prevent burnout [article #40].
7. **Engaging with the autistic community.** Contact with the community can help a person name and acknowledge their experience of burnout [article #40].

### Dr. Neff's eleven burnout-recovery practices
These are Neff's own additional recommended practices. She warns that the source contains a lot of information, and that you should not try to apply all of it at once. Use the practices that appeal to you and leave the rest [article #40].
1. **Set boundaries and drop unnecessary demands.** Protect your time and energy by declining non-essential activities and commitments. Prioritize your well-being and don't take on too much [article #40].
2. **Find ways to reduce sensory input.** Use noise-cancelling headphones, noise-reduction devices such as Loops, sunglasses and similar aids, "даже, если кажется, что сегодня можно обойтись без очков" (*even if it seems you could do without the glasses today*) [article #40].
3. **Take breaks.** Make sure you set aside time to relax and recharge. That may mean dedicated leisure time or simply short breaks during the day [article #40].
4. **Find alternative ways of self-expression.** Many autistic people express emotions in other ways. Examples are writing, art, making collages, writing poems or finding music that matches your mood [article #40].
5. **Don't let routine disappear.** In burnout we tend to drop routine completely. Yet routine calms the nervous system, because it tells us what to expect. It doesn't have to be strict, but a few anchor points through the day help [article #40].
6. **Do activities that soothe your senses.** Find gentle, calming ways to move your body [article #40].
7. **Spend time in nature.** Neff says nature is especially restorative for autistic people. If you can, sit by a lake, the ocean or a beautiful meadow, or just listen to nature sounds [article #40].
8. **Practice self-care.** Do things that nourish and support you. That may mean enough sleep, good nutrition, exercise, or relaxation techniques such as deep breathing or meditation [article #40].
9. **Seek support.** Don't be afraid to talk to someone about how you feel. It could be a therapist, a counselor or someone you trust in the autistic community [article #40].
10. **Create a sensory-supportive environment.** Make your living and working space as comfortable and supportive as possible. That means minimizing sensory irritants, reducing visual clutter and creating a calm, organized environment [article #40].
11. **Seek accommodations.** If you struggle at school or work, consider asking for accommodations, for example extra time for assignments or breaks [article #40].

### Pillars of an autistic burnout recovery plan
Purpose: these are the key elements, or "pillars", to think through when drafting your plan. Neff stresses that each person's plan will have its own key elements [article #40].
1. **Sensory.** Ask where you can reduce your sensory load:
   - Is there lighting you can change?
   - Can you create a sensory-detox space at home?
   - Do you use weighted blankets, noise blocking or compression clothing?
   - Can you remove irritants (perfume, chemical smells, bright light) from your main living space?

   These may seem like small things, but "всё это имеет накопительный эффект" (*it all has a cumulative effect*). The reasoning is that the sensory system is on heightened alert during burnout, so blocking unnecessary input can make a big difference. Pitfall: many people diagnosed with autism late in life are so disconnected from their bodies that they take a "push through the discomfort" attitude to sensory problems. Neff says these irritants nonetheless contribute to burnout, and removing them helps [article #40].
2. **Sleep.** Sleep is often a problem for autistic people. Paying attention to sleep schedules, sleep hygiene and sleep support helps. Prioritizing sleep is called a critically important part of burnout recovery [article #40].
3. **Important activities.** These are activities that bring joy and energize you. They are probably linked to your special interests, or to repetitive physical movements that calm you, such as rocking, rhythmic dancing or rolling. Be sure to make room for activities that restore and recharge you [article #40].
4. **Movement.** Finding movement that feels pleasant, enjoyable and restorative is described as vital. It may be repetitive movement or movement that gives proprioceptive input, such as rocking or lifting weights. Caveat: your body is recovering, so be careful with your movement regime [article #40].
5. **Demand reduction.** Do a "инвентаризацию требований" (*demand inventory*) of your life and see which non-essential demands you can drop for a while. The article gives four examples (see *Demand inventory* in the Practical playbook) [article #40].
6. **Rest.** Neff: "Я не могу достаточно подчеркнуть это" (*I cannot stress this enough*). Autistic burnout is the physical response of your body, mind and nervous system to constant overload, so you need rest, and a lot of it. Rest can mean focusing on sleep, resting during the day or spending time with your special interest. The key distinction is to aim for rest that feels *restorative*. Endless TV may feel restful but is unlikely to restore you. Alternatives are reading or listening to a book about your special interest, or doing something that soothes your sensory system [article #40].
7. **Accommodations.** Now is the time to think seriously about which accommodations you already have and what could be added. For many people the first step is working out what your body needs. Spend time analyzing your environment: which irritants are there, and what could support you? If interoception problems make it hard to tell what you need, consider starting with activities that build interoceptive awareness. The article points to a two-part piece on interoception, but its content is not in this source (!) INCOMPLETE [article #40].

### Prevention through self-knowledge
Neff's closing word on recovery is that the best strategy is to prevent autistic burnout. Self-knowledge is critical for this: knowing your triggers and recognizing early signs of burnout [article #40].
- **Early signs vary by person.** For some people they include heightened sensitivity to sensory input. For others they include depersonalization and detachment from reality [article #40].
- **What early detection lets you do.** Spotting early signs tells you it is time to leave a situation and arrange time for sensory rest [article #40].
- **Planning ahead.** Self-knowledge also helps you plan trips, travel and daily rhythms so they include time for sensory detox and rest [article #40].

Working on preventing future burnout is described as critical to ongoing recovery and maintenance. These proactive steps help prevent burnout and build a sustainable life [article #40].

### Deep recovery: unmasking, internalized ableism and grief
Neff uses "Глубоким Восстановлением" (*Deep Recovery*) here for deep psychological work: removing masks and learning new ways of engaging with the world [article #40].
- **Why masking needs this work.** Masking embeds itself deep in the psyche. It goes beyond imitating scripts and body postures and can turn into a habit of constantly pleasing others. Masking and people-pleasing make healthy boundaries hard to keep, and we often exhaust ourselves trying to earn approval. These deeper problems often have to be addressed in order to build a more sustainable life [article #40].
- **Who needs more than rest.** If you are a highly masking autistic person, or are recovering from autistic burnout, you may need more than rest and extra supports. You may need to rebuild your identity to include your neurodivergence. For many people burnout is the moment they discover their autism. It can be the first time they understand that their life simply isn't working: they are exhausting themselves trying to function in an allistic world, and it does not work [article #40].
- **Two additional tasks.** If this matches your experience, recovery may also include 1) overcoming internalized ableism and 2) making space for grief [article #40].

**1. Overcoming internalized ableism.** Internalized ableism means the beliefs and biases that come from living in an ableist society, and they shape what we expect of ourselves. Neff's examples: "Я должен быть способен работать 40-часовую рабочую неделю" (*I should be able to work a 40-hour week*) and "Я не должен нуждаться в адаптациях" (*I shouldn't need accommodations*). Internalized ableism produces many unrealistic expectations. We hold on to allistic "normality" and do not respect our own sensory, communication and physical needs, and we will keep wearing ourselves out until we deal with that ableism and those expectations. It often leads to people-pleasing, which makes healthy boundaries hard to create and keep. If our identity depends on how pleased others are with us, we stay stuck in a crazy cycle of over-performing and burning out. Ways to do this deep work are reading, journaling, writing, talking with other autistic people, or working with a therapist or coach [msg #40, article #40].

**2. Working with grief.** Grief work goes hand in hand with overcoming internalized ableism. As we fight our ableism we also come up against our limitations, and grieving them is normal. "Чтобы принять что-то новое, вам нужно оплакивать то, что вы теряете" (*To accept something new, you need to mourn what you are losing*). To accept our limitations we must mourn the image of ourselves we are letting go of. Neff's own example: after discovering her autism she realized her chronic-fatigue symptoms would never magically disappear. She would always live in a body that needs more rest and is limited by its sensory needs. She grieved what this meant: careers she will no longer pursue, and the ways it limited her social world and her role as a parent. She had to grieve her self-image in order to accept herself as she is [article #40].

### Three main characteristics of autistic burnout

### Deep breathing protocol
Purpose: to cope with the fight-or-flight reaction by activating the body's relaxation response. During burnout the body often gets stuck in a stress state and needs additional help to complete the stress cycle; deep breathing can tell the nervous system that there is no danger and help us go through the whole stress cycle. Breathing slowly and consciously can slow the pulse, lower blood pressure and reduce muscle tension, lowering the intensity of the emotional reaction [file #167].
1. Breathe in deeply through the nose for 4 counts and hold the breath for 1–2 seconds [file #167].
2. Breathe out slowly through the mouth for 6 counts [file #167].
3. Check that the breath reaches the diaphragm: put a hand on your belly and check that it rises and falls. If you are lying down, put a pillow on your belly and watch whether it moves with your breathing — that is how you confirm that you are breathing deeply [file #167].

### Progressive muscle relaxation (PMR)
Purpose: PMR means tensing and then relaxing different muscle groups. It works by activating the relaxation response and lowering the intensity of our emotional reaction to stressors; systematically tensing and releasing muscle groups lets tension go and spreads relaxation through the body, which signals safety to the brain. With PMR you deliberately create tension in the muscles and then release it [file #167].
1. Start with the feet: tense them by curling your toes. Hold for 5 seconds, then release. Repeat once more [file #167].
2. Move to the next muscle group in this order: calves, thighs, belly, chest, arms, shoulders/neck, face, and finally the whole body [file #167].
3. The first time you do it, the guide's author advises using a recording that walks you through the exercise; one example recording is provided, but other versions can be found [file #167].

### Six-step calming-place visualization
Purpose: visualization is presented as a powerful self-care tool — focusing on calming pictures or scenarios can reduce the physical and emotional impact of stressors; making it part of the daily routine can build a sense of calm and help distance yourself from painful thoughts and emotions. Some people find music useful — binaural beats, drum sounds or other music that induces a trance-like state, which helps you be more present in the visualization; such playlists can be found on Spotify, YouTube and Yandex.Music [file #167].
1. Find a quiet, comfortable place where you can focus on the present moment without distractions [file #167].
2. Choose a calming picture or scenario to focus on — a place you have been before, a calm beach, a wild forest or a favourite memory from the past [file #167].
3. Take a deep breath and let yourself fully immerse in the picture or scenario; focus on all 5 senses — notice the colours, tastes, sounds, smells and tactile sensations connected with the place [file #167].
4. If your mind starts to wander or get distracted, gently bring attention back to the imagination and the sensations of your place [file #167].
5. Keep visualizing the calm place and stay attentive to sensations in the body for at least a few more minutes [file #167].
6. When you feel ready, slowly return to the present moment; refocus on the sensations from stimuli present in the room to ground yourself again in the here and now [file #167].

### Stress cycle (4 phases) and how to complete it
The model (*Цикл стресса*) [file #167]:
1. **Stress activation** — an external stressor activates the stress response in the body [file #167].
2. **Mobilisation for action** — the stress response gives energy for an effective response [file #167].
3. **Removal of the stressor** — the stressor is gone, whether it was a predator or a looming deadline, but the internal stress remains [file #167].
4. **Body recovery** — in this phase the body notices the change in the situation and takes action to complete the stress cycle. This allows a return to an equilibrium that fits the new, stress-free situation; both body and emotions stabilise, recovering from stress. The body relies on safety signals to complete this step [file #167].

The lesson: removing the stressor does not by itself end stress; the body needs safety signals and completing actions. Eight ways to complete the stress cycle (*Завершите цикл стресса*), shown as an illustrated circle [file #167]:
1. Laugh [file #167].
2. Shake it off / use stimming [file #167].
3. Do something creative [file #167].
4. Move — energetic movement [file #167].
5. Breathe [file #167].
6. Hug (or use deep pressure) [file #167].
7. Socialise [file #167].
8. Cry [file #167].

Dose: spending **20 minutes a day** on activities that help you complete the stress cycle can help release the stress and emotions accumulated during the day. ### Sleep hygiene and the sensory sleep checklist
Sleep hygiene — «ПРИВЫЧКИ, КОТОРЫЕ ПОМОГУТ ХОРОШО ВЫСПАТЬСЯ» (*habits that will help you sleep well*) — in four groups [file #167]:

3. **Avoid stimulants:** limit caffeine, alcohol and psychoactive substances before bed; try not to use electronic devices for 2 hours before sleep [file #167].

### Cognitive shuffling
What it is: a practice in which you mix up thoughts to the point where they no longer make any sense; it moves the mind from a state of vigilance to sleepiness. Why it works: the thoughts are mixed so the mind can no longer find a linear or logical meaning in them; this interferes with the evaluating part of our mind and the parts involved in planning, problem-solving and rumination — the thinking process that keeps you from falling asleep. If you want guidance, the MySleepButton app uses cognitive shuffling techniques to help you fall asleep [file #167].
Self-guided instructions:
1. Pick a word, for example *сад* (garden). Start with the first letter *с* and think of a word beginning with it, for example *сумка* (bag). Picture a bag. Then think of another word starting with *с* and picture it. Continue until you run out of words starting with *с* [file #167].
2. Move to the next letter of the word — *а* — and repeat the process [file #167].
3. Continue until you fall asleep. If stressful thoughts flood in and capture your attention, acknowledge them and redirect attention back to shuffling thoughts [file #167].

### Energy sparks and energy drains
Why: when coping with burnout it is very important to understand what gives us energy and what takes it away [file #167].
- **Energy sparks** — think of them as personal energy boosters: things that make your day brighter and deposit extra energy into your energy bank account. They are moments, activities and even people that fill you with joy, creative energy and new strength. Finding sparks is as important as finding drains [file #167].
- **Energy drains** — conversely, they take away your strength almost imperceptibly, usually lowering vitality and leaving you depleted. Identifying these leaks is like arming yourself with a shield: it helps you see how to reduce the flow of spent energy and reduce stress overload [file #167].

Four sources of sparks [file #167]:
1. **Creativity** — time spent on creative activities can add energy and help you ground yourself and catch a sense of your true self [file #167].
2. **Special interests** — nothing recharges like energy from special interests (as long as we don't overdo it, which, the guide notes, sometimes happens even to the best of us) [file #167].
3. **Vitality** — vitality-related activities are those that bring joy and fill you with energy; they can be tied to special interests or your values, or to repetitive calming movements (rocking, rhythmic dancing, skating) [file #167].
4. **Nature** — nature has a strong restorative effect on autistic people; if possible, sit by a lake, the sea or a beautiful field, or just listen to nature sounds or admire greenery. Green therapy (eco-therapy) is said to work wonders for a burnt-out nervous system [file #167].

Five example sparks [file #167]:
1. **Solitude or quiet time** — moments alone or in a quiet setting can help you recharge [file #167].
2. **Special interests** — time for hobbies, interests and activities that excite you can noticeably add energy [file #167].
3. **Structured routines** — the guide states it is known that consistency and predictability in daily routines reliably raise energy levels [file #167].
4. **Physical exercise** — exercise (or movement that brings joy) stimulates the release of endorphins, dopamine and serotonin; these neurotransmitters play an important role in regulating mood and reducing stress and support a sense of well-being [file #167].
5. **Calming sensory input** — certain sensory moments, like being embraced by calm music or comfortable textures, can awaken inner warmth and a feeling of vitality [file #167].

Five example drains [file #167]:
1. **Social interactions** — certain types of social interaction or prolonged socialising can deplete energy reserves [file #167].
2. **Overstimulation** — environments with lots of sensory input (noisy places, crowds) can cause strong stress [file #167].
3. **Changes in routine** — sudden changes can drain energy; for those who rely on a predictable environment to self-regulate, such changes create extra stress, make adaptation harder and leave you feeling depleted [file #167].
4. **Certain tasks or activities** — tasks requiring intense attention or going beyond your comfort zone can deplete energy reserves [file #167].
5. **Emotional stress** — situations causing emotional distress or anxiety can be exhausting [file #167].

2. Supports comfort and lowers anxiety [file #167].

### Know your boundaries — 4 reasons and 4 steps

### Gradual return to activity plan (3 steps)

### Self-advocacy: why it is hard
The guide links self-advocacy and burnout: you probably already know that lack of access to accommodations and support affects burnout, and perhaps that self-advocacy matters for getting support and accommodations [file #167].
Two factors make it hard for autistic people [file #167]:
1. **Masking history** — when we have spent our whole life trying to fit in through camouflaging and masking, the last thing we want is to stand out; this can lead to people pleasing, which makes it hard to ask for what we need [file #167].
2. **Not knowing how** — what to say, what to ask for [file #167].
Basics: self-advocacy may sound like something big and unusual, but in essence any action that helps you stand up for your needs is a form of self-advocacy. Neurodivergent people (autistic and ADHD people) often struggle with it for various reasons [file #167]:
- sensitivity to rejection [file #167];
- shame about one's needs [file #167];
- people pleasing, a trauma response [file #167];
- not wanting to stand out [file #167];
- simply not understanding how to voice the request [file #167].
Difficulties with self-advocacy are named as one of the reasons study and work can be so hard for neurodivergent people [file #167].

### Self-advocacy equation
«Самораскрытие + Просьба = Самоадвокация» (*self-disclosure + request = self-advocacy*) [file #167].
- Self-advocacy can be hard because it often involves disclosing personal information. The depth of disclosure varies — from an exhaustive statement («Я аутичн_ая», *I am autistic*) to a detail-focused one (I am sensitive to sensory stimuli) [file #167].
- Self-disclosure can also exist on its own, for its own reasons — to tell more about yourself and build connections, to share part of your identity with others. When you combine disclosure with a request, it becomes self-advocacy [file #167].

### Self-advocacy script library (7 situations)
Scripts to use as-is or as a base for your own requests (gender-neutral forms in the original) [file #167]:
- **Eating out:** (1) I'd feel better in a quieter place — can we take a table further from the busy area? (2) I'm sensitive to certain textures — could the dish be made without [specific texture]? (3) I prefer milder flavours — could you recommend a dish with fewer spices? [file #167]
- **Doctor's appointments:** (1) I take in information more easily in written form — may I get a summary of our conversation when we finish? - **Phone calls:** (1) Email communication works better for me — can we prepare a written summary after the call? (3) I'm more effective when discussions are structured — can we outline the key points before going into details? [file #167]
- **Social events:** (1) I need a break from the noise — can we find a spot further from the loud music to continue talking? (2) I may need breaks — can we plan the event so there is time without activities? (3) I prefer structured activities — can we include games with clear rules? [file #167]
- **Work meetings:** (1) I work better when the speaking order is clear — can we all take turns so all ideas are heard? ### Your turn: building your own self-advocacy script (4 steps)
1. **Situation** — first, think of a situation you recently faced where a small change would have helped (changing tables, changing the lighting, asking someone to speak more directly) [file #167].
2. **Unmet need** — then determine which need was not met in that situation (a sensory need, a communication need or something else) [file #167].
3. **Self-disclosure** — next, decide what level of disclosure is comfortable for you (partial, full) and write it down. 4. **Request** — finally, think of what request would have supported you at that moment and write the script of your request; then combine everything into your full statement (disclosure + request) [file #167].

### Relearning your boundaries after autistic burnout
Source: a post the channel translated, credited as "Оригинал" (original) to the Instagram author dr.salenajustice. It explains why life after autistic burnout is different and what relearning boundaries looks like [msg #613].

**Starting point.** "Больше нет возможности «преодолевать» как раньше, потому что твои прежние границы были построены на маскинге и выживании." (There is no longer a way to "push through" as before, because your former boundaries were built on masking and survival.) Now the body asks for "О жизни, которая действительно подходит именно тебе" (a life that actually fits you) [msg #613].

**What you may have been able to do before burnout** (and now can't):
1. Mask all day ("Маскироваться весь день").
2. Agree to everything ("Соглашаться на всё").
3. Push through overload ("Преодолевать перегруз").

"Но теперь... ты не можешь." (But now... you can't.) [msg #613]

**Survival mode: what high-masking autistic people often do:**
1. Ignore their sensory needs.
2. Push through shutdown states ("Преодолевают состояния «отключки»").
3. Try to keep up with neurotypical expectations.

The post's conclusion: "Твои прежние границы не были твоими настоящими возможностями." (Your former boundaries were not your real capacities.) [msg #613]

**What slowing down makes visible.** The post says "Выгорание заставляет тебя замедлиться" (burnout forces you to slow down), and while slowed down you begin to notice:
1. What actually brings you pleasure.
2. What drains you.
3. What you need to feel safe and balanced.
[msg #613]

**What relearning your boundaries can look like:**
1. **Take more breaks**, even when your brain says "ты должен продолжать" (you must keep going).
2. **Say "no"**, even if you feel guilty.
3. **Rethink productivity** ("Переосмысливать продуктивность"). The post does not say how.
4. **Choose rest before the shutdown arrives** ("Выбирать отдых до того, как наступит «отключка»"). Rest becomes preventive, not something earned after collapse.
5. **Build routines that support your nervous system.**
[msg #613]

### Workbook for finding restorative activities: purpose and how to use it
The channel forwarded a free PDF, "Рабочая тетрадь для поиска восстанавливающих занятий" (Workbook for finding restorative activities, "100+ идей"). It was written by the author of the Telegram channel "вход в пустоту" (@justenterthevoid) [msg #532, file #532].

- **Who it is for.** It is aimed first at neurodivergent people with autism spectrum disorder (РАС) and ADHD (СДВГ), because it is built around sensory stimuli and recovery through activities that bring "сенсорные удовольствие, комфорт и успокоение" (sensory pleasure, comfort and calm). The author says people without ASD or ADHD can also use it if they want calming activities in their routine "помимо очевидного по типу сон-еда-спорт" (beyond the obvious ones like sleep-food-sport). The author stresses she actually counted and wrote more than 100 ideas [msg #532].
- **Why it matters (preface).** The preface makes four points [file #532]:
  - We live with busy schedules, life troubles, sensory stimuli and daily overloads. It is normal to sometimes feel the world is too loud, too fast or too chaotic.
  - Neurodivergent people need self-regulation methods that suit them personally. These relieve tension, restore inner balance and recharge energy, and they are often linked to the senses, sensations, movement and hobbies.
  - *Sensory overload:* people with ASD and ADHD are often more sensitive to their surroundings, which is called sensory hyper- or hyposensitivity. Loud sound may bother one person, bright light may make another anxious, and a third may struggle to focus in clutter. Well-chosen activities reduce the impact of these factors.
  - *Recovery after stress* and *maintaining energy:* after an overloaded day or social activity it can be hard to return to calm. The workbook helps find what works best, "будь то успокаивающий запах, любимый вид движения или мягкое тактильное одеяло" (whether a calming smell, a favourite kind of movement or a soft tactile blanket). Constant fighting with sensory overload drains energy, and using such activities regularly helps both to cope with burnout and to prevent it.
- **How to use it.** Each section covers one aspect (smells, sounds, touch, movement, creativity, «и т.д.» in the source). Each section contains (1) questions to help understand your preferences and reactions and (2) activity suggestions to try for relaxing and recovering. It can be used alone, with a therapist or with loved ones. The aim is to create your own personal "рецепты" спокойствия и комфорта ("recipes" of calm and comfort). The closing principle: "вы уникальны, и ваши способы восстановления тоже. Экспериментируйте, записывайте свои ощущения и находите то, что помогает именно вам." (you are unique, and so are your ways of recovering. Experiment, write down your sensations and find what helps you specifically.) [file #532]
- **Disclaimer in the file.** The workbook says it is informational and educational and does not replace professional medical or psychological help. It advises always consulting a doctor, psychiatrist or other qualified specialist about your health and not delaying a visit [file #532].

### Preparation: self-reflection questions about rest
Purpose: before building relaxing practices and self-care habits, the workbook asks you to understand your attitude to rest and self-help and your past experience. It explains that attitudes to self-care are often formed by personal experience, social attitudes and expectations, and that these strongly affect how we see rest. Answering the questions shows which habits help and which need changing. The questions [file #532]:
1. **Special interests.** What are my special interests and which topics motivate me? How could they be used for relaxing or restoring energy?
2. **Simple joys.** What simple things have made me happy? The examples are childhood pleasures: buying a sweet after school, cooking with family, making a fresh bed. Which of these simple joys can I bring into my life now?
3. **Maladaptive strategies.** Which maladaptive strategies, thoughts or habits do NOT help me? The examples are scolding myself when tired and things go wrong, and shutting myself off and being unable to ask for help. How can I change them into more supportive and constructive ones?
4. **Resource allocation.** How do I distribute my resources? Do I give enough time and energy to self-care? Does my daily plan include rest and not only work and obligations? When I'm exhausted, how do I recover?
5. **Draining areas.** Which areas of life and which situations drain me most? Do I sleep enough to feel restored? Is it hard to relax after work? Do social interactions fill me up or drain me? Do I regulate social media use, or absorb information for hours and deprive my brain of rest?
6. **Limiting beliefs.** Which limiting beliefs stop me caring for myself? The example is "if I haven't done enough today, I haven't earned rest". Which beliefs keep me from accepting myself while resting?
7. **Recognising the need for rest.** What signs or conditions do I set before allowing myself to relax? Which physical and emotional cues could tell me I'm tired or overloaded?
8. **Preferred type of rest.** Do I like to "switch off" from everything (lying down, not talking to anyone, doing nothing, silence, lights off)? Or do I like "active" rest (switching between activities, distracting myself, redirecting energy into cleaning, pleasant activities, dancing, physical activity)? Do I look for new stimuli, familiar stimuli, or no stimuli at all? [file #532]

### Sensory module 1: Sounds ("Звуки")
**Questions to explore your preferences** [file #532]:
1. Do you like listening to music? If so, which kind: fast, calm, instrumental?
2. Do nature sounds calm you (rain, rustling leaves, birdsong, splashing water)?
3. Do you like white noise (a fan, an air conditioner, a quiet TV in the background)?
4. Do you prefer silence or light background noise?
5. Are there sounds you find especially unpleasant, such as loud voices, harsh signals or ringing?
6. How do you react to crowd noise or background music in public places?
7. Does singing or humming help you calm down?

**Activity ideas** [file #532]:
- *Nature sounds:* listen to recordings of rain, sea waves, birdsong or the forest. Use an app to create your own natural soundscape. Walk in a park or forest and listen to the sounds around you.
- *Music:* make a playlist of your favourite relaxing music, for example slow instrumentals, songs with a clear rhythm, or even classical music. Try meditative music or ASMR sounds (whispering, light tapping). If you have instruments, play simple melodies or just improvise.
- *White noise and background sounds:* use a white noise generator, or choose "розовый шум" (pink noise), which the workbook describes as softer and more muffled. Turn on a quiet fan or rain sounds on YouTube as a background for work or rest. Try café background sounds if you need light noise to concentrate.
- *Silence:* if you prefer silence, create a "тихое место" (quiet place) at home. Use earplugs or noise-cancelling headphones to block outside sound.
- *Active interaction with sound:* drum your fingers or pencils in rhythm with music. If you like singing, hum favourite songs or wordless melodies. [file #532]

### Sensory module 2: Tactile sensations ("Тактильные ощущения")
**Questions** [file #532]:
1. How do you feel about touch? Do you prefer soft or firmer touch?
2. Do you like the feeling of weight, for example from weighted blankets or clothing?
3. How do you react to cold or hot surfaces?
4. Which textures do you find pleasant: soft fabrics, rough surfaces, smooth plastic?
5. Does water calm you? Do you like a warm shower, a hot bath, or cool dousing?
6. Do you have favourite tactile objects: pillows, throws, toys, fabrics?
7. How do you feel about clothing? Do you like something tight (such as sportswear) or loose things?
8. Have you tried tactile stimulators such as stress toys, balls or textures, and which do you like?

**Activity ideas** [file #532]:
- *Pressure of touch:* a weighted blanket for rest or sleep. Massage with light or strong pressure. Hugging a pillow or soft toy. Wrapping yourself in a soft throw, or trying a compression blanket.
- *Temperature:* a warm shower or bath. A hot water bottle or cold compress on specific areas; you can run an ice cube along your arm. A contrast shower to "переключить" (switch) the body's attention.
- *Exploring textures:* handle different materials such as fur, smooth fabric, rough stone, soft plastic, plasticine, knitting yarn or beads. Try stress toys (squishies, squeeze balls, textured mats). Use brushes, sponges or rollers for body massage.
- *Tactile creativity:* kinetic sand, play dough or plasticine. Finger-drawing in sand, rice or semolina. Pottery or clay modelling.
- *Active interaction with the body:* stretch resistance bands. Rub your hands together to feel warmth. Lightly pinch or massage your fingers, wrists and shoulders. [file #532]

### Sensory module 3: Smells ("Запахи")
**Questions** [file #532]:
1. Do you like smells in general, or prefer to minimise them?
2. Which scents do you find pleasant: fresh (citrus, herbs, mint), warm (vanilla, cinnamon, coffee), natural (forest, rain, flowers), sweet (chocolate, caramel)?
3. Are there smells that cause you discomfort or headache?
4. Do you use aromatherapy, and which essential oils or incense do you like?
5. How do you feel about food smells? Do fresh baking, herbal tea or cooking food calm you?
6. Does any smell remind you of pleasant moments or places?
7. Are there smells that help you focus or, conversely, relax?
8. How do you feel about scented candles, diffusers, perfume and scented creams?

**Activity ideas** [file #532]:
- *Relaxation through smell:* an aroma lamp or diffuser with your favourite essential oils. Scented candles with warm or calming scents such as lavender, vanilla or sandalwood. Herbal sachets (lavender or chamomile) for sleep.
- *Pleasant smells in daily life:* a hand cream or perfume with a light, unobtrusive scent that makes you happy. A few drops of essential oil in the bath or shower. Scented laundry products with a smell you like.
- *Natural scents:* a forest walk to smell pine, earth or rain. Fresh flowers or sprigs of herbs at home. Making herbal tea and inhaling its aroma before drinking.
- *Food-smell therapy:* bake something fragrant like a cinnamon roll or cookies. Brew freshly ground coffee or make cocoa. Cut citrus fruit (oranges, lemons) and breathe in the smell.
- *Create a "запах-комфорт" (comfort smell):* find a scent that calms you and use it when stressed, for example before sleep or during breaks.
- *Be mindful of triggers:* if some smells irritate you or cause unpleasant emotions, avoid them. [file #532]

### Sensory module 4: Visual stimuli ("Визуальные стимулы")
**Questions** [file #532]:
1. How do you feel about bright colours? Do you like saturated, contrasting colours or muted, calm shades?
2. Do you like patterns and textures in interiors or clothing? Which patterns feel pleasant: geometric, natural, abstract?
3. Can certain colours or pictures calm you, such as shades of blue, green or warm colours?
4. How do you feel about minimalism versus detail-rich surroundings, and which helps you feel calmer?
5. Do you like watching nature: sunset or sunrise, rain and drops on glass, moving clouds, swaying leaves or a candle flame?
6. How do you feel about visual stimulation through media: videos, animations, meditative clips such as game playthroughs or soap-cutting videos?
7. Does an organised space (a clean desk, neatly arranged objects) help you focus or relax?
8. Does a certain light calm you: warm dim lighting, natural window light, twinkling fairy lights?
9. Do you like watching moving elements such as hourglasses, bubble lamps or an aquarium?

**Activity ideas** [file #532]:
- *Relaxing visual practices:* create cosy lighting with night lights, fairy lights or candles for soft light. Meditative observation of natural movement: rain outside, a candle flame, water in a stream or aquarium. Relaxing videos with meditative images such as ocean waves, a starry sky, or animations with smooth colour transitions. Adult colouring books with anti-stress patterns, or your own drawings.
- *Working with colour and space:* colour therapy, meaning adding elements of your favourite colours to your space (pillows, throw, rug, curtains). An organised environment: remove extra objects and create visually calm surroundings. Natural elements: house plants or bouquets in view. Art therapy: drawing, collages or jigsaw puzzles with images you like.
- *Visual tools for concentration:* visual timers such as hourglasses or bubble lamps to structure time. Decorate your workspace with motivating or calming images. Make a vision board of pictures that inspire you or remind you of your goals.
- *Observing nature:* walks in scenic places such as parks, forests and embankments. Watching stars, sunsets, sunrises or cloud shapes. If you can't get outside, virtual walks or nature videos.
- *General rules:* create a safe visual space by removing irritating details, excess decor or clutter. Use colour to support mood: "Тёплые цвета создают уют, а холодные помогают расслабиться и успокоиться" (warm colours create cosiness, cool ones help you relax and calm down). Find balance: if bright, chaotic stimuli tire you, reduce the number of visual irritants around you. [file #532]

### Sensory module 5: Tastes ("Вкусы")
**Questions** [file #532]:
1. Which tastes do you love most: sweet, salty, sour, bitter, umami (rich, meaty or mushroomy)?
2. Are there tastes that irritate you or cause unpleasant sensations?
3. How do you feel about food textures: crunchy (chips, nuts), soft and creamy (yogurt, purée), dense (meat, bread), liquid (soups, smoothies)?
4. Which drinks give you a feeling of comfort, for example tea, cocoa or water with lemon?
5. Do you have favourite "calming" dishes or snacks, such as comfort food (pasta, soups, hot dishes) or snacks (fruit, dried fruit, cheese)?
6. Does cooking help you relax or switch off?
7. Do you like experimenting with new tastes, or prefer familiar, proven dishes?
8. How do you feel about aromatic seasonings and spices, and which feel pleasant?
9. Are there foods that help you feel better physically, for example when low on energy or stressed?
10. How do you feel about food rituals, such as a quiet lunch in silence or a beautifully set table?

**Activity ideas** [file #532]:
- *Comforting food practices:* cook a favourite dish that reminds you of safety or warm moments. Try sweet or salty snacks and find ones that give pleasure without overload. Create a drink ritual: brew tea with a favourite flavour, make cocoa or homemade lemonade.
- *Experimenting with new tastes:* add new ingredients you might like (herbs, spices, sauces). Experiment with textures to find a comfortable balance of soft and crunchy. Try unusual combinations such as sweet with salty (cheese with honey).
- *Creativity in the kitchen:* bake cookies or another dessert to distract yourself and create cosiness. Make a bright salad or a smoothie from fresh fruit and vegetables. Recreate a favourite café dish at home, such as coffee with milk foam or a restaurant recipe.
- *Food for relaxation:* cook dishes that are eaten slowly (soups, fondue, pasta). Eat small portions while focusing on taste and texture. "медитацией через еду" (meditation through food): sense the taste, texture and temperature of each bite.
- *General rules:* listen to your body and eat what you really want at a comfortable pace. Avoid overload: if strong or sharp tastes are hard for you, choose mild, neutral dishes. Create a pleasant atmosphere and eat in comfortable, relaxing surroundings without extra noise or chaos. [file #532]

### Sensory module 6: Physical activity ("Физическая активность")
**Questions** [file #532]:
1. Which kinds of physical activity do you like: walking, running, dancing, cycling, yoga or stretching, strength training, swimming, sports games (tennis, football)?
2. Which physical activity do you prefer outdoors?
3. Which activities are hard for you or cause unpleasant sensations?
4. How do you react to rhythmic movement? Do you like moving your head or hands in time with music?
5. Are there habits (rocking, patting with your hands) that calm you?
6. Do you feel relief after active movement, and which kinds?
7. How do you feel about body-based activities such as massage, stretching or other procedures?
8. Which physical activity helps you release tension or lower anxiety?
9. Do you prefer solo or group activities?
10. Are there exercises that help you be more focused and calm? What role does regular exercise play for you, and have you noticed it improve your mood or well-being?

**Activity ideas** [file #532]:
- *Light, relaxing exercise:* nature walks, slow or brisk, in a park, forest or city streets. Stretching and gentle yoga with simple poses such as child's pose, cat-cow or gentle bends. Dancing to favourite music, moving however you want.
- *Energetic activities:* jumping on the spot or with a skipping rope; "Даже 2-5 минут помогут снять напряжение и сбросить лишнюю энергию" (even 2-5 minutes will help release tension and burn off excess energy). Jogging or cycling, which the workbook says can calm the mind and help focus. Interval exercises, for example 30 seconds of squats then 30 seconds of rest, repeated several times. Swimming: water helps you relax, and swimming relieves physical and emotional tension.
- *Rhythmic movement:* rocking the body back and forth, a calm rhythmic movement that can be meditative and relaxing. Rhythmic forward bends at a calm pace. Gently swinging a leg or patting your palms on your knees, which the workbook says help regulate emotions. [file #532]

### Seven types of stimming checklist ("Стимминг")
**Definition.** "Стимминг (от англ. self-stimulatory behavior) — это повторяющиеся движения, звуки или действия, которые помогают человеку справляться с эмоциями, сенсорной перегрузкой, тревожностью или скукой." (Stimming, from the English self-stimulatory behavior, is repetitive movements, sounds or actions that help a person cope with emotions, sensory overload, anxiety or boredom.) People with ASD and ADHD often use it for self-regulation: to calm down, to concentrate or to reduce tension [file #532].

**Stance and safety caveat.** The workbook says that although stimming may sometimes seem socially unacceptable, "в нем нет ничего плохого" (there's nothing wrong with it). The main thing is to make sure your ways of stimming do not harm you. Habits that cause pain or leave scars should be reconsidered. Its example is snapping a rubber band on the wrist so it hits and causes pain [file #532].

**Types of stimming.** Readers tick the ones they like [file #532]:
1. **Auditory:** snapping fingers near the ears, repeating the same melody or sound, frequently touching the ears.
2. **Vocal:** repeating words or sounds many times; coughing, purring or humming; drawn-out breathing or repetitive vocal sounds.
3. **Tactile:** stroking or pinching the skin, opening and closing fists, tapping fingers on surfaces.
4. **Visual:** blinking or rolling the eyes, watching light move, looking at objects from different angles.
5. **Vestibular (balance-related):** rocking the body back and forth, turning around one's axis, pacing back and forth.
6. **Gustatory and olfactory:** sniffing things or food, chewing objects (for example hair ties).
7. **Whole-body:** rocking the whole body, jumping or spinning, shaking the limbs.

**Questions for exploring your stimming** [file #532]:
1. Which repetitive movements or actions help you feel calmer?
2. Which type of stimming do you use most (auditory, tactile, visual, «и т.д.» in the source)?
3. Which situations make you stim more: anxiety or stress, sensory overload, boredom or excess energy?
4. Which objects help you stim (pens, stress toys, clicking objects)?
5. In which situations could you add stimming (breaks at work or school, before sleep, during meals)?

**Stimming ideas** [file #532]:
- *Tactile:* squeeze balls or stress toys; textured mats or soft fabrics to stroke; running beads, bracelets or cords through your fingers; hobbies with pleasant textures such as modelling, knitting, embroidery, sewing or diamond painting.
- *Vestibular:* rocking on a chair or rocking chair; light jumps on the spot or on a fitness ball; rolling on the bed; balance boards or walking a balance path (for example, lay a rope on the floor and try to walk along it straight, like a tightrope).
- *Auditory:* listening to repetitive sounds or white noise; finger-snapping or playing an instrument; ASMR sounds (rustling paper, water drops).
- *Visual:* watching shiny or iridescent objects, snow globes, hourglasses; using fairy lights or LED strips. [file #532]

### Social roles, care and supportive practices module
**Rationale.** The workbook says recovery and stress relief sometimes come through interacting with others or through performing roles that matter to you. Examples are moments of caring for yourself, loved ones, animals or something important that brings satisfaction and warmth. For neurodivergent people these can be especially useful because they give structure and a sense of being included in life, and they provide emotional support. Social roles are defined as specific actions and duties we perform toward other people or within a group [file #532].

**Examples of social roles** [file #532]:
1. Caring friend.
2. Caring parent or partner.
3. Helper or volunteer.
4. Community member (for example in a hobby club).
5. Teacher or mentor.
6. Owner of a pet or plant.
7. Organiser (of events, meetings, projects).

**Reflection questions on roles:** (1) Which social roles in your life bring the most pleasure and satisfaction? (2) What exactly do you like in them: a sense of usefulness, structure, communication? (3) Which roles cause stress or fatigue, and how can you reduce their impact? (4) Do you feel a need to help others, and in what forms (listening, supporting, practical help)? [file #532]

**Activity ideas for roles:** caring for pets (petting a cat, walking a dog, feeding or playing with a pet). Joining interest groups, online or offline, to share knowledge or hobbies or just chat. Helping friends and family (cooking for someone, helping with small tasks, just listening). Caring for plants (watering, repotting, tending house plants or a garden). Volunteering (helping animal shelters, joining charity events or online projects) [file #532].

**Self-care.** "Забота о других невозможна без заботы о себе." (Caring for others is impossible without caring for yourself.) Self-help practices are actions that support physical, emotional and mental well-being. Beyond the practices in the workbook, they can include anything else that helps you feel better. Reflection questions: (1) How do you show care for yourself, and what helps you feel better? (2) Which small rituals or actions bring comfort and recovery? (3) Are there self-care habits worth trying, such as a gratitude journal or meditation? (4) How much time can I set aside for self-care? [file #532]

**Self-care activity ideas** [file #532]:
1. *Relaxation rituals:* a cup of favourite tea or coffee, a warm bath, a favourite book or film.
2. *A letter to yourself:* write words of support and care to yourself, as you would to a friend.
3. *A rest day:* organise a day when you do only what gives you pleasure.
4. *Planning rest:* make a list of small "островков отдыха" (islands of rest) for each day, such as 10 minutes of music, a walk or aromatherapy.
5. *Gratitude journal:* write down three things that brought you joy or comfort today.

**Social interaction: balance and support.** For many neurodivergent people, social interaction can be both a source of energy and a cause of sensory and emotional exhaustion, so balance between time with others and time alone matters. Reflection questions: (1) How do you feel after talking with different people, and who supports and energises you? (2) Which forms of communication are most comfortable: meeting in person, texting, calls? (3) Do you have a supportive environment where you are understood and accepted? (4) How can you reduce discomfort from communication when it is necessary? [file #532]

**Social activity ideas** [file #532]:
1. *Messaging loved ones:* text messages or voice notes are sometimes easier and more pleasant than live conversation.
2. *Limiting contact:* find tactful ways to say "no" to extra meetings when tired. The workbook's concrete example is to prepare a social-media avatar explaining that you want a break from communication, and to set it on days when you want solitude.
3. *Quality time with loved ones:* instead of long meetings with rambling talk, focus on short but meaningful time together, such as tea, a game or discussing a film.
4. *Support groups:* join online communities where people share experience and support each other.
5. *"Социальный таймер" (social timer):* decide how much socializing is comfortable for you and take pauses when needed.
6. *A home "ward":* if you don't want to socialize but want to care for someone, get a home "подопечный" (ward): a pet, a plant or a crystal-growing kit, something or someone to look after. [file #532]

### Monotony and structure module ("Монотонность и структура")
**Rationale.** The workbook says stability and repetition can bring a sense of calm and safety. Monotonous or structured activities relieve stress, calm thoughts and create predictability. For many neurodivergent people this is an excellent self-regulation method, especially when the world feels chaotic or overwhelming [file #532].

**Reflection questions** [file #532]:
1. Which repetitive or orderly actions help you relax?
2. How often do you use monotonous activities to restore inner balance?
3. Do you have favourite structured rituals (sorting, cooking, organising space)?
4. Which activities please you by their predictability?
5. In which situations do monotonous actions help you focus or cope with anxiety?
6. Do you like rearranging and laying out objects at home by some structure (by colour, by size)?

**Activity ideas: monotonous actions** [file #532]:
- *Sorting and transferring:* sort beads, buttons or small objects by colour or shape; pour grain or pebbles from one container to another.
- *Drawing and writing:* draw patterns or mandalas with repeating elements; write words or phrases in neat handwriting along lines.
- *Caring for things:* fold clothes, organising them by category or colour; polish or wipe surfaces slowly and carefully.

**Activity ideas: structured actions** [file #532]:
- *Working with instructions:* assemble construction sets (such as Lego) or puzzles step by step; follow recipes for simple dishes.
- *Organising space:* arrange books by colour, size or alphabet; do a big clean-up of your computer by sorting files, folders and documents; organise stationery, laying out pens, pencils and notebooks in a set order.
- *Creating rituals:* start the morning with the same sequence of actions (for example tea, writing the day's plans, slow breathing); create an evening ritual (sorting things on the desk, preparing tomorrow's clothes).
- *Handicrafts:* knit or weave bracelets following a simple pattern; sew or embroider from a pre-drawn scheme. [file #532]

### Cognitive stimulation module ("“Когнитивная” стимуляция")
**Rationale.** The workbook says mental stimulation, games and humour play an important role in emotional and cognitive health. Intellectual tasks and creative activities let you escape anxious thoughts, activate the brain and enjoy the process [file #532].

**Reflection questions** [file #532]:
1. Which intellectual tasks or games bring you joy?
2. In which situations do you use humour to cope with anxiety or stress?
3. Do you like puzzles, board games or quizzes, and which ones especially?
4. When did you last laugh wholeheartedly, and what made you laugh?
5. Which creative or mental activities help you relax and enjoy yourself?

**Activity ideas: mental stimulation** [file #532]:
- *Puzzles and problems:* crosswords, sudoku or logic puzzles; jigsaw puzzles with different numbers of pieces; simple logic problems or riddles.
- *Learning something new:* learn new foreign words or phrases; try the basics of a musical instrument; watch an educational video on a topic of interest and take brief notes.
- *Creative exercises:* write short stories or poems from given words or pictures; draw a comic based on a funny situation from your life; invent unusual uses for simple objects (for example, how else a cup could be used besides drinking).

**Activity ideas: games and entertainment** [file #532]:
- *Board and card games:* simple board games such as Jenga, Monopoly or Uno, online or with friends; card games such as memory games or solitaire.
- *Logic and creativity games:* take part in quizzes or write your own questions; play an association game, naming words or phrases that come to mind when looking at an object or picture.
- *Digital games:* mobile or computer games that train memory, attention or reaction speed (for example Tetris or puzzle games); simulators or relaxing casual games (for example Stardew Valley, Sims). [file #532]

### Small steps method ("методику маленьких шагов")
Purpose: this is the workbook's bridge from understanding your preferences to actually putting rest into your life. The rationale: "Часто люди не начинают заботиться о себе, потому что цель кажется слишком большой или трудной." (People often don't start caring for themselves because the goal seems too big or difficult.) The fix is to start with simple, accessible actions that fit easily into daily life [file #532].
1. **Choose one goal.** "Старайтесь выбирать одну цель" (try to choose one goal).
2. **Break it into small steps.** The workbook's example: if your goal is to rest more, start by putting a 10-minute break into your schedule for breathing exercises, stretching or a walk, or by setting aside 15 minutes for a cup of tea in silence without gadgets.
3. **Track how you feel.** Note how you feel while doing these steps.
4. **Use the printable exercises.** The author suggests printing the exercises below or opening them in an editor, to build rest into your routine (the *Situation - resource-goal - strategies table* and the *Self-care diary*).

## Detailed insights

### No quick fix: patience as part of recovery
Neff says this is the place in a blog where she would like to offer a "простой трехэтапный процесс" (*simple three-step process*) that GUARANTEES recovery in six weeks. She cannot, because there is no magic recipe and no quick fix. If you have spent the last few years overextending yourself, ignoring your sensory needs and functioning above your capacity, recovery will not happen overnight. Patience is one of the hardest parts of recovery, and you need compassion and patience toward yourself and toward the process. Instead of a formula she offers many thoughts and practices. Her overall message is still hopeful: with planning, purposefulness, self-care and creative thinking you can build a more sustainable life [article #40].

### Rethinking "normality": why going back is not the goal
Autistic burnout is not something you can easily "перешагнуть" (*step over*). That idea assumes burnout is temporary and that after recovery you go back to life as it was. For many autistic people, though, the demands and challenges of their usual life are exactly what caused the burnout. Recovery therefore often requires a significant overhaul of your life, including re-evaluating your values and creating a more sustainable lifestyle. This is the reasoning behind the two-phase model and behind rethinking "normality" in deep recovery [article #40].

### How to approach the advice
Neff stresses that the tips are a menu, not a program. Some come from autistic adults interviewed in Raymaker's research and some are her own. "Это много информации и идей, не пытайтесь применять их все сразу" (*This is a lot of information and ideas, don't try to apply them all at once*). Choose the ones that appeal to you, because in a recovery plan "иногда меньше значит больше" (*sometimes less is more*) [article #40].

### Burnout as a signal
In her summary Neff says you are not alone in this struggle. A recovery plan that fits you and your specific needs is key. It can include self-care strategies, setting boundaries, reducing demands and seeking support from friends, family or professionals. Burnout can also be a signal to reflect on yourself, overcome internalized ableism and face the sadness that comes with recognizing your limitations. It can be a chance to re-evaluate and reorganize your life so it fits your true self better [article #40].

### Relevance to ADHD
The channel author adds a framing note: "Автор пишет именно об аутистическом выгорании" (*the author writes specifically about autistic burnout*). However, the author has both ADHD and autism (СДВГ и РАС) and finds the described experience strongly relatable. In the author's opinion the guide may therefore be useful for neurodivergent people with autism and with ADHD [msg #40].

### Why self-care matters during burnout
When fatigue and heightened sensitivity from burnout affect your state, devoting time to self-care is compared to being wrapped in a warm hug. Activities such as art, meditation, yoga or gardening support emotional recovery because they allow self-expression; creating a cosy environment with comforting elements — blankets, calming smells and music — grounds you emotionally and provides comfort. These practices help manage stress, build resilience and restore a sense of balance [file #167].

### Affirmation cards
The guide offers printable affirmation cards to stick somewhere or carry with you for when you need to hear supportive words. The ten affirmations [file #167]:
1. I believe in my ability to overcome difficulties and thrive [file #167].
2. I trust my instincts and prioritise my mental well-being [file #167].
3. I let go of guilt for needing more time and self-care [file #167].
4. I respect my boundaries and honour my need for solitude when I need it [file #167].
5. I am patient and compassionate with myself during my recovery [file #167].
6. My worth is inherent from birth and does not depend on productivity and achievements [file #167].
7. I acknowledge my limitations and give myself permission to move at my own pace [file #167].
8. I accept my unique view of things and my abilities, which do not change despite burnout [file #167].
9. I accept my special sensory experience and allow myself moments of calm [file #167].
10. I believe in my ability to find moments of joy and calm in hard times [file #167].

## Practical playbook

### Demand inventory
List every demand in your life, then ask which non-essential ones you can drop for a while. The article's prompts [article #40]:
- **Cooking.** If you cook regularly, can you temporarily use more ready-made food, takeaway or subscriptions for ready-meal delivery?
- **Clothing.** Can you design a uniform to wear every day or week, and drop the demand of choosing clothes?
- **Work demands.** Are there work demands you can give up?
- **Parenting demands.** Are there parenting-related demands you can give up?

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The article is about autistic burnout. Extending it to ADHD is the channel author's personal view ("на мой взгляд" — *in my opinion*), not a claim the article makes [msg #40].
- Neff explicitly offers no guaranteed method or timeline. Recovery speed and form vary, and each person's pillars differ [article #40].
- Movement during recovery should be careful, because the body is still recovering [article #40].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the article uses "глубокое восстановление" (*deep recovery*) in two slightly different senses. First it is the second, structural phase of recovery (restructuring life, values, relationship with food, work and sleep). Later it is "deep psychological work" on unmasking, internalized ableism and grief. The source does not say whether these are the same phase or nested [article #40].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the labels of the worked examples in the paper plan ("Rest", "Load reduction") do not match the nine-item list it gives (see *Recovery plan on paper*) [article #40].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the linked companion articles, on what autistic burnout is and the two-part piece on interoception, are not included in this source [article #40].
- The Raymaker findings are reported second-hand through Neff's summary; the source gives no sample size or study details [article #40].
- The cover image only repeats the title, "Восстановление аутистического выгорания" [img #40].

- The guide repeatedly frames all tools as options, not prescriptions: not every tool works for everyone; try one or two that interest you; trying everything leads to overload [file #167].
- Product links in the sensory-kit pages are explicitly examples, not recommendations [file #167].
- Claims such as nature having a strong restorative effect on autistic people, structured routines reliably raising energy, and exercise releasing endorphins, dopamine and serotonin are stated in the guide without cited studies [file #167].
