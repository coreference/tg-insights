# Regulating the neurodivergent nervous system

## What this covers

This topic gathers everything the channel taught about how the neurodivergent (ND) nervous system works and how to regulate it: the window of tolerance, hyperarousal and hypoarousal, dorsal vagal shutdown, the vagus nerve and vagal tone, heart rate variability, and an extensive toolbox of practices that either lower activation (calming) or raise it (energising), including bilateral stimulation through 8D audio and in-the-moment somatic techniques from the author's own therapy. Most of the material is translated from the clinical psychologist Megan Anna Neff (Neurodivergent Insights). According to the source it matters because ND nervous systems tend to get stuck in fight/flight or freeze, which undermines rest, recovery from burnout, sleep and everyday coping — and because, in the author's words, our difficulties are not "all in our head" [msg #35, #578, #34].

## Core ideas

- **The ND nervous system is physiologically different, not imagined.** The author likes the idea that "наша нейроотличность не означает, что все проблемы в нашей голове" (*our neurodivergence does not mean all problems are in our head*): many daily difficulties really are tied to how our nervous system works, and we deserve to know this and to be taken seriously by doctors [msg #35].
- **Regulation is the key to physical and emotional balance.** The ND nervous system often gets stuck in either over- or under-activation; the goal is a "golden middle" inside the window of tolerance where we feel comfortable and can handle daily tasks [msg #103].
- **The window can be widened.** There are natural baseline differences (including neurodivergence), but by observing one's own nervous system and doing regulating exercises one can, over time, build capacity to handle stress and change [file #159, msg #56].
- **Each deliberate shift builds vagal tone.** Every time we notice a stress state and move it in a more regulated direction, we increase vagal tone (nervous-system flexibility) and regain control over the body [msg #578].
- **Rest without regulation may not restore.** If the system stays stuck in fight/flight or freeze after the "danger" has passed, rest time does not deliver its full restorative effect — which harms burnout recovery [msg #578].
- **Regulated does not mean emotionless.** In a regulated state the author still feels all the emotions, but without the sensation that the nervous system is doing "5000 оборотов в секунду" (*5000 revolutions per second*), so the emotions no longer overload her [msg #729].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Window of tolerance
Purpose: a model of our capacity to self-regulate ("Наша способность саморегулироваться"), used to recognise what state we are in and choose a response [file #159].

- **Definition.** The window of tolerance is the optimal state of the nervous system in which we are attentive, adaptive and engaged. In it we cope effectively with stress and change; the sympathetic ("fight or flight") and parasympathetic ("rest and digest") systems work in harmony; we adapt easily to our surroundings. It is also called a "regulated nervous system". It is the zone in which we can healthily handle strong emotions and stress, with a wide range of coping options and resilience to the demands of daily life. Characteristic feelings: calm, stable, engaged in the present. The window is described as "ваш уровень стрессоустойчивости" (*your level of stress resilience*) [file #159].
- **Width matters.** The wider the window, the more we can bear without harm to mental health, the more effectively we handle stress and the more easily we recover after overload [file #159, msg #56]. The channel's framing post asks: do you struggle with anxiety or depression, often feel detached, sluggish and tired, or conversely regularly feel heightened anxiety, overexcitement and confusion, and find it hard to stay engaged and focused through the day? If yes, the cause may be a narrow window of tolerance that limits your inner potential [msg #56].
- **Leaving the window.** Inside the window, gas and brakes work together as in a car and we respond calmly to challenges. If stress is too strong, we leave the window, lose self-regulation and fall into a stress state — either hyperarousal or hypoarousal. Either the sympathetic or the parasympathetic system takes control to protect us and help us cope, "даже если это означает поведение, которое может быть нам не на пользу" (*even if this means behaviour that may not be good for us*) [file #159].
- **Hyperarousal (гиперактивация).** Sympathetic system dominates. Feelings: anxiety, aggression, fear. Physiological signs: rapid heartbeat, fast breathing, raised blood pressure. #103 adds the states it causes: anxiety, irritability, restlessness, tension [file #159, msg #103].
- **Hypoarousal (гипоактивация).** Parasympathetic system dominates. Feelings: fatigue, apathy, detachment/alienation. Physiological signs: slowed heartbeat, slow breathing, digestive problems. #103 adds: low mood or depression, low energy, sleepiness, lack of motivation [file #159, msg #103].
- **Up- vs down-regulation as the response.** Raising activation helps get out of a depressed/shut-down state; lowering activation helps calm down when overexcited or anxious [msg #103].

### Car metaphor of the autonomic nervous system
The presentation asks the reader to imagine: the sympathetic nervous system is the **gas pedal**, the parasympathetic system is the **brakes**, and the vagus nerve is the **driver** who controls both. The vagus nerve is a cranial nerve governing many automatic physiological processes (heart rate, breathing, blood pressure, digestion, reproductive functions). It is part of the parasympathetic system: it activates it while suppressing the sympathetic system, slowing heart rate, breathing and pressure, enhancing digestion and relaxing the body [file #159].

**Vagal tone.** Low tone → difficulty adapting to stress and a narrow window of tolerance. High tone → easy adaptation and a wide window. Conclusion stated on the slide: the higher the vagal tone, the better we cope with stress [file #159]. Elsewhere vagal tone is glossed as "гибкость нервной системы" (*flexibility of the nervous system*) [msg #578]. ### Why the window is narrower for some people
Factors that narrow the window, per the presentation [file #159]:
1. **Traumatic experiences** — people who grew up in a traumatic environment experience overload more often.
2. **Neurodivergence** — people with OCD, ADHD and autism are more sensitive to stress and have an initially less flexible nervous system. This means it is hard for us to adapt to changes and easier to lose control over emotions. ND people often have an amplified stress response: more stress hormones and harder recovery.

### Two-step nervous-system mapping (worksheet)
Purpose: learn to understand your own window before trying to widen it. The presentation first instructs: observe your nervous system — notice how you feel (breathing, tension, thoughts) and identify which state you are in (hypo-, hyperarousal or window); track your reactions — write down what causes you stress and understand how you react to it [file #159].

1. **Step 1 — Map your nervous system ("Картография НС").** The first step toward control over your body is becoming aware of the current state of your nervous system and being able to identify it at any moment. For each state, describe sensation, thoughts and behaviour. The worked example:
   - *Hyperarousal* — sensation: irritable, angry, anxious, agitated; thoughts: "Я просто хочу убраться отсюда" (*I just want to get out of here*), "пусть это прекратится" (*make it stop*); behaviour: likely to shout or react sharply.
   - *Window of tolerance* — sensation: energetic, creative, calm, happy, peaceful; thoughts: "Я справлюсь с этим" (*I can handle this*), lots of ideas; behaviour: more sociable, often starts a new creative project.
   - *Hypoarousal* — sensation: foggy mind, feeling disconnected, exhaustion, heaviness; thoughts: "Я хочу спать" (*I want to sleep*), "Я чувствую себя отстраненным" (*I feel detached*); behaviour: withdrawal, half-speech, disengagement, indifference [file #159].
2. **Step 2 — Map your triggers and helpers.** Once you can tell where you "are", learn to identify your usual triggers (and the helpers that keep you inside your window), sorted as physical / emotional / interpersonal. The worked example:
   - *Hyperarousal triggers* — physical: heat, discomfort, being surrounded by irritating sounds; emotional: anger, irritation, shame; interpersonal: when I feel used or abandoned/rejected.
   - *Window helpers* — physical: calming, repetitive movements; sitting in a comfortable position, wearing comfortable clothes; interpersonal: talking with a friend at a deep level.
   - *Hypoarousal triggers* — physical: too much going on around me (too many people, sounds); emotional: sadness, overwhelm, shame; interpersonal: emotionally tense conversations — when people are angry [file #159].

### Ways to widen the window of tolerance (10 practices)
From the presentation slides "Увеличение вашего Окна толерантности" [file #159]:
1. **Breathing practices.** Slow, deep breathing activates the vagus nerve and hence the parasympathetic system; it helps relieve anxiety, focus and strengthen connection with the body. Conversely, fast, shallow breathing activates the sympathetic system and creates activation when the body is in a frozen/numb state — so breathing can be used in both directions.
2. **Cold exposure.** Studies (per the slide) show cold exposure increases vagal activity and HRV and reduces sympathetic responses. Options: adding 30 seconds of cold water at the end of a shower, immersing the face in a bowl of ice water, or putting ice on the back of the neck. Cold on the neck was found especially effective.
3. **Exercise and movement.** Exercise was shown to increase HRV by stimulating and toning the vagus nerve. Vigorous movement (long walks, running), weightlifting and somatic movement are named as excellent ways to raise vagal tone. Weightlifting has the extra benefit of deep pressure on the joints, which many ND people find relaxing. Somatic movement is especially useful for treating trauma and relaxing the body mindfully. Dr. Sam Zoranovich is recommended as a source of ND-friendly somatic movement exercises.
4. **Mindfulness and meditation.** Contemplative practices have many mental-health benefits; meditation is linked to reduced brain inflammation, better memory and better ability to absorb new information, and to increased HRV and vagal tone. Mindfulness in particular can activate the vagus nerve, especially when breathing exercises are included.
5. **Probiotics and omega-3.** The vagus nerve is deeply connected with the gut (it is responsible for digestion), so when the gut is stressed, the vagus nerve is stressed too. Caring for the gut with probiotics and omega-3 in turn cares for the nervous system.
6. **Laughter.** Studies (per the slide) show laughter stimulates the vagus nerve by inducing diaphragmatic breathing; about ten minutes of laughter a day has been shown to have many health benefits. Suggestion: add more humour to life or try targeted laughter meditations.
7. **Vibration, singing and humming.** Very powerful ways to activate the vagus nerve. One study reportedly showed that chanting "om" can deactivate the limbic centre of the brain responsible for threats and emotions. The vagus nerve is connected with the vocal cords and the muscles at the back of the throat, so using the vocal cords mindfully activates it directly.
8. **Massage.** Mindful person-to-person touch stimulates the vagus nerve and increases oxytocin, the bonding hormone. It can come from another person, but simple self-massage also works; a video by Suki Baker is cited as a good example.
9. **Havening.** A form of self-soothing that creates a sense of safety through comforting, therapeutic touch; the idea is that soothing touch increases serotonin, which calms. Specific movements: cupping the cheeks with the palms, holding a hand on the chest, or crossing the arms (as if hugging yourself) and stroking the shoulders and upper arms.
10. **Relaxation exercises.** These act directly on the vagus nerve to calm the body and bring it out of dysregulation. They can be used before sleep, to strengthen oneself in the morning, and to recover after stressful events; they include various breathing techniques, progressive muscle relaxation (PMR) and meditations.

### Nervous-system state shifting (смена состояния нервной системы)
Purpose: make rest actually restorative and widen the window by switching the nervous system into a mode of safety. Source: the author's translation of Neurodivergent Insights material [msg #578, file #578].

- **Definition.** When we are in a stress state and can **(A)** identify it and **(B)** do something to lower or raise activation and enter a more regulated zone, we are doing nervous-system state shifting. It helps handle stress, and "как бонус" (*as a bonus*) each time we catch ourselves in stress and correct it to a regulated level, we increase vagal tone [file #578].
- **Step 1 — notice the state.** Pay attention to which state your nervous system is in [file #578].
- **Step 2a — if mobilised** (sympathetic dominance, too much excitation, need to calm down): do down-regulating activities, i.e. ones that reduce excitation and activation of the body. The post says excessive sympathetic activity must be balanced with relaxation methods [file #578, msg #578].
- **Step 2b — if immobilised** (parasympathetic dominance — numbness, sluggishness): do up-regulating activities, i.e. ones that increase the body's energy and wakefulness. Up-regulation is useful when you need a surge of energy to stay awake for driving, an exam, work or other activities. The post names activating breathing, movement and sensory activities that "wake up" the body [file #578, msg #578].
- **Explore what works for you** — the author stresses it is important to investigate what specifically helps you, using the activity lists below [msg #578].
- **Why:** "Каждый раз, когда вы намеренно выводите свою нервную систему из стрессовой реакции и возвращаете ее в состояние безопасности, вы увеличиваете вагусный тонус." (*Every time you deliberately bring your nervous system out of a stress response and return it to a state of safety, you increase vagal tone.*) [file #578]

### Down-regulating vs up-regulating activity lists
All from the attached PDF [file #578].

**Down-regulating (calming) core actions:** breathing practices (slow, deep breathing), tapping, grounding, chanting mantras, or stimming to discharge excess energy. Named tools: progressive muscle relaxation; tapping; binaural beats and calming music; guided visualisation; breathing practices; yoga.

**Up-regulating (energising) core actions:** activities that mobilise the body for action — tapping, rocking, dancing, movement, pacing back and forth, creating pressure or friction (e.g. rubbing hands). Named tools: fast breathing techniques; listening to energetic music; lifting weights; doing a stimulating activity; brisk walking; exposing yourself to sunlight.

**Contrasting qualities of experience** (calming ↔ energising):
- familiar situations or activities ↔ new situations or activities
- slow pace ↔ fast pace
- slow, rhythmic, repetitive movements ↔ fast, rhythmic movements
- repetitive, calm music or sounds ↔ loud, fast, energetic music
- warm temperatures ↔ cold temperatures
- soft and few colours ↔ bright colours/variety
- weak stimuli (smells, tastes) ↔ strong or intense stimuli
- quiet setting ↔ loud setting
- dim lighting ↔ bright lighting
- repetition ↔ surprise
- stability ↔ irregularity/spontaneity
- deep pressure/weight ↔ movement

**Calming (down-regulating) activities:**
- rocking; rocking in a rocking chair
- wrapping up in a warm, heavy blanket
- taking a hot shower or bath
- focusing on calming scenes such as a sunset or a peaceful meadow
- using soft lighting to create a cosy atmosphere
- sipping a warm drink, e.g. soothing herbal tea
- listening to soft or slow music
- following slow, even rhythms
- enjoying background sounds or relaxing music
- using soothing or gentle scents (essential oils, lotions, candles)
- applying deep pressure through hugs or massage
- tapping with a bean bag
- humming, chanting mantras or singing
- practising yoga
- green therapy (time in nature)
- using soft materials or textures such as a cosy blanket or soft pillow

**Energising (up-regulating) activities:**
- holding ice cubes
- eating fruit ice
- sour or spicy sweets
- chewing a lemon slice
- frozen fruit smoothies
- dancing to fast music
- splashing the face with cold water
- sitting in a cool room
- looking at unexpected visual stimuli
- changing patterns of light and shadow
- invigorating nature sounds (birdsong)
- listening to fast music
- strong scents (essential oils, lotions, candles)
- light touch
- a brisk walk in fresh air
- touching rough or prickly textures
- taking a cold shower or bath
- crunching ice cubes
- rubbing menthol cream into the skin or using mint or eucalyptus essential oils
- standing in front of a fan
- doing a few star jumps
- running cold water over the wrists
- chewing gum/mints

### Self-reflection exercise on "overreacting"
Purpose: counter shame when told you are "слишком бурно реагируете" (*overreacting*) or "чересчур чувствительны" (*too sensitive*). Premise: the autonomic nervous system works automatically, like sneezing — it fires without conscious control. If you recognise that your body easily goes into fight-or-flight (sympathetic mode), reflect on [msg #373]:
1. What thoughts and beliefs have you formed about these experiences?
2. Do these thoughts help you, or do they cause shame and increase stress?
3. How could you look at these situations anew to develop a kinder attitude toward yourself?
The exercise is taken from Megan Anna Neff's workbook "Нейроотличная нервная система" (*The Neurodivergent Nervous System*) [msg #373].

### Bilateral stimulation via 8D audio
Question posed: is 8D audio "EMDR therapy, only in music format"? The author's answer: it is not real EMDR, but 8D music can have a similar calming effect, helping cope with stress and anxiety in the moment [msg #351].

- **How it works.** 8D audio uses equalisation, panning and effects to create the sensation of sound moving around the listener's head — an immersive experience as if you were in the centre of a concert hall with musicians moving around you. Headphones are required for the full effect. It works on the principle of bilateral stimulation, similar to EMDR, which may help "balance" the brain, giving relaxation and mental clarity [msg #351].
- **What bilateral stimulation (BLS) is.** A psychotherapeutic method based on alternating stimulation of the left and right hemispheres; it can be visual, auditory or tactile. BLS is a key component of EMDR therapy used for PTSD and other psychological trauma; research, per the post, shows BLS may help process traumatic memories by reducing their emotional intensity. Its mechanism is linked to the orienting reflex — the nervous system's natural reaction to new stimuli — which diverts attention from anxious thoughts and promotes relaxation [msg #351].
- **Safety.** BLS is considered safe for most people but may cause unexpected reactions in people with heightened sensitivity to sensory stimuli or certain neurological conditions [msg #351].
- **Potential benefits:** (1) better concentration and focus, especially for people with ADHD; (2) reduced anxiety and stress through the calming effect; (3) a sense of "clearing the brain" of intrusive thoughts. Important: these benefits are only subjective listener reports; there is not yet enough research proving this self-help tool effective [msg #351].
- **Possible side effects:** dizziness or nausea in some listeners; sensory overload, especially in autistic people [msg #351].
- **Not recommended:** listening for a long time; running an EMDR session on yourself using music or other variants of the bilateral stimulation used in EMDR [msg #351].
- **Bottom line.** 8D music does not replace professional therapy but can be a useful tool for managing stress; as with any new method, listen to your body and stop if discomfort appears [msg #351].

## Detailed insights

### Dorsal vagal shutdown (hypoarousal)
Dorsal vagal shutdown, also known as **hypoarousal**, is a type of "freeze response" that happens when facing overwhelming stress or trauma. It is a natural, normal reaction that lets the body save energy and protect itself when we cannot fight or flee; however, getting stuck in it can badly worsen quality of life [msg #60].
Symptoms may include: fatigue; dissociation; numbness; foggy mind; immobility; depression; fainting [msg #60].
It is important to understand that the state signals deeper psychological problems such as chronic stress or trauma, rather than being a standalone disorder: it arises as a symptom of ongoing issues (sustained stress, unresolved trauma or other mental-health problems), pointing to a deeper need for intervention. Treatment targets these underlying problems through therapy, medication and lifestyle changes aimed at stress management and self-care [msg #60].

### Why regulation matters for rest and burnout recovery
The ND nervous system can get stuck in fight/flight or freeze in response to everyday stressors, unable to switch automatically into recovery and rest when the "danger" has passed. As a result, even when we set time aside to rest, we may not get its full restorative effect — which hurts burnout recovery, because rest time is a valuable resource we pin our hopes on. Techniques that switch the nervous system into safety mode make rest more effective and widen the window of tolerance (see *Nervous-system state shifting*) [msg #578].

### Author's case: somatic discharge of hyperarousal in therapy
Working on the nervous system was the first self-help accessible to the author before she received treatment for САТК (the abbreviation is not expanded in this post) and therapy for cPTSD (кПТСР), and it still helps her. Her psychotherapist is certified for cPTSD work, trained in nervous-system stabilisation, knows the window of tolerance, and takes into account the author's other diagnoses, since each adds its own nuances to how the nervous system works [msg #729].
- **Technique 1 — free discharge movement.** When the author's activation rose during a session, the therapist suggested standing up together and finding a body movement comfortable for her that would help "release" excess energy. The author swayed side to side, dangling her arms "как-будто у меня вместо рук рукава" (*as if I had sleeves instead of arms*). This released the energy that was flooding and overloading her [msg #729].
- **Lesson on unmasking.** Writing this, she realised this is how unmasking should happen: without the therapist's suggestion she would most likely have sat holding everything in, and a shutdown would have happened at home, as it had before [msg #729].
- **Technique 2 — single-leg balance.** Next, standing on one foot with the other knee bent and balancing (the therapist did it all alongside her). This switched the brain further, because it got busy with the important task "нам надо не упасть" (*we must not fall*). The author's opinion: such natural shifting of the brain's attention helps well in the moment and offloads the nervous system [msg #729].
- She files both as ways of moving the nervous system from hyperarousal to a regulated state [msg #729].

### Author's experience with 8D audio
The author liked the first audio (The Tucker Zone — warning: it contains a knock on the door) because while listening she visualised everything happening, which felt like training in being present. As a person with severe dissociation she finds it a useful way to hook her attention. The sound is very realistic, so she advises not to set volume to maximum. The songs she likes, but they make her motion-sick because the movement is much faster, so she can listen to only one at a time. As a handy way to cope with stress she likes it a lot; she cannot say how effective it is, but it does switch her mind, as attention quickly latches onto the rhythm and the changing position of the sound [msg #351].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The 8D audio benefits are explicitly only subjective listener reports; there is not enough research proving effectiveness. Do not self-administer EMDR with music or other BLS; avoid long listening; stop at discomfort; autistic and sensory-sensitive people may get overload, dizziness or nausea [msg #351].
- Several research claims (cold exposure raising HRV, the "om" chanting study, ten minutes of laughter, meditation and brain inflammation) are stated in the translated presentation without naming the studies [file #159].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the presentation lists "ИСР" alongside vagal tone on the meditation slide; it is likely a typo for ВСР (HRV), but the source does not say [file #159].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: pages 23–26 of the presentation, the linked articles (#34, #35, #56, #60, #103) and all video posts (#445, #499, #500, #505, #527, #529) carry content not available in the export [file #159, msg #34, #35, #56, #60, #103, #445, #499, #500, #505, #527, #529].
- Dorsal vagal shutdown is framed as a symptom of deeper problems, not a disorder in itself; treatment is aimed at the underlying stress or trauma [msg #60].
- Tapping appears in both the down-regulating and the up-regulating lists in the PDF; the source does not explain how the same action serves both directions — (!) UNCLEAR [file #578].
