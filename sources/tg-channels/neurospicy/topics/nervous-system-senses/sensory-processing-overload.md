# Sensory processing, overload and sensory regulation

## What this covers

This topic collects the channel's material on sensory overload in adults with ADHD and autism: the definitions of sensory regulation, dysregulation and self-regulation; the channel's central thesis that sensory regulation is the foundation on which emotional, behavioural and executive-function regulation rest; practical sensory regulation strategies (a sensory safety plan, environment modification, mindfulness and self-care, sensory-friendly activities); a first-person account of overload being mistaken for panic attacks; a comparison of stimming in ADHD and autism; and a short note on visual snow. According to the source, sensory overload "может значительно оказывать влияние на нашу жизнь, доставляя дискомфорт и вызывая постоянные циклы выгорания" (*can significantly affect our life, causing discomfort and triggering constant cycles of burnout*), so addressing it can significantly improve quality of life and help break the endless burnout cycle [msg #41].

## Core ideas

- **Sensory overload is common in both ADHD and autism, but the stated cause differs.** In ADHD the cause is difficulty *processing* sensory information; in autism it is difficulty *regulating* sensory input (see *Sensory overload in ADHD vs autism*) [msg #171, #172].
- **Sensory regulation is the foundation of self-regulation.** When the sensory system is out of balance, emotion regulation, focus and attention, control of actions and behaviour, and executive functions all suffer (see *Sensory regulation as the foundation of self-regulation*) [msg #46, #174, #175].
- **Sensory regulation should be prioritised.** Traditional approaches that target emotions or behaviour directly are, in the channel's presentation, ineffective when sensory dysregulation is present (see *Prioritising sensory regulation*) [msg #182, #183].
- **Sensory overload is stress, and it can masquerade as panic.** The presentation states "Сенсорная перегрузка = стресс" (*sensory overload = stress*) [msg #184]; a translated first-person post describes years of believing one had panic attacks that were in fact overload (see *Overload mistaken for panic attacks*) [msg #638].
- **Stimming is regulation and communication, not bad behaviour.** In ADHD it relates more to attention and releasing energy; in autism to self-regulation and sensory balance; both deserve respect (see *ADHD vs autism stimming comparison*) [msg #733].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### What sensory regulation is and what it looks like
Definition (presentation card): "Сенсорная регуляция - это процесс обработки сенсорной информации и управления уровнем возбуждения" (*Sensory regulation is the process of processing sensory information and managing the level of arousal*). It influences our ability to self-regulate and to adapt to the environment, and it allows us to filter, interpret and adapt to sensory stimuli [img #168].

The six markers of working sensory regulation ("Как выглядит сенсорная регуляция?" — *what does sensory regulation look like?*) [img #169]:
1. **Focused attention** — attention can be held on what matters.
2. **Optimal response to sensory stimuli** — reactions are proportionate, neither excessive nor absent.
3. **Smooth transitions between activities** — switching from one activity to another is not disruptive.
4. **Comfortable interaction with sensory experiences** — sensory input is engaged with without distress.
5. **Emotional balance** — emotions stay in equilibrium.
6. **Enjoyment of sensory experiences** — sensory input can be a source of pleasure.
[img #169]

### What sensory dysregulation is and what it leads to
Definition: "Сенсорная дирегуляция [sic] - это состояние, при котором наша система обработки сенсорной информации не справляется с сенсорными стимулами" (*Sensory dysregulation is a state in which our sensory processing system cannot cope with sensory stimuli*). It can lead to six outcomes [img #170]:
1. **Overload** (перегрузка) — too much input.
2. **Underload** (недогрузка) — too little input; the source thus frames dysregulation as two-directional, not only "too much".
3. **Increased or decreased sensitivity to sensory stimuli** — hyper- or hypo-sensitivity.
4. **Difficulty concentrating.**
5. **Emotional problems.**
6. **Reduced functioning** (снижение функциональности).
[img #170]

The concluding card adds that sensory dysregulation can deeply affect emotional experience in two opposite ways — "усиливая чувствительность или приводя к отключению" (*heightening sensitivity or leading to shutdown*) [img #185].

### Sensory overload in ADHD vs autism
The presentation devotes one card to each condition, with the same structure: cause → manifestations → what is important.

**ADHD** [img #171]:
- Statement: people with ADHD often experience sensory overload.
- Cause: difficulties *processing* sensory information.
- Manifestations: (1) emotional outbursts; (2) difficulty concentrating.
- What matters: (1) recognising the influence of sensory overload; (2) developing strategies to manage it.

**Autism** [img #172]:
- Statement: sensory overload is also common among autistic people (the card's OCR reads "артимом" [sic], i.e. autism).
- Cause: difficulties *regulating* sensory input.
- Manifestations: (1) overwhelming reactions to sensory stimuli; (2) heightened sensitivity to sensory input.
- What matters: (1) understanding the impact of sensory overload; (2) developing strategies for navigating and coping with the sensory environment.

The 2024 article post adds that in today's world, with an endless flow of information, stimuli and irritants every day, people with ADHD may feel sensory overload especially acutely, and everyday sensory impressions can turn into unbearable load. The article (written by an author drawing on personal experience and professional knowledge in clinical psychology) covers sensory processing, common symptoms of overload of the senses, and ways to ease or prevent the discomfort of overload; its stated purpose is to help readers adapt to and understand their own sensory needs [msg #25].

### Self-regulation and its three components
Definition: self-regulation is the ability to manage thoughts, emotions and behaviour. It helps cope with difficulties, make decisions, manage emotions and maintain balance [img #173]. It includes:
1. **Self-awareness** (самосознание) — recognising and understanding our emotions, thoughts and physical sensations.
2. **Self-control** (самоконтроль) — the ability to manage impulses and regulate our behaviour even in difficult situations.
3. **Self-monitoring** (самонаблюдение) — tracking our own progress, reflecting on our actions and making adjustments as needed.
[img #173]

### Sensory regulation as the foundation of self-regulation
The channel's central model: sensory regulation is the base layer; emotional, behavioural and executive regulation sit on top of it. The original article post lists four areas affected when the sensory system is in imbalance [msg #46]:
1. **Emotional regulation** — sensory dysregulation makes it harder to control our emotions.
2. **Focus and attention** — our ability to concentrate suffers when the sensory system is in imbalance.
3. **Management of actions and behaviour** — sensory dysregulation prevents us from effectively controlling our actions and reactions.
4. **Executive function problems** — sensory dysregulation can overload the brain, and as a result executive function suffers.
The article states that sensory regulation "служит основой для различных других **систем саморегуляции**" (*serves as the basis for various other self-regulation systems*), including emotions, executive functions and behaviour, and that different sensory regulation strategies can improve life [msg #46].

The presentation spells the link out [img #174, #175]:
- Sensory regulation is the foundation for self-regulation; it influences emotions, behaviour, attention and relationships; dysregulation interferes with self-regulation [img #174].
- Impact of sensory overload: it makes managing emotions harder; weakens attention and executive functions; reduces control over actions; interferes with regulating relationships [img #174].
- Improved sensory regulation supports self-regulation, creates a balanced sensory environment and helps cope with overload [img #175].
- Conclusion: sensory regulation is the basis of self-regulation; strengthening sensory regulation improves self-regulation; developing both systems allows one to function successfully in a world of sensory stimuli [img #175].

**Layer 1 — Sensory regulation and emotion regulation** [img #176, #177]:
- Impact of overload on emotions: it disrupts emotional well-being; causes heightened emotional reactions; leads to irritation, anxiety and nervous breakdowns [img #176].
- Emotion regulation is defined as the ability to understand, manage and respond to emotions, and as a set of skills for recognising and managing emotions [img #176].
- Link: sensory regulation provides the basis for emotion regulation. "Хорошая сенсорная регуляция = более стабильное эмоциональное состояние" (*Good sensory regulation = a more stable emotional state*); problems with sensory regulation = difficulty managing emotions [img #177].
- Conclusion: meeting sensory regulation needs = more successful emotion regulation [img #177].

**Layer 2 — Sensory regulation and behaviour regulation** [img #178, #179]:
- Sensory dysregulation leads to overload; disrupts attention, focus and emotions; overloads both the cognitive and the emotional system; and weakens three abilities: (a) concentrating on tasks, (b) filtering out non-essential stimuli, (c) taking part in tasks and activities [img #178].
- Behaviour regulation is the ability to manage and modulate actions, reactions and behaviour. It includes: (1) self-control; (2) impulse management; (3) making considered decisions; (4) adapting to change and new environments; (5) maintaining socially acceptable behaviour [img #178].
- Mechanism: problems with sensory processing (overload / high sensitivity) affect behaviour regulation because sensory difficulties trigger stress reactions, and stress makes it harder to modify actions and behaviour [img #179].
- Conclusion: sensory regulation and behaviour regulation are interconnected; sensory dysregulation can negatively affect behaviour regulation [img #179].

**Layer 3 — Sensory regulation and executive functions** [img #180, #181]:
- Executive functions are cognitive processes for: planning; organisation; concentration; impulse regulation; achieving goals. They include working memory, flexible thinking, self-control and problem solving. They matter for goal-directed behaviour, decision making and task management [img #180].
- Interrelation: sensory problems overload the brain and affect executive functions; sensory regulation supports executive functions and improves regulation of attention and concentration. Conclusion: the two are closely linked; supporting sensory regulation raises effectiveness and improves cognitive functions [img #181].

### Prioritising sensory regulation: building the foundation for effective regulation
Purpose: argues for the order of intervention — sensory first [img #182, #183].
- **Traditional approaches**: focus on regulating emotions or behaviour; do not take sensory regulation into account; are ineffective when sensory dysregulation is present [img #182].
- **Sensory regulation**: the foundation for effective self-regulation; it provides stability for regulating emotions and behaviour [img #182].
- **Three ways to remove sensory dysregulation**: (1) creating a sensory-friendly environment; (2) providing sensory breaks; (3) using sensory-oriented interventions and therapy [img #182].
- **Result**: a solid base for self-regulation is built, and the effectiveness of emotion and behaviour regulation increases [img #183].
- **Comprehensive approach**: takes into account the interconnections between sensory, emotional and behavioural regulation and paves the way to successful self-regulation [img #183].

The concluding card summarises the method: by prioritising sensory regulation — integrating and adjusting sensory input, managing levels of arousal, attention and emotional reactions — we create an environment that improves emotional resilience and regulation skills, contributing to overall well-being. "Признание важности сенсорной безопасности раскрывает силу саморегуляции" (*Recognising the importance of sensory safety unlocks the power of self-regulation*). Sensory regulation forms the basis for navigating the world with more comfort and well-being; whether you are a neurodivergent person or a caregiver, implementing sensory regulation strategies creates a supportive environment and strengthens self-regulation skills [img #185].

### Three sensory regulation strategies
Goal: support sensory regulation and improve well-being [img #184].
1. **Sensory safety plan (план сенсорной безопасности).**
   - Identify the actions/experiences that give you regulated sensory input — the card's examples: calming sensory activities, music, sensory toys.
   - Draw up a plan for managing sensory triggers and sensory-saturated environments (i.e. decide in advance what you will do when you meet a trigger or must be in a high-stimulation place).
2. **Environment modification.**
   - Adjust the lighting and reduce noise.
   - Use visual supports and organisation systems.
3. **Mindfulness and self-care (Сознательность и самоуход).**
   - Practise mindfulness techniques: deep breathing, meditation, mindful (body) scanning.
   - Prioritise self-care: sensory breaks and releasing excess energy.
Reminders on the card: "Сенсорная перегрузка = стресс" (*sensory overload = stress*) and "Обучение расслаблению - важный инструмент" (*learning to relax is an important tool*) [img #184].

### Sensory-friendly activities
Definition: "активности, которые учитывают индивидуальные сенсорные потребности человека" (*activities that take a person's individual sensory needs into account*) [msg #54].
Benefits for neurodivergent people listed by the author [msg #54]:
1. Fewer stimulating impressions that lead to overload.
2. Creating a predictable and controllable environment.
3. Offering different sensory options to adjust the level of stimulation.
4. Developing self-awareness skills and understanding of one's sensory needs.
5. Fewer distractions and better concentration.
6. Higher motivation and engagement.
Effects on general well-being [msg #54]:
1. Lower stress and anxiety.
2. Higher levels of happiness and well-being.
3. Better quality of life.
How to use the author's list: it is only an example of possible activities; the important thing is to observe what you personally like and what suits you, use the example to compose your own list, and use that list at the moment you need it. The author closes: do not be ashamed of the features of your nervous system, since caring for it can significantly improve quality of life [msg #54].

### Overload mistaken for panic attacks
A translated first-person post (source: FINCHOFAUTISMLIFE) titled "Это были не панические атаки. Это была перегрузка." (*These were not panic attacks. It was overload.*), subtitled "Мой аутичный мозг пытался мне что-то сказать" (*My autistic brain was trying to tell me something*). The author spent years believing they had panic attacks, but it was stimulation overload in an autistic adult [msg #638].

1. **Symptoms that looked like panic**: rapid heartbeat; tightness in the chest; the urge to run away; tears out of nowhere. "Naturally, I thought: this is anxiety." [msg #638]
2. **The mismatch that gave it away**: the episodes were not connected with anxiety or fear. They happened in specific conditions — noisy places; bright light; crowds; constant conversations; or having to do too many things at once [msg #638].
3. **Turning point**: learning about sensory overload made everything clear: "Я не паниковал(а), я был(а) перегружен(а)" (*I wasn't panicking, I was overloaded*) [msg #638].
4. **How overload feels** (distinguishing signs): the brain seems to switch off; noise and light become unbearable; sudden irritability or "shutdown"; a sudden feeling of cold or heat; an acute urge to run away or hide; sometimes even losing the ability to speak [msg #638].
5. **Understanding**: being autistic, their nervous system perceives the world differently, which means they can get overloaded "даже если "ничего не случилось"" (*even if "nothing happened"*) [msg #638].
6. **What they do now instead of blaming themselves**: take breaks in silence; allow themselves to stim; avoid bright light and chaotic places; say "no" without guilt; plan rest time after socialising; use grounding techniques; use daytime naps [msg #638].
7. **Message to the reader**: if this is familiar, you are not being dramatic, not broken and not "too sensitive"; you may be autistic, sensory-sensitive or both. "Твоё тело просто просит покоя" (*Your body is simply asking for rest/peace*) [msg #638].

### ADHD vs autism stimming comparison
Translated post (source: ashahaidelhi). Stimming (self-stimulatory behaviour) is often misunderstood, yet it is one of the most important ways children (and adults) with autism and ADHD regulate their inner world. The comparison runs along five dimensions [msg #733]:

1. **When stimming appears**
   - ADHD: often during boredom or low concentration; helps regulate attention and relieve tension; usually decreases with full engagement.
   - Autism: often during sensory overload or strong emotions; gives a sense of comfort and predictability; may intensify with stress, emotional arousal or in new places.
2. **Purpose**
   - ADHD: relieving restlessness; staying alert in boring situations; works as a way to "wake up" the brain ("разбудить" мозг).
   - Autism: calms the nervous system; helps cope with excess sensory information; provides self-soothing and emotional regulation.
3. **Typical kinds**
   - ADHD: nail biting; chewing pencils/clothes; tapping or drawing; bouncing or jiggling a leg.
   - Autism: rocking back and forth; hand flapping or spinning; lining objects up in a row; repeating words, phrases or sounds.
4. **Impact on daily life**
   - ADHD: perceived as "fidgeting" in class; can genuinely improve concentration when allowed; often wrongly perceived as a lack of discipline.
   - Autism: may look unusual to others; sometimes misread as "strange behaviour"; extremely important for managing emotions and sensory needs.
5. **Adults' reactions**
   - ADHD: adults often say "sit still" or "stop it"; support includes stress toys, movement and chewing aids.
   - Autism: sometimes considered "inappropriate"; support means safe conditions for stimming and spaces that take sensory needs into account.

Key thought: stimming in ADHD is more connected with attention and releasing energy, in autism with self-regulation and sensory balance; both kinds are natural and deserve respect. Instead of trying to stop stimming, offer safe options — stress toys, chewing accessories, sensory pauses or calm spaces; these small supports help children feel understood. "стимминг — это не «плохое поведение», а способ коммуникации" (*stimming is not "bad behaviour" but a way of communicating*) [msg #733].

### Visual snow
A forwarded post (from the channel Аутичные переводы) defines visual snow as a constant visual symptom in which people see millions of flickering particles before their eyes around the clock, like television static when the TV is mistuned. The post says visual snow syndrome is common in particular among people with autism spectrum disorder. The forwarding author learned about it only at 18 and was very surprised that not everyone sees this way [msg #137].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The ADHD/autism cause distinction (processing vs regulating sensory input) is stated on slides without further explanation or evidence [msg #171, #172].
- The claim that traditional emotion/behaviour-focused approaches are ineffective under sensory dysregulation is the presentation's position; no studies are cited [msg #182].
- The panic-vs-overload account is a single person's translated experience, not a diagnostic criterion; it does not claim panic attacks never occur in autistic people [msg #638].
- The stimming comparison is a generalised translated infographic-style post; its categories (e.g. typical stims per condition) are tendencies ("часто", "обычно" — *often, usually*), not rules [msg #733].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the full texts of the three articles, the activities list and the video in #450 were not available [msg #25, #41, #46, #54, #450].
- Several slide transcriptions contain OCR typos ("дирегуляция", "артимом", "звоции"); the meanings (dysregulation, autism, emotions) are clear from context [msg #170, #172, #176].
