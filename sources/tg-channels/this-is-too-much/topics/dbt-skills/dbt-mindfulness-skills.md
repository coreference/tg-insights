# Mindfulness in DBT: WHAT skills, non-judgment and urge surfing

## What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's teaching on mindfulness as the foundation module of dialectical behavior therapy (DBT): why people with borderline personality disorder (BPD, ПРЛ) are taught it first, how DBT mindfulness differs from other traditions, and three concrete DBT mindfulness skills — the WHAT skill (observe, describe, participate), non-judgmentalness and effectiveness. It also covers the urge-surfing technique for cravings, how to interpret strange body sensations during meditation, and a list of criticisms and limits of mindfulness. According to the author, mindfulness matters because you cannot regulate what you cannot see: it is the base on which emotion regulation, crisis skills and interpersonal effectiveness are built [msg #35, #36].

## Core ideas

- **Mindfulness is the prerequisite for emotion regulation.** To regulate something you must be able to see and understand what you are dealing with; noticing one's inner experience is a learned skill, not a default human capacity (see *Six reasons people with BPD learn mindfulness*) [msg #35].
- **DBT mindfulness is practical, broken into simple actions and mostly informal**, and it is integrated into all other DBT skill modules (see *Five specifics of mindfulness in DBT*) [msg #36].
- **Non-judgment is a skill, not a personality trait**: you notice evaluative statements and rewrite them as descriptions of facts and feelings [msg #50].
- **Cravings are waves**: they can be very strong but don't last long; suppressing them makes them stronger, observing them without acting lets them pass, usually within 20–30 minutes (see *Urge surfing*) [msg #43].
- **Effectiveness means choosing what serves your long-term priorities**, sometimes at the cost of something else you want, even justified retribution [msg #153].
- **Odd sensations in meditation are normal** and do not require stopping or opening the eyes [msg #4].
- **Mindfulness is not a panacea** and carries real risks and misuses (see *Six problems of mindfulness*) [msg #39].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Six reasons people with BPD learn mindfulness
Purpose: answers the common client question «Меня эмоции регулярно разрывают на части, как мне поможет сидеть в позе лотоса и наблюдать за мыслями как за листьями на реке?» (*"My emotions regularly tear me apart — how will sitting in the lotus position and watching my thoughts like leaves on a river help me?"*). Lena Foer notes that mindfulness training is one of the first blocks clients go through in DBT, and that many don't know why it exists and why it comes first. This post opened a month-long series on mindfulness [msg #35].

1. **You can't regulate what you can't see.** Analogy: tidying a room is an easy task; now imagine doing a full spring-cleaning of an apartment that is new to you, blindfolded — it becomes a multi-hour feat of groping around and guessing where things are. Emotion regulation is the same: you need to observe how your thoughts are connected with your emotions, how emotions show up in bodily sensations, and which way each emotion pushes you. Understanding this is not a "default" human ability; it is something we learn (or don't) during life. The main goal of mindfulness practice is to train the ability to notice and observe one's inner experience, on a par with observing the life around you [msg #35].
2. **Naming emotions lowers their intensity.** Research (unnamed in the post) shows that naming emotions by itself reduces their intensity and pressure. A "black cloud" of unknown affect is far less manageable, as well as far scarier, than understandable emotions you can describe — so mindfulness also works directly for emotion regulation [msg #35].
3. **Relationships.** People with BPD often struggle in communication with people important to them. Mindfulness skills help notice what you are doing and what the Other is doing in an interaction, where someone really reacted aggressively and where they did not; they help notice what you want from the relationship and how far your actions match those intentions. This moves you away from acting on autopilot — and not only in communication [msg #35].
4. **Impulse control.** Impulsivity is a BPD symptom and often leads to risky behaviour and ill-considered decisions. Mindfulness can help develop more control over impulses by observing thoughts and feelings instead of reacting to them immediately with action [msg #35].
5. **Life happens in the present.** Mindfulness teaches you to catch the slipping "now", stay in contact with it and get joy from direct contact with life [msg #35].
6. **Less suffering.** When we forget mindfulness, the mind wanders between past and future, between worries about what will happen and regrets about what happened. Staying in the "here and now" reduces the amount of suffering in life [msg #35].

### Five specifics of mindfulness in DBT
Lena Foer: mindfulness is a key component of DBT but looks somewhat different from other approaches or, for example, Buddhism [msg #36].
1. **Mindfulness is the base for all of DBT.** The mindfulness skills are designed so that they later integrate into ways of regulating emotions, skills for acute crisis states and ways of increasing interpersonal effectiveness [msg #36].
2. **"Applied" practices for addictions and destructive urges.** DBT has mindfulness practices for working with addictions or any other destructive impulses, because such impulses are not rare in BPD (urge surfing below is one such practice) [msg #36].
3. **Tilted toward emotion regulation.** DBT mindfulness skills are "sharpened" a bit more toward emotion regulation [msg #36].
4. **Broken into simple actions.** Ordinary mindfulness techniques are split into separate, easier-to-carry-out actions [msg #36].
5. **Mostly informal practice.** Many informal practices and few formal ones: you almost never need to sit in meditation, but you often need to observe your everyday life [msg #36].

### DBT WHAT skill: observe, describe, participate
One of the first mindfulness skills in DBT. "WHAT" means what to do to be in a state of mindfulness; there are three options: observe, describe, participate [msg #36].

**1. Observe (наблюдать):**
- Start by observing your surroundings, thoughts, feelings and any physical sensations without reacting to them [msg #36].
- Observe without condemnation, without evaluations, without criticism [msg #36].
- Don't react to thoughts, emotions and urges wherever they push you — just notice them [msg #36].
- Don't try to hold on to experiences to examine them better or feel them more deeply — let them leave freely [msg #36].
- Don't try to turn away from discomfort — acknowledge it and be kind to it (and to yourself) [msg #36].
- Stay alert to the present moment; if you get distracted, bring yourself back without judgment [msg #36].
- Pay attention to input from your five senses — sight, sound, smell, taste and touch [msg #36].

**2. Describe (описывать):**
- Use neutral, non-evaluative words to describe your inner experience and what you see [msg #36].
- Don't get stuck on the content — call a thought a thought and a feeling a feeling [msg #36].
- The content of thoughts doesn't always match reality, so it is more fitting to describe them as «Мне пришла в голову мысль, что я ужасный человек» (*"The thought came to me that I am a terrible person"*) instead of «Я ужасный человек, просто ужасный» (*"I am a terrible person, just terrible"*) [msg #36].

**3. Participate (участвовать):**
- Participating means being fully immersed in what you are doing. Sometimes it's easy — e.g. snowboarding down a mountain — and sometimes harder, e.g. doing something mundane like washing dishes [msg #36].
- Participate fully in the experience. If you are taking a shower, participate in that moment: notice how the gel feels on your skin, the water on your body — be in the moment [msg #36].
- Participate in every experience, whatever it brings you [msg #36].
- Practise letting go of self-focused thoughts such as «Как я выгляжу» (*"How do I look"*) or «Как у меня дела?» (*"How am I doing?"*). If they come, notice them and gently bring yourself back to participating [msg #36].

### Non-judgmental skill (безоценочность)
Purpose, as framed by Lena Foer: for people who want to be able to support themselves, build relationships in a healthier way, regulate emotions better, and see more often the world as it really is, without "black" or "rose-tinted" glasses [msg #50].

What it is: noticing your evaluative, criticizing, condemning statements — those that split the world into black and white, good and bad, i.e. statements more related to your interpretation than to facts — and, having noticed them, trying without self-condemnation to rephrase them non-judgmentally [msg #50].

Examples given (judgmental → non-judgmental description of what is happening) [msg #50]:
- «Я идиотка, что забыла дома ключи» (*"I'm an idiot for forgetting my keys at home"*) — judgmental.
- «Я забыла дома ключи и очень злюсь, что теперь мне придется лезть домой через форточку» (*"I forgot my keys at home and I'm very angry that now I'll have to climb in through the window"*) — non-judgmental description.
- «Пойти домой по темной улице было глупым решением» (*"Walking home down a dark street was a stupid decision"*) — judgmental.
- «Мне страшно на темной улице и я виню себя за то, что решила по ней пойти» (*"I'm scared on the dark street and I blame myself for choosing to walk down it"*) — non-judgmental description.

The lesson visible in the examples: the non-judgmental version replaces a verdict ("idiot", "stupid") with the facts plus the emotion actually felt (anger, fear, self-blame) [msg #50].

How to practise [msg #50]:
1. At any time, simply by observing your own words and thoughts.
2. Through exercises — see *Snowflake non-judgment exercise* below.

### Snowflake non-judgment exercise
Described by the author as "hardcore": take scissors and a sheet of paper and try to cut out a snowflake, as in childhood for New Year. While cutting, unfolding and looking at the result, try NOT to evaluate it. Her spoiler: «это сложно!» (*"it's hard!"*) [msg #50].

### Urge surfing (серфинг желаний)
Purpose: "how not to do what you very much want to do but know will be bad for you" — e.g. checking the news every 10 minutes, overeating, reading social media, abusing alcohol or other substances. Urge surfing is a psychological technique from work with addictions: you don't do what the addiction pushes you toward, while staying aware of it. Rationale: attempts to simply switch away and pretend the craving isn't there make the craving stronger. The technique likens an acute urge to ocean waves, which can be huge but pass quickly; a craving likewise can be terribly strong but doesn't last long [msg #43].

Steps [msg #43]:
1. **Notice** that an urge to do something has appeared. Relate to the urge non-judgmentally and don't condemn yourself for it.
2. **Observe the urge without doing what it pushes you to do.** Notice how it gets stronger, then weaker. Notice how your thoughts try to find a reasonable excuse to do what you want so much. Watch the temptation arise to do it half-consciously, in passing.
3. **Wait until the craving passes.** It usually takes 20–30 minutes, after which the urge subsides — the author stresses this figure comes from samples of people with addictions.

What you can do while the craving lasts (complete list) [msg #43]:
- hold a piece of ice in your hand;
- take a warm shower;
- watch funny animal videos;
- light a scented candle;
- imagine yourself in some very relaxing place.

Limits: if the issue is not news or blogs but hard drugs, relying solely on a mindfulness technique would be naive, in the author's words — "I think that's obvious anyway" [msg #43].

### Effectiveness skill (эффективность)
Definition: effectiveness is one of the six DBT mindfulness skills — in the moment we try to relate to our priorities for the future, decide what to do next, and think about the short- and long-term consequences of different actions. The skill's essence is the ability to make such decisions while listening to yourself in the process [msg #153].

Everyday examples from the author [msg #153]:
- Not yelling at a partner who is annoying you, because you remember that the relationship matters and you want it to continue.
- Remembering why you went to medical school when, instead of preparing for an exam, you want to watch YouTube all night.

Historical illustration: in an interview, Harald Jähner, author of "Wolfszeit" (*Wolf Time*) on the rebuilding of post-war Germany, was asked how former Nazis and their victims could work together and build a shared country. His answer (as quoted in Russian): it worked because of something "we despised when we were young — drawing a line beyond which a general amnesty was declared. We simply won't remember and look at who did what in the past"; if old Nazis stopped openly dreaming of Hitler and spreading propaganda, they were accepted. He calls this possibly blindness or making things too convenient, but it led to anti-fascists, former Nazis and persecuted Jews working in one office; "if you are a highly moral person, it's hard to accept that silence helps build a new society. Yet, fatally, that's how it was." Lena Foer calls it hard to imagine a clearer (and also more unpleasant) example of effectiveness: ex-Nazis, anti-fascists and Jews together decided that creating a new society on new principles mattered more to them than retribution for yesterday's crimes and habitual hatred. Her lesson: sometimes, to do something truly important, you have to give up something else — for example, just retribution [msg #153].

### Six problems of mindfulness
Lena Foer notes that she, DBT therapists in general and society talk a lot about the positive effects of mindfulness, so "for variety" she lists its problems [msg #39]:
1. **Not a panacea.** In severe crisis states it is unlikely to help unless you have many years of experience with long meditations — yet it is very often sold precisely as a panacea [msg #39].
2. **Deceptively straightforward.** "Sit and watch your breath, what could go wrong?" In practice different people meet different difficulties: some lack instructions, others lack understanding of what to do with what they find during meditation. Solving these problems is easier with someone to consult — or with a lot of persistence and books [msg #39].
3. **Can become a new reason for self-criticism.** If you lose sight of the emphasis on kindness and self-compassion, practice can easily turn into another reason for self-criticism — because perfect mindfulness doesn't exist and a mind drifting into unrelated thoughts is normal [msg #39].
4. **Can be misused for avoidance or forced acceptance.** Mindfulness can be used to distract yourself, or conversely to push yourself to accept real problems in the outside world that ought to be solved — for example, a destructive relationship or a dysfunctional work environment [msg #39].
5. **Can indirectly feed social inequality** by putting first the approach "everyone is responsible for their own mental well-being". Taking responsibility yourself is not bad, but this way systemic social problems can be overlooked [msg #39].
6. **Risk of encountering something you weren't ready for.** When you closely observe internal processes, you may notice something hard to process on your own; for such cases it is good to have a mindfulness trainer or a psychologist [msg #39].

## Detailed insights

### Strange sensations during meditation are normal
The (anonymous) channel post reports that during mindfulness practice, skills-training participants often describe strange sensations: becoming lighter or heavier, body proportions changing, a feeling of floating in water. Trainers usually advise opening the eyes and continuing to meditate that way to avoid the sensations. The author's view: this is rather excessive attention to a normal process [msg #4].

Supporting passage from the book *Mindfulness in Simple English*: people experience all manner of phenomena in meditation — itches, tingling, deep relaxation, lightness, floating, feeling oneself growing, shrinking or rising in the air; beginners often get excited. Mechanism given there: "As relaxation sets in, the nervous system simply begins to pass sensory signals more efficiently. Large amounts of previously blocked sensory data can pour through, giving rise to all kinds of unique sensations." It "does not signify anything in particular. It is just sensation." Instruction: employ the normal technique — watch it come up, watch it pass away, don't get involved [msg #4].

The author's own condensed Russian rendering of the mechanism differs in emphasis: cut off from its natural volume of sensory data, the nervous system overstates the significance of the specific data that remain — hence the odd sensations. Key point: everything is going normally, you can relax and keep meditating as before; encouraging fears about a natural process is, in the author's words, «ну, такое» (*"well, not great"*) [msg #4].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- (!) UNCLEAR: the claim that naming emotions reduces their intensity is attributed to "research" without naming the studies [msg #35].
- The 20–30 minute craving duration comes, as the author emphasises, from samples of people with addictions; she does not claim it for other populations [msg #43].
- Urge surfing alone is described as naive for hard drug use [msg #43].
- (!) CONTRADICTION (mild): the book passage explains odd sensations as previously blocked sensory data flowing through more efficiently as relaxation sets in, while the author's "essence" paraphrase says the nervous system, cut off from its natural sensory input, overstates the remaining signals. Both conclude the sensations are harmless [msg #4].
- The author's stance in #4 runs against common trainer practice (advising participants to open their eyes to avoid the sensations) [msg #4].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: effectiveness is called "one of six" DBT mindfulness skills, but the source here does not list all six; the WHAT skill's three options and non-judgmentalness appear in other posts, but the full set of six is not enumerated [msg #153, #36, #50].
- Mindfulness is unlikely to help in severe crises without long meditation experience, and may surface material that needs a trainer or psychologist (see *Six problems of mindfulness*) [msg #39].
