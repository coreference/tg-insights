# BPD in relationships: rejection sensitivity, mentalization and loneliness

## What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel taught about how people with borderline personality disorder (BPD; Russian ПРЛ) experience other people. It covers mentalization and its distortions (hypermentalization, mentalization deficit), rejection sensitivity in BPD and ADHD and its possible brain basis, emotional empathy across diagnoses, loneliness in depression and BPD, the smaller and more exclusive social networks of people with BPD, and the interpersonal-hypersensitivity phenotype that explains controlling behaviour in close relationships. The source treats this as important because rejection can be felt as uncontrollable pain. It also says that loneliness is one of the central experiences in BPD and affects treatment outcomes, and that putting all social support into one relationship makes the whole system fragile [msg #3, #11, #66, #251, #267].

This part adds one post and its linked Telegraph longread by Katya Klimochkina on the **favorite person (FP)** in borderline personality disorder (BPD; Russian ПРЛ). It covers who the FP is and why the attachment forms. It gives two self-recognition checklists, one for having an FP and one for being an FP, and lists what the FP gets from the person with BPD. It then follows the course of the relationship: obsessive attachment, idealization, splitting into devaluation, burnout and ending. It closes with what can be changed. The author treats the topic as important because an FP relationship is satisfying at first but over time often becomes unhealthy and makes BPD symptoms worse. Its ending is unbearable pain that strengthens the fear of abandonment [msg #113, article #113].

**Relation to the existing topic file.** The existing file mentions the "favorite person" only as one of the intense, exclusive relationships in a BPD social network (msg #251), and lists idealization-to-devaluation in seconds as a sign of mentalization deficit (msg #198). This part **extends** both: it defines the FP in full and explains splitting as the mechanism behind the angel-to-villain switch. It ties rejection sensitivity (msg #66, #190) to the FP dynamic. The ending is consistent with the "widen the network" recommendation (msg #251), but this post proposes a different remedy: the person with BPD learning to notice and slow down their own patterns [msg #113, article #113].

## Core ideas

- **Hypermentalization is not specific to BPD.** A January 2022 meta-analysis found no special link between BPD and hypermentalization. People with psychological disorders in general "read into" others more than people without disorders. Using over-interpretation to confirm a BPD diagnosis is therefore a poor idea [msg #3].
- **Rejection can hurt like physical pain.** Perceived dislike, even just a poker face, activates the same brain areas as physical pain. In ADHD, and in rejection sensitive dysphoria, the brain may regulate this pain-like activity poorly [msg #66].
- **Fear of rejection in BPD may have a neural correlate.** A recent study found that the rostromedial prefrontal cortex, which normally responds to social rejection, stays inactive in people with BPD [msg #190].
- **Mentalization is the "glue" of the self and of relationships.** In BPD it can be deficient, and under strong stress it can be lost altogether [msg #198].
- **Loneliness in BPD is not only about the number of people.** People with BPD have smaller, less diverse networks centred on a few intense relationships. They still report more subjective loneliness even when people are around [msg #11, #251].
- **Exclusive relationships give closeness but make the system vulnerable.** Widening the network is a clinical recommendation, so that a conflict with one person stays a conflict with one person [msg #251].
- **Interpersonal hypersensitivity as a separate BPD phenotype.** It explains why someone may try to control a relationship precisely because it matters so much [msg #267].

- **Many, though not all, people with BPD need a favorite person.** The FP is a personal kind of superhero, a "rescuer" they lean on for emotional support, look up to or idealise. The author's framing: every city needs a hero, Gotham has Batman and New York has Spider-Man; "Не каждому, но многим людям с пограничным расстройством личности нужен favorite person" (Not everyone, but many people with BPD need a favorite person) [msg #113].
- **An external support system cuts both ways.** While the hero is near, everything is under control and the person feels safe, accepted and calm. But fear of abandonment and rejection is one of the most important variables in BPD. So when the support system "fails" by not answering a message, moving to another country or a big fight, the whole support collapses [msg #113].
- **Nobody chooses it.** Neither the FP nor the person with BPD consciously chooses this role. The attachment forms in response to an unmet need that suddenly gets met [article #113].
- **FP relationships tend to destroy themselves.** The demand for attention keeps growing until it suffocates the FP and the relationship ends. The ending confirms the very fear of abandonment that drove the relationship [article #113].
- **It is no one's fault, but it can and should be changed** [article #113].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Mentalization vs empathy vs hypermentalization (meta-analysis summary)
The channel's first post summarised a meta-analysis on BPD and hypermentalization in five points [msg #3]:
1. **Mentalization** is a person's ability to understand what is going on inside another person in a given situation. Empathy is different: it includes not only understanding but also emotionally living through the other's state [msg #3].
2. **Hypermentalization** is a tendency to build excessive, inadequate models of what is happening with another person. It is a false certainty that we understand what the other person thinks and feels [msg #3].
3. **How BPD therapy uses this.** BPD therapy puts emphasis on giving up hypermentalization, because it has been considered typical of people with BPD. The rule is: do not guess what the other person thinks, and accept that we know no more about their feelings than they have told us. The author calls this a simplification, but it helps counter the opposite extreme, the certainty that we fully understand another's state [msg #3].
4. **What the meta-analysis found.** The popular idea was that hypermentalization occurs in BPD far more often than in other psychological disorders. The meta-analysis, published in January 2022, says no: there is no special link between BPD and hypermentalization. People with psychological disorders "додумывают" (read into, fill in) more about others than people without disorders, "и на этом все" (and that's it) [msg #3].
5. **Implications.** Trying to confirm a BPD diagnosis on the basis of over-interpretation is "так себе идея" (a so-so idea). The thought "я много додумываю, потому что у меня ПРЛ" ("I read into things a lot because I have BPD") is also not supported by science [msg #3].

### What mentalization helps you understand (four functions)
The author defines mentalization as the ability to understand one's own and others' behaviour as the result of inner states: thoughts, feelings, wishes and intentions. Her topical example: *Катя на меня кричит не потому, что ненавидит, а потому, что устала/ хочет есть/ расстроена* (Katya is yelling at me not because she hates me, but because she is tired / hungry / upset). Mentalization helps to understand [msg #198]:
1. why I feel what I feel;
2. why another person does what they do;
3. where my emotions end and someone else's begin;
4. that reality can differ between what is in my head and what is in other people's heads [msg #198].

### Signs of mentalization deficit in BPD
When mentalization is missing (a deficit), the author lists how it looks in BPD [msg #198]:
1. **Not distinguishing one's own feelings**, especially when they are contradictory [msg #198].
2. **Quickly attributing intentions to others**, e.g. «Он меня игнорирует, потому что я ему не нравлюсь» ("He is ignoring me because he doesn't like me") even when that is not true [msg #198].
3. **Getting lost in relationships**, not understanding which part is one's own contribution and which is the other person's [msg #198].
4. **Switching from idealization to devaluation in seconds.** In her words, from "он - краш" (he's a crush) to "он - днище" (he's the bottom/trash) [msg #198].
5. **"What I feel now is the truth"**, e.g. «Если я сейчас в ярости, значит, я ужасный человек» ("If I'm furious right now, it means I'm a terrible person") [msg #198].
6. **Inner emptiness**, because the person does not feel a stable "self" [msg #198].

Under **strong stress** a person with BPD can "lose mentalization" entirely, feeling neither themselves nor the other, only pain, many emotions and chaos [msg #198].

The **glue metaphor**: mentalization is like glue where glue is badly needed. Without it a person [msg #198]:
- internally, does not know who they are;
- in relationships, does not understand that others cannot read minds (and that they cannot either);
- in behaviour, does not know what they really want or feel.

Developing mentalization lets you understand who you are, take yourself and others into account, and "жить как-то поприятнее, кажется" (live somewhat more pleasantly, it seems) [msg #198].

### Five components of the capacity for mentalization
Technically, the "capacity for mentalization" is the skill of [msg #198]:
1. **Naming and distinguishing one's feelings** [msg #198].
2. **Being aware of one's thoughts and desires** [msg #198].
3. **Guessing at others' experiences without "thinking for them"** («думать за них»), which keeps it apart from hypermentalization [msg #198].
4. **Understanding that thoughts are thoughts, not facts**, and that emotions are also just emotions, not facts [msg #198].
5. **Seeing that emotions come and go** rather than defining one's whole being or existence [msg #198].

### Three manifestations of rejection sensitivity
In the post on rejection sensitive dysphoria (RSD), the author lists what people with rejection sensitivity may do [msg #66]:
1. **Anticipatory anxiety.** They feel strong anxiety or other negative emotions while waiting for negative feedback, winding themselves up ("сами себя подогревая") [msg #66].
2. **Trouble with neutral or ambiguous situations.** If the other person is not beaming with a smile but gives mixed signals or behaves neutrally or coldly, this is read as non-acceptance, with the corresponding consequences [msg #66].
3. **Strong reactions to perceived rejection, ignoring or devaluation**, leading to anger or extreme sadness and strong anxiety [msg #66].

### The rostromedial prefrontal cortex (rmPFC) model of fear of rejection in BPD
**The finding.** A recent study the author cites found that the rostromedial prefrontal cortex, which normally responds to social rejection, stays inactive in people with BPD. This inactivity may explain the heightened sensitivity to and fear of rejection that often torment people with BPD. The full mechanism is laid out in the Telegraph longread by Katya Klimochkina and the channel (@bpdflowers) [msg #190, article #190].

**What the rmPFC normally does (step by step, per the longread)** [article #190]:
1. **It fires when a situation is appraised as rejection.** Excitation in the rmPFC normally appears when a situation is evaluated as a «ситуации отвержения» (rejection situation). The article's examples: we were not listened to; someone was rude; someone stared at their phone instead of looking at us during a conversation; someone interrupted; someone was late; or, "что-то «повеселей»" (something "more fun"), the date turned around and walked off into the sunset before a first date had even begun [article #190].
2. **It lets people reinterpret or downplay negative social interactions** by considering alternative explanations, e.g. "Он, вероятно, занят" ("He's probably busy") instead of "Он меня ненавидит" ("He hates me") [article #190].
3. **It locates the cause "outside".** "In the norm" the brain responds to social rejection as if something were going wrong in the environment: something out there (во вне) is the cause of the perceived rejection. The article's example: «Дам подруге скидку за не молниеносный ответ на мое сообщение, ведь время 23:59 и может она уснула уже, поздно же» ("I'll give my friend a pass for not answering my message instantly — it's 23:59, maybe she's already asleep, it's late") [article #190].
4. **It reads others' behaviour through their mental and emotional state.** The rmPFC activates to try to understand others' behaviour in the context of their mental and emotional state, "что-то на «эмпатичном»" (something on the "empathic" side), for the sake of coexisting among people and with friends [article #190].
5. **It switches on repair.** The cortex's activity of "searching for alternative explanations" instead of "they hate me" may be tied to attempts to maintain close social bonds, which matter for survival and for quality of life. Activity in this area as it were "включает" (switches on) attempts to fix the situation [article #190].

**What goes wrong in BPD.** In BPD this area stays inactive, which raises the article's puzzle: the feeling of rejection is there, but the brain zone responsible for it is not engaged. The study put forward the "занятное предположение" (curious hypothesis) that this very lack of activity explains why people with BPD feel slights more acutely [article #190]:
- **The hypothesis.** The brain cannot sustain or amplify activity in the rmPFC, an important node of the **mentalization network** [article #190].
- **The mentalization network** is responsible for understanding other people's emotions, intentions and social signals. The rmPFC, a key area in it, is responsible for self-referential thinking, emotion regulation and differentiating one's own emotions from other people's intentions [article #190].
- **No reappraisal.** In BPD this process apparently breaks down, leading to an inability to mentally reframe a negative social experience; the lack of activity does not allow the reappraisal to happen. The article's contrast: where a person without BPD finds an explanation "in the environment" (a colleague was rude because they are overloaded), a person with BPD "возьмет «огонь на себя», обвинит себя" (will "draw the fire onto themselves", blame themselves). The opportunity to find an alternative explanation for the rejection, rethink the situation and regulate oneself does not arise [article #190].
- **The feedback loop.** The feedback between fear of rejection and mentalization-related brain activity may strengthen the expectation of being slighted. Instead of using mentalization as protection against fear of rejection, people with BPD may experience an uncontrollable stream of negative thoughts and emotions that reinforces heightened expectations of rejection — in simpler words, they «читают мысли других» ("read other people's minds") and «накручивают себя» ("wind themselves up") [article #190].

**Four manifestations of reduced rmPFC activity.** Both the post and the longread summarise that reduced activity in the rmPFC in BPD may show up as [msg #190, article #190]:
1. **Difficulty reinterpreting ambiguous social signals.** A neutral or unintentional slight is taken as intentional, e.g. "косо посмотрели" (someone looked at me askance) [msg #190].
2. **A heightened tendency toward negative attribution**, assuming that others have hostile intentions or do not want to deal with me «из-за меня» ("because of me") [msg #190].
3. **Emotional dysregulation** that makes the pain of rejection unbearable and increases avoidance or the likelihood of extreme emotional reactions [msg #190].
4. **A vicious circle.** "Felt rejection" further weakens the mentalization system, which makes emotions harder to regulate in future social interactions [msg #190].

As a result, people with BPD may experience an uncontrollable stream of negative thoughts and emotions. They "накручивают себя" (wind themselves up) that loved ones will leave and that everyone around is judging them, largely because of how this brain area works. The author calls her own headline («Страх отвержения при ПРЛ из-за неработающего мозга», "Fear of rejection in BPD due to a non-working brain") silly and clickbaity, but the topic "огонь" (fire) [msg #190].

**The good news.** According to the longread, breaking the feedback loop between fear of rejection and these brain processes with targeted psychotherapy strategies may help improve emotion regulation and social functioning. Its conclusion: "психотерапия помогает не бояться отвержения, а еще меняет активность в мозге, ура!" (psychotherapy helps you not fear rejection, and also changes brain activity, hooray!). The longread does not name which psychotherapy strategies are meant [article #190].

### Interpersonal hypersensitivity model (Gunderson and Lyons-Ruth)
**What it is.** The author arrived at it through the social-networks post and the Gunderson and Lyons-Ruth paper, which she describes as a large-scale collection of "ВСЕ теоретическое о ПРЛ" (everything theoretical about BPD). Descriptions of BPD usually stop at emotional instability, impulsivity, fear of abandonment and rejection, emptiness and desolation. These authors went further and proposed that **interpersonal hypersensitivity**, being especially sensitive to rejection and to signs of losing a relationship, be treated as a separate phenotype of BPD [msg #267].

**What "phenotype" means here.** It is not a separate diagnosis and not an official BPD subtype. It is a relatively independent set of features through which the disorder can manifest [msg #267].

**How it works.**
1. **Relationships become regulators.** Relationships become especially significant, including for regulating one's state. The author's example: you need a close person to calm down so as not to cry about work [msg #267].
2. **Everything inside the relationship gains huge weight.** Signs like these can be felt as a potential threat to the bond, and therefore as very significant events [msg #267]:
   - the other's unavailability (not answering messages for more than 3 minutes);
   - a change in their attitude (not putting hearts on all the reels);
   - criticism or evaluations;
   - distance.
3. **Hyperactivation under threat.** In Gunderson's model, a threat of "loss of connection" can trigger a state of hyperactivation. The person may move to **fight reactions**, such as anger or aggressive behaviour, or **flight reactions**, such as withdrawal and avoidance. These reactions can in turn feed conflict or distance in the relationship [msg #251].
4. **The paradox.** When the relationship is stable, close relationships can give a lot of support and a sense of connection, "эйфория принятости" (the euphoria of being accepted). At the same time the person becomes especially sensitive to signs of possible rejection or loss of that bond. Exclusive relationships give much closeness and support, but they make the system vulnerable. The author's illustration: one moment all is affectionate; then something happens (real or perceived), rejection is felt, and the person loses what was just the meaning of life [msg #251].

### Gene–environment–developmental model of controlling interpersonal strategies
**The question it answers.** Why does a person start doing "дурацкие штуки" (stupid things) with relationships, such as control, pressure or manipulation, if those relationships are SO important to them? The article proposes viewing this behaviour through a **gene–environment–developmental model**. The author compares it to the biosocial model in DBT or the diathesis-stress model, "но может быть не совсем..?" (but maybe not quite..?). She approves that it does not look for a single cause like «это из-за отца» ("it's because of the father") or «это характер» ("it's character") [msg #267].

**Components:**
1. **Individual vulnerability**, e.g. high sensitivity to interpersonal stressors [msg #267].
2. **A specific environment and developmental experience.** How predictably close adults responded to the child's need for help, closeness and comfort, and how well the child could get the needed response through ordinary childhood means [msg #267].
3. **Gradually formed interaction strategies.** For example, **controlling interpersonal strategies**: ways of influencing another's behaviour to get or keep the needed degree of closeness and availability. The authors discuss **controlling-caregiving** and **controlling-punitive** strategies. Their implicit logic: «Мне очень важно, чтобы ты был рядом, НО я не уверен, что ты останешься рядом, ПОЭТОМУ мне нужно как-то повлиять на то, что ты будешь делать» ("It's very important to me that you're near, BUT I'm not sure you'll stay, SO I need to somehow influence what you'll do"). In practice this can look very different: excessive demands for closeness, control, anger, punishment, or excessive caretaking of the other [msg #267].

**Lesson and caveat.** If someone controls a partner because losing the relationship feels subjectively very threatening, that helps explain why the behaviour arose. It can still be destructive for the relationship. The final paradox: the more important the relationship is for one's sense of safety, the stronger the urge to control what happens inside it. Attempts to control another person can themselves become a source of conflict and distance [msg #267].

### Widening the social network as a clinical recommendation
**The problem.** The author cites a study on social networks in BPD (PMC11708991). People with BPD on average have smaller and less diverse social networks, and intolerance of aloneness is linked to how the circle of contacts is structured. The typical network of a "conditional person with BPD" consists of very intense, exclusive relationships (a romantic partner, a "favorite person", a therapist) and fewer "lighter" ties: acquaintances, colleagues, people from different areas of life. If much of the social support is concentrated on one person, that person's unavailability, conflict with them or distance from them matters far more. It stops being "мы сегодня поссорились" (we argued today), and most of the social-connection system "и кукушечка" (and one's sanity, lit. "the little cuckoo") is under threat [msg #251].

**The recommendation.** Widening the person's social network is described as an actual clinical recommendation. The author's tongue-in-cheek summary is "Ваше лечение ПРЛ: идите ищите друзей" (your BPD treatment: go find friends). Widening means adding [msg #251]:
1. different social groups;
2. colleagues from work or study;
3. relationships of lower emotional intensity;
4. communities around shared interests.

**The goal.** The aim is not to teach the person to stop needing close relationships. It is to make sure that loneliness does not mean losing the whole system of connection with people. Besides "the most important person" there should also be colleagues, friends, an occupation of one's own, a community, familiar places and activities. Then a conflict with one person stays a conflict with one person, instead of automatically turning into «я снова один, меня никто не любит, и я не понимаю, как дальше жить, ааааа» ("I'm alone again, nobody loves me, and I don't understand how to go on living, aaaa") [msg #251].

**Limits.** More people is not a full solution: "проблема (и решение) не только в том, чтобы «найти побольше людей», но и не без этого" (the problem, and the solution, is not only to "find more people", but not without it either). The author's resigned note: you will have to talk to people and leave the house [msg #251].

### Who the favorite person is
For a person with BPD, the FP is "больше, чем просто лучший друг" (more than just a best friend): "Это тот, кто может «сделать день» или сломать его и всю жизнь заодно" (It is the one who can "make the day" or break it, and the whole life along with it). The FP is often idealised and seen as a source of stability, self-affirmation and emotional support, the centre of attention and attachment [article #113].
- The person with BPD relies heavily on the FP for emotional support, seeks attention and validation from them, and looks up to or exalts them [article #113].
- The FP is the object of complete attachment and extreme love. This goes beyond what others usually call a best friend or loved one [article #113].
- The FP is perceived as caring, understanding and supportive. They give warmth, care and confidence that everything will be ok and solvable. They become indispensable for emotional well-being and almost the only way to regulate oneself and get through "приколы жизни" (life's curveballs) [article #113].
- **Who can be an FP:** a friend, a family member, a crush, a romantic partner, or anyone who cares, from a mentor or teacher to colleagues or managers [article #113].

### Why someone needs an FP
The person needs "that one" person to give them the sense of safety and acceptance they do not feel within themselves. According to the author, neglect (ignoring the child's needs) or an invalidating (devaluing) childhood environment is "чаще норма, чем редкость" (more often the norm than the exception) in people with BPD. If the people who raised them did not form a close bond, the need for stable, loving, supportive relationships stays unmet. The FP is there to give what was missing in childhood and did not form within oneself [article #113].

### Five signs that you have a favorite person
1. **Jealousy** of other people or of the FP's activities [article #113].
2. **Needing a lot of attention** from this person: messages, meetings, initiative coming from them [article #113].
3. **Fantasising about the person**, inventing scenarios where you spend time together [article #113].
4. **Striving to please** them and not allowing yourself to disagree [article #113].
5. **Swinging between "hot and cold" perceptions** of the person, from god-like to a fiend from hell [article #113].

### Five signs that you are someone's favorite person
1. **You are their first contact.** Good news, bad news, a chat, or a need for help: all of it goes to you [article #113].
2. **You feel responsible for their mood changes**, and for their life in general [article #113].
3. **You think about your close person with BPD when making decisions** [article #113].
4. **You constantly reassure them.** You feel you must provide calm and confidence [article #113].
5. **You feel admired and needed** [article #113].

### Ten things the favorite person receives from the person with BPD
1. The feeling of being put on a pedestal [article #113].
2. A great deal ("мноооого") of attention and praise [article #113].
3. They will do everything possible for you, "хоть мир спасут" (even save the world) [article #113].
4. An attachment to you that resembles obsession and can sometimes feel suffocating [article #113].
5. Pressure to constantly reassure the other person of their self-worth [article #113].
6. Quarrels and arguments, especially when your actions trigger a feeling of abandonment in the other [article #113].
7. Constant testing of your boundaries [article #113].
8. A lot of pain and resentment if you don't reply at once [article #113].
9. Sharp changes in behaviour: one moment you are adored and valued, the next suddenly devalued and criticised [article #113].
10. Extreme jealousy when you are around other people or busy with work or hobbies [article #113].

The first character calmly says that people aren't on their phones 24/7. The second says "They hate me and I'm gonna find out why". ### Course of an FP–BPD relationship
1. **Formation of attachment.** The relationship is marked by strong obsession, whether romantic or platonic. The FP accepts the person with BPD as they are and gives a feeling of freedom and self-expression, without fear of being judged or feeling a burden. A belief appears that the FP will save them in any situation and from any problem. The more time they spend together, the more obsessed the person with BPD becomes. The FP gradually becomes someone you rely on for almost everything and cannot live without. In a client's words: «Favorite person - это тот, без кого, как мне кажется, я не смогу жить» ("A favorite person is someone without whom, it seems to me, I won't be able to live") [article #113].
2. **Dependence amplifies symptoms in the FP's absence.** The FP meets the need for constant attention and approval, which worsens BPD symptoms when the FP is not around. Jealousy, fear of being abandoned and intense emotional reactions are common when the FP interacts with others or shows signs of distancing [article #113].
3. **Idealization phase.** Emotional attachment to the FP intensifies [article #113].
4. **Splitting and devaluation.** When the FP fails to meet expectations, talks to someone else, or doesn't reply to messages instantly, they are immediately devalued. This brings anxiety and sadness, sometimes anger and panic attacks (mechanism below) [article #113].
5. **Escalation to suffocation.** The person with BPD needs ever-increasing attention and approval to get the necessary sense of care. The responsibility and pressure on the FP to meet these needs, such as constant contact, become "too much", impossible to satisfy and simply suffocating. The suffocation comes with intrusiveness, disregard for boundaries and walking on eggshells, where everyone behaves overly carefully while expecting an explosion [article #113].
6. **Ending.** When the relationship becomes unbearable it has to end, either abruptly with a scandal or by quiet fading; the author says the form doesn't really matter. What matters is that for the person with BPD it is unbearable pain that can worsen their condition and crush them. It often leads to resentment and a stronger fear of abandonment, "ведь к этому все и пришло" (since that is exactly where it all ended up) [article #113].

### What the person with BPD can do to keep the FP longer
The author stresses that this is "не чья вина" (nobody's fault). These are features of how people with BPD perceive and build relationships, and changing them "можно и нужно" (is possible and necessary). Her list [article #113]:
1. Help yourself notice your own behaviour patterns [article #113].
2. In some moments, don't rely on the "loud thoughts" in your head [article #113].
3. In some moments, slow yourself down in demanding care [article #113].
4. In some moments, cope on your own [article #113].

The promised result: the FP is much more likely to stay near for much longer. Her overall verdict: FP–BPD relationships really are about strong emotional attachment, insecurity and fear of abandonment. They bring satisfaction at first but over time often become unhealthy and aggravate BPD symptoms, so noticing and changing this "очень даже можно и стоит того" (is quite possible and worth it) [article #113].

## Detailed insights

### Rejection sensitive dysphoria: definition and traits
For some people, rejection or criticism is not just hurtful but "жуткая неконтролируемая эмоциональная боль" (terrible uncontrollable emotional pain). The author says this is not because you are not tough enough; sometimes it comes from features of brain structure. Rejection sensitive dysphoria is when a person feels extremely strong emotional pain tied to rejection, criticism or refusal. "Dysphoria" comes from an ancient Greek word for a strong, all-consuming feeling of pain or discomfort. The pain is triggered by disapproval, criticism, devaluation or failure, by anything perceived as «меня не принимают» ("I'm not accepted"). It is emotional but felt physically. It leads to difficulty regulating emotions, ranging from an outburst of anger or a crying fit to long, devastating sadness [msg #66].

Traits of people with "rejection dysphoria" [msg #66]:
- They are shy and find it hard to believe in themselves.
- Some become people-pleasers ("пипл плизерство") to protect themselves from potential disapproval.
- Others become thorough perfectionists for the same reason. They compensate for fear of failure with a drive for perfection and total avoidance of anything risky, even anything that could hypothetically lead to failure.
- Constant anxiety comes "в подарок" (as a free gift).
- Despite all the "how to avoid criticism" lifehacks, once the idea of rejection seeps into the head, a stormy reaction follows, from sudden rage to deep depression [msg #66].

### Why rejection hurts so much: the brain explanation (RSD and ADHD)
The author stresses that "никто ничего не знает наверняка" (nobody knows anything for sure). There are suspicions that the cause lies in features of brain structure and that the causes are similar to ADHD [msg #66]:
- **Social rejection is processed like physical pain.** Perceiving that someone dislikes you activates the same brain areas as physical pain. It does not take criticism or disapproval; a poker face or an unclear, detached reaction can be enough. The brain processes it as if you were really kicked in the stomach, not just looked at askance [msg #66].
- **Weaker filtering in ADHD.** People with ADHD differ in how the brain regulates internal communication. In particular, brain areas responsible for filtering and controlling signals from different sources are less active. As a result, people with ADHD often struggle to process sensory information effectively, and sensory overload from bright light or changing surroundings can spoil life. In the author's words: maybe it was noisy, maybe someone really gave negative feedback; the brain is unsure, but the consequences are already there [msg #66].
- **Therefore pain-like activity is poorly regulated.** The ADHD brain may also struggle to regulate pain-like activity. This could explain why people with rejection dysphoria feel refusal as much more emotional and painful: "Мозг не распознает и запутывается" (the brain doesn't recognise and gets confused). So "a kick in the stomach", "a very bright blinding light" and "criticism aimed at me" may feel similar, and more intense than for an ordinary person without ADHD or rejection dysphoria [msg #66].
- **Heritability.** ADHD has a genetic component and usually runs in families. By the author's analogy, rejection dysphoria, and with it thin-skinnedness toward criticism, is inherited too ("Весело" — "Fun") [msg #66].

### Loneliness in depression vs BPD
The author summarises a Frontiers in Psychiatry article (2020). **Main idea:** loneliness weighs on people with depression, but throwing oneself into socializing by willpower is not always worth it, because perceptual distortions can make the state worse. For people with BPD loneliness is also often hard, but careful immersion into social life can bring good results [msg #11].

Details [msg #11]:
- Loneliness can affect treatment outcomes for many illnesses, but it especially affects people with depression and BPD. These are probably the loneliest of all patient groups with mental disorders. Loneliness probably influences the development of depression and may be one of the main experiences of people with BPD.
- **Depression's "wall".** People with depression are thought to have a "стена" (wall) around them that prevents them from perceiving their interactions with other people. In the same way they may "not notice" pleasant events that sometimes happen to them, and say quite sincerely in therapy that nothing good happened today. Their perceived loneliness may therefore not depend on the objective amount of contact.
- **Rejection anxiety can outweigh loneliness.** Interacting with people can sharply raise anxiety and worry for a person with depression because of the risk of rejection, and subjectively this anxiety may feel worse than the loneliness itself. Accordingly, contact with people, and even just looking at photos of pleasant people, benefits people with low loneliness more than those with high loneliness.
- **A self-reinforcing loop.** The link between feeling lonely, social isolation and negative evaluation of social company may form a self-reinforcing loop.
- **BPD is different.** Many people with BPD can switch between social withdrawal and contact-seeking behaviour. A large social network can protect them against loneliness, because even in a withdrawn state it lets them come out of isolation little by little. For people with BPD, small steps toward people are more likely to bring relief from loneliness [msg #11].

### Subjective loneliness persists even with people around
What does a network give? People around. Do people solve loneliness? "Неа(" (Nope). It is better with people, but even with people around, people with BPD on average still report more **subjective loneliness**. It is not only about how many people are around: one can have lots of contacts and still experience the connection as insufficient. The author recalls the old VK-style quote: "нет одиночества более сильного, чем когда находишься одна среди толпы" ("there is no loneliness stronger than being alone in a crowd") [msg #251].

### The author's doubts about mentalization
The author was looking into mentalization because of a new wave of interest in it among specialists around her, while weighing whether to go deeper. Her doubts [msg #198]:
1. **Scope is unclear.** Mentalization seems to be everything, from emotions and thoughts to awareness of self and others. So what exactly is meant: self-reflection, empathy, theory of mind? [msg #198]
2. **MBT vs DBT.** MBT (Mentalization-Based Treatment) is effective for BPD but, compared with DBT, "проигрывает в результатах" (loses on results). Her explanation: the approach is cumbersome, and using it requires really getting it, which takes much more energy than dunking one's head in a basin (an allusion to DBT's cold-water distress-tolerance technique, "если вы понимаете о чем я" — "if you know what I mean") [msg #198].
3. **Is it just a basic human setting?** Isn't some understanding of self and others a basic human capacity (theory of mind too, which Lena recently mentioned)? If the capacity is innate and develops naturally but is disrupted by an unstable environment (as in BPD or trauma) or by neurodivergence, what exactly is disrupted: the ability to "feel feelings" or to "talk about feelings"? She admits she got buried in the question ("Закопалась") [msg #198].

### Emotional empathy across diagnoses (author's bot survey)
The author built a Telegram bot to test emotional empathy with the EES scale and shared results after 509 people took it [msg #228]:
- **Sampling disclaimer.** The link was posted in two channels: one for neurodivergent people, one for people with diagnoses involving intense emotions such as BPD. It was also posted on her Facebook. The sample is therefore very specific and unlikely to reflect the "average population" [msg #228].
- **Overall.** Mean score 44.14, noticeably higher than in the original EES study (33). She explains this by gender skew: the original had equal numbers of men and women, her sample was heavily female, and her results are close to the female average [msg #228].
- **Range and shape.** The range was very wide, from –120 to 122. The distribution had a "dip" in the centre and was shifted toward the poles. Her possible explanation: 133 participants reported neurodivergence, and in neurodivergent people empathy is more often either high or low rather than "average" [msg #228].
- **Gender.** Women (394): 47.95 (original 44). Men (88): 27.68 (original 23). Other/unspecified (27): 42.11. Overall the results match the scale authors' data; bot respondents were only slightly more empathic than the 1970s study respondents [msg #228].
- **By diagnosis.**
  - No diagnosis (176): 43.56, almost the same as women in the original paper.
  - Neurodivergence: 36.77.
  - Affective disorders (depression, bipolar): 59.77.
  - Personality disorders: 27.20.
  - Anxiety disorders: 36.30.
  - Stress disorders (PTSD, cPTSD and others): 62.
- **Conclusion.** Having a diagnosis did not lower scores on average. Sometimes it substantially raised emotional empathy. The results confirm the trends the scale authors described but add accents for specific communities [msg #228].

### Splitting: from angel to the worst person on earth
**Definition.** Splitting (расщепление, сплиттинг) is the term for how people with BPD can quickly switch between loving someone and hating that same person [article #113].

**Mechanism.** It happens when very strong emotions stun the person and the fear of being abandoned fires. This helps the person with BPD quickly stop seeing the loved one in an ideal, angelic light and start seeing them in a devilish one. Why? "Расщепление - это то, как мозг защищает от чувства брошенности" (Splitting is how the brain protects against the feeling of being abandoned) [article #113].

**Examples of the thought switch** [article #113]:
- About a partner: from "Она/Он - лучшее, что когда-либо случалось со мной" (She/He is the best thing that ever happened to me) to "На самом деле я ему/ей безразличен" (Actually he/she doesn't care about me).
- About work: from "I'm doing amazingly at my job, I have an amazing team, we work together amazingly, AMAZINGLY" to "Мой босс и коллеги думают обо мне гадости, ненавидят меня и смеются надо мной. Зачем мне нужно это терпеть?" (My boss and colleagues think nasty things about me, hate me and laugh at me. Why should I put up with this?). This example shows that splitting is not limited to romantic FPs.

**Rejection sensitivity feeds it.** The author brings in rejection sensitivity again ("опять она" — "again"). Expecting refusal and abandonment heightens vigilance. It increases aggressiveness or the wish to control the other, to cling, or the opposite, to ignore them. "Мысли меняют поведение, лишь увеличивая убежденность в обесценивающих мыслях" (Thoughts change behaviour, only increasing the conviction in the devaluing thoughts). In other words, the behaviour driven by the expectation of rejection produces evidence that seems to confirm it. This complements the controlling-strategies model in the existing file (msg #267) [article #113].

**Devaluing the FP hurts the person with BPD too.** Devaluing your own personal angel is painful in itself. You doted on them, "ваш лучик света в темном царстве" (your ray of light in the dark kingdom), a symbol of stability and safety. Now you bury them under a ton of devaluing thoughts that distort the FP's face. The author's image is a time-lapse video of an apple core left to rot: your person turns into a rotten core because perception changed [article #113].

## Practical playbook

- **Do not guess at others' minds.** BPD therapy's anti-hypermentalization rule: assume you know no more about another person's feelings than they have told you (a simplification, per the source) [msg #3].
- **Do not self-diagnose from over-reading.** "I read into things a lot" is not evidence of BPD [msg #3].
- **Reframe others' behaviour through inner states.** Use the mentalizing move from the author's example: they are shouting because they are tired, hungry or upset, not because they hate you [msg #198].
- **Depression vs BPD.** In depression, do not force socializing by willpower. In BPD, take careful small steps toward people [msg #11].
