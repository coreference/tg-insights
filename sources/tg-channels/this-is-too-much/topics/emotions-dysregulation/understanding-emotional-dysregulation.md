# Understanding emotions and emotional dysregulation

## What this covers

This topic explains what emotional dysregulation is (and its opposite, emotion regulation), how it manifests openly and through indirect warning signs, what it leads to and what typically causes it. It also covers the channel's models — including a full account of Lisa Feldman Barrett's theory of constructed emotion and its six steps to better emotional granularity — of what emotions are: a "smoke detector" that often misfires, something that can be changed only indirectly, something that consumes energy, and — through the neuroscience concept of frustrative non-reward — why anger can feel disproportionate when expectations are broken. According to the authors, lacking regulation skills harms relationships and daily life and raises the risk of depression and anxiety, while recognising early signs can prevent many crises [msg #27, #56].

## Core ideas

- **Dysregulation = difficulty regulating and expressing emotions**; regulation = the learnable ability to identify, name and manage emotions and triggers and use coping strategies. DBT skills training is described as being exactly about learning this [msg #27, #61].
- **Emotions are a smoke detector that misfires**: they shout that something is urgent, we are built to believe them unconditionally, and that unconditional belief is where pain and harmful impulsive attempts to stop it begin [msg #57].
- **Emotions cannot be changed directly** — only through attention, thoughts and actions [msg #40].
- **Emotions cost energy** — the stronger the emotion (positive too), the more rest is needed afterwards [msg #49].
- **Dysregulation can be invisible until the "explosion"**, so recognising indirect signs is very valuable [msg #56].
- **Dysregulation is a transdiagnostic symptom**: characteristic of BPD, complex PTSD and bipolar disorder, and possibly a problem in autism, ADHD, anxiety, depressive and eating disorders [msg #61].
- **Emotions are constructed by the brain** (Barrett's theory, in Katya's longread): they are concepts and predictions the brain builds to manage the body's energy budget; they are real, but what they say is not necessarily true, and emotional granularity can be trained [article #187].
- **Anger can be a reaction to the gap between expected and actual outcome**, not only to the loss itself (frustrative non-reward) [msg #260].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Definition of emotional dysregulation vs emotion regulation
Lena Foer defines emotional dysregulation (эмоциональная дисрегуляция) as a term for difficulties in regulating and expressing emotions; it can show up as extreme emotional reactions and abrupt switches between emotional states. Emotion regulation, by contrast, is the ability to manage one's emotions and express them in a healthy and adequate way. It includes three abilities: to identify and name one's emotions; to recognise and manage the triggers that lead to strong emotions; and to use coping strategies to stabilise one's state. Lacking these skills can lead to difficulties forming and maintaining relationships, difficulties in everyday life, and an increased risk of mental illnesses such as depression and anxiety [msg #27].

Katya Klimochkina's definition adds: dysregulation is difficulty managing and expressing emotions that can manifest as intense, sudden and uncontrollable reactions that may not correspond to the current context. Her example: nothing special happened — just an idiotic remark by the other person or an inappropriate joke — and you are in a rage "ready to tear and smash" or become unbearably sad to tears. The symptom spans the whole range of problems with emotional control, from emotions that are too intense and seem to start "from nothing" to emotional numbness, "когда смотрю в точку и ничего не чувствую" (when I stare at one point and feel nothing). She explains this as the reason the channel speaks about more than one disorder: the symptom underlies many disorders, differently in each — it is a characteristic problem in BPD, a problem in complex PTSD and in bipolar disorder (БАР), and may be a problem in autism (РАС), ADHD, anxiety and depressive disorders, and even play a significant role in eating disorders (РПП). She calls it "Симптом старуха шапокляк, всем насолил" (the Old Lady Shapoklyak symptom — it has spited everyone; Shapoklyak is a mischievous character from a Soviet cartoon) [msg #61].

### Three strategies for developing emotion regulation skills
Lena Foer lists strategies aimed at developing emotion regulation skills, noting that these, along with other skills, are what the DBT skills training does; in general, she says, the training is precisely about mastering ways to regulate one's state and thereby raise quality of life [msg #27].
1. **Mindfulness (осознанность)** — developing non-judgmental attention to one's own thoughts and feelings [msg #27].
2. **Identifying and managing one's own triggers** — studying what causes strong emotions and finding ways of coping with them in advance [msg #27].
3. **Using coping strategies** — for example techniques such as deep breathing or muscle relaxation [msg #27].

### Changing emotions indirectly via attention, thoughts and actions
Lena Foer calls this something she wants to remind clients of and to remember herself. We cannot change emotions directly: you cannot simply want to and stop being anxious, stop being angry, or feel love again — "Люди так не работают" (people don't work that way). We can change our emotions by changing our **focus of attention**, our **thoughts** and our **actions**. Her three examples [msg #40]:
- If we are terrified and cannot change the situation right away, we can imagine ourselves in a beautiful, safe imaginary place (attention/imagery).
- If we want to stop being angry at someone, we can try to think about how the world looks from that person's point of view (thoughts).
- If we want to feel love, we can look through old photos with loved ones (action).

Exercise she proposes: ask yourself "what would I like to feel right now?" and then "which actions, thoughts and focus of attention could help me with this?" [msg #40].

### Emotions as a smoke detector (two basic shifts)
Lena Foer answers "why do emotions bring so much pain?": because we misunderstand them; changing the attitude to one's own emotions makes life easier [msg #57].
- **Emotions are like hunger.** The evolutionary meaning of emotions, like hunger, is to draw your attention to something, to show that THIS is important now and that you urgently need to do something about it. Her illustration of an emotion's voice: "Эхей, этот парень сейчас тебя недооценил, СРОЧНО покажи ему, как он неправ!" (Hey, this guy just underestimated you, URGENTLY show him how wrong he is!). Emotions literally shout their significance and push us in a direction, just like hunger: after half a day without food it is hard to think of anything but a burger; if a pack of stray dogs appears next to you on an empty street, it is hard to think of anything but escape routes. Emotions, like hunger, are the body's "датчик дыма" (smoke detector) that starts ringing whenever, in our brain's opinion, we urgently need to fix something [msg #57].
- **The crucial difference.** Hunger is very straightforward (at least without eating disorders): hunger appears — eat. Emotions are more complicated. Her five "not always" lines: fear does not always mean we are in danger; feeling love does not always mean we should throw ourselves into someone's arms; being angry does not always mean we should take up an axe; feeling shame does not always mean we face expulsion from the community; feeling guilt does not always mean we did something wrong [msg #57].
- **The misfiring detector.** Emotions are a smoke detector that goes off for a fire, for a cigarette smoked on the balcony, and sometimes just for no reason. Yet we are built to believe it unconditionally — and we do. That is where the pain begins, along with impulsive attempts to stop it somehow, attempts that often harm us [msg #57].

The path to a healthier relationship with emotions consists of two basic things [msg #57]:
1. **Perceiving emotions as a "smoke detector"** — simply a device inside us that tells us from time to time "происходит какая-то фигня" (some crap is going on).
2. **Understanding that this device glitches** — sometimes when we hear the siren, it went off by mistake.

At that moment it is important to exhale, slow down and try to figure out whether there really is a fire — and if there is, what will help handle the situation with the least losses. The more often we remember this while experiencing emotions, the easier — gradually — life becomes [msg #57].

### Volume-knob metaphor of emotion regulation
Katya Klimochkina: when you manage or regulate your emotions, you can direct what you feel and how you react — in theory. Many people learn this in childhood and develop the skill as they grow up; it is also a key component of the ability to adapt and be resilient to the challenges of reality. Emotion regulation can be pictured as a volume knob for your feelings: with it you can make feelings not too loud, background, muted. In dysregulation the brain cannot regulate emotional signals — the knob does not work as it should, making emotions "louder" and harder to manage. You try to do something, solve a task, concentrate and think, and your brain seems to have turned on an action movie with explosions at maximum volume: "Регулятор не регулирует" (the knob doesn't regulate) [msg #61].

Example of learned ("working") regulation: children eventually outgrow the anger outbursts that are frequent at age 2–3. In early childhood tantrums are a normal, expected part of development; as children grow older they usually learn to manage their emotions, which is why tantrums become less frequent and, in some children, stop by preschool age [msg #61].

### Manifestations of emotional dysregulation (eight features)
Katya Klimochkina's list of how a broken "regulator" shows [msg #61, #62]:
1. **Strong emotions** — people with dysregulation often experience emotions more intensely than others, including strong anger, sadness, worry or irritability [msg #61].
2. **Rapid mood changes** — someone may go from love to hate in a short time, even without visible reasons [msg #61].
3. **Difficulty with emotions subsiding** — it can be hard to calm down once upset [msg #61].
4. **Impulsivity** — acting on emotions without thinking about consequences, which can lead to impulsive acts: self-harm or aggression, saying nasty things and similar [msg #61].
5. **Interpersonal problems** — others may find it hard to predict how the person will react, which leads to strained relationships [msg #61].
6. **Emotional sensitivity** — what seems an insignificant problem to others can trigger a strong emotional reaction [msg #61].
7. **Emotional numbness** — in some cases dysregulation leads to numbness, when people struggle to feel any emotions at all, resulting in a feeling of emptiness [msg #61].
8. **Duration of symptoms** — dysregulation symptoms are usually persistent and ongoing, affecting various areas of life [msg #62].

### Consequences of emotional dysregulation
"Wrapped into consequences", these difficulties can look like [msg #62]:
1. Problems managing your mood, so you feel stuck or unable to make yourself feel better, especially in a negative mood or with depression or anxiety (the source itself ends this list with "и т.д.", i.e. open-ended).
2. Easily falling into despair over small inconveniences or annoyances.
3. Mood swings.
4. Impulsive behaviour.
5. Losing one's composure.
6. Constant irritability or anger in the intervals between anger outbursts.
7. Emotions interfering with pursuing goals and achieving desired results.

Some of the most serious consequences [msg #62]:
1. Verbal outbursts (shouting, screeching or crying).
2. Aggressive, violent and self-harming behaviour (toward objects, animals or people, and oneself).
3. Problems maintaining friendships and relationships in general.

### Six common causes of emotional dysregulation
Katya Klimochkina's list of the most common causes [msg #62]:
1. **A feature of the nervous system** — e.g. you screamed a lot as an infant, or you may be neurodivergent.
2. **No skills** — nobody taught you.
3. **"Until you scream, you get nothing"** — your environment gives you what you want only when you are already in affect, never earlier.
4. **Biological vulnerabilities** — not sleeping, not eating, being tired, PMS, being ill.
5. **Too much emotional stuff happening at once**, so you can't manage.
6. **Believing regulation is "not for you"** — e.g. the sense that without drama things aren't right.

She closes with self-observation questions: which of the above shows up in you? In which situations? What might the causes be for you personally? [msg #62]

### Eight indirect signs of emotional dysregulation
Lena Foer: regulating emotions can be a hard task for people with BPD; emotions can be all-consuming, hard to cope with, to regulate oneself or to switch to important tasks — they simply throw you out of everyday life. Sometimes it is obvious you are in a crisis, but there are situations where emotional tension is high yet you seem completely fine — at least until an "explosion" happens. The ability to distinguish indirect signs that you are emotionally not okay is very valuable: it can protect from many crises and let you take care of yourself in time. The signs [msg #56]:
1. **Vivid imaginary arguments** — you are absorbed in picturing conflicts or arguments with other people in imagined scenarios.
2. **Extreme need for reassurance** — you feel you badly need others, right now, to tell you how important you are to them, that they are definitely not going to end the relationship, that they love and value you.
3. **Strong fear of abandonment** — you replay fears of being left, betrayed or replaced over and over, even when you have no reason to worry about it.
4. **Black-and-white thinking** — you see in an event, phenomenon or person only infinitely good or infinitely bad, and thinking over the details absorbs you strongly and feels incredibly important right now.
5. **Impulsive actions** — suddenly, "out of nowhere", you want to do something impulsive that differs from your usual behaviour, for example buy a mountain of junk food or a couple of bottles of alcohol, or urgently buy several new dresses.
6. **Acute self-criticism** — you scold yourself strongly and painfully for something, even if it happened quite long ago.
7. **Identity shift** — you notice, or loved ones tell you, that your current judgments, values, image, view of yourself are very unlike how you usually behave, and all this changed suddenly and abruptly.
8. **Emotional numbness** — you notice you feel nothing at all, are unusually detached, numb, dropping out of what is happening.

Recognising these signs is not a reason to judge yourself for them but an opportunity to gently and with curiosity try to understand yourself in the moment [msg #56].

### Frustrative non-reward model of anger
Katya Klimochkina asks why we are sometimes infuriated not by the problem itself but by the fact that everything went wrong. In neuroscience, *frustrative non-reward* is frustration caused by the absence of an expected reward or result. You counted on a certain outcome, spent time, planned actions — and did not get it (and, she jokes, you're "lucky" if you thought about it for a long time) [msg #260].
- **Her example:** she left cat food on the table to be given to street cats; in the evening she found that the only food her own cats eat had vanished. The problem is not so much that the food ran out: "Нарушилось ожидание: я рассчитывала на один результат, а получила противоположный." (The expectation was broken: I counted on one result and got the opposite.) [msg #260]
- **In the brain:** when the result does not match expectation, the brain processes this error and assesses its significance; involved in particular ("в частности", the author's own hedge) are the anterior insula and the cingulate cortex — areas associated with processing significant events, conflicts and the need to change behaviour [msg #260].
- **Reaction:** frustration can strengthen the tendency to immediately remove the obstacle or react to it. If the situation can't be fixed right now, anger may be directed at the source of the error [msg #260].
- **Definition nuance:** frustration is not just disappointment but a state in which an obstacle prevents getting the expected result and provokes a reaction. But frustration does not always cause anger, and anger does not necessarily lead to aggression [msg #260].
- **Why it matters:** the concept helps explain why a reaction sometimes feels disproportionate — we react not only to what we lost but to the sharp discrepancy between the expected and actual result. And when the situation can no longer be fixed, the urge to do something immediately may remain, even if shouting is completely useless [msg #260].

## Detailed insights

### Emotions cost energy
Lena Foer, prompted by a weekend of intense news, reminds that a thought which often seems non-obvious is true: "эмоции тратят силы" (emotions spend energy). The stronger your emotions, the more energy goes into them and the more you need rest when the emotional wave subsides. Strong positive emotions also spend energy, and after them you may also need to recover and rest. If you feel tired and empty after an emotionally intense stretch, this is normal and natural, and giving yourself a breather and time to recover can be an excellent idea [msg #49].

### Theory of constructed emotion (Lisa Feldman Barrett) — the longread
Katya Klimochkina introduces Lisa Feldman Barrett's theory of constructed emotion (теория сконструированных эмоций) as a view of what emotions are, where they come from and how they shape our lives. She positions it for readers ready to look at "something between" Bartlett's cognitive schemas, relational frame theory and thinking traps, "and surely something else", but applied to emotions. Her longread is, in her own words, a loose translation of an article ("вольно перевожу статью"), with occasional additions of her own as translator (e.g. the Bartlett "schema" remark) [msg #187, article #187]. The article's argument runs in eight parts, reproduced below in order.

#### 1. Emotions are concepts (ideas created by the brain)
- **Main premise.** The theory got its name from its main premise: emotions are concepts, ideas that are created by the brain [article #187].
- **The brain must interpret ambiguous data.** Picture your brain sitting in your skull, receiving all kinds of data from the senses — eyes, ears, nose, skin and mouth. These data are informative but ambiguous; they have to be interpreted. Example: the brain may "think": what is this rectangular source of light with changing colour patterns? A window! [article #187]
- **Past experience as the shortcut.** The brain constantly tries to make sense of incoming data, and one of the simplest ways is to use past experience as a guide: if it can match current experience to memories from the past, that saves a great deal of time and energy. But going through thousands of old memories one by one would take too long [article #187].
- **Concepts as compressed experience.** Instead, the brain uses concepts. A concept is a compressed version of hundreds or thousands of past impressions. Katya adds, as the "free translator", that Bartlett called something similar "schemas". Instead of remembering every encounter you have ever had with a "chair", the brain stores a "representation of a chair"; next time you meet a chair, it only needs to match it to this concept to understand what it sees [article #187].
- **"What is it like?" rather than "What is it?"** Concepts are like labels or categories the brain creates to give meaning to the world. When you see something new, the brain does not ask "Что это?" (What is this?) but "На что это похоже?" (What does this resemble?) — it constantly tries to put everything you perceive into a category you already have, which is much easier than understanding something from scratch [article #187].
- **Applied to emotions.** The idea that we use concepts to make sense of experience is not new; Barrett applied it to the tangled, subjective world of emotions. Emotions like "fear", "sadness" and "disappointment" are concepts like any others. Just as the brain interprets a stream of light as a "window", it can interpret bodily sensations as "fear" or "disappointment". These emotions do not feel like concepts because we experience them very intensely — "Но это так" (But that is how it is) [article #187].
- **Barrett's school-shooting example.** Barrett tells how she watched news about a recent school shooting. In the moment it seemed she was reacting directly to the news: she felt terrible grief and sadness, and tears seemed to come to her eyes by themselves. A more accurate description, in her words: she had long ago learned that "sadness" is what can arise when certain bodily sensations coincide with a terrible loss; using fragments of past experience (her knowledge of shootings and her earlier sadness about them), her brain quickly predicted what her body should do to cope with such a tragedy. Its predictions produced a racing heartbeat, flushing in the face and stomach cramps; they made her cry — an action that would calm her nervous system — and gave the arising sensations meaning as sadness. In other words, her experience of sadness was a "симуляция" (simulation), a prediction of how her body would react to the news. Sadness was not a pure reaction to something happening outside; it resulted from a complex interaction of systems that made a self-fulfilling forecast of what her organism needed to cope [article #187].

#### 2. Emotions are predictions
- **Why "simulation" and not just "interpretation".** Because the brain does not passively watch incoming data from the outside world; that would make its decisions very slow, potentially threatening survival. To act faster, the brain begins reacting before it gets all the data — it creates a "simulation", a forecast of what it thinks may happen next. The brain constantly builds best guesses about what should happen and then prepares to act according to them [article #187].
- **Football example.** If your brain guesses you are playing football, it may predict all kinds of likely scenarios from past experience — a chance to run to the goal, fast balls flying at your head, attackers approaching from any side — and prepare the body in advance, redirecting blood flow to certain muscles or becoming more alert to balls flying past or at you [article #187].
- **Reading example.** The same happens with mental activity: as you read, your brain predicts which word or idea is likely to come next, based on your previous reading experience. These forecasts save energy and help you read faster than without "pre-prepared forecasts". As the largest and most energy-consuming organ in the body, the brain places great value on this efficiency [article #187].
- **Airport example (emotions).** The same process happens with emotions: when you drive to the airport to meet a friend you have not seen for years, your brain is busy predicting the joy and happiness you may soon feel. So you feel happy before the event happens, and happier still when you actually see them [article #187].
- **Prediction as default mode.** Prediction is so fundamental that some scientists consider it the brain's basic default mode of operation (the article names the "default network"). Your brain cannot help building predictive models of every experience you have or might have [article #187].
- **Conclusion: simulations are more real to us than the physical world.** What we see, hear, touch, taste and smell are simulations of the world, not reactions to it. We may think perception is determined by events in the world, but much of what we see is based on internal predictions; sensory data merely influence perception [article #187].
- **Evidence from vision.** The visual cortex receives only 10% of its connections from the retina; the remaining 90% come from other brain areas that predict what we might see [article #187].
- **When predictions are wrong.** The brain can change its predictions to fit what the senses report — but it is just as likely to do the opposite: keep the original prediction and filter incoming data to fit it. In a sense the brain is "programmed to perceive illusions": you live in a carefully built world of your own making, steered by particles of sensory information. Once predictions are accurate enough, they filter perception and determine what you are able to see in the first place. This can become a closed loop in which the brain sees only what it believes and then believes what it sees [article #187].

#### 3. Interoception and the body budget
- **The body is part of the "outside" world for the brain.** From the brain's point of view, our body is just another part of the external world it must explain, and it uses the same predictive mechanism to interpret sensations from inside the body — changing heart rhythm, sensations of breathing, rumbling in the stomach, contraction and dilation of veins [article #187].
- **Bodily sensations have no objective meaning.** These purely physical sensations feel strong because they come from inside, but they have no objective meaning. The same stomach discomfort can just as easily be "explained" as [article #187]:
  1. Hunger (if you are sitting at the dinner table).
  2. An oncoming illness (if it is flu season).
  3. A broken heart (if you are going through a breakup).
  4. Certainty that the defendant is untrustworthy (if you are a judge in a courtroom who has not had lunch yet).
- **Interoception defined.** The process of interpreting these bodily sensations is called интероцепция (interoception). It is run by an "interoceptive network" in the brain that takes information from internal organs and tissues, hormones in the blood, the immune system and many other factors and labels it with concepts like "hunger" or "heartbreak". Such emotions may feel as if they come straight from the body, but they are created by the interoceptive network, mostly from your predictions [article #187].
- **Purpose: managing the body budget (бюджет организма).** Everything the body does, inside and out, requires energy. To manage the "body budget" across hundreds of body parts and billions of cells, the brain must constantly forecast the organism's energy needs — just as a finance department needs a budget to forecast where money will be needed, the brain makes forecasts and adjustments about when and where energy will be needed [article #187].
- **Budget changes are felt as emotions.** Many "budget changes" are experienced emotionally: a lack of energy in the muscles can feel like "exhaustion"; lack of sleep can be read as "worn out and overloaded"; absence of positive social interaction can be perceived as "loneliness". These emotions are not objective facts but concepts created from sensory data, cultural knowledge and the history of social interactions. Interoception evolved to balance the body budget; experiencing emotions is a fortunate (and sometimes unfortunate) side effect [article #187].
- **Practical implication.** A "bad feeling" is not evidence that something is wrong; it may simply mean you are overloading your organism, have gone "over budget". "Эмоции реальны, но то, что они вам говорят, не обязательно реально" (Emotions are real, but what they tell you is not necessarily real). Knowing that "negative" emotions are just the brain's way of saying reserves are running low, we can make deliberate decisions to replenish those reserves instead of resorting to less healthy coping mechanisms [article #187].

#### 4. Experiential blindness (эмпирическая слепота)
- **Why emotions feel like reactions to the world.** Even with all the scientific evidence, it can be very hard to believe emotions are internally generated concepts driven by mental simulation; in the moment they feel strong and overwhelming, like a wave carrying us away against our will. The reason lies in how the brain uses concepts: concepts are not just labels for what we passively observe — we need them in order to perceive things at all. A concept serves as a lens (or sometimes a filter) for what we can see in the first place [article #187].
- **Paris café example.** On holiday in a Parisian café, sipping fine wine and eating cheese, you overhear a French couple at the next table. The conversation contains all the information needed to understand it, but if your mind lacks the set of concepts known as "the French language", it will seem meaningless to you [article #187].
- **Definition.** This is "experiential blindness" — the inability to perceive something for which you do not yet have a concept. We do not perceive the world directly but in our mental simulation, and without a concept of something we cannot include it in the simulation [article #187].
- **Barrett's TED-talk snake demonstration.** The article reproduces two images from Barrett's TED talk (their image transcriptions contain no text; the article describes them). Looking at the first picture, your neurons work like crazy trying to catch something other than black-and-white blobs; the brain searches its library of concepts, makes thousands of guesses and weighs probabilities to find a category for the image. After seeing the second picture and returning to the first, you can probably now see a snake. The image has not changed, but your brain now has a new concept — a "концептуальную линзу" (conceptual lens) that lets it fill in missing information. The process is so automatic that you probably cannot go back to what you saw before, even if you try to "unsee the snake" [article #187].
- **Why concepts feel like reflexes.** Concepts let us perceive things in a world that always gives incomplete, ambiguous information, recognising things quickly and (usually) accurately while saving time and energy. But this happens so invisibly and automatically that our senses feel more like reflexes than constructions; we feel no agency in the simulations we run [article #187].
- **Happiness as an example.** This explains why "happiness" can feel like a reaction to external events rather than a product of the brain: even before the brain finishes classifying a situation as "happy", it is already simulating happiness. External perception meets internal construction before you are aware of it, so it seems happiness happens to you while the brain is actively constructing it [article #187].
- **Self-fulfilling prediction.** The more you expect happiness to come, the more you prepare for it and the more likely you are to experience it (as in the airport example). "Даже на неврологическом уровне вы создаете свою собственную реальность" (Even at the neurological level you create your own reality) [article #187].

#### 5. Emotional granularity (эмоциональная детализация / эмоциональная гранулярность)
- **No concept, no perceived emotion.** One of the hardest implications of the theory: if a person has no concept to describe an emotion, they cannot perceive it. They will still have the bodily sensations but cannot label them precisely. So the range of emotions a person can experience is limited by their emotional granularity — the ability to construct and identify more precise emotional experiences [article #187].
- **Extreme low-granularity example.** Imagine someone able to distinguish only "good" and "bad" feelings. With only imprecise information about what is going on in their body, they will find many of life's difficulties hard to handle; they will be blind even to their own feelings [article #187].
- **Precision is efficiency.** Making sense of bodily sensations takes energy, and organising a huge amount of sensory data into a general feeling like "happiness" takes a lot. With a more precise concept — e.g. the Korean word jeong (정) for a feeling of attachment to a close friend — the brain needs less effort to construct that narrower concept. "Точность приводит к эффективности" (Precision leads to efficiency) — the biological result of higher emotional granularity [article #187].
- **Affective (emotional) realism.** When you feel an emotion without knowing its precise cause, you are likely to perceive it as information about the world rather than as your own perception of the world. This is called affective or emotional realism: it makes us take supposed "facts" about the world that are actually created by our feelings. It can trap us in an emotional world of our own making without realising we have imprisoned ourselves [article #187].
- **Granularity can be improved.** If you learn to distinguish finer meanings of "feeling great" — happy, content, excited, relaxed, joyful, hopeful, inspired, proud, adoring, grateful, blissful — or of "feeling lousy" — angry, irritated, anxious, spiteful, grumpy, remorseful, gloomy, depressed, alarmed, terrified, offended, scared, envious, sad, melancholic — the brain gains far more options for predicting, classifying and perceiving emotions. High granularity gives a much bigger toolkit for responding flexibly to challenges and lets us tailor actions to the underlying causes of emotions rather than to their immediate manifestation [article #187].

#### 6. Constructing social reality
- **Co-regulation.** Emotions are generated internally but do not stop there; we use them to build our social reality. When you interact with people you know and like — partner, friends, lovers, children, teammates or other close ones — you synchronise heart rate, breathing and other physical signals, with tangible benefits. Something as simple as holding a loved one's hand or keeping their photo on your desk can improve body budgeting and reduce pain. We also use other people to regulate our body budget [article #187].
- **Concepts regulate other people's bodies too.** Concepts like "fear", "anticipation" and "contempt" are used by our brain to manage other people's organisms as well. Once we create an emotion concept and label it with a word, we can share it, letting others see what we see and thereby reshaping how their brains work. Once we share a concept, I can simply say a word to trigger predictions in your brain — "своего рода лингвистическую телепатию(!)" (a kind of linguistic telepathy(!)) [article #187].
- **Emotion concepts are learned, not hard-wired.** Instead of a limited set of emotions built in at birth, nature gave us raw material to build a conceptual system, including emotion concepts. Thanks to adults who deliberately spoke emotion words to us, we gained the ability to perceive not only physical objects but ideas that exist only in people's minds: goals, intentions, preferences and their own emotions [article #187].
- **Cultural transmission.** Over time, this passing of emotional knowledge from generation to generation — through stories, traditions, myths, fables or anything else we can pass on — lets each generation shape the brain structure of the next. This body of knowledge is the essence of our civilisation as much as the books in our libraries [article #187].

#### 7. Modern culture and the body budget
- **Modern life disrupts the budget.** Once you understand the body budget, much of modern culture seems designed to disrupt it (and make us take "credits and loans"). The article's list [article #187]:
  1. **Refined sugar** in much of our food negatively affects the body budget.
  2. **Early waking and late sleeping** for study and work: over 40% of Americans aged 13–64 regularly do not get enough sleep, causing chronic "irrational spending of the body's funds".
  3. **Advertising** plays on insecurity, suggesting friends will judge us badly unless we look or buy a certain way — and social rejection is damaging to the "budget".
  4. **Social media** gives even more opportunities for social comparison.
  5. **Constant phone use** keeps us from truly relaxing.
- **Chronic mismatch → chronic unhappiness.** All emotional experience depends on the brain's predictions about what the organism needs. If those predictions chronically diverge from real needs, it is hard to rebalance them; the organism already responds poorly to warning signals from the body. If predictions go unmet long enough, you will feel chronically unhappy without knowing why [article #187].
- **Self-medication.** When we feel unhappy, we self-medicate: thirty percent of all medicines consumed in the US are taken to cope with some form of distress. We use alcohol, drugs, TV series and sugar to reach a semblance of "budget balance", at the terrible price of addiction and obesity. In recent decades it has become clear that the immune system influences far more diseases and harmful conditions than imagined — diabetes, obesity, heart disease, depression, insomnia, cancer, memory decline and other cognitive functions associated with premature ageing and dementia — and the immune system suffers when the body budget goes out of balance [article #187].
- **Depression as a chronic budget deficit.** Depression can be seen as a continuous chain of negative thoughts and feelings, each feeling triggering the next thought and vice versa. The brain fixates on negative past experience and keeps drawing on an already "overtaxed" budget; alarm signals from the body are ignored. Body and mind get caught in a closed loop of uncorrected predictions, trapped in an unfavourable past when metabolic demands were high. Because the budget is permanently in debt, it tries to cut costs; the simplest way is to stop moving and stop paying attention to the world. If a depressed person starts avoiding people, others can no longer help balance their body budget. This is "неумолимая усталость от депрессии" (the relentless fatigue of depression) [article #187].
- **Adverse upbringing.** The same cycle applies to people who grow up in adverse conditions lacking basics like safety, food and sleep. These conditions alter the interoceptive network, lowering the brain's ability to regulate its budget accurately throughout life, which increases lifetime risk of cardiovascular disease, arthritis, diabetes, cancer and other illnesses [article #187].

#### 8. A new view of personal responsibility
- **Are we responsible for our concepts?** Not for all of them: you cannot choose the concepts you absorbed in childhood. But as an adult you can choose which experiences to expose yourself to, and these shape the concepts that ultimately determine your actions. Responsibility, from this view, lies in conscious choices aimed at changing your concepts [article #187].
- **Emotions are malleable.** The theory holds that every aspect of our emotions is malleable and flexible: you are not at the mercy of mythical emotional circuits hidden in some ancient part of your brain. "Вы лучше контролируете свои эмоции, чем думаете" (You control your emotions better than you think). You cannot snap your fingers and instantly change feelings, but there are six practical steps to improve emotional granularity over time (see *Six steps to improve emotional granularity* below) [article #187].

#### Closing words of the article
All six approaches can turn a negative spiral of suffering into simple physical discomfort. Pain is inevitable, but it does not necessarily mean something is wrong in your life. No technique is guaranteed to work every time, but each opens the possibility of a healthier body, more fulfilling relationships and a more flexible and rich emotional life. Constructed emotions do not promise that we will somehow gain full control over our feelings: emotions are inherently uncertain, and that very uncertainty makes a vivid emotional life possible [article #187].

### Six steps to improve emotional granularity (from the constructed-emotion longread)
Purpose: practical steps, "based on the latest research findings" per the article, to improve emotional granularity over time and rebalance the body budget [article #187].
1. **Try looking at things from a new point of view.** What: deliberately adopt new perspectives. Why: per the theory, the concepts we hold directly affect the state of the organism and therefore emotional perception; concepts do not exist in some abstract realm separate from biology, and learning or changing concepts (also called mental models) directly affects how the body works minute by minute. How: by "trying on" new views like new clothes we can "test" different modes of body budgeting — just as we can allocate more money to one budget category, we can do the same with our body budgets. Examples: travelling abroad, talking to different people, reading literature, getting new experiences; these open different ways of meeting human needs that we may want to borrow [article #187].
2. **Reframe your feelings.** What: whenever you feel bad, recognise what is actually happening — you are experiencing unpleasant affect based on interoceptive sensations. How: with practice you can learn to break an emotion into its components instead of letting it become the lens through which you see the world. Example: general, ambiguous "anxiety" can be split into "tension in the upper back", "rapid heartbeat" and "clenched jaw"; this separation strips the sensations of some of their emotional power. Also: try to pinpoint what you feel by being mindful of different body parts, or look for more immediate physical causes such as hunger, dehydration or lack of sleep [article #187].
3. **Talk about what you feel.** Why: one of the most effective ways to question the mind's overly dramatic interpretations is to talk about them with others; open expression gives a degree of objectivity and lets others show empathy and understanding. Evidence cited: studies found that men who expressed many emotions but did not label them had the highest levels of cytokines — proteins that cause inflammation in the long run; breast-cancer survivors who clearly label and express their emotions have better health and visit doctors less often after surgery. The article insists this is not simple self-help advice: talking about feelings significantly improves health and makes you happier [article #187].
4. **Move your body.** Why: sometimes predictive links between body and mind are so strong that they are hard to break consciously; the body is the "spare exit". How: walking, yoga, stretching, lifting weights or other exercise can resynchronise signals between body and mind and rebalance the body budget. Reasoning: all animals use movement to regulate their organisms — a dog with too much glucose may run or circle to burn it. Humans are unique in being able to change their budget with purely mental concepts, but when that fails, a quick run or other physical activity can fix things [article #187].
5. **Expand your vocabulary.** Why: there is strong evidence, the article says, that emotional granularity is closely tied to linguistic granularity — the more detailed your vocabulary, the more precisely the brain can determine what is happening in the body and adjust the budget. Evidence cited: people with higher emotional granularity visit doctors less, take fewer medicines and spend fewer days in hospital due to illness; lower granularity is associated with major depressive disorder, social anxiety disorder, eating disorders, autism spectrum disorders, borderline personality disorder and general anxiety and depression. How: read complex literature, watch films with complex characters, look up words you don't know [article #187].
6. **Write about your experience.** Why: the world of concepts and the world of biology are inseparable; the brain relies on models of what is or may be happening when making "budget decisions", and we can consciously influence and enrich those models by what we expose ourselves to. Writing is one of the most effective ways to shape the concepts the brain builds directly: it makes thinking concrete, outside the head, where it can be more objectively assessed, analysed and changed. Words on the page can be reflected back to us, forming another prediction loop in which we have far more options [article #187].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- Katya Klimochkina hedges the frustrative non-reward concept: it is studied in various experimental paradigms, in animals and humans; it is not the name of a single universal mechanism and not proof that any everyday flare of anger is caused by it [msg #260].
- The hunger analogy holds "at least if you have no eating disorders" — Lena Foer's own qualification of hunger being straightforward [msg #57].
- The emotion-regulation strategy list in #27 is explicitly partial (the author says DBT training covers these plus further skills); the additional methods are not described in this post [msg #27].
- The longread is a loose translation of an article with translator's additions; the original article and the studies behind its numbers are not identified in the source. Its own hedges: no technique is guaranteed to work every time, and constructed emotion does not promise full control over feelings [article #187].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the two snake images from Barrett's TED talk carry no transcribable text; only the article's description of them is available [article #187].
- The smoke-detector model (#57) and the constructed-emotion longread (#187) agree in spirit — emotions are signals that can be wrong, a "bad feeling" is not proof something is wrong — but come from different authors and frames [msg #57, article #187].
