# Self-criticism, people-pleasing and social comparison

## What this covers

Three posts about ways people turn against their own needs and worth. The first explains what a habitual self-critical inner monologue does to a person and gives an experiment plus seven techniques for softening the inner critic [msg #48]. The second defines people-pleasing, explains why it so often arises in complex PTSD (as a survival strategy) and in borderline personality disorder (out of fear of losing relationships), and describes how it is worked on in therapy and on one's own [msg #135]. The third explains why other people's happy news can bring sadness and envy alongside genuine joy, through social comparison theory [msg #262]. The source frames these as important because self-criticism raises the risk of psychological problems and damages relationships [msg #48], and people-pleasing can lead to stress, burnout and loss of identity [msg #135].

## Core ideas

- **Self-criticism is a habit with real costs.** For many people self-critical, condemning and blaming thoughts happen almost automatically, without any particular reason; this constant negative monologue harms mental health, motivation, mood and relationships (see *Harms of the negative inner monologue*) [msg #48].
- **The inner critic can be noticed and softened.** The first and most important step is learning to notice self-critical thoughts; after that a set of techniques helps question, distance from and reformulate them (see *Seven techniques*) [msg #48].
- **People-pleasing is often a learned protection.** It can occur in anyone, but it is very common in cPTSD, where it began as a way to survive, and in BPD, where it follows from fear of losing significant relationships [msg #135].
- **People-pleasing is changed by testing it.** The main therapeutic tool is behavioural experiments: trying the new behaviour and seeing whether the feared consequences actually happen [msg #135].
- **Others' happiness can bring joy and sadness at the same time.** Through upward social comparison, especially with people who shared our "baseline", another person's milestone can become a yardstick for our own life; one feeling does not cancel the other [msg #262].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Voice-recorder experiment (hearing your own self-criticism)
Purpose: to make visible how your habitual self-talk actually affects you, and to build motivation to change it. Lena Foer asks: "Задумывались ли вы когда-нибудь, как такие мысли влияют на вас?" (*Have you ever thought about how such thoughts affect you?*) [msg #48].
1. **Record.** Turn on a voice recorder and say out loud the familiar unpleasant things you habitually say to yourself in your head [msg #48].
2. **Listen.** Play the recording back [msg #48].
3. **Notice the effect.** Concentrate on how the recording affects you. The author expects that it will most likely be very unpleasant — "И это - то, что вы слышите день за днем, в своей голове." (*And this is what you hear day after day, in your head.*) [msg #48].
The post then asks whether, by this point, the reader has the motivation to change something [msg #48].

### Seven techniques for dealing with self-critical thoughts
Purpose: what to do with critical thoughts once you have decided to change them. Lena Foer's list, in order [msg #48]:
1. **Learn to notice them (the first and most important step).** If you can notice self-critical thoughts, you can stop yourself at the moment you are thinking them. Test for telling self-criticism from other thoughts: ask whether you would say this, in this way, to a child or a close friend. If not, you should not say it to yourself either [msg #48].
2. **Remind yourself that thoughts don't always match reality,** even when in the moment it seems so. You can probably recall examples when, under the influence of emotions, something seemed unshakeable truth, and later, when you calmed down, it no longer looked so clear-cut [msg #48].
3. **Ask whether the thought helps or harms.** Consider whether this thought is bringing you benefit or harm right now — whether it helps or makes things worse [msg #48].
4. **Give your inner critic a name.** This helps some people. With a name you can say to yourself something like "А, это Петрович снова смуту вносит" (*Ah, that's Petrovich stirring up trouble again*) [msg #48].
5. **Say the thought out loud or imagine it printed on a T-shirt or badge.** Sometimes it helps to say critical thoughts aloud, or to imagine you made yourself a T-shirt or a badge with this thought on it; that way it may be easier to see how implausible or cruel it sounds [msg #48].
6. **Rephrase it without judgement.** Reformulate what sounds like self-criticism in non-evaluative terms. Example: "я неудачница" (*I'm a failure*) can become "Я сделала больше ошибок в экзамене, чем ожидала, и очень расстроена" (*I made more mistakes in the exam than I expected, and I'm very upset*) [msg #48].
7. **Imagine saying it to a close friend.** Picture saying the critical thoughts to a close friend; if you feel dissonance ("другу бы я такого не сказала!" — *I'd never say that to a friend!*), try to reformulate your thought the way you would treat a friend in such a moment [msg #48].

### Behavioural experiments for people-pleasing
Purpose: the way people-pleasing is worked on in therapy. Short answer per the source: through behavioural experiments. When you try a new behaviour, you can see for yourself how people react to it and whether there is any threat [msg #135].
- **Example:** your partner is visiting, you are very tired, but you are afraid that if you ask them to leave they will be offended or upset or tell you that you are being rude. The experiment is to politely ask them to leave anyway and observe whether this leads to the consequences you expected [msg #135].

### Three self-help practices against people-pleasing
What you can do on your own, per Lena Foer [msg #135]:
1. **Don't answer questions and requests immediately — take a pause.** Use the pause to listen to yourself and decide what exactly you want. Script from the source: "Хмм, полы помыть? Мне нужно немного подумать о своих планах на день, я отвечу в течение получаса" (*Hmm, wash the floors? I need to think a bit about my plans for the day, I'll answer within half an hour*) [msg #135].
2. **Slow down.** People who people-please often try to speak faster, take up less space and not burden others too much with themselves. Try slowing your speech, don't be afraid of pauses between sentences; pack your purchases at the checkout and take out your payment card at a normal (not panicked) pace [msg #135].
3. **Notice your typical people-pleasing situations and prepare in advance.** Observe the situations in which you most often try to please others and, before them, tune yourself in advance to awareness of your own wishes and needs. For exactly these situations you can wear a bracelet or stick a little rhinestone on your phone as a reminder [msg #135].

### Social comparison theory and the "same baseline" reference point
Purpose: explaining why happy news about others (a former crush getting married, an emigrated friend becoming "СЕО всех СЕО" — *CEO of all CEOs*, a childhood friend having a baby while another already has a second one) can produce envy, sadness and the thought that everyone else's life is working out while yours is "чет не очень" (*kinda not great*) — "Ощущение, будто ты где-то отстала, хотя вообще-то не участвовала ни в какой гонке" (*A feeling that you've fallen behind somewhere, although you weren't actually taking part in any race*). Katya Klimochkina offers social comparison as "одна из возможных причин" (*one of the possible reasons*) [msg #262]. Components:
1. **Festinger's idea.** The author recalls discussing Festinger in a social psychology course: he proposed that we evaluate our own opinions and abilities partly by comparing ourselves with other people — especially when there is no clear objective criterion for judging how well we are doing [msg #262].
2. **Life achievements work the same way.** There is no universal age by which one must marry, have a child, buy a flat or figure out who one wants to be (the author adds, struck through, "Даже, если бабушка/общество говорит иначе" — *Even if grandma/society says otherwise*). Yet other people's events can involuntarily turn into reference points [msg #262].
3. **The "same baseline" (один base line).** It is especially easy to compare ourselves with people whose lives once intersected with ours: former classmates, childhood friends, people we studied with or started our careers with become a kind of starting point. We know what they were like before and can involuntarily compare their current life with our own [msg #262].
4. **Direction of comparison — upward comparison.** Looking at someone who has what we lack is upward social comparison. It can hint at what we want, but it can also make our own life look less attractive by contrast [msg #262].
5. **Comparison is not necessarily conscious.** A wedding photo is just a photo, but in the context of a shared history it can become an occasion to evaluate not the other person's relationship but our own prospects, decisions and achievements [msg #262].
6. **We compare non-comparable things.** We compare other people's visible events with our whole life, including what didn't work out, what is still uncertain and what we never talk about publicly [msg #262].

## Detailed insights

### Harms of the negative inner monologue
Lena Foer lists four ways the negative monologue in your head harms you [msg #48]:
- It increases the risk of developing psychological problems [msg #48].
- It lowers motivation and increases the sense of helplessness: the more you tell yourself you are incapable of something, the more you believe it [msg #48].
- It intensifies depressive moods [msg #48].
- It harms relationships, because you become more vulnerable, prone to interpreting neutral phrases negatively, and demanding that loved ones talk you out of all the sad things you tell yourself [msg #48].

### What people-pleasing is
People-pleasing is behaviour in which a person puts others' needs and wishes above their own, often to the detriment of their own wellbeing. It includes a constant striving for approval, avoidance of conflict and a feeling of responsibility for other people's happiness. Such behaviour can lead to stress, burnout and loss of identity [msg #135].
- **Example (Masha):** Masha always agrees to help colleagues with their tasks even when she has a lot of work herself, because she fears people will think badly of her if she says "no". As a result she often has to stay late at work, sacrificing her personal time, and feels exhausted and resentful. Her tendency to please leads her to neglect her own needs and wellbeing [msg #135].

### Why people-pleasing is linked to cPTSD and BPD
- People-pleasing can occur in anyone, but it is very often found in borderline personality disorder and complex PTSD [msg #135].
- **In cPTSD** it originally arises as a way of surviving: in a traumatic situation, doing everything you are asked can be a way to save your life or reduce harm. Example: if your father beats you and shouts at you over every little thing, you may try to please him out of a wish "не провоцировать его лишний раз" (*not to provoke him unnecessarily*). After the trauma, in everyday life, pleasing others can persist in people with cPTSD as "талисман от всех возможных конфликтов и агрессии в их адрес" (*a talisman against all possible conflicts and aggression directed at them*) [msg #135].
- **In BPD** people-pleasing can be a consequence of the fear of losing significant relationships if one fails to please a partner or friend [msg #135].

### Social comparison: evidence, mixed feelings and the better question
- The Meier and Johnson review (see *Evidence and numbers*) found the effect of others' success is not uniform [msg #262].
- The conclusion: someone else's happiness can at the same time cause sincere joy and sadness about oneself. "Одно чувство не отменяет другое" (*One feeling doesn't cancel the other*) [msg #262].
- The author suggests the more useful question is not so much why we envy, but **why exactly this person becomes our reference point** — what in our shared history makes their life especially suitable for comparison with our own? [msg #262]

## Evidence and numbers

- A review by Meier and Johnson (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35184028/), cited by the author, examines the connection between social comparison, envy and social media use. According to its results, other people's success sometimes inspires and sometimes causes envy and dissatisfaction with oneself; the effect depends on the context, on the person and on how exactly they compare themselves with others [msg #262].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The author's own hedges: the naming technique helps "некоторым людям" (*some people*), and saying thoughts aloud helps "иногда" (*sometimes*) — not presented as universal [msg #48].
- People-pleasing is explicitly not exclusive to cPTSD/BPD: "Пипл плизерство может быть у кого угодно" (*Anyone can have people-pleasing*) [msg #135].
- Social comparison is offered as only one of the possible reasons for feeling bad at others' good news, and its effect depends on context, the person and how they compare [msg #262].
- The Festinger material is recalled from a university course rather than cited directly ("был какой-то предмет" — *there was some course*) [msg #262].
