# Depression, seasonal depression and grief

## What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's material on depression that does not look like the textbook picture: seasonal affective disorder (SAD) with its symptoms, causes, treatments and prevention; a checklist of five non-obvious symptoms of depression co-written by channel co-author Katya Klimochkina for Wonderzine; the stages of grief described from the inside on the day of a shocking loss; and a 2026 review on the neurobiology of prolonged grief disorder. The source's reasoning for why it matters: autumn melancholy is normal, but for some people it becomes a real disorder that is treatable and partly preventable [msg #83, #84]; depression "is easy to overlook" even when the person knows its classic symptoms, and its rarer forms can be dangerous (suicide risk, impulsive acts) [msg #145, #144]; and grief is a natural process of accepting a new reality whose normal course is worth knowing [msg #102].

## Core ideas

- **SAD is depression with a seasonal pattern, not a separate diagnosis.** It is distinguished from ordinary autumn blues by real impairment and by a seasonal pattern observed for a couple of years; it differs from non-seasonal depression mainly by weight gain and oversleeping [msg #83].
- **The causes of SAD are unclear and contested.** There are biological hypotheses (retinal sensitivity, biological clock, serotonin, melatonin, vitamin D) and environmental ones (less light means less movement, socializing, fresh air and new experiences). Because the environmental risk factors are the same as for any depression, some clinicians see no point in separating seasonal from non-seasonal depression [msg #83, #84].
- **Severity decides the response.** Severe depression is treated with antidepressants, sometimes with light therapy and/or psychotherapy; a mild but sliding state is a cue to work on risk factors with a lowered, realistic bar [msg #84].
- **Light therapy has mixed evidence.** Lena Foer calls its effectiveness unproven, with recent studies looking doubtful and research very heterogeneous [msg #83, #84].
- **Depression can look like its opposite.** Hypersexuality, overworking, mood lifts, brain fog and unexplained bodily pain can all be signs of depression; "у депрессии нет лица" (depression has no face) [msg #137, #145].
- **Grief moves through stages that are not linear** and normally takes a year to a year and a half; any change felt as a loss can trigger it [msg #102].
- **Prolonged grief may be a disorder of motivation and reward**, not only of emotional pain: the brain keeps striving toward an unavailable person [msg #264].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### SAD symptom list and how it differs from non-seasonal depression
Purpose: recognize when autumn mood changes go beyond "хандры под грустную музыку" (moping to sad music) and bring substantial problems — then one may speak of seasonal depressive disorder. Lena Foer lists the symptoms [msg #83]:
1. Sadness and feeling depressed most of the day, almost every day [msg #83].
2. Anxiety [msg #83].
3. Craving for carbohydrate food and weight gain. **This is the first difference from non-seasonal depression:** in non-seasonal depression weight loss and weight gain occur equally often, while in seasonal depression it is usually gain [msg #83].
4. Extreme fatigue and lack of energy [msg #83].
5. Feelings of hopelessness or worthlessness [msg #83].
6. Problems concentrating [msg #83].
7. Irritability or feeling wound up (взвинченность) [msg #83].
8. A feeling of heaviness in the arms and legs [msg #83].
9. Loss of interest in usually pleasant activities, including withdrawing from social activities [msg #83].
10. Sleep problems. **This is the second difference:** in seasonal depression excessive sleepiness is more common, while in "classic" depression too little and too much sleep are equally likely [msg #83].
11. Thoughts of death or suicide [msg #83].

Diagnostic rule: SAD is spoken of when, in addition to the symptoms, there are a couple of years of observations of the seasonal pattern. Spring–summer depression also exists but is much rarer. SAD is more common in women and in people living far from the equator and close to the poles [msg #83].

### Hypotheses about what causes SAD
Lena Foer stresses these are hypotheses, not settled facts [msg #83, #84]:
1. **Change of the biological clock** [msg #83].
2. **Disrupted serotonin production** — sunlight regulates its level [msg #83].
3. **Vitamin D deficiency** [msg #83].
4. **Excess melatonin**, which is produced when it gets dark [msg #83]. The follow-up post details the biological version: in particular people a special sensitivity of the retinal receptors may make the shortening day hit the sleep/wake centres especially hard, melatonin production rises, and this is how autumn depression is triggered. Weak points of this theory: light therapy (which should then fix the problem) does not help everyone, and neither does influencing melatonin levels [msg #84].
5. **Negative beliefs about winter** (the author jokingly crosses out the continuation "darkness, horror, cold and chaos") [msg #83].

### Environmental risk factors of autumn/winter depression
Causes of illness can be roughly divided into biological (what went wrong in the body) and environmental (how the outside world influenced it). The environmental factor is usually named "less light", but Lena Foer unpacks what that really means [msg #84]:
1. Cold, rain and snow make going out unpleasant, so physical activity drops [msg #84].
2. Cold also means hot radiators, dry air and less oxygen behind a closed window [msg #84].
3. Going out less means less social life and communication [msg #84].
4. And fewer impressions, pleasant activities, new places and "движухи в целом" (action in general) [msg #84].
Together these are the typical risk factors for depression — which is exactly why not everyone sees sense in splitting depression into seasonal and non-seasonal: the risk factors look too similar [msg #84].

### SAD treatment options
As listed in the first post [msg #83]:
1. **Light therapy** — but its effectiveness is not proven and the latest studies look rather doubtful [msg #83].
2. **Vitamin D as a supplement.** Caveat: vitamin D overdose also happens, so before starting it is better to see a doctor and find out what your personal level is [msg #83].
3. **More time outdoors** — under daylight, even if pale; the recommended amount is from 30 minutes to 2 hours a day [msg #83].
4. **Cognitive-behavioural therapy and antidepressants**, together or separately — as for any other depression [msg #83].
The follow-up adds the severity logic: when depression is already present and strong, people are usually prescribed antidepressants, sometimes adding light therapy and/or psychotherapy [msg #84].

### Prevention self-check: 7 risk factors when the mood starts sliding
When to use: the state is still okay but seems to be "crawling down" (for example, it got worse in previous autumns). Knowing the causes gives ideas for therapy and prevention; here it means working on risk factors by checking how you personally are doing with [msg #84]:
1. **Social life** — how often you meet and talk with people you like [msg #84].
2. **Physical activity** — and warily crawling out to the shop counts too [msg #84].
3. **Time outdoors** [msg #84].
4. **Pleasant/new experiences** — do you have a habit of adding such things to your days? [msg #84]
5. **Stress level** [msg #84].
6. **Variety of diet** — because the necessary levels of vitamins and microelements are what we get from food [msg #84].
7. **Alcohol and psychoactive substance use** — their presence can also worsen the situation [msg #84].
How to apply it: in a near-depressive state (and in autumn gloom generally) influencing these factors is hard, so lower the bar to a realistic one rather than aiming for perfect — "5 минут на улице каждый день лучше, чем 2 часа на улице раз в месяц" (5 minutes outside every day is better than 2 hours outside once a month) — and praise yourself even for small achievements. It may also make sense to check your vitamin D level and add it if needed [msg #84].

### Light therapy protocol
Optional add-on, per Lena Foer [msg #84]:
1. Find a lamp by searching "лампы для светотерапии" (light therapy lamps) [msg #84].
2. It must give 10,000 lux [msg #84].
3. Switch it on in the morning for 15–30 minutes [msg #84].
4. Do not look directly at it; do your own things nearby [msg #84].
Caveat: the evidence of its effectiveness is mixed and the studies are very heterogeneous [msg #84].

### Five non-obvious symptoms of depression (Wonderzine checklist)
A carousel forwarded from the magazine Wonderzine, headlined "От гиперсексуальности до болевых ощущений: 5 неочевидных симптомов депрессии" (From hypersexuality to pain: 5 non-obvious symptoms of depression) [img #137]. It was compiled with clinical psychologist and trauma specialist Katya Klimochkina (channel co-author) and CBT/DBT psychotherapist Anastasia Petrova; text by Alisa Popova. Its premise: depression is easy to miss even with classic symptoms (negative thoughts, low self-esteem, apathy), because apathy collides with self-criticism — «Я просто ленивый, слабый и безвольный», «Не могу взять себя в руки», «Мне просто нужно собраться» ("I'm just lazy, weak and spineless", "I can't pull myself together", "I just need to get it together") — and every negative thought feels like absolute truth about oneself and the world. All symptoms deserve attention, but this checklist covers rarer ones [msg #145].

1. **Hypersexuality.** Depression is usually pictured as a person who wants nothing, especially not sex. But recent studies say depression can both lower and raise sexual desire. It is individual, because each person copes with unpleasant emotions in their own way: one closes off and tends toward social isolation, for another sex can become comfort, or seem to. Seeking positive emotions and support in sex is not always a good idea: a high level of depression raises the likelihood of risky sexual behaviour. Sometimes hypersexuality takes a compulsive form, and masturbation, porn, fantasies or promiscuous sex become a form of self-help. It is important to remember that a person in depression is more vulnerable to violence. Hypersexuality should not be ignored: it can become one of the directions of work with a psychotherapist and usually responds easily to medication, since one of the best-known side effects of some antidepressants is a dulling of sexual desire [img #138].
2. **"Brain fog" (туман в голове).** Depression can reduce cognitive functions, namely: working memory (needed to orient in the moment and understand the world around us); long-term memory (recalling knowledge and skills learned long ago); the ability to direct attention where you want and need, rather than where it drifts by itself; reaction speed; and decision-making ability. "Brain fog" is the name for scattered attention — the brain seems not to work, there is confusion and a feeling of losing contact with it; focusing on tasks is hard, reaction time and all thinking slow down, forgetfulness appears, and a feeling of an impenetrable wall between me and my knowledge and the ability to use it [img #139]. How it shows, by domain [img #140]:
   - *Memory:* not understanding how to do even routine things (which washing-machine programme to choose, in what order to add ingredients to a long-familiar dish); "залипание" (getting stuck) on a task — you start it but don't progress; needing lots of reminders, sticky notes and to-do lists just to organize the day.
   - *Attention:* inability to finish a thought, follow a conversation, finish a film or an article; losing everything, from keys to agreed plans.
   - *Reaction time:* tasks take much longer than usual; even the simplest tasks exhaust and create the feeling the brain doesn't work, which in turn prompts procrastination.
   - *Executive dysfunction:* inability to make decisions, immersion in a catastrophic future, fear of negative consequences; inability to do two things at once — everything gets mixed up and leads to a stupor.
   As a rule, brain fog is a symptom of severe depression [img #140].
3. **Increased productivity.** Classic symptoms are loss of energy, inability to concentrate and reduced capacity for work, so heightened productivity is often not seen as a possible sign of a depressive episode. Yet there is a positive correlation between depression and workaholism; it appears most often when the person has, besides depressive symptoms, a high level of anxiety. Behind the need to overwork there is often a deep self-image of a worthless person who must earn the right to recognition. The skew toward work is especially dangerous when the person is unsatisfied with other areas of life — then work risks becoming an addiction, which ultimately leads to burnout and worsening problems. Practical rule: if you see a colleague staying until night, don't rush to praise (and reinforce) it — they may need help [img #142].
4. **Unexplained pain.** Mind and body are connected, so depression can show as unpleasant physical symptoms: migraines, joint pain, nausea, vomiting and other gastrointestinal problems. Versions of why: (a) the low serotonin and norepinephrine typical of depression amplify perceived pain; (b) the brain areas responsible for mood and for pain perception are connected, so an emotional disorder can show as physical pain; (c) depression changes lifestyle — reduced physical activity, insomnia and lack of appetite can cause uncomfortable physical states. Of course, with painful sensations one should first see a doctor and get examined; but if no objective causes are found for a long time and doctors shrug, the cause may lie in the psychological state [img #143].
5. **Mood lifts.** The first thing a doctor asks when diagnosing depression is whether your mood is low and whether you notice loss of interest in what used to bring joy — and rightly so. But a growing number of studies say people in depression can feel positive emotions and mood lifts in response to particular stimuli. People with **atypical depression** can feel joy from some events or from anticipating them; this type is also characterized by excessive sleepiness, heaviness and aching in the body and heightened sensitivity to criticism. Scientists estimate that 18–36% of people have atypical depression, which makes it harder to diagnose. In addition, sharp mood lifts against a depressive background can be symptoms of a phase shift in bipolar affective disorder — this happens at the onset of a mixed state, hypomania or mania (the words "mixed state, hypomania or" are missing from the OCR and were read from the image). This is dangerous: if a depressed person starts feeling more energy against a background of dark thoughts, the risk of impulsive acts rises [img #144]. The most frightening consequence can be suicidal actions. Sometimes the very decision to end one's life can cause a painful elevation of mood: the person regulates the emotional state this way, "находит ответ" (finds an answer), and can be happy for a while. That is why it is said that depression has no face — even the most cheerful people may urgently need support [img #145].

### Stages of grief
Katya Klimochkina had planned a post on normal and complicated grief, and on grieving in BPD and other disorders, using Dexter, the main character of the series *One Day* (based on David Nicholls' novel). On the day of writing, while at a DBT team meeting, news that "caught everyone" (she deliberately names no names) turned the post from a post into a process she was living through. She describes the stages through her own experience [msg #102]:
1. **Shock.** Dizziness, feeling extremely cold, trembling in the body and hands. The thought that it only seemed so and the message was a glitch; googling it — true. She told colleagues she was leaving, went and lay on the floor, and just lay there; all thoughts disappeared [msg #102].
2. **Fear.** Fear that this is the end; powerlessness and helplessness; "Что делать?" (What to do?) [msg #102].
3. **Anger.** Slowly but insistently, angry thoughts and rolling waves of rage — at the world, at the system, at oneself. Mixed in: "Щепотка вины, крупица паники, много потерянности" (a pinch of guilt, a grain of panic, a lot of lostness), and a great deal of injustice. At the time of writing she was still at this stage [msg #102].
4. **Bargaining (торг).** Magical thinking: wanting to make a deal with someone — maybe I can do something and everything will be fine? Some start believing that if they pray very hard or never ever do anything bad again, they will be heard and it will turn out all right; "Если очень захотеть, то ведь можно обратить время вспять" (If you want it very much, you can turn back time), and if you want it and work hard, it will certainly happen. She notes (in crossed-out text) that waiting for official confirmation may itself be part of bargaining, or a return to denial [msg #102].
5. **Depression, a.k.a. the stage of powerlessness.** All the steps, thoughts about world conspiracies and anger at everything go away; everything becomes indifferent and empty. Not only the sad event but all of life feels meaningless: "Смысла нет, все плохо, ну ок" (There's no meaning, everything is bad, well okay). It often lasts long — months or a year depending on the strength of the loss [msg #102].
6. **Acceptance.** After a looong period of digesting the pain comes quiet acceptance — "Это не согласие, а именно смирение" (not agreement but resignation). There is nowhere to go and we keep living. Some return to their usual life, some change something and begin rebuilding everyday life and routine; positive experiences appear from time to time. The pain is still there, but not ten out of ten, 24/7 — a gradual getting used to it and return to life [msg #102].

Rules about the process [msg #102]:
- Normally grieving takes a year to a year and a half, depending on the loss — one can lose a close person, someone significant or just an acquaintance.
- Grief is not only about people: one can grieve animals, a divorce, a move or a job. Any change that feels like a loss can trigger grieving.
- There are more than five stages; they can come mixed up, swap places, be skipped, or one can go back. This is normal and natural; the whole path helps us accept a new reality in which something that was there is no longer there.

### Neurobiological model of prolonged grief disorder
Question posed: why, in prolonged grief disorder, does a person not just miss the deceased but keep feeling a strong striving toward them? Katya Klimochkina summarizes a 2026 review in *Trends in Neurosciences* on its neurobiological mechanisms [msg #264]:
1. **Main finding:** the specificity of prolonged grief may be linked not only to processing emotional pain but to the motivation and reward systems [msg #264].
2. **Mechanism:** the brain does not just record that a person was important; it helps form the drive to seek them, keep contact and get from interaction what used to be meaningful. After the death of a loved one this drive collides with the impossibility of restoring the relationship in the usual way [msg #264].
3. **Brain regions discussed:** the nucleus accumbens, amygdala, orbitofrontal and cingulate cortex, and insula — involved in processes from motivation and evaluating the salience of stimuli to emotional processing. In prolonged grief, features of their activity may be linked to persistent yearning and preoccupation with thoughts of the deceased [msg #264].
4. **Difference from depression and anxiety disorders:** cognitive control and salience-processing systems are involved in many mental disorders, but features of reward processing and motivational striving may be more characteristic specifically of prolonged grief [msg #264].
5. **Implication:** prolonged grief may depend not only on how painful the loss is but on how the motivational striving toward an unavailable person persists. This helps investigate why adaptation is sometimes hindered even when the fact of death has long been understood [msg #264].

## Detailed insights

### Normal melancholy vs disorder
Feeling melancholic when there is less sun, days are shorter, the air is colder and you want to leave the house less is normal. The disorder threshold is when mood changes with the onset of autumn (especially late autumn) go beyond moping and bring substantial problems [msg #83].

### Why the SAD/non-SAD split is debated
Lena Foer's first post started "a lively discussion with colleagues", hence a second instalment; she credits co-author Katya Klimochkina and psychiatrist Karina Eganyan for links and conversations. The upshot of that discussion is the environmental-factor argument above: since the risk factors overlap almost completely with those of any depression, the separation is questioned by some [msg #84].
