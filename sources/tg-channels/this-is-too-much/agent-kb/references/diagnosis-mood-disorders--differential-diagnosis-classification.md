# Differential diagnosis and classification: BPD, bipolar, cPTSD and HiTOP

## What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel taught about drawing boundaries between borderline personality disorder (BPD, ПРЛ) and neighbouring diagnoses — bipolar disorder (БАР), complex PTSD (кПТСР) and schizotypal disorder — and about what happens when they co-occur. It also covers the comorbidities typical of BPD, the research debate on BPD as a "trauma-spectrum" disorder, the HiTOP dimensional classification as an alternative to DSM/ICD, and a psychiatrist's checklist of what a patient is entitled to at an appointment. According to the authors, this matters because the overlapping symptoms make these disorders easy to confuse, co-occurrence is common, overdiagnosis is widespread ("гипердиагностика на каждому шагу"), and categorical diagnoses do not map neatly onto real people who often have 4–5 disorders at once [msg #25, #131, #202, #211].

## Core ideas

- **BPD and bipolar disorder are separate illnesses that overlap in symptoms.** BPD is a personality disorder, bipolar disorder a mood disorder; they co-occur in a minority of cases (roughly 10–20%), and the key differentiator is the duration and stability of the symptoms (see *Duration-and-stability rule*) [msg #25, #131].
- **BPD has a characteristic set of comorbidities** — anxiety disorders (especially GAD), mood disorders such as depression, eating disorders, plus PTSD and substance use — and elevated risk of somatic illness [msg #25, #30].
- **BPD shares a lot with complex PTSD**, which has led to research on whether BPD is a "trauma-spectrum" disorder; but the two are not the same, and the differences are subtle (see *cPTSD vs BPD differentiation*) [msg #47, #196].
- **When cPTSD and BPD co-occur, their symptoms amplify rather than add up** — across emotion regulation, identity, relationships, loneliness, shame and dissociation (see *cPTSD + BPD co-occurrence*) [article #202].
- **The BPD/schizotypal split is partly artificial** and historically rooted in the psychoanalytic concept of borderline organization [msg #5].
- **HiTOP is a dimensional, empirically derived alternative to DSM/ICD** that explains comorbidity as the result of shared underlying factors; it places BPD in the same "distress" group as PTSD, depression, dysthymia and GAD [msg #211, #212].
- **A psychiatric appointment has a minimum standard**: explanation of the condition, a named and justified diagnosis, information on treatment options and side effects, a follow-up visit, a prescription and a question about psychotherapy [msg #172, img #172].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Borderpolar symptom map (BPD-only / bipolar-only / shared symptoms)
Purpose: to understand how BPD and bipolar disorder co-exist in one person ("borderpolar", the dual diagnosis БАР + ПРЛ) and to tell them apart. Katya Klimochkina says that when both are present, the symptoms specific to each of them will show up [msg #131]. Her symptom split exists in two versions — a written list and a three-zone Venn diagram — both reproduced below [msg #131, #133].

**Symptoms exclusive to bipolar disorder (text list)** [msg #131]:
1. Mood swings over time that probably follow patterns.
2. Manic/hypomanic episodes producing extremely strong emotional experiences that have a "beginning" and an "end" and last from several days to weeks.
3. Mixed episodes: depressive symptoms within hypomanic/manic episodes.
4. Changes in the amount and quality of sleep depending on the episode.

**Symptoms occurring only in BPD (text list)** [msg #131]:
1. Intense and not always appropriate emotional reactions in everyday situations — many ordinary household moments are taken "close to heart" («близко к сердцу»).
2. Outbursts of strong uncontrollable anger, followed by feelings of shame or guilt.
3. Self-harm.
4. A constant feeling of boredom and emptiness.
5. Difficulties in relationships and in regulating emotions during interactions.

**Venn infographic** [img #133] — three zones:
- *BPD only (ПРЛ):* (1) fear of being abandoned; (2) feeling of emptiness; (3) unstable relationships; (4) difficulties understanding oneself.
- *Bipolar only (БАР):* (1) manic and hypomanic episodes; (2) severe depressive symptoms; (3) grandiosity and excessive belief in oneself; (4) reduced need for sleep and food; (5) racing thoughts; (6) increased goal-directed activity.
- *Shared (ПРЛ+БАР overlap):* (1) mood instability; (2) paranoid thoughts; (3) dissociation; (4) impulsivity; (5) anger and irritability; (6) substance abuse; (7) sleep disturbances; (8) depressive symptoms.

The text post also names the shared ground: vivid and fast emotional reactions, impulsive actions or suicidal intentions are characteristic of both BAR and BPD [msg #131]. Note that the text list places sleep changes among bipolar-only symptoms while the infographic places "sleep disturbances" in the overlap and "reduced need for sleep" under bipolar only — see Caveats.

**Order of diagnosis in dual cases.** For most people with both diagnoses, one is made before the other, because the symptoms overlap and sometimes mask each other. Bipolar disorder is often diagnosed first, which makes BPD symptoms harder to detect; over time and with treatment of one disorder, the other can become more obvious [msg #131].

### Duration-and-stability rule for BPD vs bipolar disorder
Purpose: deciding whether a picture is better explained by BPD, by bipolar disorder, or both. Klimochkina's rule: look at the duration and stability of the symptoms [msg #131].
1. **Start from the category difference.** Bipolar disorder is a mood disorder; BPD is a personality disorder. Mood is more changeable than personality [msg #131].
2. **"Always the same" → personality disorder.** If a person "always" («всегда») reacts the same way and the BPD signs listed above resonate, a personality disorder can be suspected [msg #131].
3. **"Returns to baseline" → mood disorder.** If the person changes depending on the episode, but outside the high or low state "returns" to their norm, behaves "typically for themselves" («типично себе»), has no acute emotional reactions, or is less prone to experiences of inner emptiness or abandonment, then a mood disorder is the more likely explanation [msg #131].

### cPTSD vs BPD differentiation (six points)
Purpose: separating complex PTSD from BPD, which share a lot. Katya Klimochkina developed these points for a talk at a round table on BPD and trauma and for a diagram in her book; she notes that the differences are less obvious than one would wish and that few good images and articles discuss the nuances [msg #196]. Lena Foer gives a shorter, criteria-based version of the same contrast [msg #47]; both are merged here with attribution.

0. **Starting point — why they look alike.** If PTSD is about "a single trauma that happened", cPTSD is about how "trauma changed the personality itself" («травма изменила саму личность») and affected everything, from emotion regulation to relations with the world. BPD too is often linked to adverse childhood experiences, emotional neglect, an invalidating and unstable environment, affects emotion management and changes the personality [msg #196].
1. **Emotional instability — present in both, but different.** In BPD it is emotional swings and extremes, from euphoria to despair "fast"; emotions are reactive, intense and slow to subside; triggers are mostly interpersonal situations where rejection or abandonment is felt. In cPTSD there can be emotional numbing or flooding, but if a fast swing happens, its cause is a **traumatic reaction**: an "emotional surge" of irritation or anxiety gives way to numbness and depression as the trauma model is activated, when memory brings up something from the past [msg #196].
2. **Fear of abandonment and rejection.** BPD is about fear of abandonment and rejection even when there may be no "objective" reason for it. In cPTSD such fears occur only if the traumatic experience itself led to them; if the trauma is unrelated, the fears will be about something else [msg #196]. Lena Foer likewise states that BPD includes acute fear of abandonment and cPTSD does not [msg #47].
3. **Relationships — hard in both, differently.** In BPD it is frightening to be alone, so there will be attempts — possibly "very creative" ones — to prevent it in relationships. In cPTSD a person, like most people, also does not want to feel lonely, but more typical is withdrawal into oneself, avoidance of closeness and vulnerability, isolation, distrust and avoiding contact in general [msg #196].
4. **Self-image.** A key cPTSD criterion is a **negative self-image linked to the trauma**: the person feels their badness, defectiveness, worthlessness or something else as "conclusions" of the traumatic experience that shaped the personality. In BPD self-image can also be negative, but more often the personality and "self-esteem" are **unstable**: from "I'm fine" to "I'm the bottom" («от "я норм" до "я дно"») is one swing, because of the feeling of emptiness and confused identity — not understanding who you are and being unable to "make up your mind". Klimochkina recalls (hedging "if memory serves") that a book describes this nicely from a neurobiological angle [msg #196]. Lena Foer: BPD includes an unstable self-image, cPTSD does not [msg #47].
5. **Trauma-specific symptoms.** cPTSD requires symptoms of re-experiencing the trauma (flashbacks, nightmares), avoidance of reminders of the traumatic event and a general persistent sense of threat — BPD does not; in fact BPD has no requirement of a traumatic experience or a reaction to one at all [msg #47].
6. **Contested symptoms.** Some sources treat impulsivity, self-harm and a feeling of emptiness as unique to BPD and dissociation as belonging only to cPTSD; other sources propose considering them shared between BPD and cPTSD [msg #196].

Klimochkina says these are not the only differences but the most noticeable ones, and promised to add more later [msg #196]. Her follow-up on what happens when both are present is reproduced in the next framework [msg #202].

### cPTSD + BPD co-occurrence: six domains where the two amplify each other
Purpose: Katya Klimochkina's follow-up to the differences post. It answers a question she says "remained in the air for many" and stays relevant now that "overdiagnosis is at every step" («гипердиагностика на каждому шагу»): how to tell whether a person has "cPTSD and BPD, or everything, everywhere, all at once" («это кПТСР и ПРЛ или все, везде и сразу»). She looks at how the combination shows in a person's behaviour, thinking and sensations, and how to separate the manifestations of cPTSD and BPD when they co-exist [msg #202, article #202].

**Central thesis.** When cPTSD and BPD co-exist, the symptoms do not simply add up — they amplify each other («симптомы не просто складываются, а они усиливают друг друга») [article #202]. Each domain below names the cPTSD contribution, the BPD contribution and what the combination looks like.

1. **Emotional dysregulation.** Difficulties regulating emotions become more pronounced and less predictable. Two patterns alternate: emotional numbing (cPTSD's strategy for reducing distress) and acute, sometimes stormy reactions to interpersonal triggers (BPD) — for example hyperarousal, catastrophizing, obsessive attention to threat signals. The result is a swing from "I feel nothing" («Я ничего не чувствую») to "I'm being torn apart" («Меня разрывает»). Coping differs by disorder: in cPTSD emotional pain leads to avoidance ("I just go numb to survive" — «Я просто ухожу в онемение, чтобы выжить»), whereas in BPD impulsive behaviour comes to the fore as a way of handling emotions. In the combination, impulsive attempts to "put out the emotional pain" — self-harm, unprotected sex, overeating, risky driving — alternate with periods of emotional numbness and isolation. The impulsivity is often fuelled by thoughts such as "I can't stand this" («Я не выдержу это») or "This has to stop immediately" («Надо немедленно прекратить это»). Her example of the inner voice: "I feel empty, but I need to feel at least something, and I do some risky, crazy stuff. Yes, afterwards I despise myself even more, but how else can I feel anything?" [article #202]
2. **Identity.** cPTSD often brings stable negative beliefs about oneself: "I'm bad", "I'm worthless", "I don't deserve anything good". BPD adds instability to the sense of self: not only am I "bad", I don't know why I'm like this, nor who this "I" is that feels this way. The person clings to other people's evaluations to hold on to any sense of "I", so self-images become tied to others' judgements. Her example: you want to wear short socks, a friend says only long ones are worn now — and you already feel worthless for all the short socks you have ever worn. Or the reverse: style, opinions and behaviour change with the situation or emotions, and a feeling arises that you are a fake and a pretender (for instance because you stopped wearing short socks). Her formula: cPTSD + BPD = constant changes in self-perception, unstable goals, contradictory beliefs about oneself and difficulty forming a coherent "self" [article #202].
3. **Interpersonal relationships.** Relationships feel simultaneously dangerous and necessary (she adds, as an aside, that it made her want to rewatch the film *Sid and Nancy* about the Sex Pistols). cPTSD typically uses avoidance — "the world and people are dangerous" («Мир и люди опасны»). BPD intensifies the need for confirmation of one's significance, the fear of rejection and hypersensitivity to refusal. The combo produces an ambivalent pattern: avoidance plus, at the same time, desperate attempts to get closeness. Automatic thoughts appear: "They will leave me" («Меня бросят»), "I am unworthy of love" («Я недостоин любви»), "If I open up, I'll be hurt" («Если я откроюсь, меня ранят»). Even neutral situations are interpreted as hostile. Both the search for closeness and the fear of it end in pushing others away — and then in self-punishment for being left alone again [article #202].
4. **Loneliness.** In the combination loneliness is felt in any setting — among people, alone with oneself, in conversation and in silence. It comes with a sense of otherness, inadequacy and hopelessness, and thoughts like "I can't be fixed" («Меня не исправить») and "I can't be understood" («Меня не понять»); a fear of being "too much" and/or "not enough". The feeling of being unworthy of contact sits next to an enormous wish for closeness and support — and, again, an even greater feeling of not deserving it [article #202].
5. **Shame.** In cPTSD shame is "long and sticky" («долгий и липкий») — "I'm bad". In BPD it is explosive and intolerable, often leading to impulsive actions. Combined, shame becomes unceasing, all-consuming and hard to put into words. Even a hint of vulnerability — for example some blunder or mistake one has made — leads to emotional reactivity and/or self-harm, because even a hint of vulnerability feels like a catastrophe [article #202].
6. **Dissociation.** In the combination dissociation can become a constant background: it appears with emotional overload, during conflicts, with reminders of the trauma, or even simply "in everyday life", in routine situations [article #202].

Her closing note: digging deeper, one could find dozens more nuances that make the cPTSD + BPD combination so complex and interesting, and that call for attentiveness, delicacy and expertise from specialists, so that the people behind the abbreviations can live better — and be understood [article #202]. ### HiTOP vs DSM/ICD comparison
Purpose: understanding an alternative to the two classifiers used in modern psychiatry — DSM (in America) and ICD (in the rest of the world). The post's author says she loved studying DSM and ICD and found them elegantly made until she encountered HiTOP, which is now "my new love" [msg #211].

HiTOP — Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology (иерархическая таксономия психопатологии) — differs on these points [msg #211]:
1. **How it was built.** DSM and ICD are based on expert consensus and clinical experience — roughly, "doctors agreed on it based on what they saw". HiTOP is based on a huge volume of empirical data run through statistical analysis: scientists took large databases and statistically clustered the symptoms and symptom groups that most often occur together in the population. This produced separate dimensions — e.g. fear-based disorders, or sexual problems.
2. **How diagnoses are structured.** DSM and ICD are categorical: a symptom is either present or not; enough symptoms means the disorder is present, too few means it isn't. HiTOP is a spectrum: disorders are continua with a smooth transition from full health to maximum illness and no clear boundary — which, the author says, better reflects reality.
3. **Who maintains it.** It is compiled and updated by an international community of scientists (about 70 people at the time).
4. **Hierarchical structure.** Disorders are grouped at levels from general to specific, like an inverted pyramid: at the top the broadest categories of mental problems, then large groups of related disorders, then narrower groupings, and finally specific symptoms and manifestations. (!) INCOMPLETE: the post refers to a picture of the hierarchy (a Wikipedia image of the HiTOP model) whose content is not transcribed in the source [msg #211, #212].
5. **It explains comorbidity.** In traditional systems comorbidity looks like a mysterious coincidence; HiTOP shows it is a natural consequence of disorders having common foundations: if two disorders are on the same spectrum, they co-occur not by chance but because they share developmental mechanisms. The author calls this a huge shift for clinical practice: evidence-based therapy works by category ("a protocol for depression here, one for PTSD there, don't mix them up"), but a real person often shows 4–5 disorders in parallel, so choosing a protocol becomes very hard. Therapy organized around problem types (dimensions) rather than disorders makes "what to do" far more solvable. The author knows one protocol built this way — the Unified Protocol for affective disorders — and says there are probably more.
6. **Biological validity.** HiTOP dimensions show stronger links to genetic and neurobiological markers of illness than DSM and ICD diagnoses.
7. **Clinical informativeness — and a new problem.** HiTOP is more informative because it accounts for more nuances and the severity of individual symptoms, which also solves the problem of arbitrary boundaries between disorders. But it creates a new problem: in today's world a diagnosis is like a switch («рубильник») with only two positions — present or absent — and it is unclear what to do when there are more than two positions.
8. **Transparency.** Discussions of all changes to the classification are much more transparent than with ICD and DSM [msg #212].

### HiTOP placement of PTSD and BPD (internalizing → distress)
Worked example from the HiTOP post [msg #211, #212]:
1. **Top level — internalizing disorders.** PTSD belongs to internalizing disorders, those in which people live the symptoms inside themselves rather than expressing them outward [msg #211].
2. **Next level — distress-related disorders.** One level down, PTSD belongs to disorders associated with experiencing distress. Their listed manifestations: dysphoria; anhedonia; insomnia; suicidality; agitation; appetite changes; re-experiencing; avoidance; sense of threat; dissociation. The source itself ends the list with «И другие.», marking it as open-ended in the original; (!) INCOMPLETE: no further manifestations are named [msg #211].
3. **Members of the distress group.** Together with PTSD this group contains dysthymia, depression, generalized anxiety disorder and borderline personality disorder. The author notes ("surprise, surprise") that all of these very often occur together, and HiTOP explains why: they have common factors behind them. HiTOP also better reflects how differently one and the same diagnosis can look in different people, since each will sit in different parts of the continuum on different manifestations [msg #212].
4. **Open revision — a trauma-stress subcategory.** There are arguments for singling out traumatic stress as a separate subcategory: research shows that in PTSD (as in BPD, the author adds) people show both internalizing and externalizing behaviour — aggression directed at others belongs to the latter. A separate traumatic-stress category would capture this specificity [msg #212].

### Psychiatrist appointment checklist (Elizaveta Fedorova)
Purpose: going to a psychiatrist is anxious, especially the first time, and even regular patients may not know what they have the right to expect. Psychiatrist Elizaveta Fedorova made this list for the channel of what a successful consultation includes [msg #172, img #172]:
1. **Explanation.** You were given the reasons or hypotheses for your condition — why you feel the way you do.
2. **A named, justified diagnosis.** You were given a diagnosis, it was said out loud, and you were told why this particular diagnosis is made.
3. **Provisional status of complex diagnoses.** For complex/severe diagnoses (e.g. bipolar disorder, schizophrenia spectrum, personality disorders) it was pointed out that the diagnosis is preliminary and requires observation over time to be confirmed.
4. **Treatment options.** When the diagnosis is made, you are told the possible treatment options — both medication and psychotherapy.
5. **Rationale for medication.** You are told why taking medication is worthwhile in your case; psychopharmacotherapy is prescribed according to clinical guidelines; the line of therapy is stated, and if it is not first-line, the reason is explained.
6. **No treatment without diagnosis.** If you are not given or told a diagnosis but treatment is prescribed, that is not OK — treatment can only be prescribed when there is a diagnosis.
7. **Side effects.** You are told the most common side effects of the prescribed drugs and which side effects are an absolute contraindication to continuing.
8. **Follow-up visit.** When medication is prescribed, a repeat appointment is scheduled to track the drug's effectiveness and safety — usually on average after 2–6 weeks, depending on the diagnosis, current state and treatment.
9. **Paperwork.** You are given a prescription for the prescribed medication and a written conclusion of the appointment.
10. **Psychotherapy question.** You are asked whether you are in psychotherapy; if not, you are told whether it should be added. If psychotherapeutic treatment is needed, you are offered therapy approaches with evidence-based effectiveness for your case.

## Detailed insights

### BPD and bipolar disorder: definitions and comorbidity
Lena Foer defines the two as separate mental illnesses that can be confused due to overlapping symptoms. BPD is a personality disorder characterized by an unstable sense of self, difficulty maintaining stable relationships, impulsive behaviour and difficulty regulating emotions. Bipolar disorder is a mood disorder with significant swings in mood, energy and behaviour: periods of elevated mood (mania or hypomania) and, in most people, periods of depression; these episodes can be very intense and disrupt a person's ability to cope with daily life [msg #25]. Katya Klimochkina's definitions match: bipolar disorder is an affective disorder with swings from (hypo)manic to depressed; BPD is characterized by instability of behaviour, mood, self-perception and relationships [msg #131].

Comorbidity pattern (Lena Foer, citing a review article): other personality disorders were diagnosed more often than BPD in patients with bipolar disorder; and conversely, other Axis I disorders — depression, substance abuse and PTSD — are diagnosed more often in patients with BPD than bipolar disorder is. So BPD's most frequent companions are not bipolar disorder [msg #25]. Klimochkina says this statistic raises the question of how exactly two diagnoses with overlapping symptoms co-exist and how they differ — answered by the *Borderpolar symptom map* and the *Duration-and-stability rule* [msg #131].

### Other disorders that accompany BPD
Lena Foer describes BPD as a mental illness marked by instability of the sense of self, relationships and mood, with sharp mood swings, impulsive behaviour, difficulty regulating emotions, often a history of unstable relationships and frequent struggle with self-destructive thoughts and behaviour. BPD is associated with [msg #30]:
1. Anxiety disorders — first of all generalized anxiety disorder.
2. Mood disorders — for example, depression.
3. Eating disorders.

People with BPD may also have an increased risk of developing somatic (physical) illnesses due to impulsive behaviour, possible dependence on alcohol and psychoactive substances, and difficulty managing stress. Her recommendation: if you or someone you know struggles with BPD, seek professional help; with proper treatment both BPD symptoms and co-occurring disorders can be managed [msg #30].

### BPD as a "trauma-spectrum" disorder
Lena Foer lists what BPD and cPTSD share: problems with emotion regulation and oscillation between suppressing feelings and expressing them with extreme intensity, difficulty building relationships, dissociative symptoms and a tendency to self-destructive behaviour (among other things). In addition, by various estimates 30 to 90% of clients with BPD experienced abuse, physical or sexualized violence and neglect in childhood. All of this together is also characteristic of cPTSD, so there is now quite a lot of research on whether BPD is a "trauma-spectrum" disorder altogether, including interesting data on similar neurobiological mechanisms behind the two disorders. Her conclusion: this does not mean cPTSD and BPD are the same (see the differentiation framework), but studying BPD with an emphasis on its possible traumatic nature may add something new to our understanding of it [msg #47].

### Why BPD and schizotypal disorder are often diagnosed together
The channel explains that psychiatrists often give the same patients both BPD and schizotypal disorder, and that this has a historical basis. The psychoanalysts who first used the term "borderline" applied it to a state more severe than neurotic but without a psychotic break with reality. Since this was a spectrum — neurotics, borderlines, psychotics — there was no rigid division, and socially isolated patients, less adapted to social life, also fell into the borderline level of organization. By 1975 the first definition of BPD appeared that entered the DSM and began to be used for diagnosis; in it, for the first time, people with social isolation, closer to the schizoid spectrum, were split off into a separate disorder — schizotypal. So the division is partly artificial, and these categories can really be close in individual traits [msg #5].

## Evidence and numbers

- **BPD ↔ bipolar comorbidity (Lena Foer, citing a review in Dialogues in Clinical Neuroscience by Zimmerman, 2013):** about 20% of patients with BPD had bipolar disorder; about 10–20% of patients with bipolar disorder were diagnosed with BPD. Although comorbidity rates are significant, in the vast majority of cases (80–90%) each disorder is diagnosed without the other [msg #25].
- **By bipolar type (Katya Klimochkina, citing articles on co-existing diagnoses):** about 20% of people with bipolar II receive a BPD diagnosis; about 10% of people with bipolar I have BPD [msg #131].
- **Childhood adversity in BPD (Lena Foer, "by various estimates"):** 30–90% of clients with BPD experienced childhood abuse, physical or sexualized violence and neglect [msg #47].
- **HiTOP consortium size:** about 70 scientists worldwide [msg #211].
- **Follow-up after starting medication:** usually 2–6 weeks on average (Elizaveta Fedorova) [msg #172, img #172].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- (!) CONTRADICTION: the comorbidity numbers differ slightly between authors. Lena Foer gives ~20% of BPD patients with bipolar disorder and 10–20% of bipolar patients with BPD [msg #25]; Klimochkina gives ~20% of bipolar II and ~10% of bipolar I patients with BPD [msg #131]. These may describe different samples/breakdowns; the source does not reconcile them.
- (!) CONTRADICTION: sleep. Klimochkina's text lists changes in sleep amount/quality as exclusive to bipolar disorder [msg #131], while her infographic puts "sleep disturbances" in the shared BPD+bipolar zone and only "reduced need for sleep and food" as bipolar-only [img #133]. Similarly, dissociation and paranoid thoughts appear as shared in the infographic, while depressive symptoms appear both as shared and (as "severe depressive symptoms") as bipolar-only [img #133].
- (!) CONTRADICTION: whether impulsivity, self-harm, emptiness (BPD) and dissociation (cPTSD) are disorder-specific or shared is itself disputed between sources, as Klimochkina notes [msg #196]; Lena Foer lists dissociation among features common to BPD and cPTSD [msg #47].
- The 30–90% childhood-adversity range in BPD is "by various estimates" — a very wide spread [msg #47].
- HiTOP's own limitations per the author: a dimensional diagnosis does not fit the present binary "switch" logic of diagnosis; traumatic stress may need its own subcategory because PTSD and BPD involve both internalizing and externalizing behaviour [msg #211, #212].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the HiTOP hierarchy picture is external and not transcribed [msg #212]; the first image of the cPTSD+BPD longread could not be saved, so whatever it showed is unavailable [article #202].
- The cPTSD + BPD analysis is Klimochkina's own descriptive reasoning, illustrated with first-person inner monologues rather than data; she notes there are "dozens more" nuances not covered [article #202].
- Klimochkina's cPTSD/BPD points are her own findings for a talk and book, explicitly "not the only" differences, with a promised continuation [msg #196].
- The psychiatrist checklist flags that complex diagnoses (bipolar, schizophrenia spectrum, personality disorders) should be presented as preliminary pending observation over time [msg #172, img #172].
