# Recognizing PTSD and complex PTSD

## What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's material on telling apart an ordinary (if severe) reaction to a terrible event and post-traumatic stress disorder, and PTSD from complex PTSD. It also covers traumatic invalidation as a form of emotional abuse that contributes to BPD and cPTSD, the "cascade" model of what happens in a person at the moment of terror, medical PTSD explained through the Enduring Somatic Threat model, and the connections between trauma, chronic pain and migraine. The source frames this as important because people often ask where the boundary lies ("Где грань между переживанием ужасного опыта и развитием ПТСР") and because the consequences — of invalidation, of trauma on the body — are what therapy (e.g. for BPD) actually works with [msg #110, #23].

This part adds Lena Foer's long guide to complex PTSD (кПТСР), published as a Telegraph longread linked from post #96. It covers how PTSD arose as a diagnosis, its three core symptoms, how Judith Herman's research produced the idea of complex PTSD, the ICD-11 symptom set, who develops cPTSD and under what conditions, traumatic invalidation as a cause in its own right, what therapy aims at and how the CBT branch does it, and three things worth knowing about the course of the disorder [msg #96, article #96].

The author's reason for writing: cPTSD is one of the young and "loud" diagnoses of modern psychiatry; it still does not exist in the USA and only recently appeared officially in the rest of the world, though it has been discussed since the 1980s. It is often confused with borderline personality disorder, is much argued about, and myths are already forming around it. She calls her text long "(но все равно слишком короткий для масштабов темы)" (*but still too short for the scale of the topic*) [msg #96].

**Relation to the existing knowledge file.** The existing file describes PTSD through Katya Klimochkina's four-cluster list plus additional symptoms (#110), and distinguishes cPTSD by added relationship difficulties, shame/guilt, worthlessness and emotion-regulation problems (#24, #195). This post **extends** that: it gives the historical "three whales" version of PTSD, the official ICD-11 cPTSD structure (3 + 3 symptoms), nine further common symptoms, the conditions and causes of cPTSD, the point that adults can develop it, the US/DSM position, and a treatment section. It **extends #23** (nine forms of invalidation) with the claim that traumatic invalidation by itself leads to the same trauma symptoms. It does not contradict the earlier material. The three-symptom PTSD core here and the four-cluster list in #110 are two framings of the same picture; the "negative changes in thinking and mood" cluster from #110 has no separate place among the three core symptoms here, and the guilt-type thoughts appear under re-experiencing instead [msg #96, article #96].

## Core ideas

- **A traumatic event is not yet PTSD.** A trauma reaction is acute distress that fades week by week; PTSD is a persistent reaction lasting more than a month that spreads into many areas of life (detail in *Trauma reaction vs PTSD*) [msg #110].
- **PTSD vs cPTSD is about the number/duration of traumatization.** PTSD typically follows a single event; complex PTSD comes from prolonged or repeated trauma and adds relationship difficulties, shame/guilt, worthlessness and emotion-regulation problems [msg #24, #195].
- **Invalidation is trauma too.** Repeated rejection, ignoring or denial of a person's thoughts and feelings is a form of emotional abuse that contributes (not as the only cause) to BPD and is discussed as a contributor to cPTSD [msg #23].
- **Trauma lives in the body.** Traumatic experience is often accompanied by somatic illness and chronic pain; the type of trauma, the degree of dissociation and emotion-regulation capacity shape how pain is felt [msg #165].
- **Medical PTSD breaks the "danger is over" logic.** When the threat came from one's own body, the source of danger does not disappear; the EST model describes an internal, ongoing threat and a threat system over-sensitized to bodily signals [msg #253].

- **PTSD came from 20th-century wars and was long understood only through soldiers and strangers' attacks.** For decades nobody linked it to children or to women who suffered domestic violence [article #96].
- **Complex PTSD = the three PTSD symptoms + three more.** These are emotion-regulation problems, negative self-perception and relationship problems. ICD-11 includes it; the US DSM does not. Its symptoms are usually more persistent and severe than those of ordinary PTSD (see *ICD-11 complex PTSD symptom model*) [article #96].
- **cPTSD is not only a childhood story.** Repeated or chained traumatic events cause it, and adults in trafficking, captivity or war zones can develop it [article #96].
- **There is no ranking of "serious enough" trauma.** Invalidation alone produces the same trauma symptoms. So the author says to look at both the event and the symptoms [article #96].
- **cPTSD maintains itself through avoidance.** It rarely passes on its own. Therapy goes in sequence: first emotion regulation, then trauma work, usually for about a year [article #96].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Nine forms of traumatic invalidation
Definition: "Травматическая инвалидация - это форма эмоционального насилия, при которой мысли, чувства и опыт человека неоднократно отвергаются, игнорируются или отрицаются" (*Traumatic invalidation is a form of emotional abuse in which a person's thoughts, feelings and experience are repeatedly rejected, ignored or denied*). The author (Lena Foer) lists nine forms, each with an example phrase [msg #23]:

1. **Blame (Обвинения)** — reproaches for things the person is not guilty of, or for which in principle nobody can be guilty. Example: “если бы не ты, мы бы сейчас все не разболелись” (*if it weren't for you, we wouldn't all have gotten sick now*) [msg #23].
2. **Control (Контроль)** — treatment implying the person is incompetent without supervision. Example: “сама ты не в состоянии сделать что-нибудь правильно” (*you're not capable of doing anything right on your own*) [msg #23].
3. **Denial of reality (Отрицание реальности)** — being told that one's perception of the facts is wrong. Example: “Только дураки в таких ситуациях плачут” (*Only fools cry in situations like this*) [msg #23].
4. **False interpretations (Ложные интерпретации)** — interpreting the person's behaviour or motives in a negative light. Example: “Ты специально это сделала, чтобы мать разозлить!” (*You did that on purpose to make your mother angry!*) [msg #23].
5. **Ignoring (Игнорирование)** — not paying attention to what the person says or does. Example: “Опять ты о своих хотелках, дай передохнуть” (*There you go again about your little wants, give me a break*) [msg #23].
6. **Emotional neglect (Эмоциональное пренебрежение)** — absence of a caring or loving response from close people. Example: “Ну что ты заладил, не до тебя” (*Why do you keep on about it, I have no time for you*) [msg #23].
7. **Criticism (Критика)** — reproaches, mockery, name-calling, insults. Example: “Ну что, неужели тебе хотя бы тройку поставили на контрольной?” (*So, did they really give you at least a C on the test?*) [msg #23].
8. **Unequal treatment (Неравное обращение)** — treating the person as less significant; discrimination based on personal traits. Example: “Отдай игрушку брату, он ее заслужил, а ты нет” (*Give the toy to your brother, he deserved it and you didn't*) [msg #23].
9. **Exclusion (Исключение)** — removing the person from, or refusing to invite them into, a group that matters to them. Example: “Мы едем всей семьей кататься на лыжах, но ты с нами не пойдешь” (*The whole family is going skiing, but you're not coming with us*) [msg #23].

Effects: invalidation can deeply affect a person's sense of dignity and self-esteem and can contribute to mental disorders such as borderline personality disorder; its contribution to complex PTSD is also discussed. The author stresses that invalidation is a contributing factor to BPD, **not the only cause** — but it makes its destructive contribution, and its consequences are specifically what is worked with in BPD therapy [msg #23].

### PTSD symptom clusters (how to recognize PTSD)
Katya Klimochkina defines PTSD as a stress disorder that develops after exposure to a traumatic event, and lists the symptoms by cluster [msg #110]:

**A. Intrusive symptoms (Навязчивые симптомы)**
1. **Flashbacks** — vivid, distressing memories of the event with the sense of reliving the experience [msg #110].
2. **Nightmares** — recurring, distressing dreams connected to the event [msg #110].
3. **Intrusive thoughts** — unwanted, distressing thoughts or images of the event that break into consciousness [msg #110].

**B. Avoidance symptoms (Симптомы избегания)**
4. **Avoiding triggers** — avoiding people, places, activities or situations that remind of the event [msg #110].
5. **Emotional numbness** — feeling emotionally detached or numb; avoiding feelings or conversations connected to the event [msg #110].
6. **Avoiding thoughts or feelings** — trying to push away memories, thoughts or feelings tied to the event [msg #110].

**C. Negative changes in thinking and mood**
7. **Negative thoughts** — persistent, distorted beliefs about oneself, others or the world, e.g. "Это моя вина" (*It's my fault*) or "Мир опасен" (*The world is dangerous*) [msg #110].
8. **Loss of interest** — in activities or hobbies that used to bring pleasure [msg #110].
9. **Detachment** — feeling emotionally cut off from others, a sense of alienation [msg #110].
10. **Difficulty remembering details** — trouble recalling important aspects of the traumatic event [msg #110].

**D. Changes in arousal and reactivity**
11. **Hyperarousal** — constant nervousness, heightened vigilance, being easily startled [msg #110].
12. **Irritability or anger** — frequent irritability, outbursts of anger or aggressive behaviour [msg #110].
13. **Difficulty concentrating** — trouble focusing, concentrating or making decisions [msg #110].
14. **Sleep disturbances** — trouble falling asleep, staying asleep, or restless sleep [msg #110].

**E. Further possible symptoms**
15. **Physical symptoms** — headaches, stomach aches or other unexplained body pains [msg #110].
16. **Guilt and shame** — about the traumatic event or its consequences [msg #110].
17. **Relationship difficulties** — including social isolation or conflicts [msg #110].
18. **Substance misuse** — using psychoactive substances to cope with or muffle distressing symptoms [msg #110].

**Threshold:** symptoms persist **more than one month** and significantly impair daily life [msg #110].

What counts as a traumatic event: directly witnessing or experiencing an event that threatens life, safety or physical integrity, to which the person may respond with intense fear, horror, helplessness or extreme arousal. Examples given: terrorist attacks, natural disasters, accidents, physical or sexualized violence, combat, sudden loss of a loved one, witnessing violence [msg #110].

### Trauma reaction vs PTSD — key differences
1. **Experiencing a traumatic event** is a one-time event in which people react to an immediate threat or danger. It can cause temporary distress or emotional shock that gradually decreases: "С каждым днем и неделей - вам лучше, спокойней, меньше мыслей о пережитом. Появляется жизнь "вне травмы"" (*With every day and week you feel better, calmer, fewer thoughts about what happened. A life "outside the trauma" appears*) [msg #110].
2. **Developing PTSD** means a long, persistent reaction to the trauma, often reaching beyond the event itself: the event is over, but the experience of trauma remains a month or more later, and the symptoms start affecting different areas of life — from sleep to communicating with others or leaving the house. "Новый день вызывает новую боль и со временем лучше не становится" (*Each new day brings new pain and it does not get better with time*) [msg #110].

Conclusion of the author: taking part in a traumatic event can cause distressing reactions, while PTSD involves persistent and exhausting symptoms that substantially affect everyday life. The practical test is the trajectory (gradual improvement vs persistence) and the duration (over a month) plus impact on functioning [msg #110].

### PTSD vs complex PTSD
- **PTSD** is spoken of when the consequences of trauma persist over a long time; it usually develops after a **single** traumatic event [msg #24, #195].
- **Complex PTSD (кПТСР)** is spoken of when there were **multiple episodes** of traumatization — prolonged or repeated trauma [msg #24, #195].
- Beyond the PTSD symptoms (memories, anxiety, avoidance), cPTSD adds: difficulties in relationships, persistent shame or guilt, and a sense of one's own worthlessness [msg #195]. The cPTSD group program described in #24 is aimed at people who, in addition to PTSD symptoms, have difficulties with **emotion regulation, a negative self-image and problems building relationships** — i.e. the same added cluster [msg #24].
- Why these extra symptoms appear: prolonged trauma disrupts the development of a sense of safety and stability [msg #195].
- Link to BPD: people with borderline personality disorder fairly often have experience of psychological trauma, in many cases repeated, in forms including physical, sexual or emotional abuse; such experiences can have a long-term effect on the ability to regulate emotions, form stable relationships and cope with stress [msg #24].

### Threat-response cascade model (six stages)
In the authors' book (Katya Klimochkina's team) the **cascade model of reactions** is used to understand what happens to a person at the moment of terror and to answer the question "почему не убежал?" (*why didn't they run away?*). The model treats threat reactions as assembled into one chain of steps that we pass through when we sense danger. The cascade runs in **six stages**, and in any specific case only some of its elements may be visible [msg #208].

### Enduring Somatic Threat Model (EST) — medical PTSD
Purpose: explaining PTSD after life-threatening medical events (heart attack, stroke, traumatic childbirth, amputation, severe illness, or other life-threatening situations). The author says there are many models of medical trauma but one should start with EST [msg #253].

1. **The logic of "ordinary" PTSD:** something dangerous happened → the danger ended → the psyche keeps reacting as though the danger still exists → hence all PTSD symptoms, from the sense of threat to avoidance [msg #253].
2. **Why medical trauma breaks this logic:** the event ended, but the source of danger has not disappeared — "ты все еще в своем теле" (*you are still in your body*). The scheme "it's all over and there's no more danger" does not work; the author ironically calls this a "new level" of PTSD — medical PTSD [msg #253].
3. **Internal, ongoing threat:** unlike classical trauma, where the threat is usually external (accident, assault, war), in medical trauma the threat can be internal and extended in time. After external trauma a person can gradually return to an environment where nothing dangerous happens; after medical trauma one cannot hide from the threat source — "тело всегда с собой" (*the body is always with you*) — and the body constantly reports things: the heart beats harder, the chest tightens, dizziness, pain appears, breathing changes. This substantially changes what PTSD and its treatment look like [msg #253].
4. **What the person may continue to experience** (making the threat internal): pain; bodily sensations; symptoms of the illness; limitations; fear of deterioration; consequences of treatment [msg #253].
5. **Same sensory signal as the original threat:** the body presents the same type of sensory signal that in the past was associated with real and uncontrollable threat [msg #253].
6. **Somatic threat cues:** for someone without such experience, most of these sensations attract no attention at all (the author invites the reader to notice their breathing or heartbeat now — probably felt only after reading the sentence, while before it everything "ran" by itself). After a threatening medical event, bodily sensations, pain and changes in how the body works can become **somatic threat cues** — signals reminding of danger. The author notes this is very close in meaning to somatoform disorders and chronic pain [msg #253].
7. **Sensitization loop** (per EST, after medical trauma the person may become more sensitive to bodily signals): sensation → "something is wrong" → increased arousal and attention to the body → even more noticeable sensations → memories of the threatening event [msg #253].
8. **Real ongoing vulnerability:** the illness can recur; a symptom can genuinely need attention; the person may need exams, medication or repeated treatment. So medical trauma creates a strange situation: one lives simultaneously with a real, continuing bodily vulnerability and with a threat system that has become too sensitive to that body's signals after event X [msg #253].
9. **Reframing medical PTSD:** it is not quite a story about the psyche failing to understand that danger has ended. Sometimes the problem is that the danger really has not ended, and the person lives with the belief "моё тело постоянно может сообщить мне, что сейчас опять начинается что-то опасное" (*my body can at any moment tell me that something dangerous is starting again*) [msg #253].

The author notes the topic does not end with one model [msg #253].

### Three core symptoms of PTSD ("three whales")
The details of the diagnosis changed over time, but PTSD rested, "как три кита" (*like three whales*), on three symptoms [article #96]:

1. **A continuous sense of threat spread across life.** The author's example: "Сидишь ты у себя дома, в Висконсине, ешь суп, Вьетнам в прошлом" (*You're sitting at home in Wisconsin eating soup, Vietnam is in the past*). Yet you feel that if you relax, someone will attack you from behind. This leads to poor sleep, constant tension, constant tiredness, irritability and bouts of aggression [article #96].
2. **Re-experiencing symptoms** — the "Vietnam flashbacks" famous in meme culture. They take several forms [article #96]:
   - *Classic flashbacks*: a sudden cut back into the trauma. You were mowing the lawn, a sharp sound rings out, and you again see the terrible accident in which you almost died and live through everything you felt then. In fact a car just drove by, you are still in the middle of the lawn, sweating, heart pounding, trying to catch your breath.
   - *Flashbacks without images* — emotions only.
   - *Intrusive thoughts* about what happened or about one's role in it: “я виноват, что выжил” (*it's my fault I survived*), “могла ли я это предотвратить? Что я сделала не так” (*could I have prevented it? What did I do wrong*), “почему это случилось со мной” (*why did this happen to me*). Such thoughts can haunt a person for years.
   - *Nightmares*: re-experiencing that happens in sleep rather than while awake.
3. **Avoidance**, which in PTSD becomes "second nature". Anything that recalls the people, things or events of the trauma, even thoughts and emotions, can plunge one back into the past and so seems dangerous in itself. A long list of things to stay away from builds up: crowds, the military uniform in the wardrobe, loud shouting, sharp sounds, familiar smells. Even things that used to be an important part of life drop out, such as going with the family to watch holiday fireworks. "Так избегание обкрадывает человеческие жизни" (*This is how avoidance robs people's lives*) [article #96].
   - **Safety behaviour (охранительное поведение)** comes "as a bonus" to avoidance. These are actions that are supposed to ward off the threat; in essence they are also avoidance, only less obvious. The author's examples: putting pepper spray in your pocket "just in case" when going to the supermarket; carrying a blister pack of tranquilizers when meeting friends in case emotions overwhelm you; mentally planning what you will do, and what you will hit with, if an intruder climbs in while you smoke on the balcony [article #96].

Historical caveat the author draws: all these examples involve war or, at most, assaults by strangers and accidents, "потому что именно так и понимали ПТСР десятилетиями" (*because that is exactly how PTSD was understood for decades*) [article #96].

### ICD-11 complex PTSD symptom model
In ICD-11, cPTSD consists of the three PTSD symptoms above (avoidance, re-experiencing, constant sense of threat) **plus three additional symptoms** [article #96]:

1. **Problems with emotion regulation.** Emotions are very strong, hard to bear and can swing sharply, and there is a feeling that they could destroy you. The reaction to them varies:
   - sometimes constant emotional numbness;
   - sometimes impulsive or self-harming behaviour and suicidal thoughts;
   - chaos in life, or the opposite, desperate attempts to control absolutely everything;
   - trying to drown emotions out with addictions;
   - or simply a life full of emotional pain [article #96].
2. **Negative self-perception.** It comes from the trauma and is tied to it. Usually there is a lot of self-hatred and self-disgust, and a sense of guilt (for what happened, or for everything in general). There is helplessness (at the moment of trauma and always), and shame about oneself. The feeling of being bad may need no justification in the moment and simply follows the person through many different contexts and situations [article #96].
3. **Problems building relationships.** The person's role in relationships may go to one of two extremes [article #96]:
   - making partners into rescuers, relying on and depending on them completely and giving up one's own agency (“я ничего не могу без нее” — *I can't do anything without her*);
   - or the opposite: not trusting others and finding it unsafe to open up, show feelings or talk about oneself. Relationships stay superficial, ideally with people with whom nothing "serious" could come of it.

Complex PTSD symptoms are usually **more persistent and more severe** than those of ordinary PTSD [article #96].

### Other common symptoms of complex PTSD (nine items)
Beyond the core six, the author lists these as common in cPTSD [article #96]:

1. **Dissociation.** It may arise in response to any threat, or only to events that recall the trauma.
2. **Distorted perception of the perpetrator.** The person who caused harm may seem all-powerful. The relationship with them may feel incredibly important, or revenge on them may seem the most important thing in life.
3. **Suicidal thoughts and attempts.**
4. **Alcohol and drug abuse.**
5. **Symptoms of depression.**
6. **Psychotic symptoms.** These include suspiciousness, paranoia, and unusual or extremely intense ideas that do not fit reality.
7. **Physical health complaints.**
8. **Loss of a sense of meaning in life and of faith**, whether religious or simply faith in one's own future.
9. **A feeling of despair and hopelessness.**

### The "recipe" for complex PTSD (four conditions)
For both PTSD and cPTSD, diagnostic criterion "A", the starting point of diagnosis, is the presence of traumatic episodes. For cPTSD these are either **repeated episodes of trauma** or **many different terrible events forming a chain** in a person's life. Generalizing, the author gives a rough "recipe" [article #96]:

1. **You are vulnerable.** This can be because you are a minor, have no savings of your own, or have nobody willing to stand up for or protect you. It can be because you depend in some way on the perpetrators, or because, being neurodivergent, you read social signals less well.
2. **You cannot simply get out of the situation or protect yourself in it**, and you feel helpless before the threat.
3. **The situation lasts a long time or repeats.**
4. **What you face is an attack on your identity, dignity, humanity or freedom**, and there is a threat to your life, safety or bodily integrity.

## Detailed insights

### Ten most common questions about complex PTSD — and simple answers
The post #195 answers the first three questions and, remarking that if PTSD could (barely) fit in one post, cPTSD "это точно лонгрид" (*is definitely a longread*), continues in a Telegraph longread co-authored by Katya Klimochkina and the channel @bpdflowers. The post and the article together give all ten questions and answers, written in the second person as a client would ask them [msg #195, article #195]:

1. **"Why am I constantly tense or feel in danger?"** — The brain has become used to staying in "threat mode" (режим угрозы) as a way of protecting itself. The feeling of tension and danger comes from the nervous system having become hypersensitive to signs of threat — even in objectively safe situations. This mechanism often forms after trauma, when the brain starts mistakenly perceiving neutral or minor signals as potential threats; e.g. a loud sound, a sharp gesture or a tense tone can trigger the fight-or-flight response [msg #195, article #195].
2. **"How is PTSD different from complex PTSD?"** — Answered in *PTSD vs complex PTSD* above (single event vs prolonged/repeated trauma; added relationship difficulties, shame/guilt, worthlessness, because prolonged trauma disrupts the development of a sense of safety and stability) [msg #195, article #195].
3. **"Why is it hard for me to trust people and build relationships?"** — Traumatic experience can disrupt the perception of safety in relationships, making trust hard to establish. Survivors may fear being abandoned or betrayed, which leads to strong anxiety and hypersensitivity to possible signs of rejection. This can show up as avoiding closeness and difficulty setting healthy boundaries, because the trauma shapes the perception of other people as potential sources of threat. In response a person may either withdraw or strive for excessive closeness in order to "protect" themselves from the presumed threat [msg #195, article #195].
4. **"Why do I have emotional outbursts or feel 'empty'?"** — Emotional dysregulation in complex PTSD is often linked to a constant sense of threat and the absence of effective strategies for processing emotions. When the trauma has not been properly processed, this can lead to sharp mood swings that are felt as emotional outbursts. The inability to process emotions adequately leads to strong mood swings and to a feeling of inner emptiness — so outbursts and emptiness are two faces of the same unprocessed-emotion problem [article #195].
5. **"Why do I blame myself for what happened to me?"** — Self-blame in cPTSD is often an attempt to make sense of the trauma and to regain the sense of control that was absent during the event itself. When a person goes through repeated trauma or prolonged abuse, they may start searching for an explanation that makes what is happening more understandable and controllable. Thoughts like "если бы я поступил по-другому, этого не случилось бы" (*if I had acted differently, this wouldn't have happened*) are an attempt to get rid of the powerlessness that arises at the moment of trauma. Such thoughts intensify guilt because they create the illusion that the event could have been prevented had the person behaved differently. This creates a cycle of self-blame that prevents the emotional state from normalizing [article #195].
6. **"How do I stop intrusive memories and flashbacks?"** — These memories are the brain's way of trying to "process" the trauma. To stop them, what matters is learning to manage one's reaction to them. What works best is creating a physical or emotional "anchor" (якорь) to the present moment — e.g. touching an object, bringing attention back to the present, describing the objects in the space where you are right now — to restore the feeling of safety. In the long term, imaginal exposure (экспозиция в воображении) helps [article #195].
7. **"Why do I feel detached, as if watching my life from the outside?"** — This state is called dissociation — the mind's protective reaction to strong emotions. The feeling of detachment or of watching one's life from the side often arises as a defence against the trauma experienced. Dissociation helps a person cope with excessive emotional tension by letting them "switch off" from the pain. It can be triggered by emotional overload or by undigested memories the brain cannot process at once [article #195].
8. **"How do I stop repeating self-harming behaviour?"** — Self-harm is often formed as a way of coping with emotional pain. To stop it, one needs to learn to replace it with healthier ways of self-regulation, in this order: (a) start by **identifying the triggers** that provoke the urge to harm oneself; (b) **develop alternative strategies** — distraction techniques (e.g. physical activity, or using intense sensations such as squeezing ice in the palms) or relaxation methods (deep breathing, meditation); (c) **apply these strategies regularly** — this gradually reduces the intensity of self-harm impulses and teaches coping with emotional pain more constructively [article #195].
9. **"Why do I keep ending up in unhealthy relationships?"** — The trauma may have shaped one's beliefs about oneself and other people. Ending up in unhealthy relationships in cPTSD is often linked to repeating behaviour patterns based on traumatic experience, in which love or attachment is associated with illness or instability ("с болезнью или нестабильностью"). To change this, one should start analysing past relationships and identify which behaviour patterns lead to these cycles repeating [article #195].
10. **"Is recovery from cPTSD possible, and what does it take?"** — Yes: CBT, DBT, schema therapy, exposure, MBT (mentalization-based therapy) or other scientifically proven psychotherapy methods allow one "оставить прошлое в прошлое" (*to leave the past in the past* [sic: the source has "в прошлое"]) [article #195].

The article closes with a meme image (English text): "me when i'm actively experiencing trauma — I'M OKAY." vs "me years later when i actually process what happened — NO, I'M NOT." — contrasting saying one is fine while trauma is happening with admitting one is not fine years later, once what happened is actually processed; the article adds no commentary to it [article #195].

### PTSD and the body: which pains and somatic problems
Traumatic experiences are often accompanied by somatic illnesses and chronic pain, due to the physiological and psychological consequences of trauma and stress. The source lists [msg #165]:
- Muscle tension.
- Headaches or migraine attacks.
- Changes in pain and sensitivity: heightened pain sensitivity or reduced pain perception, as well as numbness or tingling.
- Gastrointestinal problems: digestive problems, e.g. irritable bowel syndrome.
- Cardiovascular symptoms: rapid heartbeat or high blood pressure.
- Dizziness and fainting or a light-headed feeling.
- Breathing problems: hyperventilation and shortness of breath.
- Skin problems: rash, hives or eczema may develop or worsen.
- Impaired immune function: PTSD can weaken the immune system, making people more susceptible to illness.

### Trauma type shapes pain perception
So far no substantial differences in pain perception between people with and without PTSD have been found; what matters is "что привело к травме" (*what led to the trauma*) [msg #165]:
- **Military PTSD** — may show a **raised pain threshold** (feeling less pain), possibly due to dissociation or acquired stoicism (the ability to endure pain without reacting); noticed e.g. in war veterans [msg #165].
- **Accident-related PTSD** — shows a **lowered pain threshold** (pain felt more strongly), possibly linked to anxiety and pain catastrophizing: pain feels much worse than objective parameters indicate. Why: one is afraid it will hurt, so focus goes to those sensations — "Где фокус - там и усиление в ощущениях" (*Where the focus is, there the sensations intensify*) [msg #165].

### The role of dissociation in pain
Intense dissociation — disconnection from one's own emotions and feeling separate from oneself — can **reduce** pain perception. Those with less pronounced dissociation may feel pain more intensely, often in connection with anxiety (as in accident-related PTSD) [msg #165].

### BPD and pain: the paradox
In borderline personality disorder, which is associated with early-childhood trauma, a known phenomenon is a psychophysiologically **higher pain threshold** on one hand and an increased **risk of chronic pain** on the other. This was explained by reduced body awareness and suppression of emotional experience. A link was also found between more blunted situational pain and the risk of chronic pain in BPD [msg #165].
Why: one hypothesis is that this raises sympathetic activity and allostatic load, which in many ways is a risk factor for chronic pain. The author's "translation": reduced sensitivity to acute pain results from stress-induced analgesia or dissociation and changes in how the brain regulates the affective-motivational component of pain; heightened sensitivity to chronic pain is explained as an **inability to self-regulate emotionally** [msg #165].
A similar pain experience is observed in depression: one feels so bad emotionally that this "моральная боль" (*moral/emotional pain*) jumps over to the body [msg #165].

### PTSD and migraine
- About 54% of people with PTSD experience persistent headaches and chronic migraine attacks, according to research the author cites [msg #165].
- A study of PTSD and migraine frequency among Hurricane Katrina survivors found a link between **intrusive PTSD symptoms** (nightmares, flashbacks, intrusive memories or thoughts) and **increased headache frequency** [msg #165].
- Conversely, having migraine episodes is itself a risk factor for developing PTSD — at least according to conclusions of Japanese researchers [msg #165].

### Origin of the PTSD diagnosis
PTSD owes its existence to the wars of the 20th century. By World War I, the loudness of the guns, the variety of weapons and the scale of fighting had reached levels the human psyche coped with badly. European psychiatric clinics filled with veterans back from the front who shied away from military uniforms, shook, cried and could barely stand. Film already existed but ethics in psychiatry did not yet, so recordings of how it looked survive (the author links a YouTube video of such footage). The diagnoses then were "shell shock" or "war neurosis". World War II and the Vietnam War added to psychiatrists' understanding, and the PTSD diagnosis gradually took shape. For a long time the idea that it could apply to children or to women who suffered domestic violence was entirely absent [article #96].

### Judith Herman and the birth of cPTSD
The author stresses that the American researcher Judith Herman's idea was unusual for its time (the 1980s). Herman studied how the experience of incest affects children. She found that children who suffered sexualized violence from parents had the same PTSD symptoms as combat veterans, but not only those. The children's symptoms were broader: what happened did not just echo in their memories and reactions, "оно влияло на их личность" (*it affected their personality*) [article #96].

### Why there is no cPTSD in the USA
Although Herman was American and worked in America, the US uses its own classification of mental disorders, the DSM. Unlike the international one, the DSM has never had cPTSD and still does not. Meeting after meeting, American scientists agree that cPTSD is simply complicated PTSD and that no separate diagnosis is needed. Therefore therapies for cPTSD in the USA (the author's example: "DBT PE") are PTSD therapies, "ну а как еще" (*well, how else*) [article #96].

### Complex trauma is not only childhood trauma
There is a legend that complex trauma develops only in those who went through such events in childhood. The author says this is not so: trafficking, captivity, being in combat zones and other prolonged traumatizing events can lead to cPTSD in adults [article #96].

### Typical causes of cPTSD (non-exhaustive list)
The author gives an approximate list and stresses it is "не исчерпывающий!" (*not exhaustive!*) [article #96]:
1. Slavery.
2. Prostitution.
3. Captivity.
4. Torture.
5. Bullying.
6. Prolonged sexualized or physical violence.
7. Repeatedly witnessing violence against others.
8. Abuse or neglect of children.

Most people with cPTSD have a **combination of several types of events**. The author's composite example: bullying at school, sexualized violence from a distant relative at the summer dacha, and physical violence against the mother at home [article #96].

### "My trauma isn't a real trauma" and traumatic invalidation
Many people with cPTSD ("МНОГИХ", *MANY*, in the author's capitals) are sure their trauma is not as much of a trauma as other people's: "those people have trauma, mine is nothing". The author says there is no classification of the "seriousness of reasons for trauma". Someone may recover from torture in a prison colony in a couple of months. Someone else may live for decades with agonizing flashbacks from what others would call “ну, отец лупил, с кем не бывает” (*well, father beat me, who hasn't been through that*) [article #96].

On top of this, **traumatic invalidation**, which is when you are told you made up all your experiences, leads to **the same trauma symptoms** as everything listed above. This adds a causal claim to the nine forms of invalidation described in #23. The practical conclusion: "Вот поэтому важно смотреть на травматическое событие - и на симптомы" (*That is why it's important to look at the traumatic event — and at the symptoms*) [article #96].

### Six shared tasks of complex PTSD therapy
There are many therapy approaches for cPTSD, but they solve similar tasks [article #96]:

1. **Distinguishing present from past.** If you notice when a flashback has started, recognise it as a flashback, and see that then and now are not the same, part of the suffering goes away.
2. **Changing the relationship to memories.** The aim is that they stop being a source of threat and become simply memory, even if of very bad events.
3. **Changing the relationship to safe people, places and objects that frighten because they recall the trauma.** This is work on avoidance. Its purpose is to gradually bring back into life what feels important but is unavailable because of avoidance.
4. **Building the ability to notice, name and regulate emotional and bodily states**, and to understand what caused them.
5. **Growing self-compassion and self-sympathy.** This means fewer self-critical thoughts and moving away from the feeling of helplessness.
6. **Learning to build close and healthy relationships.**

### CBT route: DBT skills training plus prolonged exposure
In the cognitive-behavioural branch where the author works, these tasks are addressed through two lines of work [article #96]:

1. **DBT skills training (тренинг навыков диалектико-поведенческой терапии).** It helps the client cope better with crisis states, regulate their own emotions, understand what is happening in the moment, handle their states and build healthier relationships with others.
2. **The prolonged exposure protocol**, which solves three tasks:
   - **Changing the relationship to memories.** The client returns to them in a safe setting. Gradually they conclude that the memories are not dangerous, do not destroy them and carry no threat, and the memories stop causing even strong emotions. What used to give no peace becomes "просто чем-то, что было и прошло" (*simply something that was and has passed*).
   - **Giving up avoidance.** People gradually learn, in safe conditions, to bring back into their lives what they would like to have but which is very frightening because of its association with the trauma. Moving step by step and in measured doses toward frightening (but safe!) things, they can stop fearing them altogether and make life richer and filled with meaning.
   - **Processing trauma-related emotions.** Discussing the traumatic events with the therapist, the client gradually comes to see them differently. Perhaps for the first time they realise they were not to blame, that there is nothing to be ashamed of and no reason to hate themselves. This builds a healthier and more self-compassionate view of what happened.

**Sequence and duration:** this all happens in order. First clients learn to regulate their states and cope with emotions at least a little, and only then move to trauma work. As a rule it is long-term therapy, taking **about a year** [article #96].

### Three more things to know about cPTSD
1. **Comorbidity.** cPTSD is rarely the only diagnosis. Other disorders often accompany it: anxiety, depressive, sleep and personality disorders and addictions. All of them complicate diagnosis and worsen quality of life and the prognosis in therapy [article #96].
2. **Self-maintenance through avoidance.** Both PTSD and cPTSD sustain themselves. The more one avoids thoughts, memories, emotions and safe but trauma-reminding things, the stronger the belief that the threat is real. That is why cPTSD rarely passes on its own [article #96].
3. **Where to get help.** It is best to go to a therapist who works with cPTSD, but self-help is also possible (see *Tools and resources mentioned*) [article #96].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- Invalidation is a contributing factor to BPD, explicitly **not the only cause**; its role in cPTSD is phrased as "говорят о вкладе" (*it is said to contribute*) [msg #23].
- Pain findings are hedged: dissociation and stoicism "possibly" explain the raised threshold in military PTSD; anxiety/catastrophizing "possibly" the lowered one in accident PTSD; the BPD mechanism is "an assumption" [msg #165].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the six stages of the cascade model are not described in the provided source [msg #208].
- The cPTSD Q&A answers are deliberately "simple answers" (простые ответы); mechanisms such as self-blame as an attempt to regain control are stated by the authors without cited studies [article #195].
- EST is presented as a starting model; the author says many models of medical trauma exist and the subject does not end with one [msg #253].

- **Diagnostic status differs by system.** cPTSD is in ICD-11 but not in the US DSM, where scientists consider it complicated PTSD. The author presents this as a fact about the field, not as her own position [article #96].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the author names "DBT PE" as an example of US cPTSD therapies without explaining it. It plausibly refers to DBT combined with prolonged exposure (the combination she describes for her own branch), but the source does not spell this out [article #96].
- (!) UNCLEAR: in the source, the three prolonged-exposure tasks are numbered 3–5, continuing the two-item list of work lines. They are sub-items of point 2 (prolonged exposure), not additional lines of work, and are reproduced that way here [article #96].
- The list of causes is explicitly "not exhaustive". The "recipe" is introduced with "примерно" (*roughly*) [article #96].
- The author hedges her own scope: the text is "still too short for the scale of the topic" [msg #96].
