# Self-image, identity and emptiness in BPD

## What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's posts on the self in borderline personality disorder (ПРЛ, BPD): chronic inner emptiness as a symptom (from a study the channel retells), the shape and causes of BPD's "low unstable self-esteem", why a quiet and predictable life can feel unbearable to people with BPD so that crisis feels safer, and a book chapter arguing that a disturbed self ("нарушение самости") is the core of BPD. It also includes a comparative overview of how self-perception changes in depression, bipolar disorder, complex PTSD and personality disorders. According to the source, emptiness is closely tied to impulsive acts, self-harm, suicidal behaviour and social problems, and if a broken sense of self is the core of BPD, it explains why the other symptoms appear — they are secondary [msg #6, #197].

This part adds one post by Lena Foer and the Telegraph longread it links to, "Поиски идентичности: откуда берется чувство "Я" и как его развить" ("Searching for identity: where the sense of 'I' comes from and how to develop it"). The post continues a series of posts on self-esteem and goes one level deeper, to what self-esteem grows from: the sense of identity, the feeling of "I". It explains what the "I" is, what self-awareness is made of, why people with BPD have biological reasons to struggle with finding their "I", and what can be done to grow identity deliberately [msg #87, article #87].

**Relation to the existing topic file:** the earlier posts describe the *disturbed self* in BPD — its signs, its place as the proposed "core" of BPD (#197), chronic emptiness (#6) and low unstable self-esteem (#85, #86). This part **extends** them in three ways: (1) it places identity *beneath* self-esteem ("На него опирается и самооценка" — "self-esteem rests on it as well"); (2) it adds a brain-based explanation (late-maturing neocortex, posterior cingulate and left medial prefrontal cortex, cortex–hippocampus connectivity), which differs from the "core" chapter's neurophysiological hypothesis that a fragmented self reduces prefrontal control (#197) — there the self disturbance is the cause and prefrontal control the effect, here brain differences are presented as a cause of identity difficulty; and (3) it is the first item in the topic giving **practical strategies** for building identity, rather than describing the disturbance. It also echoes the "core" chapter's finding that the self can change: the longread says identity can be helped "при любых состояниях (в том числе и ПРЛ)" ("in any condition, including BPD") [msg #87, article #87].

## Core ideas

- **Chronic emptiness is disconnection.** In the study the channel retells, people with BPD experience chronic emptiness/numbness as a feeling of being cut off from themselves and from others, linked to dissatisfaction and aimlessness; a clearer identity, functioning relationships and meaningful work are hypothesized to reduce it (detail in *Two sources of chronic emptiness*) [msg #6].
- **Emptiness drives impulsive behaviour.** Because emptiness can feel unbearable, people with BPD may act impulsively, self-harm or behave suicidally to "fill" it or to find any alternative to it [msg #6].
- **Low self-esteem in BPD has a specific shape** — a chronic, pervasive sense of one's own inadequacy, rooted in a fundamental "something is wrong with me", with unrealistic standards, a focus on flaws, comparison and vulnerability to criticism (see *Five features*) [msg #85].
- **Its causes are developmental and self-reinforcing** — adverse childhood experience, invalidation, rejection, emotional and relationship difficulties, perceived criticism, black-and-white thinking and self-sabotage (see *Eight causes*) [msg #86].
- **Stability itself is a trigger.** For people with BPD a calm life can provoke anxiety, emptiness and identity disorientation, so crisis feels safer and more familiar (see *Six patterns triggered by stability*) [msg #91].
- **A disturbed self may be the core of BPD.** A book chapter the author read proposes that BPD's many presentations rest on a broken sense of self; this factor was the most stable over a year of treatment, yet it did strengthen with proper therapy — "this core can be changed" [msg #197].
- **Self-perception differs by disorder** and can hint to clinicians where to look, though it cannot alone justify a diagnosis (see *Self-perception across mental disorders*) [msg #64].

- **Identity is the ground of self-esteem.** Everyone has a unique "I", formed by thoughts, feelings, memories and relationships; it makes us individual and separates us from everyone else, and self-esteem rests on it. People with BPD (and not only them) often have trouble finding their "I" and feeling it as something stable [msg #87, article #87].
- **Facts about yourself are not yet an identity.** Separate scattered answers about oneself are not the same as a firm inner certainty about what one is; the "magic dust" that integrates them is self-awareness (see *What the "I" consists of*) [article #87].
- **Self-awareness has four building blocks**, only some of which come "by default" (see *Four building blocks*) [article #87].
- **In BPD there are biological reasons for difficulty finding the "I"**, but identity can still be worked on (see *Three-part brain model*) [article #87].
- **Identity can be grown deliberately** through mindfulness, metacognitive thinking and strengthening autobiographical memory (see *Three ways to assemble a self*) [article #87].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Two sources of chronic emptiness (from the study retold in #6)
The channel summarizes a study of chronic emptiness as a BPD symptom (BPD and Emotion Dysregulation journal, linked in the post). Background: inner emptiness is one of the DSM-5 symptoms of BPD and is understood as a component of unstable self-identity, alongside self-criticism and dissociative experiences. Recently, researchers have broadened the concept to include a sense of detachment and disconnection both from oneself and from other people [msg #6].

1. **Disconnection from oneself.** According to the study, emptiness arises from a sense that there is no identity, or that identity is unstable, plus difficulties with self-direction, values and goals. Disconnection from oneself and the resulting emptiness often lead to feelings — and the realization — that life is aimless [msg #6].
2. **Disconnection from others.** Another possible source of chronic emptiness. It may be linked to distorted ideas about other people, to experiencing oneself as dysfunctional, and to the fact that positive social experience is hard to take in, whereas negative experience is absorbed easily. If so, chronic emptiness may stem from a deficit in the representation of "self in relation to others" ("дефицита представления "себя по отношению к другим"") [msg #6].

**Proposed mechanism (an assumption the post reports):** people with BPD feel strong emotions, are emotionally sensitive, their emotions usually last long and are hard to regulate. Difficulty understanding and tolerating inner experiences leads to suppressing them, and suppression leads to inner emptiness. Hence people with BPD may engage in maladaptive behaviour to "fill" the emptiness — which is why chronic emptiness is associated with impulsivity, self-harm and suicidal behaviour. Emptiness can seem unbearable, and people act impulsively to find at least some alternative [msg #6].

**Protective factors:** the new finding of this particular study is that engagement in some activity helps suppress or distract from experiencing emptiness. Involvement in a profession and in relationships can serve as a protective buffer against disconnection from oneself and others. The hypothesis is that a better understanding of one's own identity, functional relationships and work that gives meaning reduce the intensity of the feeling [msg #6].

**Paradox:** many people find the feeling of emptiness disturbing, yet deliberately induce it in order to endure stress — as an attempt to regulate strong emotions and prevent behavioural disturbances [msg #6].

### Five features of "low unstable self-esteem" in BPD
Katya Klimochkina notes that "low self-esteem" is formally an everyday concept ("житейское понятие"), and that self-esteem issues differ between people and disorders. In BPD it shows up as a chronic and pervasive feeling of one's own inadequacy, negative self-perception, fluctuations in understanding oneself and one's identity, plus problems with self-respect and self-criticism. What lies underneath — five elements [msg #85]:

1. **Feeling of inferiority (чувство неполноценности).** It is about a fundamental "со мной что-то не так" ("something is wrong with me"). The person with BPD experiences themselves as damaged and inferior in their very essence; the belief is deep and solid. They are convinced they are broken, defective and that nothing will help — and, the author writes, this self-feeling is "просто отличный антидот для любого антидепрессанта" ("simply an excellent antidote to any antidepressant"). One touch of this deep abyss of self-inadequacy can secure a day or two (in a good case) of self-torment, distress and suffering [msg #85].
2. **Unrealistic standards.** People with BPD may feel they must excel in every area of life — an impossible and exhausting expectation of oneself. Failing to meet these standards further feeds the sense of inadequacy [msg #85].
3. **Focus on flaws.** Analogy: if you stub your little toe, all your attention goes to the pain and switching away is hard. If the "toe" is your supposed weaknesses and shortcomings, this focus leads to self-criticism and self-condemnation — a closed circle of suffering [msg #85].
4. **Comparative view.** Trying to "exit" that circle, a person finds someone to compare with — in Instagram stories or among acquaintances — to reassure themselves that they're not so bad after all, since someone else is worse. The author argues comparison is rarely in our favour, especially when we do it ourselves: "Сравнивая себя с другим - мы всегда проиграем(" ("Comparing ourselves with another, we always lose") [msg #85].
5. **Vulnerability to criticism.** A consequence of the above: feedback may be perceived and interpreted as an attack on self-worth, even when it is constructive. Fragility and sensitivity make direct communication almost impossible, because any hint of threat to that fragility switches on defence, and intense emotions join the fight [msg #85].

### Eight causes of low self-esteem in BPD
The follow-up post ("where do the legs grow from" — i.e. the roots) lists the causes [msg #86]:

1. **Childhood / adverse childhood experience.** In the biosocial model of BPD development, the environment — the people involved in upbringing — matters. Such upbringing often involves adverse childhood experience: neglect, being ignored, emotional/physical/sexualized abuse. Such a history does not leave one indifferent: having once heard or felt something negative directed at oneself, a person can feel it always — hence the feeling of inadequacy [msg #86].
2. **Invalidation.** Not only abuse is trauma, but also ignoring, devaluing and disbelieving someone's emotional experiences. This attitude to emotions strengthens the belief that "something is wrong with me"; devaluing a person and their emotions leads to negative ideas about oneself [msg #86].
3. **Rejection.** One significant BPD symptom is a feeling of abandonment, which grows out of a history of unstable relationships in childhood and examples of rejection and abandonment by significant parental figures. If those close to you don't care, the idea arises: who would want you at all? — and one starts believing in one's own unattractiveness and defectiveness [msg #86].
4. **Emotional difficulties.** Another BPD symptom: when people struggle hard to manage their emotions and it does not come easily, this can lead to a sense of instability and self-doubt [msg #86].
5. **Relationship troubles.** Conflicts, misunderstandings and difficulty maintaining stable relationships can make people doubt their social skills and their worth [msg #86].
6. **Perceived criticism.** Hypersensitivity to perceived criticism: interpreting neutral or even kind feedback as harsh criticism, which can reinforce negative beliefs about oneself [msg #86].
7. **Perceptual distortions — black-and-white thinking.** "All or nothing" dichotomous thinking leads us to see ourselves simplistically and "in extremes" — either idealizing or (more often) seeing ourselves as damaged to the core [msg #86].
8. **Self-fulfilling prophecy.** If someone believes they are inherently inadequate and defective, they may engage in self-sabotage — actually behave so that everyone nods in agreement with the "inadequacy" hypothesis — and the person becomes more entrenched in the belief about themselves [msg #86].

### Six patterns triggered by stability — why a calm life feels unbearable
Katya Klimochkina describes a paradox: instead of feeling secure in stability, people with BPD may feel anxiety or awkwardness from a "quiet and calm life", which is itself a trigger for many emotional and cognitive reactions. The main patterns stability can trigger [msg #91]:

1. **Fear of abandonment.** When things are going well and life is calm and pleasantly uneventful, something starts to itch inside, and one begins to expect that "что-то плохое вот-вот нагрянет" ("something bad is about to strike"). The fear of some disaster is very straining and leads to constant worry, which is exhausting. Not wanting to be exhausted, one needs to "создать треш" ("create the mess"), possibly with one's own hands — because living in chaos is much more understandable and familiar [msg #91].
2. **Intolerance of emptiness.** The absence of strong emotional experiences can intensify the feeling of emptiness, which is very hard to bear. This fear of emptiness can push a person to create problems or conflicts as a way of testing the loyalty and commitment of others (partners, family members) — thus removing even a hint of contact with that emptiness. Quarrels that seem to come out of nowhere belong here [msg #91].
3. **Need for external stimulation.** Stability may be perceived as a lack of the necessary arousal or external triggers, producing monotony and therefore boredom. Routine causes discomfort because it can leave a person alone with their thoughts and emotions, which can be overwhelming. Emotionally charged situations, as external triggers, give food for other thoughts that also hurt — but anger or resentment are not as terrible as emptiness [msg #91].
4. **Difficulty self-soothing.** If life is calm but inside it isn't — e.g. one is overwhelmed by raging emotions — how to calm down? That requires self-soothing skills, the ability to normalize one's own state independently, which is hard for those used to seeking external sources of emotion regulation: impulsive actions, self-harm, substance use, or the same scandals in relationships. People with BPD may find it hard to comfort or calm themselves when strong emotions are *STRONG*, so a restless life offers more opportunities to turn "outward" to regulate emotions [msg #91].
5. **Identity instability.** Stability implies no strong emotional swings and no external chaos, which can disorient people with an unstable sense of identity: when everything around is calm, there's nothing to hold on to in understanding who I am and what matters to me. The author's analogy: which song from a new playlist will you remember sooner — a monotonous one, or one that stands out with a memorable beat, a drop or some other trendy device? So with self-understanding: if life is monotonous, it's hard to see oneself; if life is a rollercoaster with drops, there's simply no time to think about identity and the question bothers you little [msg #91].
6. **Problems with routine.** Routine may be perceived as limiting or boring, feeding a pull toward chaos and unpredictability. Everyday chores and plans can feel restrictive and press with a sense of confinement. Repetition and predictability are perceived as an absence of the novelty and excitement one strives for — because that is external stimulation, needed largely to regulate one's state and escape emptiness [msg #91].

Conclusion of the post: if plans, predictability and calm pastimes create so much inner tension, it is quite understandable why a crisis feels like the safer and more familiar state [msg #91].

### The "core of BPD" model — disturbed self as the core
Source: a chapter on the "self" and the "core" of BPD in a book on dissociation and BPD that Katya Klimochkina's working group read collectively in a clinic a couple of years earlier (she previously told about its idea that BPD is a "right-hemisphere disorder" and promises to cover that separately). She stresses this is "Не пересказ, но попытка понять" ("Not a retelling, but an attempt to understand") [msg #197].

**The question.** For many years there have been disputes over whether BPD has a "core" — something common to all people with the diagnosis. Under DSM, BPD is diagnosed with any combination of 5 of 9 criteria, which gives **256** possible variants of the diagnosis. This raises the question: does BPD have a single core, or is it just a label for complex, unclear cases? [msg #197]

**The hypothesis.** Suppose the core exists and it is a disturbance of the self (the sense of oneself, one's identity, inner wholeness). Then BPD is a set of stable characteristics (long-term and persisting over time), and despite the diversity of presentations everything is tied to the core [msg #197].

**Components of the core (three):**
1. **Disturbance of the self** — identity problems, chronic emptiness, fear of abandonment, unstable relationships [msg #197].
2. **Emotional dysregulation** — intense anger, emotional instability, suicidality [msg #197].
3. **Impulsivity** — risky behaviour, self-harm [msg #197].

**What "disturbance of the self" means — five signs:**
1. the person does not understand who they are [msg #197];
2. feels empty, or as if "внутри «ничего нет»" ("there's 'nothing' inside") [msg #197];
3. fears being abandoned, even when there is no real threat [msg #197];
4. reacts sharply in relationships, quickly switching from love to hate [msg #197];
5. cannot feel themselves stably — as if the "I" keeps changing all the time [msg #197].

**Stability over treatment ("the pleasant part (?)").** The self factor proved the most stable over time during treatment (it changed least over a year), unlike impulsivity and suicidal thoughts, which can go away faster. However, even the self strengthened significantly in the course of proper therapy — that is, "ядро можно менять" ("the core can be changed") [msg #197].

**Why it matters.** If we accept that the centre of BPD is not intense emotions, not self-harm and not unstable relationships but a destroyed sense of "I", it becomes clear why those symptoms appear — they are secondary. Summary: BPD is when a person's sense of self is broken, and this deeply affects everything: feelings, behaviour, relationships [msg #197].

**Further ideas from the chapter** (the author says she doesn't know how to tie them together, but finds them interesting):
- **Neurophysiological hypothesis:** if the self is fragmented or vulnerable, control by the prefrontal cortex decreases, which amplifies impulsivity and emotional reactions. The author is sceptical (in struck-through text): how did they connect "self" and "prefrontal cortex" in one sentence, and how was this "measured"? [msg #197]
- **Genetics:** genetic studies show dysregulation is more heritable, while disturbances of the self more often form in interpersonal relationships. Her verdict: "Makes sense!" [msg #197]
- **Dissociation** is a "распад самости" ("disintegration of the self"), pointing to a deep rupture in the integrity of identity. She says she is still thinking this over [msg #197].

### Self-perception across mental disorders
Lena Foer: many mental disorders affect how a person perceives themselves. Self-perception cannot be the only argument for a diagnosis, but it can hint to psychologists and psychiatrists which direction to look. Her overview of mood disorders, PTSD and personality disorders [msg #64]:

1. **Depression** — a deeply negative view of oneself, a sense of one's own worthlessness and hopelessness, an acute sense of guilt, dissatisfaction with one's appearance; these are among the diagnostic criteria and vivid features of depression [msg #64].
2. **Bipolar disorder** — sharp mood swings including depressive and manic episodes. Depressive states as above; manic or hypomanic phases are often accompanied by elevated self-esteem. Self-perception ranges from feeling "немножко красавчик и молодец" ("a bit of a handsome good guy") to seeing oneself as a genius, invincible and immortal [msg #64].
3. **Complex PTSD** — arises from prolonged exposure to traumatizing situations. People may feel damaged or broken, different from others. Negative self-perception is often tied to the specific trauma: after sexualized violence, thoughts of one's own "dirtiness"; after bullying, ideas of weakness and helplessness, and so on [msg #64].
4. **Borderline personality disorder** — people often suffer from a feeling of inner emptiness and not understanding who they really are. Because of strong emotional swings, self-perception may fluctuate. People with BPD also often try to adapt maximally to partners, which in the short term helps create an image of a self-just-like-the-partner, but in the long term adds even more confusion [msg #64].
5. **Narcissistic personality disorder** — people look confident in their exceptionality and uniqueness, but that is only half the truth. The other half: behind the grandiosity there is always a deeply hidden horror at the thought that behind the façade lies one's own defectiveness, wrongness and badness — better not to look there oneself, and not to let others close, so nobody else knows [msg #64].
6. **Histrionic personality disorder** — people feel accomplished and great only when at the centre of someone's attention. Their self-perception usually depends strongly on others' approval and admiration: some find it in collecting Facebook likes, others in courtship and flirting [msg #64].
7. **Dependent personality disorder** — people often see themselves as helpless, unable to make decisions and take care of themselves. They rely on others for emotional and physical needs and find it hard to play an active role in their own lives [msg #64].
8. **Antisocial personality disorder** — expressed, among other things, in a sense of grandiosity and lack of remorse for one's actions. Such people often consider themselves above social rules and engage in manipulative or criminal behaviour without feeling guilt [msg #64].
9. **Avoidant personality disorder** — people often consider themselves socially inept, unable to "нормально общаться" ("communicate normally"), and strongly fear rejection and criticism [msg #64].
10. **Obsessive-compulsive personality disorder (ОКРЛ)** — people often have a rigid set of rules about how they and everyone else should be. Self-esteem is largely tied to perfectionism and ideas that everything in one's life must be controlled [msg #64].
11. **Schizoid personality disorder** — people perceive themselves as different from humans as a species on a very deep level, which often leads to isolation. When most people seem strange and emotionally distant, it would indeed be strange to keep in contact with them [msg #64].
12–13. **Schizotypal and paranoid personality disorders** — deliberately skipped, because in them self-image apparently plays a smaller role [msg #64].

### What the "I" consists of — seven question areas
Lena Foer first gives what she calls the "boring scientific answer": the "I" is the sum of all physical and psychological (cognitive, emotional, motivational, social) characteristics that make a person unique. Unpacked, the "I" is about the answers to questions like these [article #87]:

1. **Body:** how do I see my body, and what does it say about me? [article #87]
2. **Biography:** how do I interpret the facts of my biography? What does my story say about what kind of person I am? [article #87]
3. **Habitual reactions:** how do I usually react to external events? [article #87]
4. **Thoughts:** what thoughts are characteristic of me? [article #87]
5. **Emotions:** what triggers emotions in me, and which ones? [article #87]
6. **Motivation and values:** what drives me? What is important to me? What do I strive for? [article #87]
7. **Relationships:** how do I interact with others? [article #87]

She adds the list goes on "И так далее, и до бесконечности" ("and so on, to infinity") — the seven areas are examples, not a closed list [article #87].

**Pitfall — answering the questions is not enough.** "If you rushed to answer the questions above hoping to finally understand yourself, I have bad news for you." Having separate, scattered answers is not the same as having a firm inner certainty about what one is. For random facts to turn into a whole and coherent self-image, "magic dust" has to be added (as with Peter Pan) — and the magic dust here is the process of self-awareness ("процесс самосознания") [article #87].

### Four building blocks of self-awareness
Definition: self-awareness (самосознание) is a complex cognitive process in which we direct attention inward, analyse, integrate and store information about ourselves, and identify ourselves with this "I". Broken down into "Lego bricks", it consists of [article #87]:

1. **Consciousness (сознание).** Put simply — awareness of what is happening inside and outside us. To some degree it is always present while we are awake; it usually "comes included" ("идет в комплекте") [article #87].
2. **Metacognitive thinking.** The process of observing what you have just thought. The ability to notice and critically examine your own thoughts does *not* come by default — it has to be developed separately [article #87].
3. **Autobiographical memory.** Not just memory of past experience, but building connections between different memories and how we showed ourselves in them. Remembering your past experience is, the author says, extremely important for a stable idea of your identity [article #87].
4. **Self-analysis and mindfulness (самоанализ и осознанность).** The ability to notice your emotions, bodily sensations, behavioural reactions and thought processes, and the connections between all of these [article #87].

### Three-part brain model applied to self-awareness
Lena Foer offers "a pinch of neuroscience", using a conventional division of the brain into three parts [article #87]:

1. **Reptilian brain** — responsible for instinctive behaviour [article #87].
2. **Limbic system** — manages emotions [article #87].
3. **Neocortex (cerebral cortex)** — responsible for thinking, self-analysis and mindfulness [article #87].

Matching the building blocks of self-awareness to these parts, she concludes that understanding oneself needs the cortex specifically — and its strong connection with the other parts of the brain [article #87].

**Age and the late-maturing cortex.** The neocortex lags behind the other parts of the brain in development and becomes a fully working tool only by the age of twenty to thirty. Her practical reading: it is a big relief if you are younger — part of your difficulty understanding yourself can easily (and fairly) be put down to age and the speed of cortical development [article #87].

**Autobiographical memory and BPD.** Autobiographical memory is regarded as an indirect indicator of identity integration. In its work the **posterior cingulate cortex** and the **left medial prefrontal cortex** play a decisive role, and, she writes, there are many papers saying that precisely these areas may be underdeveloped or show developmental anomalies in BPD. The **hippocampus** and surrounding areas also matter, and here too there are problems: in BPD the connection between the cortex and the hippocampus is impaired. Overall, this means people with BPD have biologically determined reasons to find it hard to find their "I" [article #87]. (!) INCOMPLETE: the "many papers" are not named or cited.

**Caveat in the source:** problems with identity can occur not only in BPD, and in any condition (including BPD) a person can help themselves sort out their identity [article #87].

### Three ways to assemble a self
Under the heading "Идеи: как собрать себе “селф”" ("Ideas: how to assemble a 'self' for yourself"), the longread gives three strategies, each targeting one of the building blocks above [article #87]:

1. **Practise mindfulness.** *What:* meditation and informal mindfulness practices — being present in the moment, openness and attention to sensations, thoughts and emotions. *Why:* these develop the observer position and help you better notice what is happening inside you. The more often and better you notice what goes on in you, the more material you have for assembling an identity [article #87].
2. **Develop metacognitive thinking.** *What:* noticing your own thoughts — this is taught in cognitive-behavioural therapy, but you can also do it on your own. *How:* analyse your own thought processes through observation and self-analysis. *Why:* it helps you understand how you arrive at decisions and reactions and, more generally, what drives you [article #87].
3. **Improve autobiographical memory.** *What/how:* write down significant life events and reflect on them. *Why:* this can strengthen your connection with those memories, giving you material for generalizing and gradually integrating many separate episodes into an "I" [article #87].

## Detailed insights

### BPD self-image in relation to partners
In Lena Foer's comparison, a BPD-specific mechanism of identity confusion is maximal adaptation to a partner: it gives a short-term borrowed self-image ("себя-такого-же-как-партнер", "self-just-like-the-partner") but worsens confusion about who one is in the long term [msg #64]. This fits with the "core" model's sign of sharp love-to-hate swings in relationships and with the stability post's point that conflicts can be created to test partners' loyalty and escape emptiness [msg #197, #91].

### Emptiness as a common thread
Across the posts, emptiness recurs as the state other behaviours are organized around: impulsive acts and self-harm to "fill" it (study), picking quarrels or seeking emotionally charged situations because anger or resentment are "not as terrible as emptiness", and routine being avoided because it leaves a person alone with overwhelming inner states [msg #6, #91]. In the "core" model, chronic emptiness is listed as part of the disturbance of the self [msg #197].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The emptiness mechanism (difficulty tolerating emotions → suppression → emptiness) is presented as an assumption ("Есть предположение"), and the protective role of identity, relationships and meaningful work as a hypothesis ("Есть гипотеза") [msg #6].
- "Low self-esteem" is flagged by the author as an everyday rather than formal term [msg #85]. Both self-esteem posts promise separate detailed posts on each factor/cause; (!) INCOMPLETE: those follow-ups are not part of this topic's messages [msg #85, #86].
- The "core of BPD" is explicitly conditional ("Допустим, что ядро есть" — "Suppose the core exists"), and the author says her text is an attempt to understand, not a retelling. She openly questions the neurophysiological hypothesis (how the "self" was linked to the prefrontal cortex and how it was measured) and says she is still digesting the dissociation idea [msg #197]. (!) INCOMPLETE: the source does not specify the study, sample or measure behind the finding that the self factor changed least over a year of treatment [msg #197].
- Lena Foer hedges that schizotypal and paranoid personality disorders were skipped because self-image "судя по всему" ("apparently") plays a smaller role in them [msg #64]. She recommends a psychoanalytic book despite not sharing the psychoanalytic approach [msg #65].

- The three-part brain division is introduced as conventional ("Мозг условно делят") — a simplification, not a precise anatomical claim [article #87].
- The neuroscience claims (posterior cingulate and left medial prefrontal cortex anomalies, impaired cortex–hippocampus connectivity in BPD) are attributed to "множество статей" ("many papers") without specific references; (!) INCOMPLETE: no studies are named [article #87].
- (!) CONTRADICTION (direction of causality, relative to the existing file): the "core of BPD" chapter (#197) hypothesizes that a fragmented self *reduces* prefrontal control, and the author there doubts how "self" and "prefrontal cortex" were linked; this longread presents cortical/hippocampal differences as a biological *reason for* identity difficulties. The two posts are by different authors and the source does not reconcile them [article #87].
- The post itself notes identity problems are not unique to BPD ("и не только у них" — "and not only them") [msg #87].
