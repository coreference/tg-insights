# Radical acceptance and validation

## What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's material on two DBT-related acceptance skills: **validation** of other people (and of oneself), taught as six levels for supporting loved ones in times of war and disagreement, and **radical acceptance / accepting reality**, taught through its definition, the situations where it applies, ten practical tips and ten reflection questions. It closes with a Q&A answer by psychologist Lena Foer on how to perceive a life of continuous problems "more lightly" by combining mindfulness, acceptance and self-kindness. The source frames these skills as a response to a painful world: invalidation "destroys relationships", and fighting a reality that cannot be changed keeps painful emotions going [msg #75, #76, #88, #171].

## Core ideas

- **Validation is acknowledgement, not agreement.** You can recognise someone's thoughts and feelings as understandable and legitimate while disagreeing with them — which makes validation a way to care for people whose views differ from yours. Full detail in *Six levels of validation* [msg #75].
- **Invalidation damages relationships.** Rejecting, ignoring or judging another's experience creates distance and pain, especially in a hard world [msg #75].
- **Fighting reality increases suffering.** People tend to fight what causes them physical or emotional pain; the mind doesn't realise this works against it, producing suffering instead of relief and keeping painful emotions alive (citing Linehan, 1993) [msg #76].
- **Acceptance is not approval, giving up, or forgiveness, and it does not remove pain.** It reduces suffering and the strength of emotions, takes time, and is a prerequisite for moving on. Full detail in *Ten tips for accepting reality* [msg #76, #88].
- **Radical acceptance is for what cannot be changed** — uncontrollable circumstances, the past, other people's behaviour, chronic pain, unexpected changes [msg #88].
- **Life can be full of problems AND valuable.** Accepting uncertainty and changeability improves well-being; mindfulness, acceptance ("as if") and self-compassion are ways to get there, while admitting honestly that sometimes life is simply hard [msg #171].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Six levels of validation
**Purpose / when to use:** to support loved ones and other people when the world is frightening — the post was written when "войн в мире стало еще больше" (*there are even more wars in the world*) — and to care for important people even when you disagree or hold different views. Validation means accepting your own or another's experience as understandable and legitimate; the levels are the "how" [msg #75].

**Definition.** Validation "не подразумевает согласия или одобрения, а означает **признание и принятие** мыслей, чувств, ощущений и поведения другого человека как понятных и имеющих место быть" (*does not imply agreement or approval, but means acknowledging and accepting another person's thoughts, feelings, sensations and behaviour as understandable and legitimate*). Key rule: "**Можно признавать и не соглашаться.**" (*You can acknowledge and not agree.*) [msg #75]

**What it replaces — emotional invalidation.** When emotions run high, in a fight, when we are not ready to listen to each other or ourselves, we fall into emotional invalidation: our thoughts and feelings are rejected, ignored or judged. We may blame, condemn, deny or minimise the importance of another person's experience. The author notes that even if we don't mean it in substance, "по форме все грешим" (*in form we all sin*). Invalidation destroys relationships and creates distance; it is painful and unpleasant not to be understood or listened to, and it is especially destructive "когда мир такой как сейчас" (*when the world is the way it is now*) [msg #75].

The six levels:

1. **Being present (Присутствовать).** Give someone your full attention and show compassion; be interested and listen actively. Examples from the author: holding someone's hand; listening and doing nothing but listening; listening to 10-minute voice messages to the end; walking at night to a friend's home to sit with her while she cries and tells how hard it is. "Скажем нет многозадачности" (*Let's say no to multitasking*). Why it works: one common reason people feel uncomfortable around strong emotions is that they don't know what to say — and often the answer is simply presence, just sitting together in silence [msg #75].
2. **Reflecting (Отражение).** Summarise what the other person said — the familiar phrases like «правильно ли я понял…» (*did I understand correctly…*) and retelling what was said. Why: it is an effective way to make sure two people really understand each other and are on the same wavelength. We may perceive events differently, and sometimes understanding — and showing that you understood — "дорогого стоит" (*is worth a lot*) [msg #75].
3. **Mind-reading (Чтение мыслей).** Guess what someone might be feeling or thinking based on the situation. Purpose: to help the person label their emotions accurately, because being able to name feelings is a step toward regulating them. Some people may be unclear about what they feel because they were not allowed to have their feelings or learned to fear them. How: when someone describes a situation, notice their emotional state; then either name the emotions you hear, or guess what the person might be feeling. Example: «Кажется, что тебе страшно и ты злишься из-за происходящего в мире» (*It seems you're scared and angry about what's happening in the world*) — "это валидация" (*this is validation*) [msg #75].
4. **Understanding in the context of experience and biology.** Behaviour can be understood in the context of the person's history and biology. Experience example: if your loved one emigrated to Israel or Armenia and now there is a new escalation and geopolitical conflict, it may be impossible for them to react to the situation wisely. Validating at this level means understanding their experience: «Я понимаю твою фрустрацию и непонимание что делать, ты уже переживал нечто похожее и знаешь как это тяжело» (*I understand your frustration and not knowing what to do — you've already been through something similar and know how hard it is*). Biology: highly sensitive people have a biological predisposition to feel some situations more intensely and vividly, so validating a reaction by this feature of their nervous system can also be validation [msg #75].
5. **Normalising (Нормализация).** Recognise that emotional reactions are often normal responses to specific situations. Knowing your emotions are normal is useful for everyone; for an emotionally sensitive person, knowing that someone else was also "на эмоциях" (*emotional*) in that situation is validating. Example: «Конечно, ты беспокоишься. Жить в военное время пугает всех» (*Of course you're worried. Living in wartime frightens everyone*). Why: it reduces the feeling of isolation [msg #75].
6. **Radical genuineness (Радикальная искренность).** Understand someone's emotions by sharing a similar experience — sharing it as an equal. This builds a deep connection. Example: "your worry is normal and war frightens everyone" — and how do I know? I'm terrified too, so I can assume you feel something similar [msg #75].

### Ten tips for accepting reality
**Source:** a post forwarded by Lena Foer from the channel Volnovoy effekt (*Ripple Effect*), titled «Навык принятия реальности» (*The reality-acceptance skill*), tagged as a DBT skill / radical acceptance. **Rationale given before the tips:** as humans we tend to fight what causes us physical or emotional pain; our mind does not realise this works against us, causing suffering rather than relieving pain (Linehan, 1993). When you have problems controlling emotions, this tendency may show up even more strongly, but in fact the tendency to fight reality helps keep painful emotions going [msg #76].

1. **Acceptance does not mean approval.** You don't have to love reality, but to reduce your suffering you must accept it [msg #76].
2. **Acceptance also does not mean giving up** or stopping trying to change the situation [msg #76].
3. **When frustrated, remember acceptance reduces the strength of emotions.** It will take some time — hold on, don't give up [msg #76].
4. **Acceptance won't happen at once.** The harder the situation, the harder it is to accept and the more time it will take [msg #76].
5. **Recall past situations you managed to accept.** This helps accept reality even when it seems hard [msg #76].
6. **Acceptance is not the same as forgiveness.** We work on acceptance for our own sake; it has nothing to do with the other person. Forgiveness is optional; acceptance is necessary to move on [msg #76].
7. **If accepting the whole thing feels too heavy, break the situation into components** that can be accepted one by one [msg #76].
8. **Focus on the present; stay away from the future.** We cannot accept something that hasn't happened yet [msg #76].
9. **Don't try to accept evaluative judgements — it's impossible.** The skill is about accepting reality; judgements are only judgements, not facts [msg #76].
10. **Accepting reality doesn't mean the pain will disappear.** It reduces suffering, but pain is a natural part of human life [msg #76].

### Five situations for radical acceptance
**Definitions first (Katya Klimochkina):** **acceptance** "означает признание и понимание ситуации такой, какая она есть, без необходимости испытывать к ней симпатию или одобрение" (*means acknowledging and understanding a situation as it is, without needing to like or approve of it*) — acknowledging reality and deciding not to resist or fight things that cannot be changed. **Radical acceptance** is a DBT concept of complete acceptance of reality, especially when facing situations that are challenging, upsetting or out of your control; it is about acknowledging and accepting the present moment without judgement — "без оценок" (*without evaluations*) [msg #88].

When to radically accept:
1. **Unchangeable circumstances** — situations outside your control that cannot be changed, e.g. the death of a loved one, a natural disaster, and war [msg #88].
2. **Past events** — to make peace with things from your past that cannot be changed, including personal mistakes, losses or traumatic experiences [msg #88].
3. **Other people's behaviour** — which you cannot control. People have their own choices and actions, and accepting this can reduce unnecessary suffering. The author adds a crossed-out aside that letting others be themselves is "что-то на уровне фантастики" (*something on the level of fantasy*) [msg #88].
4. **Chronic pain** — for people dealing with chronic, constant pain, radical acceptance helps acknowledge limitations and find ways to cope effectively [msg #88].
5. **Unexpected changes** — when life takes an unexpected turn, e.g. sudden job loss or an unforeseen change in life circumstances such as emigration; radical acceptance helps adapt to the new reality [msg #88].

### Ten reflection questions for accepting what you disagree with
**Purpose:** help you "**принять то, с чем вы не согласны**" (*accept what you disagree with*) so you can move on and stop suffering. Ask yourself [msg #88]:
1. What exactly is the reality I am currently struggling hard to accept?
2. Am I holding any judgements or evaluations about this situation that cause me suffering?
3. What emotions do I feel facing this reality? Can I name them and acknowledge them without judgement?
4. If I fully accepted this situation as it is, how would it feel in my body? What physical sensations arise?
5. What is the cost of refusing to accept this reality? How does non-acceptance affect my well-being and relationships?
6. What would it mean to give up the need for things to be different?
7. Can I identify any "must" or "that's how it should be" statements I impose on the situation? How do these expectations feed my struggle?
8. What evidence confirms the reality I am resisting? How can I look at the facts objectively, without distortions?
9. How might acceptance open new possibilities for growth and positive change?
10. What would it be like to approach this situation with an attitude of willingness rather than resistance?

Closing reminder: radical acceptance is not about liking or approving; it is acknowledging reality and choosing not to fight it, which can lead to coping with difficulties more effectively and living "эту жизнь полегче" (*this life a bit more easily*) [msg #88].

### Clarifying a vague goal ("take the world more lightly")
**Context:** reader Masha wrote that she has many problems which she always solves successfully, yet she keeps waiting for the moment when she finishes this and this and this issue and the issues will end — but new difficulties always come. The last two years were a "мясорубка" (*meat grinder*), and it feels as if the grinding stays with her forever; she sees it as not being able to accept that she lives an adult life, and wants to perceive the world and its challenges more lightly [msg #171].

**Method (Lena Foer):** in cognitive-behavioural approaches, the first step with clients is agreeing on the request — the goal you move toward together — and the more detailed the goal, the better. "Perceive the world more lightly" is a great goal, but to move toward it you need to understand [msg #171]:
- **What does "more lightly" mean?** How would you think about your problems if you perceived them more lightly? How would you react to new ones?
- **What would change in behaviour?** What would you start doing differently — take up a hobby, invite friends out more often, go to interesting places? How often?

Answers to these questions help clarify what it makes sense to move toward [msg #171].

### Three ideas for accepting life as it is
**Claim:** research shows that accepting life as it is now — including its uncertainty and changeability — can strongly improve psychological well-being (Lena Foer; no specific study named). Three ideas for moving toward it [msg #171]:
1. **Mindfulness practices** help you live in the present moment without fixating on future problems. You can simply sit in an armchair for 15 minutes and observe your thoughts, or build mindfulness into daily life — e.g. while walking, focus on the sensations in your feet or on what you see around you. Important: it is not an instant remedy — to notice anything you need at least a couple of weeks of practice. Thich Nhat Hanh's books add "+500 к вдохновленности процессом" (*+500 to inspiration for the process*) [msg #171].
2. **Accepting difficulties as part of life** can reduce our struggle with them. Acceptance means life can be full of problems AND be valuable and pleasant. It is hard to explain how to practise acceptance in two words; DBT offers a full instruction. First of all, acting "как если бы" (*as if*) helps: as if you had already accepted your life as it is and decided to do what is valuable and enjoyable right now, without waiting for other tasks to be finished [msg #171].
3. **Self-kindness rules.** Research shows self-compassion improves our ability to cope with stress. Saying supportive things to yourself and doing pleasant things works, even if it sounds only moderately convincing at first [msg #171].

**Final note:** sometimes life really is very hard and difficult to enjoy. Denying this means slipping into excessive psychologising of everything and ignoring reality. Sometimes the best we can do is admit that it's hard and give ourselves an extra half hour lying on the couch [msg #171].

## Detailed insights

### The stream-and-stones story (Jack Kornfield)
In Jack Kornfield's book *A Path with Heart* there is a story of a man who studied meditation with a Tibetan teacher and decided to strive for enlightenment. He went to the mountains and built a small hut by a stream. When he began meditating, he noticed he could not stop his mind — it was flooded with thoughts and memories. It got to the point where he noticed his mind singing in unison with the babbling stream next to his hut. Unable to bear it, he began moving the stones in the stream, trying to make it sing a different song. **Lesson:** we often do similar things. Kornfield says that when problems arise we project our frustration onto them, as if the rain, the children or the world were the cause of our discomfort; we imagine we can change the world and become happy — but we find happiness and lose illusions "не путем перемещения камней, а путем изменения своего к ним отношения" (*not by moving stones, but by changing our attitude toward them*) [msg #76].

### Framing of acceptance in the channel
Katya Klimochkina opens the radical-acceptance post with Sheldon from *The Big Bang Theory* calling gravity heartless — and says one sometimes wants to say the same about the reality we live in. If that reality cannot be changed, the answer is what "не нравится **ни-ко-му**" (*no-bo-dy likes*): accepting it as it is [msg #88].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- Acceptance does not remove pain; it reduces suffering. It is slow, especially for hard situations [msg #76].
- Mindfulness is not an instant remedy; at least a couple of weeks of practice are needed before noticing anything [msg #171].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the "research shows" claims about acceptance improving well-being and self-compassion improving stress coping are not tied to named studies in the source [msg #171].
- Lena Foer warns against denying that life is sometimes simply hard — over-psychologising ignores reality [msg #171].
- Level 4 example: for someone already through a similar crisis, responding "wisely" may be impossible — validation here means understanding rather than expecting a wise response [msg #75].
