# How religions and spiritual traditions made sense of trauma

## What this covers

A longread by channel author Katya Klimochkina (co-credited to @bpdflowers), originally written for a book but left out of it, surveying how religious and spiritual traditions interpreted traumatic suffering long before psychotherapy: Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism and the beliefs of Indigenous peoples (Siberia, Navajo, Amazonia, Maori, Australian Aboriginal peoples). For each tradition it gives the core explanation of suffering, the texts or rites involved, where ancient texts describe what we would now call PTSD symptoms, and how the idea anticipates a modern therapeutic technique. Why it matters, per the author: the term "trauma" is only about a century old, but pain, war, loss, violence and catastrophe are as old as humanity, and people always asked *"почему это со мной происходит и как теперь жить дальше?"* (*why is this happening to me and how do I live on now?*) — religions were humanity's long-standing answers, and their logic of meaning-making still underlies trauma work today [msg #230] [article #230].

## Core ideas

- **Trauma is a new word for an ancient experience.** Psychological trauma as a concept appeared only in the 20th century (the post says "всего век назад" — only a century ago), long before DSM and ICD, yet the experiences now called consequences of traumatic experience "существовали... всегда" (*have always existed*) [msg #230] [article #230].
- **Every tradition answers "За что?" (*Why me? / What did I do to deserve this?*).** Different continents and eras, one common sense: find an explanation and answer this question [msg #230] [article #230].
- **The function of these explanations is restoring meaning, inner order and connection.** They didn't only explain what was happening; they helped return meaning, restore inner order and a sense of connection with the world, and helped people survive what often has no meaning or explanation — yet meaning is very much needed for the survival of both the species and the individual [article #230].
- **Spiritual explanations were forerunners of psychotherapy.** Religions didn't know about PTSD or CPTSD but described the same phenomena in other words; where science speaks of integrating traumatic memory or processing the personal meanings of trauma, religion speaks of redemption, purification or return of the soul [article #230].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Five ways traditions framed suffering
Across millennia, according to the longread, pain, suffering and torment were understood as one of the following (the author adds wryly "Кто во что горазд!" — *each to their own talent / whatever anyone could come up with*) [article #230]:
1. **Punishment** — suffering as retribution for sins (strongly present in Christianity and the prophetic books; explicitly *not* the main reading in Islam or Buddhism).
2. **Test / trial** — suffering as a trial of faith (Christianity's Job, Islam's ibtila).
3. **Purification** — pain that cleanses the soul or the heart (Christian asceticism and "carrying the cross", Islamic and Sufi purification of the heart).
4. **Result of karma** — consequences of past actions, including in past lives (Hinduism).
5. **Rupture of the connection between a person and the spirit world** — Indigenous/shamanic traditions (soul loss, broken tapu, destroyed Dreaming path).
Each is developed tradition by tradition under *Detailed insights* [article #230].

### Three families of spiritual answers to "Why me?"
The author's closing synthesis groups traditions by where they place the emphasis [article #230]:
1. **Abrahamic religions (Judaism, Christianity, Islam)** — emphasis on the *test of faith and moral responsibility*. Suffering asks: will you keep trusting God; did you sin; what must you repent of.
2. **Eastern teachings (Hinduism, Buddhism)** — emphasis on *understanding the causes of suffering and spiritual growth through it*. Suffering is caused (karma, ignorance, attachment) and can be understood and transcended.
3. **Shamanic traditions** — emphasis on *restoring the lost connection with the world* (body, soul, nature, ancestors, spirits), usually through collective rites.
What unites them, despite the differences: the drive to give meaning to pain, suffering and misfortune. Even when an event splits life into "before and after", faith in something larger helps a person embed the event "somewhere" and gives the possibility of making sense of it [article #230].

### Tonglen (taking and giving)
- **What:** a Tibetan Buddhist practice described as "вдох страдания, выдох сострадания" (*inhaling suffering, exhaling compassion*) or "принятие и отдача" (*taking and giving*). The author calls it her favourite ("Мое любимое!") [article #230].
- **How (as described):** through deliberate "breathing in" of others' suffering and "breathing out" of compassion, one learns to turn pain — one's own and others' — into a source of compassion and spiritual energy. (!) INCOMPLETE: the source gives no further instructions (duration, posture, sequence) [article #230].
- **Why it matters for trauma, per the author:** it already closely resembles PTSD/CPTSD work in behavioural approaches, where reworking the traumatic experience lets it be (1) integrated into memory and (2) re-understood with a different emotional colouring [article #230].

## Detailed insights

### Christianity: punishment and test, purification through pain
- **Two-fold reading.** Suffering is understood both as punishment for sins and as a test of faith; accents shifted across eras, from repentance for suffering to the rebirth of the spirit through pain [article #230].
- **Book of Job as a PTSD-like portrait.** Job, a righteous man, loses everything — children, property, health. His friends insist it is punishment, but God does not confirm their words (through their countless conversations). Job suffers, gets angry, argues with God, and lives through despair, fear and loss of meaning — states the author says resemble PTSD symptoms. His words "Страхи мои напали на меня" (*my fears have attacked me*) can, in her reading, be taken as evidence of a sense of constant threat or of intrusive thoughts. The meaning of Job's trial is trust in God even when the causes of pain are incomprehensible [article #230].
- **Gospel: suffering as part of salvation.** Jesus goes through torture and death to redeem humanity's sins. From this comes the idea that pain can be cleansing and meaningful if it is not lived through in vain — and the idea of "нести свой крест" (*carrying one's cross*): pain not as penalty but as a path of spiritual purification [article #230].
- **Ascetic tradition.** Suffering was seen as a way to get rid of pride. Monks wrote of the "ночи души" (*night of the soul*) — a state of emptiness and horror followed by a renewal of faith [article #230].
- **The harsher, collective reading persisted.** Suffering as punishment for sins and a reminder to repent intensified in times of epidemics, wars or social catastrophes, when one person's suffering was explained by the sins of the whole community. In the Middle Ages, epidemics such as the 14th-century plague were considered God's punishment of entire peoples. The prophetic books (e.g. Jeremiah) interpret any calamity as the consequence of a people's apostasy, a reading that persisted in later religious interpretations of disasters as punishment for "греховность общества" (*the sinfulness of society*) [article #230].

### Islam: trial, not punishment; patience and trust
- Difficulties and disasters are seen not as penalty but as a test of faith and a way to purify the heart; Allah tests a person to reveal the strength of their faith. The Quran (2:155): «Мы непременно испытаем вас незначительным страхом, голодом, потерей имущества, людей и плодов.» (*We will surely test you with some fear, hunger, loss of property, lives and fruits*) [article #230].
- **Ibtila (trial)** is part of the spiritual path, in which **sabr (patience)** and **tawakkul (reliance on Allah)** are shown [article #230].
- A cited saying presents trials as a sign of divine love: "когда Всевышний Аллах любит каких-то людей, Он подвергает их испытаниям" (*when Allah the Most High loves certain people, He subjects them to trials*); whoever is content gains [Allah's] contentment, whoever is discontent incurs [His] wrath. Here trial is understood as a form of purification and a manifestation of divine love [article #230].
- **Ancient description of PTSD symptoms.** Hadiths describe companions of the Prophet who, after battles, could not sleep for a long time, heard the sounds of fighting in their dreams and startled at noise. The author reads this as an example of ancient texts noticing the psychological consequences of trauma: today we would see re-experiencing (intrusions, flashbacks, nightmares) typical of PTSD [article #230].
- **Sufism.** Suffering is the destruction of the false "self", cleansing the heart of attachments and a path to God's love. For Sufis traumatic experience is not an end but a process of inner rebirth. Rumi wrote "Рана - это место, куда входит свет" (*the wound is the place where the light enters*) — "something about vulnerability and healing in pain", in the author's words. So pain becomes a point of spiritual awakening rather than mere suffering — "Во всем добавляется "доп смысл"!" (*an "extra meaning" is added to everything!*) [article #230].

### Hinduism: causes, consequences and debts
- Pain and loss are about karma (and consequences) and dharma — a duty to be fulfilled. Trials or "traumatic experience" are chains of causes and effects linking all lives; they are not accidental but the consequence of past actions and part of the spiritual path [article #230].
- **Bhagavad Gita.** Arjuna, not wanting to fight, tells Krishna of his horror before the war; Krishna replies: «Исполни свой долг. Душа не умирает, она лишь меняет тела, как человек одежду» (*Fulfil your duty. The soul does not die; it only changes bodies, as a person changes clothes*). War here is not punishment but a spiritual test and part of dharma. Pain becomes a lesson: if you go through it consciously you can purify karma and come closer to liberation (moksha). Thus, the author notes, traumatic experience can be re-viewed as "по делам полученный" (*received according to one's deeds*) and as "уплоченый долг" [sic] (*a debt paid off*) [article #230].
- **Puranas** tell of people reborn into hard circumstances because of mistakes in past lives [article #230].
- **Upanishads** treat suffering as the inevitable consequence of accumulated actions; the human goal is to step outside the cycle of suffering [article #230].
- **Ramayana and Mahabharata**: heroes go through exile, loss and violence, but each story stresses that suffering opens the way to wisdom and inner strength. The author liked the idea of "незавершённые уроки души" (*unfinished lessons of the soul*) that one manages to "finish off" in suffering [article #230].

### Buddhism: understand the causes, observe the pain
- Suffering (dukkha) is the basis of human existence. The First Noble Truth: suffering is inevitable, but it can be understood and transformed [article #230].
- The Buddha explained pain not as punishment but as a result of ignorance, attachment, fears and illusions; if a person becomes aware of the causes of suffering, they can be freed from it. Consequently, the author draws, searching for someone to blame is pointless, whereas understanding the causes of pain and the path to overcome it can be effective [article #230].
- **How: meditation.** In meditative practice (sati) a person observes pain without avoidance, learning to see it as part of life. The Dhammapada says «Из боли рождается мудрость» (*from pain wisdom is born*). The author claims: "Вся работа с тревогой в поведенческой терапии стоит на этом фундаменте" (*all anxiety work in behavioural therapy stands on this foundation*) [article #230].
- **Link to third-wave CBT.** This resembles modern ideas of mindfulness and integration of traumatic experience: in Buddhism and in third-wave CBT (ACT, DBT, MBT, in the author's list) mindfulness and compassion practices aim not at suppressing pain or trauma but at transforming pain into understanding, which opens the possibility of liberation. The author's rhetorical question: "Если я могу выдерживать боль и дискомфорт, не влияя, не мучаясь, то боль ли это еще?" (*If I can bear pain and discomfort without reacting, without torment, is it still pain?*) [article #230].
- Tonglen — see *Tonglen (taking and giving)* above.

### Indigenous beliefs: soul loss and restoring connection
- In many Indigenous cultures trauma was understood as a loss of connection between the person, body, soul, nature and the world of spirits [article #230].
- **Soul loss (Siberian peoples, the Navajo in North America, Amazonian peoples).** After severe fright or violence a person seemed to lose part of themselves and became lifeless, pale, silent or tired. The author asks whether this isn't difficulty regulating emotions, or the dissociative states familiar to people with CPTSD. The shaman performed a rite of **return of the soul**, helping restore the connection with body and nature and thereby wholeness [article #230].
- **Maori** (the source says "Гавайские и Новозеландские коренные народы" — Hawaiian and New Zealand Indigenous peoples): trauma was understood as a violation of **tapu** — spiritual balance and order. After losses, purification rituals (**noa**) were held to return harmony to the person and community — the balance of the spiritual and physical world [article #230].
- **Australian Aboriginal peoples:** the destruction of the **Dreaming path** was considered the spiritual cause of illness, suffering and misfortune; healing required restoring the connection with the land and ancestors [article #230].
- **Suffering as collective.** In all these traditions suffering is not personal but a collective event, an imbalance to be restored through shared actions — songs, rites, story. This survives, per the author, in the importance of social support, which matters on every continent to this day. And "return of the soul" practices closely resemble work on re-evaluating traumatic experience so that one can "accept" it and live on [article #230].
