# Dissociation, depersonalization and out-of-body experiences

## What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's explanations of dissociation: what it is, its two main forms (depersonalization and derealization), its causes and triggers, why it is part of borderline personality disorder (BPD), how it can become a habitual avoidance strategy that keeps a person in "continuous crisis", what out-of-body experiences (OBE) are and how they relate to complex PTSD, and practical ways to cope — above all grounding, including the 5-4-3-2-1 technique. According to the authors, dissociation matters because it is a defense that can become a source of new danger, and because it "is not something you should try to ignore" — frequent dissociation is a reason to seek help [msg #26, #45, #105, #192, #193].

## Core ideas

- **Dissociation is a separation of the self from experience.** Thoughts, emotions and identity feel as if they exist apart from the person; it shows up as detachment from the body, memory loss or "falling out" of reality. See *Definitions* below [msg #26, #105].
- **Trauma is the most significant factor.** Dissociation is a BPD symptom appearing under high stress; psychological trauma is considered the main driver of dissociative symptoms [msg #26, #105].
- **A defense becomes avoidance.** Dissociation protects during unbearable events, but once learned it can be used for any fright, which reduces awareness of real dangers and feeds a "continuous crisis" — see *Dissociation as defense turned avoidance* [msg #45].
- **Mild, transient dissociation after stress is normal; persistent dissociation is a disorder feature.** Feelings of unreality can last a few days after a shocking event; as part of a mental disorder it can be a constant experience [msg #105].
- **Out-of-body experiences are a particular version of dissociation.** They are a regulation mechanism under extreme stress and danger and have specific links to cPTSD [msg #192, #193].
- **Grounding shifts attention from inner to outer world.** Techniques like 5-4-3-2-1 do not solve problems but buy time and the possibility of a conscious next step [msg #146].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Four signs of dissociation (recognition checklist)
Lena Foer notes that dissociation can be hard to recognize because symptoms can be barely noticeable and vary from person to person. Common signs [msg #26]:
1. A feeling of detachment or disconnection from oneself or from the surroundings [msg #26].
2. A feeling of being in a dream or not fully present in reality [msg #26].
3. Memory loss or gaps in memory [msg #26].
4. A feeling of watching what is happening as if from the side [msg #26].

### Four steps when you feel dissociation
Lena Foer's sequence of helpful steps for the moment of dissociation [msg #26]:
1. **Practice mindfulness and grounding techniques.** For example, concentrate on your breathing, on the sensation of your feet on the ground, or on the sounds around you. Why: this helps you return to the present moment and reduces the sense of detachment [msg #26].
2. **Do a self-care procedure.** This could be taking a bath, a walk, or doing something you love. Why: caring for yourself reduces stress and anxiety, which contribute to dissociation [msg #26].
3. **Seek professional help.** A therapist can give support in the moment and help understand the causes of the symptom, and can help develop strategies for overcoming dissociation [msg #26].
4. **Keep a journal.** Writing down your experiences helps reveal patterns and triggers, and helps you reflect on your experience and on how you cope with it [msg #26].
The post closes with the rule that dissociation should not be ignored: «Важно помнить, что диссоциация - это не то, что вы должны пытаться игнорировать.» (*It is important to remember that dissociation is not something you should try to ignore.*) If you often notice dissociation in yourself, it is important to seek help and support [msg #26].

### Dissociation as defense turned avoidance (the "continuous crisis" mechanism)
**The phenomenon.** Lena Foer describes a frequent trait of people with BPD: a state of "continuous crisis" — a chain of hard events where as soon as one disaster ends, the next begins. Her illustration: if you didn't catch a cold, you left your phone in a taxi; if you didn't leave the phone, you ended up at night in a rough neighbourhood in the middle of a construction site — with the series continuing without end, all the time [msg #45].

**How it works, step by step** [msg #45]:
1. **Dissociation is a psychological defense.** The person begins to perceive a hard situation as if it were not happening to them, as if they are not here at all — «и никакого тут, может, и вообще нету» (*and maybe there is no "here" at all*). It can look very different: some people see what is happening from the side, others just slightly "switch off" when scared and may not even notice it themselves [msg #45].
2. **It arises in response to trauma.** It is easier to survive, for example, violence if you are "a little not here" and it is not happening to you [msg #45].
3. **It becomes a habit.** Once it has appeared, dissociation can become the habitual way of coping with fright, even small fright. «Так механизм психологической защиты становится механизмом психологического избегания.» (*Thus a mechanism of psychological defense becomes a mechanism of psychological avoidance.*) [msg #45]
4. **Avoidance raises real risk.** Example: Masha survived a car accident; now she may dissociate every time she is in a car again. If she is driving, dissociation affects her ability to drive — it is hard to notice dangers in time while trying to abstract from the outside world — so by dissociating she risks another accident. The author adds that this is not the only risk factor: there are always fools on the road, and one can end up in a crash (in an abusive situation, in any other trauma) in many ways. But «диссоциация - это движение с закрытыми глазами по дороге с дураками» (*dissociation is driving with your eyes closed on a road full of fools*), so the risks rise very substantially [msg #45].
5. **Conclusion.** «Именно сильное желание отвернуться от неприятного служит топливом непрерывного кризиса и поддерживает его.» (*It is precisely the strong wish to turn away from the unpleasant that fuels the continuous crisis and sustains it.*) [msg #45]

**What follows — two practices** [msg #45]:
- **Look for avoidance moments.** Notice moments when you want to "not think about it, let it somehow resolve itself", when you zone out into series or games or otherwise avoid being present in the outside world, or when it is hard to notice your body and the passage of time and everything feels as if through a fog [msg #45].
- **Find out in advance what you avoid.** Answer ahead of time: what are you avoiding, what are you trying not to think about, what are you trying not to face, where are you trying not to end up. These are the zones where it is critically important to switch on awareness [msg #45].
- **Rationale.** We can move on autopilot or think through and make sense of each step. Conscious movement costs more than autopilot, but in situations associated with elevated risk for you, these costs are justified [msg #45].

### Depersonalization symptom list
Depersonalization is a feeling of detachment, distance or disconnection from oneself, one's body or mind — a feeling that what is happening around you is not happening to you. A person experiencing it is cut off from their thoughts, feelings, sensations and actions. Symptoms listed by Katya Klimochkina [msg #105]:
1. Changes in perception [msg #105].
2. A distorted sense of time and space [msg #105].
3. An unreal, unstable or absent "self" [msg #105].
4. Emotional or physical numbness [msg #105].
5. A feeling of watching a film about your own life [msg #105].
6. Feeling cut off from parts of your body or your emotions [msg #105].
7. Uncertainty about the boundaries between yourself and other people [msg #105].

### Derealization symptom list
Derealization is a feeling of unreality connected with other people, places or objects: what is happening in the world around you seems unreal. With derealization the surroundings feel [msg #105]:
1. Unreal [msg #105].
2. Foggy [msg #105].
3. Dream-like [msg #105].
4. Lifeless, static [msg #105].
5. Visually distorted or blurred [msg #105].
6. Objects change shape, size or colour [msg #105].
7. A feeling of detaching oneself from the surrounding world [msg #105].
8. A feeling of looking at the world through a window pane [msg #105].
9. Feeling as if other people are robots (even if you know they are not) [msg #105].

### Four causes of dissociation
Per Katya Klimochkina [msg #105]:
1. **A natural reaction to uncontrollable trauma.** For many people, dissociation is a natural reaction to a trauma they cannot control, and develops as a way of coping with trauma (one-time or ongoing), especially prolonged childhood trauma or abuse [msg #105].
2. **Coping with very stressful experiences** [msg #105].
3. **Cultural or religious practices** — some people may dissociate within certain such practices [msg #105].
4. **Alcohol or medication** — dissociation can be a side effect of using alcohol or some medications, or of stopping them [msg #105].
Dissociation can be a symptom of PTSD, depression, anxiety, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder or borderline personality disorder [msg #105].

### Five coping measures for dissociation
Katya Klimochkina's "how to cope" list [msg #105]:
1. **Basics first** — «как не банально» (*however banal it sounds*): sleep routine, healthy eating, physical activity [msg #105].
2. **Do not use alcohol or psychoactive substances** [msg #105].
3. **Know your triggers** (see *Triggers* below) [msg #105].
4. **Use grounding techniques** — focus on the external surroundings, e.g. count all the red and blue objects around you [msg #105].
5. **Mindful breathing** — breathe "in a rectangle" (по прямоугольнику), and make the inhale a little shorter than the exhale [msg #105].

### 5-4-3-2-1 grounding technique
**Purpose.** Lena Foer calls 54321 «универсальный ключ к эмоциональной регуляции» (*a universal key to emotional regulation*); the accompanying card describes it as a technique that connects you with the present moment by exploring the five senses [msg #146, img #146].

**When to use it — the 10 indications** [msg #146]:
1. You are dissociating or have simply "fallen out" of reality.
2. You are stuck in frightening, anxious thoughts or self-criticism.
3. You can't stop ruminating.
4. You are drowning in procrastination.
5. You have an acute urge to do something you decided never to do again.
6. You feel the ground slipping from under your feet and find it hard to control yourself.
7. You are lost and don't know what to do next.
8. You have gone through severe stress.
9. You want to distract yourself and switch attention.
10. You are stuck in daydreaming.
Because it is very simple, it is accessible from many difficult states [msg #146].

**The steps** (text of the post plus the card) [msg #146, img #146]:
1. **5 — see:** count five visual objects of one colour or shape that you see.
2. **4 — hear:** four (different) sounds you can hear.
3. **3 — feel in the body:** three tactile / bodily sensations you can feel.
4. **2 — smell:** two smells you can catch.
5. **1 — taste:** one taste you can feel.
(The card's OCR text is column-scrambled; the pairing above follows the post text "five visual objects of some colour or shape, four sounds, three tactile sensations, two smells and one taste" [msg #146, img #146].)

**Why it works.** While you are counting, the focus of your attention shifts from the inner world to the outer world, which makes it easier to make a conscious decision about how to act next [msg #146].

**Limits.** «54321 не решит все ваши проблемы, не выведет вас из стресса и не поменяет ваше мышление. Но он даст вам время и возможность для того, чтобы сделать первый шаг ко всему этому.» (*54321 will not solve all your problems, will not take you out of stress and will not change your thinking. But it will give you time and the opportunity to take the first step toward all of that.*) [msg #146]

### How an out-of-body experience feels
Katya Klimochkina defines OBE as a regulation mechanism in response to very strong stress and a sense of danger. It feels like [msg #192]:
1. Seeing oneself from outside, e.g. from above or from a corner of the room [msg #192].
2. A sense of detachment from the physical body, as if "hovering" in the air [msg #192].
3. Distorted perception: time seems slowed down or unreal [msg #192].
4. A feeling of being as if in a dream, where the body moves *automatically* [msg #192].

### Causes of out-of-body experiences
Most often [msg #193]:
1. **Trauma and stress** — a strong emotional shock, e.g. during violence or an accident, can provoke OBE as a dissociative mechanism allowing one to avoid fear or pain [msg #193].
2. **Sleep disturbances** — OBE are often linked to sleep paralysis or states at the border of sleep and wakefulness [msg #193].
3. **Neurological causes** — temporal lobe epilepsy, migraines or brain injuries, especially if the temporo-parietal area of the brain is affected [msg #193].
4. **Psychoactive substance abuse** [msg #193].
5. **Meditation or deep relaxation** [msg #193].

### Five links between out-of-body experiences and cPTSD
Katya Klimochkina says she was especially interested in how OBE connects to cPTSD — the "spoiler" being that OBE is a particular version of dissociation [msg #193]:
1. **Reducing emotional intensity.** During strong stress or a traumatic moment the brain may "switch off" to reduce the intensity of emotions; OBE then feels like a mental "stepping away" from oneself to escape unbearable experiences [msg #193].
2. **Regaining safety or control.** When faced with a reminder of past trauma, a person may "switch off" in order to feel safe or regain control [msg #193].
3. **Amplifying non-belonging.** Since cPTSD is often accompanied by shame, self-blame or a *loss of connection with oneself*, OBE can strengthen the feeling of "not belonging" to one's body or to reality [msg #193].
4. **Flashbacks.** During flashbacks a person may "relive" the trauma in the first person while simultaneously feeling separated from their body [msg #193].
5. **Helplessness memories.** OBE can arise when recalling events in which the person felt helpless, frozen or overwhelmed [msg #193].

## Detailed insights

### Definitions of dissociation
Lena Foer's definition: dissociation is a psychological state in which a person's thoughts, emotions and sense of identity seem to exist separately from them. It takes various forms — a feeling of separation from one's body, memory loss, or a feeling of falling out of reality. Dissociative symptoms can be provoked by traumatic or stressful experiences, but can also arise without visible reasons [msg #26].

Katya Klimochkina's definition: dissociation is disconnection or detachment from one's sense of self (depersonalization) or from one's surroundings (derealization). Its symptoms can affect all aspects of mental functioning — memory, identity, emotions, perception and behaviour [msg #105].

### Dissociation in BPD
Dissociation is one of the symptoms of BPD and can appear in situations of high stress. The experience of psychological trauma is considered the most significant factor in developing dissociative symptoms [msg #26]. Lena Foer cites a Springer article (Borderline Personality Disorder and Emotion Dysregulation) alongside this explanation [msg #26].

### Normal versus disordered dissociation
You may have mild dissociative symptoms after a stressful or traumatic event. For example, if you witnessed an accident, what you saw may seem unreal; these feelings of "unreality" can last several days until what happened is "digested". When it is part of a mental disorder, dissociation can be a constant experience affecting a person's daily life [msg #105].

### Triggers
A trigger is a reminder of something traumatic from the past that can cause you to dissociate. It can be something you hear, see, taste, smell or touch; it can also be a specific situation or a particular way your body moves. Triggers can be very diverse things [msg #105].

### Frida Kahlo's "Accident" as an illustration of OBE
In 1925 Frida Kahlo was in a serious bus accident that caused severe injuries, multiple fractures and chronic pain. In her work *"The Accident"* («Авария») she depicted herself watching her injured body from above — a vivid image of what is often called an out-of-body experience. Katya Klimochkina reads the drawing as reflecting not only the psychological shock of the event but also how «разум может инстинктивно «покинуть» тело, когда реальность становится слишком сложной для восприятия» (*the mind can instinctively "leave" the body when reality becomes too hard to perceive*). The attached image is the drawing; its legible inscription reads roughly "…de 1926 – Frida Kahlo (Accidente)" [msg #192, img #192].

### Further features of OBE
- A sensation of "floating" outside the body and watching oneself from the side; this state is sometimes *confused* with dreams of flying [msg #193].
- The experience may be accompanied by a sense of calm, but sometimes causes anxiety or confusion [msg #193].
- Some people feel they cannot return to their body until the state ends on its own [msg #193].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The authors write in a popular-psychology register; aside from the one Springer link in #26, no studies or data are cited for the lists of symptoms, causes and OBE–cPTSD links [msg #26, #105, #193].
- (!) UNCLEAR: "breathe in a rectangle" (дышите по прямоугольнику) is named but the phases/durations of the rectangle are not specified; only that the inhale should be a little shorter than the exhale [msg #105].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the 5-4-3-2-1 card's OCR text is column-scrambled; the step-to-sense pairing is reconstructed from the post's own wording, which is unambiguous [msg #146, img #146].
- The image OCR for #192 is low-confidence (0.4); the inscription appears to say 1926 while the text dates the accident to 1925 — this may simply be the date of the drawing, the source does not comment [msg #192, img #192].
- Lena Foer stresses that dissociation can arise "without visible reasons", while also naming trauma as the most significant factor — both are stated, not reconciled [msg #26].
- 5-4-3-2-1 is explicitly presented as a first step, not a solution to stress or thinking patterns [msg #146].
