# Avoidance, rumination, anxiety and exposure

## What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's teaching on what people do to escape strong emotions and why it backfires: the emotion-as-threat attitude, five types of emotional avoidance, rumination and its "positive" relatives (luminiscing, reverie, maladaptive daydreaming, reward-focused attention, emotional amplification), recognising anxiety and anxiety disorders, and exposure therapy, from the practical "exposure lifestyle" to the inhibitory-learning view of why facing trauma helps. According to the source this matters because the suffering of people with strong emotions comes less from the emotions themselves than from the way they relate to them. Avoidance and rumination are attempts to help oneself that instead make the suffering longer and stronger and steal one's space for living [msg #63, #67, #115, #127].

This part adds one post: Lena Foer's longread «Генерализованное тревожное расстройство 101» (*Generalised anxiety disorder 101*). It covers what generalised anxiety disorder (GAD) is, the diagnostic criteria, a quick screening tool, onset age, comorbidity and risk factors, and the four factors that, according to the author, hold the disorder up "like the world on turtles": intolerance of uncertainty, positive beliefs about worry, negative attitudes to problem solving, and cognitive avoidance [msg #114, article #114]. The existing topic file mentions GAD in one line, as the anxiety disorder triggered by "a million different things", in the list of anxiety disorders from #129. This post **extends** that line into a full model. It also **extends** the topic's avoidance theme: it presents worry itself as a form of cognitive avoidance, a way of escaping fear that is rewarded with short-term relief and keeps the problem going [article #114].

The author gives two reasons the subject matters. GAD is not the most common anxiety disorder (specific phobias are), but it beats the others in how much it impairs normal functioning: working, studying, resting and building relationships [article #114]. And it is a trap: "чем больше вы пытаетесь избавиться от тревоги, тем сильнее она сжимает вас" (*the more you try to get rid of the anxiety, the tighter it squeezes you*) [msg #114].

## Core ideas

- **The problem is not strong emotions but the attitude toward them.** Seeing negative feelings as dangerous, and putting maximum effort into avoiding them, creates the suffering. Each escape confirms that emotions are unbearable [msg #63].
- **Avoidance is a short-term pseudo-solution.** It gives relief for minutes. Over time it brings worse well-being and no skills for tolerating one's states. The remedy is "don't avoid": tolerate what is unpleasant but not dangerous [msg #115].
- **Rumination is a failed coping strategy.** Like avoidance, it aims to reduce suffering but prolongs and intensifies it. It keeps you "on the crest of the wave" [msg #127].
- **Pleasant stuck thinking is also a form of escape.** Luminiscing and related phenomena reflect a "tendency toward anaesthesia and leaving reality". Such thinking helps only if it leads to action or to the thoughts fading [msg #237, #238].
- **Anxiety is about an imagined future and fear is about a concrete thing.** Anxiety is normal. It needs professional help when it is intense, long-lasting and disrupts life [msg #129].
- **Exposure works by gradually approaching what one fears instead of avoiding it.** According to the modern view, it works through new learning (inhibitory learning), not necessarily through anxiety going down [msg #67, #250].

- **GAD works like the Addams Family finger trap.** The harder you try to get rid of anxiety, the tighter it grips. GAD is described as viscous and elusive, pulling you into an endless cycle of worrying thoughts [msg #114].
- **GAD rests on four factors.** These are low tolerance of uncertainty (the most important one and specific to GAD), positive beliefs about worry, negative attitudes to problem solving, and cognitive avoidance [article #114].
- **Worry is a substitute for fear.** Worry is verbal, while fear runs on images, so worrying is less unpleasant than facing fear directly. That relief reinforces worrying, but fear passes within about half an hour while worry can last hours or days [article #114].
- **Recovery depends on beliefs and tolerance, not skill.** People with GAD solve problems as well as anyone else. What predicts improvement is believing less in the usefulness of worry, changing attitudes to problem solving, staying with fear instead of fleeing it, and tolerating uncertainty better [article #114].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### The avoidance loop: why escaping emotions reinforces the problem
Lena Foer reconstructs, in the first person, what happens inside someone who treats emotions as a threat [msg #63]:
1. **The emotion is experienced as catastrophic.** On sinking into the emotion, the person feels: emotions are unbearable; "Это никогда не закончится" (*this will never end*); "Я этого не выдержу, умру/сойду с ума/буду страдать бесконечно" (*I won't survive it, I'll die / go mad / suffer endlessly*); I must do something urgently to stop it; I'm ready to do anything to stop it; if anyone else is nearby, they must do anything to stop it [msg #63].
2. **Urgent escape.** The person urgently does anything that can distract from the pain [msg #63].
3. **Short-term relief.** In the moment they feel relief [msg #63].
4. **Long-term reinforcement.** In the long run the problem has only been strengthened. The person has "confirmed" that emotions are unbearable, can kill or drive one mad, and that the only rescue is running from them [msg #63].
5. **A lost chance to learn.** The author's metaphor: again and again "я вижу тень ужасного монстра на стене и бегу от нее" (*I see the shadow of a terrible monster on the wall and run from it*). Each time the person loses the chance to find out that there is no monster and no threat, and that they are safe. Emotions pass, they are not life-threatening, and they don't drive you mad [msg #63].

Key number: if nothing were done, the emotion would pass by itself in **30–40 minutes**. The author adds two conditions: the person does not keep spinning the thoughts connected with it, and they have left the situation that caused it [msg #63]. Her conclusion is that the core problem lies in perceiving emotion as a threat that must be urgently avoided. Emotions themselves are part of life, which is impossible without pain and emotions. "Но знаете что? Они проходят." (*But you know what? They pass.*) [msg #63]

### Five types of emotional avoidance
Katya Klimochkina lists the types of emotional avoidance that "составляют основу многих эмоциональных проблем" (*form the basis of many emotional problems*) [msg #115]:
1. **Situational (Ситуативное):** avoiding people, places and activities [msg #115].
2. **Cognitive (Когнитивное):** avoiding thoughts, images and memories [msg #115].
3. **Somatic (Соматическое):** avoiding internal sensations such as a racing pulse, palpitations, shortness of breath, feeling hot, tiredness or unwanted sexual arousal [msg #115].
4. **Protective (Протективное):** avoiding uncertainty through re-checking, cleaning, perfectionism, procrastination, talking oneself out of things, or seeking reassurance from others [msg #115].
5. **Substitutive (Замещающее):** avoiding some painful emotions by means of other emotions: through numbness, alcohol, drugs, overeating, gambling, or anything else that gives different emotions [msg #115].

### Seven reasons avoidance does not work
The author's thesis is that the consequences are the problem: "Избегание хуже, чем опыт, которого мы пытаемся избежать" (*Avoidance is worse than the experience we try to avoid*) [msg #115]:
1. **It is only a short-term pseudo-solution.** Stress and anxiety are part of life and can't be outrun [msg #115].
2. **It reinforces the belief that discomfort is unbearable or dangerous.** Ideas like «я не выдержу тревогу» (*I won't withstand the anxiety*) or «тревога = опасна» (*anxiety = dangerous*) reduce the ability to bear pain, which is still part of life [msg #115].
3. **It takes effort.** You can't "just avoid". It is tiring and energy-consuming. Refusing to see people or go to certain places is one thing. Controlling your own thinking so as not to think certain thoughts is far harder [msg #115].
4. **It often simply doesn't work.** Telling yourself not to think about something forces you to think about not thinking about it. When we try to avoid an emotion, we feel it one way or another [msg #115].
5. **It deprives us of a good-quality life and of living in the present** [msg #115].
6. **It blocks movement toward a good life in the future** [msg #115].
7. **As a favourite strategy it leads to broader harm:** feelings of helplessness, hopelessness and disappointment, relationship problems, and lower quality of life through missed opportunities that were refused [msg #115].

What to do: "**Не избегать**" (*don't avoid*). How: "**Выдерживая** то, что мы избегаем, а оно не опасно, но неприятно" (*by tolerating what we avoid, which is not dangerous but unpleasant*) [msg #115].

### The rumination process (three forms)
Rumination is defined as «мыслительная жвачка» (*mental chewing gum*): turning thoughts round in a circle, thinking again and again about something sad, hurtful or anxious, while nothing actually happens [msg #127]. Its goal, like that of emotional avoidance, is to reduce emotional suffering, but it worsens and prolongs it. Rumination keeps us "on the crest of the emotional wave" when we could long ago have swum to shore, that is, been distracted and forgotten the situation we keep "sucking on". If we let ourselves experience the emotions instead of hiding from them in rumination, they pass faster. Paradoxically, "пропаниковать" может быть легче, чем убегать от паники (*"panicking it through" may be easier than running from panic*) [msg #127]. The process takes three forms:

1. **Evaluative thoughts** focus attention on oneself or others [msg #127].
   - *Evaluating oneself:* the hope is to "become better" or fix one's mistakes, real or only perceived. Examples: "what terrible cellulite!", "if only I had gone to the gym, but I didn't, so this is what I get", "I'm always like this, I never do anything worthwhile in life". The result of focusing on "flaws" is an even stronger sense of inferiority or worthlessness [msg #127].
   - *Evaluating others:* this can give brief relief from one's own inferiority or helplessness, but we very rarely win the comparison game. Example: "Lena is a productivity guru; of course she gets up at 6 and dives into meaningful work. I don't get up that early and lack that discipline. No wonder I do so little… what a lazy seal I am." It becomes hard to be kind to oneself, and sometimes to the person one compares oneself with. Spoiled relationships may follow if envy and irritation move from thoughts and imagination into real life [msg #127].
2. **Forecasting the future / forecasting catastrophes:** imagining catastrophes that haven't happened (and probably won't) gives the hope of planning one's actions to avoid threatening consequences. Thinking about a dreadful future, we hope to control it by "writing" negative scenarios. Fantasising about possible negative events generates chronic anxiety, and frightening forecasts about a future that doesn't yet exist become the cause of a constant sense of threat. The author's self-ironic example: everyone has unsubscribed from the channel, and she has already moved to the railway station or lives under a bridge, cold and hungry [msg #127].
3. **Explanation thoughts** try to answer the question of why this happened. Sometimes a cause is found, and it may even be possible to prevent or manage the bad thing. But often the why-question has no answer. It then leads to helplessness, or it yields the answer «из-за моих недостатков» (*because of my flaws*), meaning one concludes that bad things happened through one's own fault, which only intensifies negative emotions. Example: when facing shocks or illnesses we often "drown" in explanation thoughts, thinking that if we had acted differently, come earlier or watched more closely, everything would have been different [msg #127].

The author's verdict: it is normal to want to make oneself a bit better, solve problems and control events, but rumination is a poor helper. It only makes emotional suffering stronger, more intense and vivid, "хотя в моменте почему-то мы верим в обратное" (*although in the moment, for some reason, we believe the opposite*) [msg #127].

### Exposure therapy: types, formats and graded ladders
**Why it is needed.** When something causes worry and anxiety we tend to avoid it. Examples: refusing to give talks with various excuses when performing is scary, or saying you're ill to skip a meeting with a friend that makes you uneasy. The more we avoid, the scarier it is to do the same thing next time. By giving in to the urge to dodge again and again, we strengthen our conviction that the danger is serious, and so "избегание крадет у нас пространство для жизни" (*avoidance steals our space for living*). This happens in PTSD, social anxiety, obsessive-compulsive disorder, panic disorder and phobias, "and not only" [msg #67].

**Definition.** Exposure therapy is a type of cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT). It helps people overcome fears by gradually getting closer to the things that frighten them [msg #67]. The comic shared to explain it puts it this way: you expose yourself to what you irrationally fear, bees in this case, and they become less frightening [img #68].

**Three types of exposure** [msg #67]:
1. **Imaginal exposure:** describing in detail and visualising what causes terror. Examples: being infected with a deadly virus in OCD, or the trauma story in PTSD [msg #67].
2. **In vivo (real-life) exposure:** facing the frightening object or situation in real life. Examples: going to the doctor, or small talk with a colleague [msg #67].
3. **Virtual-reality exposure:** a VR headset lets one face frightening things, such as being among snakes or spiders, in a safe and controlled environment [msg #67].

**Two formats** [msg #67]:
1. **Formal:** therapist and client draw up a step-by-step plan at the start [msg #67].
2. **Informal:** approaching what frightens us when an opportunity arises, without a plan made in advance [msg #67].

**Graded ladders (author's examples).** The principle in both is to move to the next rung once the current one can be done "without feeling too much anxiety". Throughout the process the therapist gives support and guidance [msg #67].
- *Spider phobia (arachnophobia):* (1) look at pictures of spiders → (2) once that is possible without too much anxiety, watch videos of spiders → (3) eventually try holding a spider [msg #67].
- *Fear of public speaking:* (1) speak in front of a mirror → (2) then in front of one person → (3) eventually try speaking to a small group [msg #67].

The author's verdict: exposure therapy is difficult and frightening, but very effective [msg #67].

### Exposure lifestyle (self-help, 4 steps)
Lena Foer calls «экспозиционный стиль жизни» (*exposure lifestyle*) a "great term" and describes what it means [msg #67]:
1. **First learn to notice how anxiety and fear show up in you.** Ask yourself: what do I usually feel in my body when anxious? What do I usually start doing in such moments? Some reach for the phone, some talk others' ears off, some go to the fridge. What thoughts come up when I'm scared? [msg #67]
2. **Train yourself to notice when everyday things cause you anxiety or fear** [msg #67].
3. **If the object or situation is safe, then on catching the urge to dodge, approach instead** [msg #67].
4. **Repeat this at random moments, anywhere, anytime,** "как если бы вся жизнь была вашей тренировочной площадкой" (*as if all of life were your training ground*) [msg #67].

Example: you walk past a café and think "it would be awkward to go in alone". Catching that thought, you turn toward the café and go in for a latte [msg #67]. According to the author, an exposure lifestyle can bring adventures, new stories and joy from one's own courage into everyday life [msg #67].

### Recognising anxiety and anxiety disorders
**Definition.** Anxiety arises when we think about an uncertain and frightening future and picture the bad things that might happen. Wandering through "scenario forks" and finding possible vulnerabilities and dangers, we become anxious [msg #129].

**Anxiety vs fear** [msg #129]:
- *Fear* is a reaction to something definite, such as mice, spiders or the boss. It is always tied to a concrete event or entity in the real world.
- *Anxiety* is a reaction to versions of an uncertain future that we generate ourselves. "Если источник страха снаружи, то источник тревоги - внутри нас" (*If the source of fear is outside, the source of anxiety is inside us*).

**How to recognise anxiety: three channels** [msg #129]:
1. **Thoughts:** mulling over problems of a possible future, what might happen and how to cope with it.
2. **Urge to act:** wanting either to distract oneself as much as possible and procrastinate (doomscrolling is "ideal"), or the opposite: urgently looking for solutions to problems that haven't arisen yet.
3. **Body sensations:** motor restlessness, wanting to move something, act, an "inner motor", or the opposite, freezing.

**Anxiety disorders.** Anxiety itself is a normal human feeling and it is natural to experience it. Sometimes, though, there is so much of it, and it affects life so strongly and for so long, that it is defined as an anxiety disorder. Disorders based specifically on anxiety [msg #129]:
1. **Panic disorder:** anxiety is triggered by physical sensations. Heartbeat, shortness of breath or dizziness seem to signal a serious threat (dying, fainting, going mad).
2. **Generalised anxiety disorder:** anxiety is triggered by "a million different things", from trifles to global world problems, often with many physical symptoms.
3. **Separation anxiety:** worry over separation from someone significant. More common in children, but it also occurs in adults.

Two deliberate exclusions from that list [msg #129]:
- **Social anxiety** is not on the list because it consists of both anxiety and fear. Example: before a public talk, fear arises when you think of walking on stage and seeing people looking at you (likely to happen, a concrete object). Anxiety arises when you imagine scenarios of messing up the talk (no object, only future scenarios in your head).
- **Health anxiety** is not on the list because, strictly speaking, it is not anxiety but a kind of obsessive-compulsive disorder ("шок-контент, да", *shock content, yes*). Obsessive fears of falling ill are accompanied by compulsive re-checking, check-ups, googling and preventive use of folk remedies.

### When to seek help for anxiety (4 criteria)
1. **Intensity and duration:** the anxiety is strong and lasts more than **two weeks**, every day or nearly every day [msg #129].
2. **Impact on life:** disturbed sleep, or difficulties at work, in study or in personal relationships [msg #129].
3. **Physical symptoms:** constant headaches, muscle tension, stomach problems [msg #129].
4. **You can't cope alone:** you can neither distract yourself, nor calm yourself, nor find a solution [msg #129].

Therapy can start with either a psychologist or a psychiatrist. According to the author, competent specialists will refer you to colleagues if necessary: if talking therapy is needed alongside medication, and vice versa [msg #129].

### Family of pleasant "stuck thinking" phenomena
**Starting observation.** An Instagram feed can fill with reels like «познакомилась с парнем, он дотронулся до моей руки, поэтому готовлюсь к свадьбе» (*met a guy, he touched my hand, so I'm preparing for the wedding*). The author says the "pain" of such reels is not the hasty wedding. It is the fantasising, getting stuck in thoughts and replaying scenarios and probabilities "about a shared future". These bring pleasant emotions, but it was just a touch of hands, or perhaps an act of friendliness, or just a like. She quotes "grandpa Freud": sometimes a banana is just a banana [msg #237].

**Rumination (the negative baseline):** passive, repetitive thinking about one's feelings, mistakes or past events without constructive action. It is like a viscous "swamp" of thoughts, a chewing gum of thoughts "that tastes bad". Example: replaying a conflict with a partner over and over, analysing who is to blame, returning to thoughts about one's own blunders, and nothing changes. One thinks "before sleep", blames oneself, and that's it [msg #237].

The phenomena, which according to Katya all reflect "**тенденцию к анестезии и уходу от реальности**" (*a tendency toward anaesthesia and leaving reality*), only in a pleasant direction [msg #238]:
1. **Luminiscing:** getting stuck in thoughts that seem illuminating and give a sense of revelation, usually positive and inspiring. It feels as if we are reflecting on something important and deep that fills us with feelings and meaning and gives satisfaction and a smile. In reality we often just stay in a pleasant loop of thinking without moving forward "in reality". The author notes this is not a scientific term. It is something like "positive rumination": repeatedly returning to emotionally rich thoughts, memories or images that evoke a mixed feeling, seemingly pleasant, with insight and obvious significance, but also somehow a little painful [msg #237]. Compared with ordinary rumination, luminiscing:
   - is not fully avoidant;
   - is emotionally attractive;
   - often gives a sense of meaning or self-understanding;
   - can be creative and self-exploratory;
   - but when excessive, leads to exhaustion and creates an illusion of action instead of real action [msg #237].
   Example: after a short but emotionally intense acquaintance, a person replays dialogues, intonations and glances for a long time and searches for subtext (yes, exactly like those reels). It feels as if life has gained meaning and one must keep thinking about it; the brain just won't switch off [msg #237]. Guidance by amount of evidence: if there was only a like on a story or polite communication, you can shake off this stuck state without major damage to your emotional state. On one glance you can mentally hold a whole wedding, but at some point doubts may arise. If there are more signals, or even real facts of mutual attraction, you can boldly call in an army of girlfriends to stop you in your fantasies and bring you back down to earth [msg #237]. The author's verdict: getting stuck in developing scenarios is great, but only up to a point. It is not classic rumination, because the emotions are pleasant, but the process is similar: **застревание в самоусиливающемся воспоминании** (*getting stuck in a self-reinforcing memory*). "Getting hooked" and "getting stuck" are the key words, because they show both the positive and the negative side of such thinking [msg #237].
2. **Reverie:** gentle, self-absorbed "floating" in thoughts, images and fantasies, usually on the topics most interesting to you. The author says "jokes about erotic fantasies are not really jokes" and they belong here. It is a way of leaving reality for a safe space of the mind, a pleasant breather. A "dreamy fixation" on free mind-wandering can help creativity and emotion regulation. Example: in a boring meeting you suddenly start dreaming about a trip [msg #238].
3. **Daydreaming and maladaptive daydreaming:** a more intense version. The person spends hours in positive fantasies, which brings emotional satisfaction but reduces engagement in real life. Unlike reverie, these are more intense, compulsive fantasies that are specifically an escape from reality and therefore damage it: dissatisfaction with reality appears, along with an inability to do anything about it, and the capacity to "cope with life" decreases. The author's hedged caveat ("Не утверждаю", *I'm not asserting*): if you invent the map of Middle-earth and a whole universe in your head and then actually write The Silmarillion or The Hobbit, these are not just thoughts for their own sake. There is a result in the form of behaviour, and if fantasy is realised in actions, the "usefulness of the activity" can be argued [msg #238].
4. **Reward-focused attention (cognitive elaboration):** the brain concentrates on a stimulus that gives pleasure, such as attention, liking or success, and repeatedly reactivates the related emotions. Example: someone mentally "re-watches" again and again a meeting with a person who showed interest, imagining future scenarios. According to the author, the main driving force is the dopaminergic system, which amplifies the emotional significance of the event. In short: a cyclic focus on a pleasant stimulus that causes positive emotions and a desire to repeat them [msg #238].
5. **Emotional amplification:** the brain focuses on emotions and intensifies them through repeated thinking, which can be positive or negative. Anxiety before an event grows if one thinks about it again and again, just as joy grows in anticipating meeting friends. Such amplification sometimes helps: it is a form of rehearsing the future, emotional preparation for an event. The author links it to the DBT skill *Справляться заранее* (*Cope Ahead*) [msg #238].

**Unifying criterion.** All these phenomena are variations of one process: the mind tries to make sense of emotional experience, and sometimes it gets carried away into a "happy ever after in fantasies with the first person you meet". If the process leads to action and integration of the experience ("you thought about it for a couple of days and then felt better"), it is useful. If the thoughts are cyclic and self-soothing but lead neither to change nor to the thoughts fading (without new stimuli appearing), the process turns into a "cosy swamp" [msg #238].

### From habituation to inhibitory learning: why face trauma in therapy
Katya Klimochkina calls exposure one of the most "controversial" methods of psychotherapy to this day. She says she loves it and considers it one of the simplest and most effective ways to work with everything "we avoid in vain" [msg #250].

**Definition used:** exposure means **deliberately and gradually facing what a person usually avoids** in order to change what they learned from a traumatic experience. In complex PTSD (кПТСР) this can mean working with trauma memories, situations that remind of the trauma, body sensations, emotions or avoidance behaviour [msg #250].

**The shift in the model** (described in a 2024 review in Nature Reviews Psychology that the author cites):
- *Before (habituation model):* good exposure was considered roughly "испугался > побыл в ситуации > тревога снизилась" (*got scared > stayed in the situation > anxiety decreased*). The assumption was that the person gets used to the fear: anxiety falls and, through that, the attitude to the situation changes [msg #250].
- *Now:* a drop in anxiety during exposure is **not considered a necessary condition of effectiveness**. The focus is on what new learning happens during exposure [msg #250].
- *Inhibitory learning* is learning that does not necessarily erase the old association «это опасно» (*this is dangerous*). Instead it **forms a new one that competes with it** [msg #250].

**Four new learnings in the inhibitory-learning logic** [msg #250]:
1. **Memory ≠ danger now:** "I can remember what happened and at the same time be in the present and do what I want to do."
2. **Strong emotion ≠ loss of control:** "I can feel really awful, scared or ashamed, and that doesn't mean I'll stop controlling myself or my behaviour."
3. **I can "contact" the traumatic experience:** "I don't have to immediately distract myself, dissociate, suppress the memory or leave the situation."
4. **My prediction didn't come true:** "I expected X (something terrible) to happen, but X didn't happen." The author stresses: "**И это важнее того, насколько сильно я испугался.**" (*And this is more important than how scared I got.*)

**Bottom line.** The aim of exposure is not necessarily for the person to come out with zero anxiety. Sometimes they are still scared, and sometimes the memory still causes strong emotions. But a new experience appears: "**я могу выдерживать контакт с этим воспоминанием, не используя привычное избегание, и то, чего я ожидал, не происходит**" (*I can tolerate contact with this memory without using my usual avoidance, and what I expected doesn't happen*). So the point of facing traumatic experience in therapy is not to relive it once more. It is to **gain a new experience** where the psyche had earlier learned «этого надо избегать» (*this must be avoided*) [msg #250].

### GAD diagnostic criteria (4 conditions)
According to the longread, to be diagnosed with GAD you must have all of the following [article #114]:
1. **Excessive worry or anxiety ("anxious anticipation").** You feel it most days of the week for **6 months or more**. It is about many different activities and events, not one single issue [article #114].
2. **A sense that the worry is hard to control** [article #114].
3. **At least three of these symptoms caused by the anxiety and worry:** muscle tension; difficulty concentrating; a feeling of emptiness in the head (mind going blank); fatigue; irritability; sleep disturbance; feeling "on the edge", keyed up [article #114].
4. **Significant distress and difficulties in different areas of life,** with symptoms not explained by substance use or another disorder [article #114].

**Quick self-check.** For a rough check of whether you might have GAD, the author recommends the **GAD-7** questionnaire. It has only 7 questions and gives an overall picture you can then take to a psychiatrist [article #114].

### Four-factor model of GAD ("What your GAD is made of")
The author summarises the mechanisms in a hand-drawn diagram titled «Из чего сделано ваше ГТР» (*What your GAD is made of*). At the top is **low tolerance of uncertainty** with its three sub-points: threatening interpretations of vague information; a lot of time and energy needed to decide anything; and a lot of anxiety about the consequences of decisions. Arrows lead from it down to the other three factors, each with its own numbered list [img #114]:
- **Positive beliefs about worry:** (1) helps find solutions; (2) if something happens, it won't be so bad later because I've prepared; (3) motivates me to get things done; (4) in itself helps prevent bad things; (5) makes you a responsible and caring person [img #114].
- **Negative attitudes to problem solving:** (1) perceiving the problem as frightening/heavy; (2) seeing oneself as a poor candidate for solving it; (3) anticipating everything going wrong [img #114].
- **Cognitive avoidance:** (1) worrying thoughts as a way to "talk over" (заболтать) frightening images in the brain; (2) suppressing anxious thoughts; (3) replacing positive thoughts with anxious ones; (4) using distractions; (5) avoiding situations where anxiety might arise [img #114].

Each factor is explained in full below.

#### Factor 1. Intolerance of uncertainty
This is the **first and most important factor**. It is also **specific** to GAD, meaning it can be used to tell people with GAD apart from people without it: those who find uncertainty harder to bear are the ones with GAD [article #114]. Uncertainty is constant. The author's examples are whether the rouble will rise within a week, whether the shop will have your favourite yoghurts, whether a cake has gone off after three days in the fridge, and what inflation will be by the end of the year. Life is made of millions of things we can't know for sure. Some people accept that easily, while some suffer from every such encounter, and research, as the author reports it, puts people with GAD in the second group [article #114].

Three main reasons uncertainty causes so much frustration [article #114]:
1. **Negative interpretations of ambiguous information come to mind first.** If the scariest version always comes first whenever you don't know something for sure, every such case causes frustration. The author's example: her boss calls an unscheduled meeting. Anyone would worry a little, since it might mean changes at work (new duties? something to do differently?). With GAD, she says, she would suffer far more, because her first thought would be that they want to fire her or at least seriously scold her [article #114].
2. **Maximum information-gathering before decisions.** Even in slightly unclear situations, people with GAD try to collect as much information as possible before deciding. Every decision therefore takes a lot of energy and time, and the person probably spends all that time in worrying thoughts, going through options and possible futures [article #114].
3. **Worry about decisions already made.** Were they right? Could I have done better? What will come of it now? This also uses up resources and causes a lot of distress [article #114].

These three mechanisms produce a **fear of uncertainty**, which in turn contributes to the disorder [article #114]. (In the diagram the same three items appear as the sub-points of the top factor, with arrows to the other three factors [img #114].)

#### Factor 2. Positive beliefs about worry
**Definition used.** Throughout the longread, «беспокойство» (*worry*) means not the emotion but an intrusive thinking process that often goes with anxiety. Examples are endlessly turning over what might go wrong, how to avoid it, what to choose, and what will happen if the wrong choice has already been made. People with GAD spend a lot of time in this state [article #114].

Worrying thoughts are so intrusive and persistent for **two reasons** [article #114]:
1. People with GAD usually believe strongly in the value of these thoughts.
2. The thoughts really do bring relief in the moment, even though overall they spoil life. This relief is explained under Factor 4.

**The most common positive beliefs about worry** (each is a belief held by the person, not the author's claim) [article #114]:
1. «Беспокойство помогает искать решения» (*Worry helps find solutions*). While I worry and fret, I'm looking for a way out of the situation.
2. **Worry as preparation:** if I worry in advance, then when something bad does happen it won't be such a shock, because I'll already be ready.
3. **Worry as motivation:** worry makes me act, and without it I couldn't get started.
4. **Worry as prevention:** worry itself helps prevent bad things, so if I worry they won't happen.
5. **Worry as virtue:** if I worry, I'm a good person, responsible and caring.

Different people hold different beliefs. What unites them, the author writes, is that the success of GAD therapy depends directly on how far the client manages to revise these positive beliefs and trust them less. "Те, кто начинает меньше верить им, испытывает меньше симптомов ГТР" (*Those who begin to believe them less experience fewer GAD symptoms*) [article #114].

#### Factor 3. Negative attitudes to problem solving
Problem-solving ability has **two components** [article #114]:
1. **Problem-solving skills:** identifying and describing the problem, generating solutions, choosing a suitable one, implementing it, and analysing the results.
2. **One's attitude to problem solving.**

According to research cited by the author, people with GAD are **no worse at the skill** than anyone else. What differs is the attitude [article #114]:
1. **The problem looks big and frightening.** Given its scale, it is unclear how to even approach it.
2. **Low belief in one's own ability** to solve this particular problem, or anything at all.
3. **Many thoughts about how everything could go wrong** and how unpleasant the consequences of a wrong choice or decision could be.

All three beliefs make solving anything much harder and slower and create extra ground for anxiety [article #114]. The evidence offered is a study the author describes in which GAD patients and "people from the street" first solved an imaginary problem and then came up with solutions to a real problem from their own lives. Both groups did equally well on the imaginary problem. On real problems the GAD patients had much more difficulty, which the author attributes to these attitudes [article #114].

#### Factor 4. Cognitive avoidance (why worry "pays off")
The author calls this "the most interesting part": the benefit and payoff of worry [article #114]. The mechanism has four steps:
1. **Fear is image-based.** Everyone gets frightened sometimes. Fear is usually tied to visual images: we vividly picture what scares us, and that can knock us off balance [article #114].
2. **Worry is verbal.** Unlike fear, worry is not tied to imagery. Worrying thoughts are usually verbal constructions in the mind, without pictures. So worry, however unpleasant, is less unpleasant than fear head-on, and that is why we often replace fear with worry [article #114].
3. **The relief reinforces the switch.** Replacing fear with worry is what is called **cognitive avoidance**. Because worrying thoughts are easier than fear and its images, you are reinforced: worrying feels like the best choice, and next time you take the same path faster and with higher probability [article #114].
4. **The catch: duration.** Fear, even very strong fear, is short-lived like other emotions. According to the author, it passes without a trace within **half an hour**. Worrying thoughts do not pass: they can stay with you for **hours and even days** [article #114].

**The author's example (an exam).** You are thinking about an upcoming exam. You won't get your diploma without it, the teacher is a beast, and everything is very serious. Your brain shows you an image of yourself sitting helplessly with a hard exam ticket in front of the teacher while he mocks you. That image is too heavy to stay near, so your brain finds a solution: you swap the terrible picture for worrying thoughts about how best to approach studying, what time to set the alarm, whom to ask for help, and what will happen if nobody helps. It is still unpleasant, but not as heavy as the original image, and you feel better [article #114].

**Other mechanisms of cognitive avoidance** [article #114]:
1. **Suppressing worrying thoughts and trying to distract yourself.** The pitfall: when we try not to think of the white bear, it is very hard to think of anything but it. (The diagram lists suppression and the use of distractions as two separate items [img #114].)
2. **Replacing positive thoughts with anxious ones**, in case something bad happens while you're enjoying yourself.
3. **Avoiding any situation where anxiety might arise.**

### What reduces GAD's grip (the author's summary)
The author's closing rule follows the four factors [article #114]:
1. The less you believe in the usefulness of worry,
2. the more your attitudes to problem solving change,
3. the more often you choose to stay with your fear rather than run from it,
4. and the easier it is for you to tolerate uncertainty, chaos and unpredictability,

the better you will feel and the less GAD will affect your life [article #114].

## Detailed insights

### General characteristics of GAD
- **Symptoms:** anxiety is triggered by many different everyday things. Endless worrying thoughts are often accompanied by fatigue, irritability, sleep problems, muscle tension and inability to concentrate [article #114].
- **Prevalence and impact:** not the most common anxiety disorder (specific phobias are the most common), but the most impairing for normal functioning in work, study, rest and relationships [article #114].
- **Onset:** relatively late on average, **around age 30** [article #114].
- **Comorbidity:** roughly **one in five** patients with GAD has other diagnoses as well, most often depression [article #114].
- **Course:** considered chronic. It can vary in intensity over life, receding and then returning [article #114].
- **Risk factors:** both genetic and environmental. Mistreatment and traumatic episodes in childhood are significant environmental factors. There are observations, as the author puts it, that GAD develops more often in people who were quiet and shy as children and tried not to touch anything unnecessarily [article #114].

## Practical playbook

- When an emotion seems unbearable, remember the author's estimate that, left alone, it passes in about 30–40 minutes, provided you stop spinning the thoughts and leave the triggering situation (see *The avoidance loop*) [msg #63].
- To audit yourself, check which of the five avoidance types you use, then practise tolerating what is unpleasant but not dangerous (see *Five types of emotional avoidance*) [msg #115].
- If you catch yourself replaying a pleasant encounter over and over, weigh the actual evidence: a like or polite exchange versus real facts of mutual interest. In the latter case, enlist friends to bring you "back down to earth" [msg #237].
- Test of usefulness for any repetitive thinking: does it lead to action, integration or the thoughts fading within a couple of days? If not, it is a "swamp" [msg #238].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The 30–40 minute figure for an emotion to pass is the author's statement, conditional on not continuing to spin thoughts and leaving the triggering situation. No source is given [msg #63].
- "Luminiscing" is explicitly not a scientific term according to the author, who says she recently learned about it [msg #237].
- The Silmarillion/Hobbit remark about productive daydreaming is hedged ("Не утверждаю", *I'm not asserting*) [msg #238].
- Exposure is described as "controversial" and as difficult and frightening, yet very effective, according to both authors [msg #67, #250].
- The classification of health anxiety as a form of OCD rather than anxiety is the author's framing ("strictly speaking") [msg #129].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the exposure comic ends with Hugo panicking and running away once the bee lands on him. The post does not explain whether this is meant to show a pitfall, such as moving too fast, or is just a joke [img #70, img #72, msg #68].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the post on inhibitory learning promised a fuller answer in a live stream; only the summary in the post is available [msg #250].

- The studies behind several claims (that intolerance of uncertainty distinguishes GAD; that people with GAD have intact problem-solving skills; the imaginary-vs-real problem experiment; that therapy success depends on revising positive beliefs about worry) are described but not named or cited [article #114].
- The link between childhood shyness and GAD is given as "observations", not as established fact [article #114].
- "Within half an hour" for fear to pass is the author's statement without a source. It is close to the "30–40 minutes" for an emotion in #63 of the existing file, but not the same [article #114].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the text says the three uncertainty mechanisms lead to a fear of uncertainty that "contributes" to the disorder. The diagram places intolerance of uncertainty at the top with arrows to the other three factors, which suggests it drives them. - The GAD-7 is presented as a rough screening ("на скорую руку", *quick and dirty*) that gives a general picture for a psychiatrist, not as a diagnosis [article #114].
