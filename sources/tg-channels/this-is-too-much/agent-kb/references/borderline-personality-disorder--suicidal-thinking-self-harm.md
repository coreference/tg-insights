# Suicidal thinking and self-harm

## What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's posts on suicidality and self-harm. It covers common myths about suicide and what people around a suicidal person can do, a behavioural account of suicidal thoughts as an escape habit with four ways of working with them, passive suicidal ideation (how it differs from active ideation, its triggers, the thinking errors behind it and its warning signs), and two research retellings on what predicts and precedes self-harm in people with borderline personality disorder (BPD) [msg #12, #46, #106, #177, #178].

The source gives several reasons this matters. BPD is directly linked to higher suicide risk, but suicidal thoughts are common far beyond BPD. Suicide is described as the second leading cause of death, and it usually comes at the end of a long process that can begin passively. Self-harm is a common way for people with BPD to cope with strong emotions, so knowing its specific precursors helps match the right skills to them [msg #12, #106, #178].

## Core ideas

- **Suicidal thinking is common and widely misunderstood.** BPD raises suicide risk, but people without BPD also think about suicide. Three popular beliefs about suicide are wrong (see *Three suicide myths*) [msg #12].
- **Suicidal thoughts work like an escape habit.** Thinking about death brings relief in the moment, so the brain learns to reach for it more often as stress rises. This makes such thoughts more likely and harder to shake over time. They are a symptom to notice, not a signal to act (see *Escape-habit loop* and *Four ways to work with suicidal thoughts*) [msg #46].
- **Passive ideation is quieter but still dangerous.** It has no clear warning signs, and people experiencing it are less likely to ask for help. Since suicide develops through a long process that can start passively, it is worth checking for it in yourself and others [msg #106].
- **Lack of aversion predicts self-harm.** In BPD, feeling no fear, disgust, shame or guilt toward self-harm (and sometimes feeling arousal instead) removes the internal "brakes", which makes future self-harm more likely [msg #177].
- **Self-harm has specific emotional precursors.** A study identifies ten difficulties with awareness and emotion regulation that come before self-harm in BPD. Naming the specific factors makes it easier to choose the skills that can replace self-harm [msg #178].

## Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

### Three suicide myths and their rebuttals
This post was written for World Suicide Prevention Day (10 September). It first corrects a general misconception: BPD is directly linked to higher suicidal risk, but it would be a mistake to think people without BPD don't think about suicide. On average, **one person in five** has thought about ending their life at least once. The post then takes apart three more myths [msg #12]:

1. **Myth: talking about suicide can push someone over the edge.** The author says this is false. Talking about one's own thoughts of ending one's life is important: it helps the person consider other ways of resolving the inner crisis and helps them feel understood [msg #12].
2. **Myth: once a person has decided to kill themselves, nothing can help.** The rebuttal is that people can make decisions and also change them. Sometimes changing a decision takes help from loved ones, friends or mental-health professionals [msg #12].
3. **Myth: people who talk about plans for a suicide attempt won't actually do anything.** The author's position is that however serious the person is, words about suicide are "почти наверняка крик о помощи" (*almost certainly a cry for help*). What we can actually do for the people close to us is take their feelings seriously, without trying to guess their intentions [msg #12].

### Escape-habit loop of suicidal thoughts
Lena Foer's post "Суицидальные мысли как плохая привычка" (*Suicidal thoughts as a bad habit*) explains where suicidal thoughts come from and why they persist [msg #46].

**Origin.** Suicidal thoughts come above all from wanting to escape painful experiences and a crisis that feels like it will never end. The post describes two theoretical framings and says they are fairly close to each other [msg #46]:
- Psychoanalysts call thoughts of death a **psychological defence**, a way to distance oneself from painful experience.
- Cognitive-behavioural therapists call them **cognitive withdrawal** ("когнитивным уходом"), avoiding pain through thoughts of release.

**The loop, step by step** [msg #46]:
1. A hard event happens.
2. Thoughts of death come up as a way out ("мысли о смерти как побеге", *thoughts of death as an escape*).
3. Escaping into thoughts of death brings relief.

**Why it becomes a habit.** Because it brings relief, thinking about death is a winning strategy in the moment. The brain learns that suicidal thoughts are "useful" because they make you feel better. As stress rises, so does the number of thoughts like "лучше бы меня тут не было" (*it would be better if I weren't here*). At some point the thoughts may seem to arrive on their own, against the person's will. The author compares suicidality to a chemical addiction: once there is a habit, it is very hard to rewire, step back, or simply stop thinking it. So suicidal thoughts help in the short term, but in the long term they make the same thoughts more likely to return, and the longer this goes on, the harder they may be to get rid of [msg #46].

### Four ways to work with suicidal thoughts
From the same post, this is how suicidal thoughts are worked with in therapy [msg #46]:
1. **Use emotion-regulation skills to lower emotional intensity.** The examples given are dipping your face in ice-cold water, physical exercise, or paced breathing. The point is to bring the emotional "heat" down [msg #46].
2. **Deal with the cause that triggered the thoughts**, meaning solve the original problem. The thoughts serve as an escape from a problem, so the problem itself has to be addressed [msg #46].
3. **Reduce vulnerability to crisis emotions** through a healthy daily routine and a lighter load [msg #46].
4. **Treat suicidal thoughts as a symptom, not a signal to act.** They show that something is wrong with you right now and that it is time to urgently slow down, breathe out and pull yourself back together ("пора срочно затормозить, выдохнуть и пересобраться") [msg #46].

**Disclaimer from the author.** All of the above is meant to help readers understand where suicidal thoughts come from and how they are worked with. It is not a guide to action, because "при суицидальных намерениях руководство к действию всего одно: пожалуйста, найдите психолога, который работает с этим" (*when there are suicidal intentions, there is only one guide to action: please find a psychologist who works with this*). The author adds that suicidal thoughts usually go hand in hand with feeling isolated and unable to have valuable human contact. In that sense, talking to someone who is willing to understand is already a step toward life [msg #46].

### Passive vs active suicidal ideation — differences and when to worry
**Definition.** Katya Klimochkina calls passive suicidal thinking a complex and often misunderstood phenomenon (her post links a PubMed paper on it). It means thoughts of suicide that are not connected to a direct plan or intent to harm oneself but still involve persistent thoughts of death or dying. Her examples: the thought that you want to close your eyes and disappear, or the thought, when facing inner pain, that "меня больше не должно существовать" (*I should no longer exist*). These thoughts can range from fleeting and abstract to persistent and vivid. Some people experience passive ideation as hopelessness or despair, while others experience it as detachment or disconnection from the world around them [msg #106].

**Differences between active and passive ideation** [msg #106]:
1. **Active** suicidal thoughts include concrete plans or intent to harm oneself. They often come with clear warning signs: persistent poor well-being, planning or statements about wanting to die, and saying goodbye to loved ones.
2. **Passive** thoughts, unlike active ones, have no clear warning signs and can be harder to detect.
3. People with passive thoughts may not actively look for help or support and are less likely to talk to others about their feelings or thoughts.

**Triggers and risk factors.** Common triggers for passive suicidal thoughts are traumatic events, ongoing stressors, and mental disorders: depression or anxiety, stress disorders, or personality disorders [msg #106].

**Why check for it.** The author says suicide is the second leading cause of death and rarely happens right after someone first has a single suicidal thought. It is a long process that can begin passively, with thoughts of hopelessness and despair. That is why she says it is important to check for passive suicidality in yourself and others ("важно чекать") [msg #106].

### Cognitive distortions leading to hopelessness
The post asks what fuels the hopelessness and despair that in turn lead to passive suicidal thoughts. Its answer is a tendency toward cognitive distortions or negative thinking patterns, and it explains three [msg #106]:
1. **Black-and-white thinking** means seeing things only in two extremes, good or bad, right or wrong, with no middle ground. It can make people feel they are either successes or failures, with no room for anything in between. The author says this is especially destructive for suicidal thinking: if a person feels unsuccessful in life and the failure feels total, suicide seems like the only way out [msg #106].
2. **Catastrophizing** means imagining the worst-case scenario in any situation. For someone struggling with suicidal thoughts, this can mean believing that their problems are insurmountable and there is no way out. The author calls this especially dangerous because it can lead to the hopelessness and despair described above [msg #106].
3. **Personalization** means taking everything personally and assuming everything is about oneself. This can include assuming everyone is judging you or that you are the cause of all your problems, which leads to guilt and shame [msg #106].

### Warning signs of passive suicidal thinking
The post sorts the signs to watch for in three groups [msg #106]:
1. **Verbal and behavioural indicators:** comments about wanting to die, the feeling that life isn't worth living, or talking about death in a casual or matter-of-fact way, including through sarcasm or irony.
2. **Social and behavioural changes:** withdrawing from social activities, avoiding friends or family members, or changes in mood or behaviour that seem uncharacteristic or unusual for the person.
3. **Emotional and psychological symptoms:** persistent sadness or hopelessness, anxiety or fear, or changes in appetite, sleep or concentration. Irritability, anger or aggression may also appear.

### Ten mindfulness and emotion-regulation precursors of self-harm in BPD
Katya Klimochkina retells a study (linked on PubMed) that examined in detail which difficulties with mindfulness and emotion regulation are **precursors to self-harm** in people with BPD. She starts from a known fact: people with BPD use self-harm to cope with strong emotions. She explains why breaking it into factors matters: once the specific factors are identified, it is easier to choose the set of skills needed to cope with hard states without self-harm. The ten precursors [msg #178]:

1. **Difficulty being aware of and identifying emotions.**
2. **High emotional sensitivity and reactivity**, where reactivity means the speed of the emotional reaction.
3. **Difficulty tolerating stress.**
4. **Low ability to be aware of the present moment**, when a person is more in their thoughts than in the current situation.
5. **Difficulty regulating negative thoughts and self-critical beliefs.**
6. **Hyperfocus on negative emotions** (rumination).
7. **Dissociation.**
8. **Intolerance of uncertainty** and the urge to get rid of emotions and calm down as fast as possible.
9. **No alternative strategies** for coping with all these experiences.
10. **Impulsivity and poor impulse control.**

The author explains four of these in more detail, with how mindfulness and emotion regulation help in each case [msg #178]:

- **Alexithymia (difficulty recognizing one's emotions).** A person with BPD feels so much, so intensely and all at once that there is often no chance to work out what exactly they feel. Not understanding what is happening leads to feeling overwhelmed and confused. That is unpleasant, so self-harm feels like a way out, a way to "remove" a terrible, unidentified state. *What helps:* mindfulness, which teaches you to notice emotions without judgement as they arise, rather than when it is already too late [msg #178].
- **Low awareness of the present moment.** This makes it harder to pause and see the consequences of hurting oneself or to find other ways to cope. The result is impulsive behaviour aimed at escaping emotional pain. *What helps:* focusing on the present and putting some distance between yourself and tense thoughts and emotions by watching them a little from the outside. The author notes these skills take time to learn, but they make it possible to assess the situation more soberly [msg #178].
- **Intense emotional reactions and a slow return to baseline.** This is what the term "эмоциональная дисрегуляция" (*emotional dysregulation*) usually means. Her example: a conflict happens in the morning, and by evening the emotional trail still hasn't faded. No one wants to feel pain, emotional pain included, for a long time, so self-harm becomes a strategy to get rid of it when there are no other skills for self-soothing. *Consequence:* developing emotion-regulation skills makes it possible to replace self-harm with other, more effective and healthier strategies [msg #178].
- **Dissociation**, a feeling of being cut off from your thoughts, feelings, physical sensations and even the world. The author says this state has its pros and cons, but it is uncomfortable to be in. Self-harm can serve as **grounding**, bringing the person back to the present through physical sensation. *What helps:* in her words "Прозвучит душно" (*this will sound tedious*), but mindfulness exercises can help you stay connected to your body and the present moment. They offer other ways of grounding that lower the risk of dissociation and the need for self-harm [msg #178].

The other six factors are listed without further explanation. The author directs readers to the article or to the comments for questions about them [msg #178].

**Conclusion.** Learning to tolerate emotional stress, become more aware of your emotions without judging them, and replace self-harm with other strategies takes time and persistence, but in the end it pays off [msg #178].

## Detailed insights

### Not feeling aversion to self-harm as a risk factor (study retelling)
Katya Klimochkina gives a free retelling of an article (Wiley, *Personality and Mental Health*) she calls charmingly titled: "Загадка намеренного самоповреждения: физиологические и субъективные эффекты самоконтроля при самоповреждении при ПРЛ" (*The puzzle of deliberate self-harm: physiological and subjective effects of self-control in self-harm in BPD*). The study compared how people with and without BPD react to images and descriptions of self-harm. It focused on how a person with BPD perceives such cues and how that relates to their tendency toward self-destructive behaviour in the future. Her findings, by point [msg #177]:

1. **No negative feelings toward self-harm images means higher risk.** If a person has no negative feelings toward descriptions and pictures of self-harm, this may be linked to a risk of future self-harm. In her first-person phrasing: if I feel nothing looking at photos of self-harm scars on a body, I may be more likely to use this regulation strategy on myself. People with BPD who are not afraid of hurting themselves (the article talks about cutting) may be less sensitive to self-harm. This is compared to habituation: they have grown used to the act and the image, so they see nothing special in it, which makes it easier to decide to self-harm in the future. If a person feels no disgust at self-harm, they may see it as a normal way to cope with stress, something ordinary that isn't given much weight. Her analogies: "работаю на рыбном заводе, поэтому не чувствую вони запаха рыбы" (*I work at a fish factory, so I don't notice the fish smell*). Or: if the atmosphere of the TV series «Слово пацана» (*"The Boy's Word"*) is your normal setting, violence, fights, swearing and alcohol don't hurt you because they are familiar. You have seen enough that it no longer stirs any emotion [msg #177].
2. **Discomfort is a natural brake.** "Normally", people feel uneasy when thinking about doing something that could hurt them. Behind this are the self-preservation instinct, the wish to keep oneself intact, and not wanting to feel pain. In a person with BPD, the author describes two voices that seem to struggle inside: one says to be careful, the other says it's fine to take the risk. But if you don't care (as in point 1) and are not frightened by what you are doing and what could follow, there is no discomfort either, and then it is easier to decide on something dangerous [msg #177].
3. **Shame and guilt as brakes.** What often stops us is not only fear but also shame and guilt. The author calls them the best regulators of behaviour. We learn them from early childhood, when we are given coordinates of bad and good (as the teachers see them), and they are needed for life in society to stop ourselves from doing what everyone condemns. Her example: at 2 a.m. a woman asks, "Не стыдно тебе приходить домой пьяным?" (*Aren't you ashamed to come home drunk?*). The man mumbles something guiltily in the hallway and then tiptoes around the house for several days. His emotions tormented him and held him back from the next party. The lesson she draws: if a person feels no guilt or shame about harming themselves, they will most likely do it again, because there is no conscience nagging them that could stop them [msg #177].
4. **Arousal without disgust.** People with BPD may feel physiological arousal during self-harm, such as a faster heartbeat or more sweating, without necessarily feeling disgust or other negative emotions. This arousal without disgust may increase the urge to self-harm and make it harder to resist. If a person feels excitement or anticipation rather than fear and disgust when thinking about self-harm, they may hurt themselves even more often, because it isn't as repellent and they don't hold themselves back [msg #177].
5. **Summary.** A person who feels no negative emotions when seeing self-harm images may be prone to self-harm in the future. Feeling no disgust, not seeing self-harm as something terrible, and so having no "стопов" (*stops*) or reasons to stop oneself means the urge to self-harm stays and can be acted on [msg #177].

## Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- Lena Foer explicitly says her explanation of suicidal thoughts is not a guide to action. With suicidal intent, the only guidance is to find a psychologist who works with this [msg #46].
- The study findings in #177 are hedged in the source ("может быть связано", *may be linked*; "может повышать вероятность", *may increase the likelihood*). They describe associations and possible risk, not certainties. The post is a free retelling ("вольным пересказом") of the article, so details may differ from the original paper [msg #177].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: #178 explains only four of the ten precursors (alexithymia, present-moment awareness, emotional dysregulation, dissociation). It also refers to "high emotional sensitivity and reactivity" and "intense emotional reactions" as related ideas. The other factors are listed without explanation, and readers are directed to the article [msg #178].
- (!) UNCLEAR: #106 calls suicide "вторая по значимости причина смерти" (*the second most significant cause of death*) without saying for which population or age group, or citing a source for the figure [msg #106].
- The "one in five" figure in #12 is given without a source [msg #12].
- The author of #178 notes that mindfulness and regulation skills take time and persistence to learn [msg #178].
