## BPD: definition, causes and explanatory models

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's foundational explanations of borderline personality disorder (BPD; Russian ПРЛ): a short working definition, the psychoanalytic view of "borderline" personality organization, the dialectical-behavioural (DBT) biosocial model of how BPD develops, a neuroscience finding linking the type of impulsivity to cortical folding, the history of stigma around the diagnosis, and short answers to the ten questions people most often ask about BPD. The channel frames these as several complementary answers to "what is BPD?" — it states that there will be many versions of the answer and that no single school's view is the only one [#7, #14]. The practical point the channel draws is de-shaming: BPD arises from biology plus experience, "это не ваша вина" (it is not your fault), and it responds to treatment [#191].

This part adds one post (with its linked Telegraph longread) on "quiet BPD" (тихое ПРЛ). It asks whether quiet BPD is real, whether it is a separate kind of BPD, and whether it is the same thing as "high-functioning BPD". It gives a 21-item checklist of quiet-BPD signs and 10 self-questions for suspected atypical BPD [#130, article #130]. The source says this matters because people whose BPD does not show "brightly" get asked "is this still BPD or something else?", and the main practical problem for them is getting a diagnosis [#130, article #130].

Relation to the existing topic file: the current file defines BPD by core instability, emotional amplitude, fear of abandonment and poor self-regulation (#7), and describes impulsivity as mostly outwardly visible behaviour such as aggressive acting-out, spending and substance use (#103). This post extends that picture: the same core features can be turned *inward*, so outward calm does not rule out BPD. It also adds a caveat on BPD sub-typing that the existing file does not have. The channel says theories of BPD "types" are not scientifically proven and that symptom profiles differ between people even within one diagnosis [#130].

### Core ideas

- BPD = pervasive instability. The core feature is instability of almost every aspect of life — goals, feelings, self-image, relationships, mood, behaviour — with huge emotional amplitude, weak emotion regulation and intense fear of abandonment [#7].
- Several explanatory models coexist. Psychoanalysis sees a personality organization "stuck" in the toddler's autonomy-vs-closeness conflict; DBT sees an interaction of inborn emotional sensitivity with an invalidating environment. The channel presents both, and notes the psychoanalytic hypothesis lacks scientific evidence [#14, #17].
- Impulsivity is partly biological. The type of impulsivity (reward-seeking vs reward-seeking plus aggression) has been linked to the folding of the lateral orbitofrontal cortex; knowing one's biological predisposition helps choose skills, but research is still early [#103].
- BPD was long stigmatized and considered untreatable, but today effective treatments exist (DBT, CBT, MBT, schema therapy) and many people achieve lasting improvement [#55, #191].

- Symptom sets differ between people with BPD. Some people's symptoms are more visible from outside and others' are less visible. Diagnosis has to take these differences in expression into account [#130, article #130].
- Quiet BPD is an inward expression, not a type. In quiet BPD the key BPD symptoms (intense emotions and impulsivity) are directed inward. The author says it is "не разновидность и не отдельный вид/тип ПРЛ" (not a variety and not a separate kind/type of BPD), but one way the symptoms can show up [#130].
- Quiet BPD is not "high-functioning BPD." Coping well with daily life is possible for some people with BPD, either on their own or through psychotherapy. That is still not a separate form of the disorder [#130].
- The main risk is a missed diagnosis. People with quiet BPD, or with any disorder whose presentation is atypical, struggle to get diagnosed [#130].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Short definition of BPD (core instability)
The channel calls this the shortest of the many answers it will give. BPD is a psychological condition that affects how you think, feel, behave and relate to close people. Its components as listed:
1. Core feature — instability of practically all aspects of life: goals, feelings, ideas about oneself, relationships, mood, behaviour patterns.
2. Huge emotional amplitude and difficulty with emotion regulation.
3. Strong fear of abandonment; tolerating loneliness can be hard.
4. Consequences of poor self-regulation: high-risk behaviour, self-harm, addictions.

Sources named by the author: Beck & Freeman, *Cognitive Therapy of Personality Disorders*; the Mayo Clinic website; the National Institute of Mental Health website [#7].

#### Psychoanalytic model of borderline personality organization
Purpose: the psychoanalytic answer to "what is BPD?". The channel stresses repeatedly that this is the psychoanalytic picture of the world, not the only one [#14].
1. Origin of the term. Psychoanalysts were historically the first to use "borderline", for patients who are not as stable as "neurotics" but do not have the break with reality seen in psychotic patients — i.e. on the border between the two. Later their understanding of the term became more complex [#14].
2. Borderline as a personality organization stuck at a developmental stage. "Borderline-ness" remained a type of personality organization, but people with it seem stuck in the problems of a child who is beginning to separate from the parents. Around two years old children already have some autonomy but need the parent to stay available and rescue them from any trouble; at this age children easily switch between refusing the mother's help ("я сам!" — *I'll do it myself!*) and sobbing on her lap [#14].
3. Parenting hypothesis. People with BPD were raised either by parents who denied the child autonomy, or by parents who neglected the child's need for closeness once the child had gained its tiny independence. The channel explicitly notes: this hypothesis has no scientific proof and is supported only by clinical observations [#14].
4. The central adult conflict. From the psychoanalytic viewpoint the childhood patterns persist into adulthood: when getting close to someone, people with BPD begin to fear total control and submission; when alone, they feel traumatic abandonment. This central conflict produces unstable relationship patterns, and in the end neither closeness nor distance feels comfortable [#14].
5. Use of the most basic defences (see next framework) [#14].
6. Scope caveat. In psychoanalysis the concept of borderline organization is broader than BPD in all other approaches: it includes many personality types, symptoms and disorders, for example substance abuse, bipolar disorder and impulse-control disorders [#14].

#### Three basic defences of the borderline organization
Mechanism: as we grow up we meet various threats and learn ways of reacting to protect ourselves; psychoanalysts assume that people with borderline organization use the most basic defences [#14].
1. Denial (отрицание) — "нет, этого на самом деле не происходит, я не хочу в это верить" (no, this isn't really happening, I don't want to believe it) [#14].
2. Projective identification (проективная идентификация) — unconsciously assigning one's own inner intentions or emotions to another person. The author's example: she unconsciously wants to end a conversation with her partner but believes that would be rude, so she says "я чувствую, что ты хочешь закончить наш разговор, что ж, давай" (I feel that you want to end our conversation — well, let's) [#14].
3. Black-and-white thinking (черно-белое мышление) — you are either terrible or wonderful; everything is either fine or was never fine [#14].

#### DBT biosocial model of BPD
Purpose: the dialectical-behavioural explanation of how BPD develops; DBT treatment is built on it [#17]. The 10-questions post calls it "отличная модель" (an excellent model) that stresses "biology" and "environment" as the causes of BPD [#191].
1. Biological factor — high emotional sensitivity from birth. People with BPD are born with high emotional sensitivity, which shows simultaneously as (a) a stronger reaction to stressors and (b) a longer period of returning to calm [#17]. The same point answers "why do my emotions change so sharply?": heightened sensitivity and slower restoration of emotional balance [#191].
2. Environmental factor — an invalidating environment. The environment in which people with BPD grew up — and often the one they are still in — is invalidating: denying the child's emotions, punishing them for emotions, or reacting to those emotions randomly. The result is that people with BPD have difficulty understanding, regulating and accepting their own emotions. Examples of emotional invalidation: "Плачут только девчонки" (Only girls cry), "Ты должен быть мне благодарен, а не реветь" (You should be grateful to me, not bawling) [#17].
3. Mutual, ongoing influence (transactional circle) — the two factors constantly reinforce each other:
   1. Parents, constantly facing the child's strong, unrelenting emotions, become more aggressive and less accepting.
   2. More aggressive parents destabilize the sensitive child emotionally even more, which feeds step 1 again — the cycle repeats ("И так далее") [#17].
4. Internalization. Later, people with BPD learn to invalidate their own emotional reactions themselves and view emotions in an over-simplified, unrealistic way [#17].

#### Dialectical stance of DBT (treatment built on the biosocial model)
- Primary targets: excessive emotional reactions, weak control of impulsive behaviour, self-harm and suicidality [#17].
- Why "dialectical": the therapist on the one hand accepts and validates the client's emotional pain, and on the other helps the client change the factors in life that lead to it and change coping methods to ones that are more humane toward oneself [#17].
- Central part: teaching emotion-regulation and distress-tolerance skills, together with acceptance of one's emotions [#17].

#### Two types of impulsivity by lateral orbitofrontal cortex gyrification
Purpose: to show that impulsivity is not just "letting yourself go" (распустилась) but a trait that distinguishes people at the level of brain structure [#103].
- Definition. Impulsivity is chronically poor self-control under strong emotions — situations where between "it came to mind" and "did it" there is rarely "thought about it", with no account of long-term consequences. If this is about you, the author says, it is not simply a lack of willpower but brain structure [#103].
- What impulsivity can look like (author's list):
  - use of alcohol or substances;
  - risky behaviour;
  - suicidality, self-harm;
  - impulsive spending or trips;
  - difficulties with planning and finishing tasks [#103].
- Gyrification explained. Gyri are the folds between the grooves on the surface of the hemispheres; gyrification is the amount of "wrinkles" on the cortex. The walnut-like texture gives the brain a large surface area that fits into a small skull, and that large surface underlies our many cognitive (and other) functions. Different gyri have different purposes [#103].
- The key zone. The area "за бровями" (behind the eyebrows) — the lateral orbitofrontal cortex — is where gyrification relates to impulsivity; the *type* of impulsivity depends on how folded this zone is. Scientists can measure it with a gyrification index [#103].
- Type 1 — impulsivity as reward-seeking (less folded lateral OFC): we seek positive feedback from the impulsive act. Examples: eating chocolate at 3 a.m. and getting lots of joy from it; the relief after buying something on WB (Wildberries) or ordering McDonald's at 2 a.m. [#103].
- Type 2 — reward-seeking plus aggressive acting-out (more folded lateral OFC): aggressive behaviour is added to reinforcement-seeking — when angry you make a scene, smash objects "или лица" (or faces); e.g. shouting and breaking something when angry [#103].
- Summary line: the amount of folding in the "behind the eyebrows" area determines what kind of impulsivity we have [#103].
- Why it matters (author's view): it is interesting as an idea because it helps understand who has which impulsivity; different mental disorders probably have different gyrification in this area, so a conventional MRI might one day help account for this variable and make diagnoses more precise — which the author calls tempting [#103].
- Practical takeaway: besides social learning (we may not have been taught to brake ourselves, or were rewarded for "quick reaction"), there are biological features affecting impulsivity. Knowing your biological predispositions lets you help yourself more effectively, develop skills and "расширять репертуар" (expand the repertoire) of behaviour strategies [#103].

#### Ten common BPD questions and short answers
The channel's FAQ ("10 самых распространённых вопросов… и простые ответы на них") [#191]:
1. Why does BPD arise? From a combination of hereditary predisposition, difficult life conditions (e.g. trauma or lack of support) and, as a consequence, difficulties managing emotions. "Это не ваша вина" (It's not your fault) — this is how the brain and lived experience shaped ways of responding to stress. Explained by the DBT biosocial model (above) [#191].
2. Can BPD be cured? BPD is described as a chronic disorder of the emotion-regulation system. Research, the author says, shows that with effective treatments (e.g. DBT, CBT or MBT) symptoms can decrease significantly and many people achieve lasting improvement [#191].
3. Why do my emotions change so sharply? Heightened emotional sensitivity and slower return to emotional balance (biosocial model). DBT skills, mindfulness and crisis skills can help cope with sharp mood swings [#191].
4. How to stop impulsive acts? Impulsive behaviour is often linked to overwhelming emotions, and "going with the urge" is an attempt to cope with them. The STOP skill can help pause before doing something irreversible or cringe-worthy ("кринжовое") [#191].
5. How to cope with urges to self-harm? The urge to hurt oneself or do something risky often comes from intense emotional pain. DBT skills (fast-calming methods), self-care and distraction techniques help handle these urges safely [#191].
6. Why is it so hard to build relationships? People with BPD often face fear of rejection, intense emotions and difficulty trusting others. A strong desire for closeness alternates with a desire to run away, especially when the relationship becomes too emotionally intense. Fear of being rejected *or absorbed* by a partner can lead to impulsive actions — e.g. sudden withdrawal or conflict [#191].
7. Why am I so afraid of being abandoned? This fear may be linked to past experience, neglect or unstable relationships (with parents, significant people, partners). CBT helps change dysfunctional thoughts about abandonment, and mindfulness helps manage the fear in the moment [#191].
8. How to cope with emptiness? People with BPD often have an unstable sense of self, leading to identity problems; this can cause a feeling of emptiness because the person does not feel stable in who they really are. Mindfulness practices, self-care and finding meaningful activities can gradually reduce it [#191].
9. Which treatments are most effective? DBT is especially effective for emotional instability, impulsivity, self-harm and suicidal behaviour; CBT, MBT and schema therapy have also proven effective [#191].
10. Can people with BPD have healthy relationships and a stable life? Yes: "Психотерапия + осознанная работа над собой + практика навыков" (Psychotherapy + conscious work on oneself + skills practice) = people with BPD can lead the life they want [#191].

#### Quiet BPD as inward-directed expression of BPD symptoms (outward vs inward)
Purpose: to explain how BPD can look "outwardly calm" while still being BPD [#130, article #130].
1. Historical background. Earlier theories split BPD into different "types". One of these types "обрел вес" (gained weight) and went on "жить своей жизнью" (living its own life), even though the theory of BPD types is not scientifically proven. People singled out "quiet BPD" to explain the atypical, "внешне спокойное" (outwardly calm) way some people show symptoms [#130, article #130].
2. Baseline — what BPD is here. The author defines BPD as a disorder whose key symptoms are intense emotions and impulsivity. These symptoms are usually hard to miss, both for the person and for the people close to them [#130].
3. Direction of the symptoms. When those symptoms are directed inward, one can speak of "quiet BPD". Mood swings and difficulties with other people are not strongly visible in outward reactions [#130].
4. Contrast examples given by the author:
   - *Anger:* in "ordinary" BPD a person has outbursts of anger. They swear, shout, fight or break things. In quiet BPD the anger is turned inward and is more likely to show up as self-harm [#130].
   - *Distress:* a person with "ordinary" BPD may have tantrums or cry. A person with quiet BPD becomes sullen and withdrawn [#130].
5. Same criteria, different direction. The traits of quiet BPD match the standard BPD criteria: unstable interpersonal relationships, an unstable attitude toward oneself, emotional fluctuations, and impulsive, risky, ill-considered behaviour. The difference between "ordinary" and quiet BPD comes down to the "направлению" (direction) of emotional response: outward or inward [#130].
6. Illustration (meme image). An image in the article shows the point. Text: "friend explaining that I can't possibly have BPD bc I'm never angry" / "me, who's angry 24/7 but only directs that anger to myself". The lesson is that the lack of visible anger does not mean anger is absent [#130].

#### Quiet BPD warning-sign checklist (21 signs)
Purpose: what to pay attention to if you suspect quiet BPD ("На что обратить внимание, если вы подозреваете «тихое ПРЛ»?"). The author lists these signs [#130]:
1. Self-isolation or avoidance when you feel low or depressed [#130].
2. Harming yourself and hiding it from others so they do not suspect something is wrong [#130].
3. A constant feeling of hopelessness [#130].
4. Hostility, anger or aggression directed inward, for example a harsh inner critic or self-harm [#130].
5. "Low self-esteem" or a rapidly changing picture of oneself [#130].
6. A strong fear of being rejected [#130].
7. Mood swings lasting from several hours to several days that seem invisible to outsiders [#130].
8. A habit of idealizing others and then devaluing them and distancing from them [#130].
9. Taking small things to heart, for example deciding someone is ignoring you because they have not answered your messages [#130].
10. Inability to understand others, for example thinking people are angry with you when they are not [#130].
11. Racing or distorted thoughts [#130].
12. Fear of being left alone, or fear of pushing people away [#130].
13. Self-sabotage that stops you from reaching your goals [#130].
14. Feeling "triggered and wound up", which leads to uncontrollable inner emotions such as shame and guilt [#130].
15. Finding it hard to talk about your feelings [#130].
16. A feeling of numbness or emptiness inside [#130].
17. Dissociation or derealization, i.e. feeling that you live in a dream [#130].
18. A feeling that having emotions is wrong, and attempts to suppress them [#130].
19. A feeling of being in a state of heightened vigilance (hypervigilance) [#130].
20. Unhealthy boundaries [#130].
21. Obsession with a particular person and a wish to spend as much time with them as possible [#130].

#### Ten self-questions for suspected atypical BPD criteria
Purpose: questions "которые имеет смысл задать себе при подозрении на «нетипичные критерии ПРЛ»" (that make sense to ask yourself if you suspect "atypical BPD criteria") [#130]:
1. Do you have sharp mood swings that can last several days, while nobody around you really knows what you feel? [#130]
2. Do you often feel guilt or shame, even though others may tell you that you have nothing to blame yourself for? [#130]
3. When you have a conflict with someone, is your first reaction to blame yourself? [#130]
4. In relationships or friendships, do you tend to idealize someone and then eventually decide you no longer like them, even if outwardly you do not let them know? [#130]
5. Do you feel separated from others, emptied out or numb all the time? [#130]
6. Do you sometimes feel that you are watching yourself perform ordinary actions, or that your life is "unreal"? [#130]
7. When you are angry, do you tend to suppress the feeling? [#130]
8. Do you feel like a burden to others, or that you simply take up too much space? [#130]
9. When people hurt you, do you tend to cut them out of your life instead of trying to talk about what happened? [#130]
10. When you get upset, do you tend to withdraw into yourself and not talk to anyone at all? [#130]

The source does not give a scoring threshold. The questions are offered as self-reflection prompts for people whose presentation is atypical, in the context of the diagnostic difficulty described below [#130].

### Detailed insights

#### History of stigma around BPD
For many decades of the 20th century people with BPD received neither sufficient understanding nor effective help. The labels they faced were: "Врождённые дефекты мозга" (innate brain defects), "форма шизофрении" (a form of schizophrenia), "истерия" (hysteria) and simply "дурной характер" (bad character). Even after the diagnosis was defined, these patients were stigmatized and considered difficult and incurable. The forwarded post (from the channel Чистые Когниции, published for BPD month) says the shift in views on BPD shaped the emergence of new treatment methods, which its full article by Anna Kolesnikova traces [#55].

#### Convergence between the models
Both the psychoanalytic and the FAQ accounts describe the same relationship oscillation: psychoanalysis frames it as fear of total control/submission when close versus traumatic abandonment when alone [#14]; the FAQ frames it as a desire for closeness alternating with a desire to flee, driven by fear of being rejected or absorbed [#191].

#### Quiet BPD vs "high-functioning BPD"
Because quiet BPD is less visible, the idea is "спекулируется" (speculated) that quiet BPD is the same as "high-functioning BPD". The high-functioning idea is that some people with BPD do not show their symptoms "brightly" and can manage daily life, work or study without difficulty for themselves or for the people around them [#130, article #130]. The author's verdict, given as a "Спойлер" (spoiler), is that this is possible. Some people manage it "сам" (on their own) and some with the help of psychotherapy, but it is "не «отдельный вид расстройства»" (not "a separate kind of disorder") [#130]. The two labels therefore describe different things. Quiet BPD is about the *direction* of symptoms (inward), while "high-functioning" is about *coping* with everyday life. Neither one is a distinct subtype [#130].

#### Diagnostic difficulty with atypical presentations
The problem people with quiet BPD face, and people with any disorder whose manifestations are atypical, is that the diagnosis is hard to make [#130]. The author's takeaway is that quiet BPD is "всего лишь теория прошлого столетия" (merely a theory from the last century), not a real type of disorder. Even with a BPD diagnosis, symptoms do not come as a set made "под копирку" (as a carbon copy). There are differences in how symptoms show, and these are important to consider when diagnosing [#130].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The psychoanalytic parenting hypothesis has, as the channel itself states, no scientific evidence and rests only on clinical observations; the psychoanalytic "borderline organization" is also much broader than the BPD diagnosis in other approaches [#14].
- The gyrification research is early: "Исследования только исследуют" (research is only researching), no striking conclusions yet; the MRI-for-diagnosis idea is presented as a tempting possibility, not a fact. The two impulsivity types are explicitly a simplification ("Упрощенно") [#103].
