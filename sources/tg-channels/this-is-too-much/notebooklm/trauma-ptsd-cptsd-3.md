## Neurobiology of trauma and trauma memory

### What this covers

How and why trauma changes memory: the defence mechanisms that operate during and after a traumatic event, the difference between ordinary forgetting and trauma-related memory loss, which brain structures and hormones are involved, why children "forget" more easily, how gaslighting rewrites memory, and how exposure work helps. Two later posts extend the neurobiology to the cerebellar vermis (freezing, fainting) and to the endogenous opioid and endocannabinoid systems (numbing, dissociation, fear extinction). The source frames this as important because survivors of violence are often disbelieved — accused of inconsistent accounts, deliberately omitting details and distorting facts — while the more structured account of the person accused of abuse is perceived as more truthful [#126]. The core material is a Wonderzine interview carousel titled (in translation) *Why we don't remember the events that traumatised us. And whether we need to remember them* with clinical psychologist Katya Klimochkina [#117, msg #126].

### Core ideas

- Defences keep working after the event. Fight/flight/freeze and dissociation let a person survive the moment; afterwards dissociation can be replaced by repression, denial or a "more controllable" reinterpretation such as self-blame (see *Defence mechanisms around a traumatic event*) [#118].
- Trauma memory is fragmented, not just forgotten. Ordinary forgetting is about attention; fragmentation and broken chronology are characteristic of trauma survivors, because dissociation and intense arousal block the processing needed to store memory properly [#119, img #120].
- Specific brain systems are altered — high cortisol impairs the hippocampus, the amygdala becomes hyperactive, the prefrontal cortex is weakened by prolonged stress, and the cerebellar vermis is implicated in freezing and fainting (see *Brain structures in trauma memory*) [#120, img #121, msg #209].
- Unprocessed experience returns as re-experiencing — flashbacks, intrusive images and nightmares signal that something happened but was not processed in memory [#121, img #122].
- Children forget and distort more easily, because their brains are still forming and because blaming themselves is less frightening than accepting that their parents failed them [#123, img #124].
- Memory cannot simply be erased, but it can be changed — e.g., by gaslighting [#125].
- Exposure work develops the ability to look at the horrors of one's life without horror; other trauma-memory methods share the same essence [#126].
- The opioid and endocannabinoid systems are the brain's "natural anaesthesia"; their disruption underlies fragmented memory, emotional numbing, dissociation and intrusive re-experiencing [#231, msg #232].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Defence mechanisms around a traumatic event
Purpose: explains what the psyche does during and after trauma and why memory ends up absent or distorted. Klimochkina describes a sequence of protective strategies [#118]:

1. During the event — fight, flight, freeze, and dissociation. The well-known «бей, беги, замри» (fight, flight, freeze) reflex explains what happens to psyche and body at the moment of trauma. Dissociation belongs to these reactions: strong emotions «выключают» (switch off) our capacity to feel, tear us away from the body and from reality, make us go numb. Why it helps: because the terror and pain are not felt, the moment can be survived [#118].
2. After the event — repression and denial. The "defences" do not disappear after the event. Dissociation may be replaced by repression and denial of reality: the person goes into refusal and says nothing happened. For a time this strategy helps — by suppressing the replay of what was lived through, the brain tries to wall itself off from re-experiencing the horror [#118].
3. Reinterpretation (re-framing) of the event. The brain also likes to reinterpret the event. If we find another, more controllable point of view, we do not feel helplessness and hopelessness. Example: replacing «как же такое произошло» (how could this have happened) with «я виновата в произошедшем» (I am to blame for what happened) makes the experience easier for the brain to take in — certainty and "logic" appear. Pitfall: self-esteem does not improve; such reinterpretation produces an enormous feeling of guilt [#118].

#### Brain structures in trauma memory
Purpose: a map of which structures are involved and what goes wrong in PTSD/CPTSD.

1. Emotional arousal vs information processing (general principle). The intense emotional arousal that triggers dissociation affects memory formation. We cannot simultaneously experience emotions and process information — partly why people "in affect" often later do not remember what happened. These are different brain areas that find it hard to work at the same time; emotions are more important than "processing", so processing is not prioritised in the moment [#120].
2. Stress hormones. When the organism is under stress, hormones (adrenaline and cortisol) influence the functioning of brain areas involved in memory processing [#120].
3. Hippocampus. A brain structure necessary for memory consolidation — the transfer of memories from short-term to long-term memory. High cortisol can disrupt the hippocampus and change its structure, causing difficulty processing and consolidating memories of high-stress events. There are studies showing changes in hippocampal size and in its ability to form new neurons in people with complex PTSD [#120].
4. Amygdala. The organism's «система тревоги» (alarm system) that reacts to danger. In PTSD it becomes hyperactive and hypersensitive, so many people with traumatic experience constantly feel on edge and cannot relax. Amygdala reactivity also prevents the experience from being processed correctly: emotionally charged events remain more prominent and intrusive in memory and can be re-lived as flashbacks or nightmares [#121].
5. Prefrontal cortex. Important for analysing information, regulating emotions and integrating memory into a whole. Changes in this area due to prolonged stress make it harder to regulate emotional reactions to traumatic memories and to suppress intrusive thoughts about the past [#121].
6. Cerebellar vermis. The central part of the cerebellum, regulating emotional intensity and involved in freezing and fainting — written out in full under *The cerebellar vermis and freezing* below [#209].

#### The horror-film analogy of exposure work
Purpose: answers «Как вспомнить то, что было вытеснено?» (How to remember what was repressed?). Exposure work is one part of psychotherapy for people with PTSD and CPTSD [#126].

- The analogy. Imagine watching a horror film as a child: you were scared, closed your eyes, plugged your ears, ran out of the room, muted the sound or fast-forwarded — and remembered only the scariest moments. But if you watch the film from the first to the last minute, you can grasp the plot and make sense of it. And if you watch it several times, it becomes less scary: it becomes predictable and stops provoking a storm of emotions [#126].
- The task of exposure work is «развить способность смотреть на ужасы собственной жизни без ужаса» (to develop the ability to look at the horrors of one's own life without horror). It helps get rid of re-experiencing symptoms, change the meaning of what was lived through, and thereby change the person's view of themselves and their current life [#126].
- Caveat: it is important to do this together with a psychotherapist trained in it [#126].
- Other methods. Besides exposure there are many effective approaches to working with traumatic memories, but their essence is analogous: to help a person come into contact with unformed and frightening experience in a safe setting [#126].
- Where work starts. With PTSD and CPTSD clients, therapy often begins with «крупиц воспоминаний» (crumbs of memories): bodily sensations, conclusions, or retellings heard from others [#122].

#### Opioid system as "emergency help" vs endocannabinoid system as "regulator"
Purpose: a model of the brain's "natural anaesthesia of the psyche" («Природная анестезия психики») and how it relates to trauma [#231].

A. Endogenous opioid system — the natural system of pain relief and emotional regulation. Endogenous opioids (endorphins, enkephalins, dynorphins) are natural analogues of morphine that the brain produces in response to pain, stress and threat. They act through μ-, δ- and κ-opioid receptors — three types of "receivers" in the brain through which the body's natural painkillers act [#231].

What it does (4 functions) [#231]:
1. Relieves pain — «встроенный ибупрофен получается» (a built-in ibuprofen, so to speak).
2. Regulates emotions — e.g., crying, hugs, physical activity or laughter raise endorphin levels and bring relief.
3. «Глушит» (muffles/dampens) the formation of memories.
4. Gives a sense of calm and pleasure.

During an extreme event (assault, disaster, violence) the brain launches protective reactions, one of which is "freeze", in which the opioid system is sharply activated [#231]:
- Endorphins dull physical pain.
- The κ-opioid system (via dynorphin) reduces the emotional reaction and can cause a feeling of estrangement — «как будто это не со мной» (as if this isn't happening to me).
- Many people with extreme stress reactions describe not feeling pain or fear during the traumatic event: body and psyche temporarily "switch off" in order to survive.

After trauma the system can malfunction in two directions [#231]:
- Hyperactivation — the person stays emotionally numb and detached, does not feel joy; pain and connection with others are dulled.
- Hypoactivation — the opposite: sharp swings of feeling, flashes of pain and fear, and emotional reactions disproportionate to the situation.

B. Endocannabinoid system — a finer "tuning" of the regulation of emotions, memory and stress: a neuromodulatory network whose main substances are anandamide and 2-AG and whose main receptors are CB1 (in the brain) and CB2 (in the immune system) [#231].

What it does (4 functions) [#231]:
1. "Brakes" excessive reaction of the brain's stress centres.
2. Influences the formation and storage of memories.
3. Regulates mood and the proportionality of emotions.
4. Controls sleep and appetite.

Normally endocannabinoids help "extinguish" the stress response after danger and return the organism to equilibrium. They also take part in fear extinction — the process in which the brain learns that the threat has already passed [#231].

In PTSD and CPTSD: anandamide levels are reduced and CB1-receptor activity in the amygdala and hippocampus is disrupted. Result: the brain cannot "brake" the memory of fear. Memories stay too vivid, return as flashbacks, and anxiety is easily activated even without real threat [#231].

C. How the systems relate (4 commonalities) [#231]:
1. Both relieve pain — physical and emotional.
2. Both regulate the response to stress and fear.
3. Both participate in how the brain forms and "weakens" memories.
4. Both can create a state of detachment and calm if activated too strongly.

Summary formula: the opioid system is «экстренная помощь» (emergency help) — it quickly switches off pain and emotions; the cannabinoid system is the «регулировщик» (regulator / traffic controller) — it helps return to balance once danger has passed [#231].

#### Coordinated vs disrupted opioid/endocannabinoid response and the dissociative pattern
Purpose: explains how these two systems link to dissociation [#232].

During a traumatic event [#232]:
- The opioid system "anaesthetises" and "disconnects" consciousness from the experience.
- The cannabinoid system helps modulate the fear reaction and memory.

Two outcomes [#232]:
- If they work in concert, the person goes through shock but can then "stabilise".
- If they are disrupted, a dissociative pattern forms: (1) memory becomes fragmented; (2) emotions are separated from events; (3) a sense arises of «со мной что-то не так, но я не чувствую и не понимаю, что именно» (something is wrong with me, but I don't feel or understand what exactly).

Research the author cites shows (a) activation of κ-opioid receptors is linked to emotional numbness and a feeling of vacuum/emptiness, and (b) disruptions in the endocannabinoid system hinder fear extinction and the integration of memories [#232].

Conclusion: these systems are key regulators of pain, emotions and memory that help the organism survive stress; under severe or chronic stress their work can be disrupted, sustaining dissociation or intrusive replay of memories. The author states that malfunctions in these systems lie at the basis of the neurobiology of memory formation during a traumatic event and at the basis of traumatic dissociation [#232].

### Detailed insights

#### Ordinary forgetting vs trauma-related memory loss
- Everyday forgetting is linked to concentration of attention, absent-mindedness or "meaningless" tasks — normal processes for a person living with lots of tasks and thoughts in their head [#119].
- Fragmented memories or broken sequence in recalling events is not so ordinary and is characteristic of people who survived trauma — whether a single trauma or multiple traumas, as in complex PTSD [#119].
- Mechanism: dissociation «выключает» (switches off) us from the world and from our emotions, and without them we cannot correctly process information to put memory "in the right place" and be able to return to it. This leads to scattered, fragmented, patchy memories of events [#119].

#### Re-experiencing and the non-integrated memory
- Missing experience in memory produces many "side effects". Re-experiencing (flashbacks, intrusive thoughts and images, nightmares) is a symptom of PTSD and CPTSD. It shows that something happened but was not processed in memory; as a result we are forced to re-live the experience in a painful and terrifying way, feeling the pain and suffering again [#121].
- We encounter a trigger and are "thrown" into the past with strong emotions that prevent us from assembling the puzzle of memories into a coherent picture. Fragmentation, incomprehensibility, wrong chronology or absence of memory are equally manifestations of a non-integrated event — «не осмысленного и не уложенного» (not made sense of and not put in place) in memory [#122].
- Distorted memories indicate that different memory systems processed the event but did not connect with each other. Trauma is encoded in fragments, which feels like an incomplete picture of the past with painful flashes and many missing puzzle pieces [#122].

#### Why children "forget" more easily
- Anyone can forget or distort what happened, but it is easier for children because their brain is still forming. It is also easier for them to develop CPTSD symptoms or another mental disorder in the process of forgetting [#123].
- An adult can rely on their own experience and the real world, which allows a more conscious reaction. A child who has, for example, experienced sexualised violence needs to explain to themselves what happened: we cannot live without cause-and-effect connections; we need at least some understanding, even if wrong or horrible [#123].
- The brain tries to avoid uncertainty and offers a hint, e.g. «ты сам виноват» (it's your own fault), and the child begins to believe it. Better "I'm to blame" than accepting the reality that the parents did not protect them and hurt them — losing one's parents is scarier than blaming oneself. Therefore a child is more likely to "forget" parts of the event and alter their understanding of it so that it fits the answer found [#123].
- Example: there was cruel treatment by relatives, and the parents did not believe the child and told them not to talk about it. The child may come to feel that it really wasn't so bad, especially once the physical pain has subsided: «Ну прикрикнули, ну замахнулись, сам виноват, надо было спать и не привлекать внимание» (Well, they yelled, well, they raised a hand, it's my own fault, I should have been sleeping and not attracting attention). Lesson: the appraisal of the event changed, and whatever did not fit the new appraisal was repressed [#124].

#### Can someone be made to forget? Gaslighting
- Klimochkina recalls Nolan's *Inception*, where (she says, "it seems") DiCaprio and Hardy discuss that you cannot change thoughts in Murphy's [sic — the character Fischer] head, but you can plant another thought that grows so much it changes the whole content of consciousness. "That's how it works": some thoughts and beliefs are replaced by others under the weight of life experience and the emotional load of those thoughts [#125].
- «ПАМЯТЬ СЛИШКОМ СЛОЖНАЯ ШТУКА» (memory is too complex a thing) to simply erase something, but it can be changed — for instance, by repeating that the way you think is not actually so, that you are wrong and cannot trust yourself [#125].
- Gaslighting is not a scientific term but is familiar to everyone; it means a strategy in which one person tries to make another doubt their own perception of reality, judgments or memories [#125].

#### The cerebellar vermis and freezing
- Origin of the post. Klimochkina was rewatching the series *Prodigal Son* (with Michael Sheen as a serial killer; she prefers his son's character, a criminal-psychologist detective with PTSD) and heard the line «Червь мозжечка» останавливает моторику при сильном страхе (the cerebellar vermis stops motor activity under intense fear). Surprised — since the cerebellum is known for muscle tone and coordination — she researched it [#209].
- What it is. The cerebellar vermis is the central part of the cerebellum, a structure at the base of the brain behind the brainstem. The cerebellum is usually associated with movement coordination, balance and fine motor skills. But modern research, she writes, shows that the vermis also participates in emotion regulation, autonomic reactions (e.g., heart rate, blood pressure) and the fear reaction under stress [#209].
- Dysfunction. Reduced activity or dysfunction of the vermis can disrupt the physiological regulation of fear. This can appear as hyperarousal (panic, trembling, rapid heartbeat) or hypoarousal / "shutdown" (freezing, fainting, dissociation) [#209].
- What is known (PTSD research usually focuses on the amygdala, hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, but the vermis also plays a role) [#209]:
  1. People with PTSD and those who experienced traumatic events in childhood show reduced vermis volume.
  2. Functional MRI shows altered vermis activity during trauma memories or strong emotions.
  3. This is linked to dissociation, derealisation, difficulties in emotion regulation, and spikes in autonomic indicators (blood pressure, pulse, fainting).
- Summary. The vermis's basic function is fine-tuning movement and balance, but through connections with the limbic system it also regulates the intensity of emotions, and under fear it influences autonomic reactions and takes part in freezing and fainting — which makes the "motor stop in fear" understandable [#209].
- Verdict on the series. Was the vermis mentioned correctly in *Prodigal Son*? «Похоже на правду!» (Seems true!) — it really participates in regulating fear and emotions, and reduced activity can lead to "shutdown" reactions. The series exaggerates, but it is based on real neurobiology [#209].

### Evidence and numbers

- Studies (unnamed) showing changes in hippocampal size and in the ability to form new neurons in people with complex PTSD [#120].
- Reduced cerebellar vermis volume in people with PTSD and in those traumatised in childhood; fMRI showing altered vermis activity during trauma memories or strong emotions (no specific studies named) [#209].
- Research the author cites (PubMed 30402683): altered μ-opioid receptor sensitivity in people with PTSD, affecting both the stress response and the capacity for pleasure [#231]; κ-opioid receptor activation linked to emotional numbness and a feeling of vacuum; endocannabinoid disruption hindering fear extinction and memory integration [#232].
- In PTSD/CPTSD: reduced anandamide and disrupted CB1 activity in the amygdala and hippocampus [#231].

## How religions and spiritual traditions made sense of trauma

### What this covers

A longread by channel author Katya Klimochkina (co-credited to @bpdflowers), originally written for a book but left out of it, surveying how religious and spiritual traditions interpreted traumatic suffering long before psychotherapy: Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism and the beliefs of Indigenous peoples (Siberia, Navajo, Amazonia, Maori, Australian Aboriginal peoples). For each tradition it gives the core explanation of suffering, the texts or rites involved, where ancient texts describe what we would now call PTSD symptoms, and how the idea anticipates a modern therapeutic technique. Why it matters, per the author: the term "trauma" is only about a century old, but pain, war, loss, violence and catastrophe are as old as humanity, and people always asked *"почему это со мной происходит и как теперь жить дальше?"* (why is this happening to me and how do I live on now?) — religions were humanity's long-standing answers, and their logic of meaning-making still underlies trauma work today [#230].

### Core ideas

- Trauma is a new word for an ancient experience. Psychological trauma as a concept appeared only in the 20th century (the post says "всего век назад" — only a century ago), long before DSM and ICD, yet the experiences now called consequences of traumatic experience "существовали... всегда" (have always existed) [#230].
- Every tradition answers "За что?" (Why me? / What did I do to deserve this?). Different continents and eras, one common sense: find an explanation and answer this question [#230].
- The function of these explanations is restoring meaning, inner order and connection. They didn't only explain what was happening; they helped return meaning, restore inner order and a sense of connection with the world, and helped people survive what often has no meaning or explanation — yet meaning is very much needed for the survival of both the species and the individual [#230].
- Spiritual explanations were forerunners of psychotherapy. Religions didn't know about PTSD or CPTSD but described the same phenomena in other words; where science speaks of integrating traumatic memory or processing the personal meanings of trauma, religion speaks of redemption, purification or return of the soul [#230].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Five ways traditions framed suffering
Across millennia, according to the longread, pain, suffering and torment were understood as one of the following (the author adds wryly "Кто во что горазд!" — *each to their own talent / whatever anyone could come up with*) [#230]:
1. Punishment — suffering as retribution for sins (strongly present in Christianity and the prophetic books; explicitly *not* the main reading in Islam or Buddhism).
2. Test / trial — suffering as a trial of faith (Christianity's Job, Islam's ibtila).
3. Purification — pain that cleanses the soul or the heart (Christian asceticism and "carrying the cross", Islamic and Sufi purification of the heart).
4. Result of karma — consequences of past actions, including in past lives (Hinduism).
5. Rupture of the connection between a person and the spirit world — Indigenous/shamanic traditions (soul loss, broken tapu, destroyed Dreaming path).
Each is developed tradition by tradition under *Detailed insights* [#230].

#### Three families of spiritual answers to "Why me?"
The author's closing synthesis groups traditions by where they place the emphasis [#230]:
1. Abrahamic religions (Judaism, Christianity, Islam) — emphasis on the *test of faith and moral responsibility*. Suffering asks: will you keep trusting God; did you sin; what must you repent of.
2. Eastern teachings (Hinduism, Buddhism) — emphasis on *understanding the causes of suffering and spiritual growth through it*. Suffering is caused (karma, ignorance, attachment) and can be understood and transcended.
3. Shamanic traditions — emphasis on *restoring the lost connection with the world* (body, soul, nature, ancestors, spirits), usually through collective rites.
What unites them, despite the differences: the drive to give meaning to pain, suffering and misfortune. Even when an event splits life into "before and after", faith in something larger helps a person embed the event "somewhere" and gives the possibility of making sense of it [#230].

#### Tonglen (taking and giving)
- What: a Tibetan Buddhist practice described as "вдох страдания, выдох сострадания" (inhaling suffering, exhaling compassion) or "принятие и отдача" (taking and giving). The author calls it her favourite ("Мое любимое!") [#230].
- How (as described): through deliberate "breathing in" of others' suffering and "breathing out" of compassion, one learns to turn pain — one's own and others' — into a source of compassion and spiritual energy. (!) INCOMPLETE: the source gives no further instructions (duration, posture, sequence) [#230].
- Why it matters for trauma, per the author: it already closely resembles PTSD/CPTSD work in behavioural approaches, where reworking the traumatic experience lets it be (1) integrated into memory and (2) re-understood with a different emotional colouring [#230].

### Detailed insights

#### Christianity: punishment and test, purification through pain
- Two-fold reading. Suffering is understood both as punishment for sins and as a test of faith; accents shifted across eras, from repentance for suffering to the rebirth of the spirit through pain [#230].
- Book of Job as a PTSD-like portrait. Job, a righteous man, loses everything — children, property, health. His friends insist it is punishment, but God does not confirm their words (through their countless conversations). Job suffers, gets angry, argues with God, and lives through despair, fear and loss of meaning — states the author says resemble PTSD symptoms. His words "Страхи мои напали на меня" (my fears have attacked me) can, in her reading, be taken as evidence of a sense of constant threat or of intrusive thoughts. The meaning of Job's trial is trust in God even when the causes of pain are incomprehensible [#230].
- Gospel: suffering as part of salvation. Jesus goes through torture and death to redeem humanity's sins. From this comes the idea that pain can be cleansing and meaningful if it is not lived through in vain — and the idea of "нести свой крест" (carrying one's cross): pain not as penalty but as a path of spiritual purification [#230].
- Ascetic tradition. Suffering was seen as a way to get rid of pride. Monks wrote of the "ночи души" (night of the soul) — a state of emptiness and horror followed by a renewal of faith [#230].
- The harsher, collective reading persisted. Suffering as punishment for sins and a reminder to repent intensified in times of epidemics, wars or social catastrophes, when one person's suffering was explained by the sins of the whole community. In the Middle Ages, epidemics such as the 14th-century plague were considered God's punishment of entire peoples. The prophetic books (e.g. Jeremiah) interpret any calamity as the consequence of a people's apostasy, a reading that persisted in later religious interpretations of disasters as punishment for "греховность общества" (the sinfulness of society) [#230].

#### Islam: trial, not punishment; patience and trust
- Difficulties and disasters are seen not as penalty but as a test of faith and a way to purify the heart; Allah tests a person to reveal the strength of their faith. The Quran (2:155): «Мы непременно испытаем вас незначительным страхом, голодом, потерей имущества, людей и плодов.» (We will surely test you with some fear, hunger, loss of property, lives and fruits) [#230].
- Ibtila (trial) is part of the spiritual path, in which sabr (patience) and tawakkul (reliance on Allah) are shown [#230].
- A cited saying presents trials as a sign of divine love: "когда Всевышний Аллах любит каких-то людей, Он подвергает их испытаниям" (when Allah the Most High loves certain people, He subjects them to trials); whoever is content gains [Allah's] contentment, whoever is discontent incurs [His] wrath. Here trial is understood as a form of purification and a manifestation of divine love [#230].
- Ancient description of PTSD symptoms. Hadiths describe companions of the Prophet who, after battles, could not sleep for a long time, heard the sounds of fighting in their dreams and startled at noise. The author reads this as an example of ancient texts noticing the psychological consequences of trauma: today we would see re-experiencing (intrusions, flashbacks, nightmares) typical of PTSD [#230].
- Sufism. Suffering is the destruction of the false "self", cleansing the heart of attachments and a path to God's love. For Sufis traumatic experience is not an end but a process of inner rebirth. Rumi wrote "Рана - это место, куда входит свет" (the wound is the place where the light enters) — "something about vulnerability and healing in pain", in the author's words. So pain becomes a point of spiritual awakening rather than mere suffering — "Во всем добавляется "доп смысл"!" (an "extra meaning" is added to everything!) [#230].

#### Hinduism: causes, consequences and debts
- Pain and loss are about karma (and consequences) and dharma — a duty to be fulfilled. Trials or "traumatic experience" are chains of causes and effects linking all lives; they are not accidental but the consequence of past actions and part of the spiritual path [#230].
- Bhagavad Gita. Arjuna, not wanting to fight, tells Krishna of his horror before the war; Krishna replies: «Исполни свой долг. Душа не умирает, она лишь меняет тела, как человек одежду» (Fulfil your duty. The soul does not die; it only changes bodies, as a person changes clothes). War here is not punishment but a spiritual test and part of dharma. Pain becomes a lesson: if you go through it consciously you can purify karma and come closer to liberation (moksha). Thus, the author notes, traumatic experience can be re-viewed as "по делам полученный" (received according to one's deeds) and as "уплоченый долг" [sic] (a debt paid off) [#230].
- Puranas tell of people reborn into hard circumstances because of mistakes in past lives [#230].
- Upanishads treat suffering as the inevitable consequence of accumulated actions; the human goal is to step outside the cycle of suffering [#230].
- Ramayana and Mahabharata: heroes go through exile, loss and violence, but each story stresses that suffering opens the way to wisdom and inner strength. The author liked the idea of "незавершённые уроки души" (unfinished lessons of the soul) that one manages to "finish off" in suffering [#230].

#### Buddhism: understand the causes, observe the pain
- Suffering (dukkha) is the basis of human existence. The First Noble Truth: suffering is inevitable, but it can be understood and transformed [#230].
- The Buddha explained pain not as punishment but as a result of ignorance, attachment, fears and illusions; if a person becomes aware of the causes of suffering, they can be freed from it. Consequently, the author draws, searching for someone to blame is pointless, whereas understanding the causes of pain and the path to overcome it can be effective [#230].
- How: meditation. In meditative practice (sati) a person observes pain without avoidance, learning to see it as part of life. The Dhammapada says «Из боли рождается мудрость» (from pain wisdom is born). The author claims: "Вся работа с тревогой в поведенческой терапии стоит на этом фундаменте" (all anxiety work in behavioural therapy stands on this foundation) [#230].
- Link to third-wave CBT. This resembles modern ideas of mindfulness and integration of traumatic experience: in Buddhism and in third-wave CBT (ACT, DBT, MBT, in the author's list) mindfulness and compassion practices aim not at suppressing pain or trauma but at transforming pain into understanding, which opens the possibility of liberation. The author's rhetorical question: "Если я могу выдерживать боль и дискомфорт, не влияя, не мучаясь, то боль ли это еще?" (If I can bear pain and discomfort without reacting, without torment, is it still pain?) [#230].
- Tonglen — see *Tonglen (taking and giving)* above.
- The section carries a meme image reading "SO THEN SHE SAYS YOU ONLY LIVE. ONCE" — a humorous nod to the rebirth theme; it carries no further content [#230].

#### Indigenous beliefs: soul loss and restoring connection
- In many Indigenous cultures trauma was understood as a loss of connection between the person, body, soul, nature and the world of spirits [#230].
- Soul loss (Siberian peoples, the Navajo in North America, Amazonian peoples). After severe fright or violence a person seemed to lose part of themselves and became lifeless, pale, silent or tired. The author asks whether this isn't difficulty regulating emotions, or the dissociative states familiar to people with CPTSD. The shaman performed a rite of return of the soul, helping restore the connection with body and nature and thereby wholeness [#230].
- Maori (the source says "Гавайские и Новозеландские коренные народы" — Hawaiian and New Zealand Indigenous peoples): trauma was understood as a violation of tapu — spiritual balance and order. After losses, purification rituals (*noa*) were held to return harmony to the person and community — the balance of the spiritual and physical world [#230].
- Australian Aboriginal peoples: the destruction of the Dreaming path was considered the spiritual cause of illness, suffering and misfortune; healing required restoring the connection with the land and ancestors [#230].
- Suffering as collective. In all these traditions suffering is not personal but a collective event, an imbalance to be restored through shared actions — songs, rites, story. This survives, per the author, in the importance of social support, which matters on every continent to this day. And "return of the soul" practices closely resemble work on re-evaluating traumatic experience so that one can "accept" it and live on [#230].
