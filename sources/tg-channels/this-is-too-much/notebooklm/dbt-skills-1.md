## Crisis survival skills: STOP, TIPP and distraction

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's DBT (dialectical behaviour therapy) skills for getting through peak emotional states: how to recognise distress, the STOP skill for interrupting impulsive actions, body-based skills that rapidly brake emotional arousal (cold water / dive reflex, ice, paired muscle relaxation, intense exercise, relaxing breathing), the ПЕРЕЖИТь acronym of distraction methods with the conditions under which avoiding emotions is legitimate, and a five-step protocol for helplessness. The author frames these as tools for situations when emotions are too strong to be lived through directly — many posts were written in response to frightening collective events ("вот как сейчас" — "like right now") [#15, #20, #107, #109]. Note: the topic title mentions TIPP, but the source never uses the term TIPP; the body skills (cold water, intense exercise, paced breathing, paired muscle relaxation) are presented under the heading of body-based regulation skills [#18].

### Core ideas

- Distress is stress beyond capacity. Ordinary stress is useful mobilisation; distress is excessive stress that makes us non-functional. It can be recognised by "putting a ruler" to emotions: above 75 on a 0–100 scale [#15].
- DBT skills are simple algorithms. DBT is described as a proven-effective therapy for borderline personality disorder with many emotion-regulation skills; a skill is "простые алгоритмы из нескольких действий" (simple algorithms of a few actions) that help stabilise if applied [#15].
- STOP is the most basic first-line skill: stop, step back, look around, act mindfully. Its hardest part is catching the moment to stop; it requires practice [#15, #42, #109, #134].
- Acting through the body is fast and crude — on purpose. Body methods work within seconds to minutes, brake very high arousal, and can halt harmful emotion-avoidance like overeating or substance abuse [#18].
- Avoiding emotions is sometimes the best option. Contrary to the therapy cliché, avoidance is "most often" ineffective but sometimes legitimate — when pain is unbearable and either blocks necessary action or comes from a situation outside your control. Distraction methods are for peak emotions only; used constantly they are likely to harm [#20, #107].
- Helplessness leads to exhaustion, not rescue. Powerlessness is a reaction to another emotion (grief, fear) that cannot be relieved; it pushes frantic searching for a way out while preventing the calm needed to solve problems [#104].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Distress ruler: recognising distress
- Definitions. Stress is a normal state of the body that helps us gather ourselves and act quickly and appropriately. Distress ("дистресс") is excessive stress that the psyche cannot cope with: it makes us non-functional, prevents us being effective in the moment and, naturally, feels very bad [#15].
- The ruler. Imagine emotional amplitude on a scale from 0 to 100, where 0 is complete calm and 100 the maximal strength of emotion. Distress is anything above 75 [#15].
- When we are usually in distress (three situations listed by the author) [#15]:
  1. When the situation around us is very stressful — "вот как сейчас" (like right now).
  2. When we feel a need to do something right now, to react immediately in any way at all.
  3. When it is a sudden emotional peak.
- What to do: the author says she will primarily offer DBT skills to get out of crisis, starting with STOP [#15].

#### STOP skill (СТОП)
Purpose / when to use. The most basic skill DBT offers first; an acronym for four actions [#15]. It helps you stop when you want to do something that may make things worse later, and change your mind — i.e. choose something healthier instead [#109]. The author links it to Viktor Frankl's idea that between stimulus and response there is always time in which we choose how to react, and "Именно здесь лежит наша свобода" (it is precisely here that our freedom lies). Sometimes that time is so short that it is hard to notice it and make a conscious choice instead of what emotions push us toward; STOP is a simple algorithm that helps obtain this freedom [#42].

What it is meant to interrupt — impulsive acts you may regret later [#134]:
- drinking alcohol or using psychoactive substances;
- shouting and quarrels;
- sinking into Netflix at midnight when it is time to sleep;
- eating a fifth dessert;
- 3 hours of obsessive thoughts about the same thing.

The author also gives the context of soothing behaviours that only look helpful: doomscrolling lowers anxiety in the moment but maintains it overall; alcohol distracts in the moment but overall only destabilises one's state; by contrast long walks or favourite music genuinely help [#109].

The four steps (Russian acronym С-Т-О-П; the English card gives STOP / TAKE A STEP BACK / OBSERVE / PROCEED MINDFULLY) [#15, #42, #109, img #134]:
1. С — Стоп (Stop). Literally stop. If you are reading the Facebook news feed, want to do something impulsively or have already begun — just freeze. This is a hard choice in the moment because you are revved up, and running on (reading, shouting, anything) is easier than not running. The card adds: "Не реагируйте! Не двигайтесь. Просто замрите." (Don't react! Don't move. Just freeze.) and "Сохраняйте контроль над собой" (Keep control over yourself) [#15, #42, #109, img #134].
2. Т — Только шаг назад (Just a step back). Step back — physically, by leaving the room, or internally, for example by putting the laptop aside. Focus attention on your breathing. Tell yourself you are taking a break — literally for a couple of minutes. The card adds: step aside, take a deep breath, distance yourself from what is happening [#15, #42, #109, img #134].
3. О — Осмотритесь (Look around / Observe). In crisis (during strong emotions) the horizon of attention shrinks to a single point in space, so even simply looking around helps: look out the window, examine the objects around you. Think about what you are feeling. Try to describe, without judgement, only the facts — what is happening, what others are doing and saying. The card: observe what is happening around and inside you, notice other people and your own feelings and sensations, "Не делайте выводы!" (Don't draw conclusions!) [#15, #42, #109, img #134].
4. П — Попытайтесь действовать осознанно (Try to act mindfully / Proceed mindfully). Think about what would be useful for you at this moment. What would be effective? Which actions could improve or worsen the situation? Only after that return to action. The card phrases the questions as: "Что я хочу от ситуации? Какие у меня цели? Какой выбор сделает ситуацию лучше или хуже?" (What do I want from the situation? What are my goals? Which choice will make the situation better or worse?) [#15, #42, #109, img #134].

Short version and practice. The skill is very simple: stop, leave the situation for a while, think over what is happening and what would be useful to do next. The hardest trick is finding the moment to stop — it needs practice, and you should not give up after the first failure. The steps can be done at virtually any moment, sometimes physically, sometimes only in your head. To test that it is possible almost always, the author proposed a game: everyone plans to do STOP at a fixed time (16:15 that day) regardless of what is going on — stop, step back (mentally is fine), look around, and think what a good continuation would be [#134].

#### Body-based regulation skills — why act through the body
Described as very simple, very direct ways to cope when emotions overwhelm, when you cannot get away from obsessive thoughts, when there is an urge to do something impulsive, or when you cannot calm down. Advantages listed [#18]:
- they work very fast, from a few seconds to a few minutes;
- they help brake very high emotional acceleration;
- they can stop "harmful" ways of avoiding emotions, such as overeating or substance abuse;
- they work in the simplest, crudest ("топорно") way.

#### Cold water (dive reflex)
Mechanism. The "рефлекс ныряльщика" (dive reflex) is a physiological reaction: when a person is immersed in cold water, the heartbeat slows and the parasympathetic nervous system activates. The parasympathetic system is responsible for inhibiting reactions and greater emotional control, so emotional overload is instantly replaced by an inhibition so abrupt it is surprising. Holding the breath plus the effect of icy water on the vagus nerve convince the nervous system that you are under water. Like other mammals, we switch on this reflex; the heart slows almost instantly and emotional reactions brake "Очень Резко (!)" (Very Sharply!) [#18, #41].

How to do it:
1. Full immersion is not necessary. Fill a basin, bowl or sink with very cold (from the fridge / icy) water [#18, #41].
2. Hold your breath and immerse your face for 30 seconds [#18, #41].
3. Use the window that follows: the effect lasts 5–7 minutes. In that time switch your attention, change surroundings, find someone to talk to — in short, insure yourself against slipping back into crisis and regroup [#18, #41].

Substitutes (in decreasing strength): if there is no basin and fridge water, press something very cold to the face, such as a bag of ice or frozen food — and don't forget to hold your breath; if that is unavailable, wash your face with cold water — though the stunning effect of the basin most likely will not follow [#18, #41].

Caution: be careful if you have heart problems [#18].

#### Ice in the hand
Squeeze a piece of ice (an ice cube from the freezer) in your hand and hold it until it melts in your palm. Holding ice hurts but is not harmful in any way, so it lets you fairly safely switch from emotional suffering to physical sensations; it is described as a good "лобовой" (head-on) way to reduce the intensity of an emotion [#41, #20, #107].

#### Paired muscle relaxation (парная мышечная релаксация)
Principle: we relax best if we have first tensed our muscles [#18].
1. Choose a word or phrase that calms you — e.g., banally, "я успокаиваюсь" (I am calming down).
2. One muscle group at a time, tense the muscles as hard as you can on the inhale.
3. On the exhale relax them as fully as possible, saying the chosen phrase to yourself.
4. Go through the whole body this way.

Practice dose: repeat often, about three times a day — then the chosen phrase on its own, without the exercise, will start to help you relax a little [#18].

#### Intense physical exercise
Why: emotions prepare the body for action, and physical exercise is the action into which they can be channelled. Dose: at least 20 minutes of intense physical load — anything from squats and running to walking up stairs. Intensity rule: raise the pulse so that you can still talk but already feel serious exertion [#18].

#### Relaxing breathing (расслабляющее дыхание)
Breathing is the most accessible way to cope with emotions when everything else is hard to do. Many people say breathing does not help them relax, but on follow-up questioning it turns out they breathed for a minute, saw no effect and quit. Do relaxation breathing for no less than 5 minutes [#18].
- Slow your breathing to 5–6 cycles per minute — in general, inhale for 5 counts and exhale for 7. If that is uncomfortable, adapt it to yourself.
- The exhale influences whether you calm down more than the inhale does, so in any variant keep the exhale longer.
- Breathe with the belly, not shy about inflating it properly on the inhale.
[#18]

#### When avoiding emotions is useful
The author argues that therapists say "avoiding emotions is bad" so often that it has become a cliché — and that it is not true. Avoidance is most often ineffective because it leads to even greater emotional suffering, but sometimes it is the best we can do. Two criteria [#20, #107]:
1. When the emotional pain is unbearable and may prevent you from acting effectively where that is necessary.
2. When the emotional pain is unbearable and the situation causing it is outside your control and cannot be resolved immediately.

The author applied this to the current events: in 2022 she wrote that what was happening "наполовину подпадает под критерий 2" (half falls under criterion 2); in the 2024 repost, "Трагедия, которая произошла сейчас, попадает под критерий 2" (the tragedy that has just happened falls under criterion 2). The caveat: if you feel able to cope with your emotions, it is better to let yourself feel and live through them; the techniques below are for situations when you feel you cannot [#20, #107].

#### ПЕРЕЖИТь — the DBT distraction acronym
DBT uses the acronym ПЕРЕЖИТь ("to live through") to remember ways of distracting from emotional suffering. Seven components [#20, #107]:
1. П — помогите кому-нибудь (help someone). Helping someone else who is also suffering from what is happening is a good solution from every angle: (a) attention switches very effectively, since most of us are strongly tuned to respond to other people's pain; (b) when helping we feel like our better selves and generally better; (c) helping can give more meaning to our own emotions, because through them we empathise with another.
2. Е — если сравнить (if you compare). It is said that in times like ours people watch either comedies to distract themselves or horror films to realise that things are still not so bad as long as zombies have not crawled out of the ground; this method is the second one. You can rewatch *Alien* and be glad you are not on a spaceship with an alien monster, or recall frightening things you have already gone through and survived. "Звучит сомнительно, но опыт показывает, что это работает" (It sounds dubious, but experience shows it works).
3. Р — используйте Различные эмоции (use Different emotions). Something surely evokes strong emotions in you — favourite novels, computer games, night raves. To switch away from emotions that feel as if they could crush you, you need other strong emotional stimuli, something capable of fully absorbing you. Recall what grips you most and is hardest to tear yourself away from — and use exactly that.
4. Е — есть другие действия (there are other activities). Some people are very good at escaping into activity when they cannot cope with feelings; the author, for example, can scrub her flat to a shine in about three hours. Look for an activity you sink into easily, in two recommended categories: (1) something that brings pleasure, e.g. self-care routines if they do; (2) something in which you feel your mastery — nothing special is needed: some people feel they are awesome at washing dishes or walking the dog.
5. Ж — постройте Железную стену (build an Iron wall). Scarlett O'Hara's "Я подумаю об этом завтра" (I'll think about it tomorrow) is a way of blocking feelings she could not bear in the moment. You can do as she did, imagine a wall, mentally go off into "внутреннюю Монголию" (inner Mongolia), or simply tell yourself "стоп, сюда мы не думаем" (stop, we don't think in this direction).
6. И — используйте ощущения (use sensations). Oscar Wilde's line "лечите душу ощущениями, а ощущения пусть лечит душа" (cure the soul by means of the senses, and let the soul cure the senses). Intense sensations switch attention even away from strong emotions: hold an ice cube until it melts (see *Ice in the hand*), use tastes — sour, spicy, bitter — or listen to very intense music.
7. Т — течение мыслей перенаправьте (redirect the flow of thoughts). Deliberately switch from the thoughts that cause crisis emotions to anything else: count passers-by on the street, solve equations in your head, choose the ideal dog breed.

Rule of use: these are methods for living through peak emotions. Used constantly they will most likely do more harm than good; but in particular hard cases only they can help [#20, #107].

#### Five steps out of helplessness (бессилие)
Model of helplessness. Powerlessness is more than an emotion — it is a complex feeling with many faces: in it we live through despair, helplessness, loss of hope, rage and panic that come in waves, joined by thoughts of the end, of escape, of searching for a way out, of denying what is happening. It is not a reaction to the situation but a reaction to another emotion that cannot be relieved, such as grief or fear. That is why one can get stuck in it for a long time: it pushes you to do something, anything, to get out, find a solution and save yourself. The trap: effective problem-solving first requires calming down and coming to your senses, but helplessness, not allowing this, pushes you further through a labyrinth with no exit. It affects thoughts, body, perception of reality and behaviour; it feels like high tension and worry and at the same time submission and freezing, like loneliness and despair; thoughts alternate between searching for a solution and feeling that none exists. The author repeats deliberately: "бессилие не ведет к решению и спасению, бессилие ведет к истощению" (helplessness does not lead to a solution and rescue, helplessness leads to exhaustion). Context: she notes there seem never to have been so many social-media posts about powerlessness and loss of hope as now [#104].

Steps [#104]:
1. Assess what is happening to you. Is there tension in the body, and if so where? What emotions are you feeling? What is happening to your thoughts? What do you want to do from this state? Would doing it help you or harm you?
2. Calm your breathing. Put your hands on your belly, take normal inhales and long slow exhales, and watch your hands rise and fall. Continue regardless of the result for at least a couple of minutes.
3. Change your thoughts. Thoughts of powerlessness, even when you really cannot change what you dislike, will not help and will make things worse. Look at the emotion of helplessness itself with compassion and kindness. Think about what is in your power and what you control. Think about people who support you and share your values. Promise yourself you will do what you can when you can, and that right now you need to accept the situation as a given in order to help yourself. Turn yourself toward hope.
4. Change your posture. Don't freeze in one position: walk, do chores, dance ("я серьезно" — "I'm serious").
5. Change the focus of attention. Switch to sensory information: smell, touch, listen, taste, look, notice sensations in the body, movement through space, body position.

Expectation: don't expect the state to change all at once within 5 minutes; repeat all of the above as needed every time the feeling of helplessness returns [#104].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- Cold water: be careful if you have heart problems; substitutes (ice pack, washing the face) likely won't give the full stunning effect; the effect lasts only 5–7 minutes and must be used to switch attention and secure support [#18, #41].
- Distraction / avoidance methods are only for peak emotions and when you feel unable to feel your emotions; used constantly they will likely harm more than help [#20, #107].
- The claim that the "comparison" method works rests on the author's statement that "experience shows" it does, while acknowledging it "sounds dubious" [#20, #107].
- Helplessness work is not a one-off: don't expect change within 5 minutes; repeat each time the feeling returns [#104].
- STOP takes practice; failing to catch the moment at first is expected [#134].

## Mindfulness in DBT: WHAT skills, non-judgment and urge surfing

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's teaching on mindfulness as the foundation module of dialectical behavior therapy (DBT): why people with borderline personality disorder (BPD, ПРЛ) are taught it first, how DBT mindfulness differs from other traditions, and three concrete DBT mindfulness skills — the WHAT skill (observe, describe, participate), non-judgmentalness and effectiveness. It also covers the urge-surfing technique for cravings, how to interpret strange body sensations during meditation, and a list of criticisms and limits of mindfulness. According to the author, mindfulness matters because you cannot regulate what you cannot see: it is the base on which emotion regulation, crisis skills and interpersonal effectiveness are built [#35, #36].

### Core ideas

- Mindfulness is the prerequisite for emotion regulation. To regulate something you must be able to see and understand what you are dealing with; noticing one's inner experience is a learned skill, not a default human capacity (see *Six reasons people with BPD learn mindfulness*) [#35].
- DBT mindfulness is practical, broken into simple actions and mostly informal, and it is integrated into all other DBT skill modules (see *Five specifics of mindfulness in DBT*) [#36].
- Non-judgment is a skill, not a personality trait: you notice evaluative statements and rewrite them as descriptions of facts and feelings [#50].
- Cravings are waves: they can be very strong but don't last long; suppressing them makes them stronger, observing them without acting lets them pass, usually within 20–30 minutes (see *Urge surfing*) [#43].
- Effectiveness means choosing what serves your long-term priorities, sometimes at the cost of something else you want, even justified retribution [#153].
- Odd sensations in meditation are normal and do not require stopping or opening the eyes [#4].
- Mindfulness is not a panacea and carries real risks and misuses (see *Six problems of mindfulness*) [#39].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Six reasons people with BPD learn mindfulness
Purpose: answers the common client question «Меня эмоции регулярно разрывают на части, как мне поможет сидеть в позе лотоса и наблюдать за мыслями как за листьями на реке?» ("My emotions regularly tear me apart — how will sitting in the lotus position and watching my thoughts like leaves on a river help me?"). Lena Foer notes that mindfulness training is one of the first blocks clients go through in DBT, and that many don't know why it exists and why it comes first. This post opened a month-long series on mindfulness [#35].

1. You can't regulate what you can't see. Analogy: tidying a room is an easy task; now imagine doing a full spring-cleaning of an apartment that is new to you, blindfolded — it becomes a multi-hour feat of groping around and guessing where things are. Emotion regulation is the same: you need to observe how your thoughts are connected with your emotions, how emotions show up in bodily sensations, and which way each emotion pushes you. Understanding this is not a "default" human ability; it is something we learn (or don't) during life. The main goal of mindfulness practice is to train the ability to notice and observe one's inner experience, on a par with observing the life around you [#35].
2. Naming emotions lowers their intensity. Research (unnamed in the post) shows that naming emotions by itself reduces their intensity and pressure. A "black cloud" of unknown affect is far less manageable, as well as far scarier, than understandable emotions you can describe — so mindfulness also works directly for emotion regulation [#35].
3. Relationships. People with BPD often struggle in communication with people important to them. Mindfulness skills help notice what you are doing and what the Other is doing in an interaction, where someone really reacted aggressively and where they did not; they help notice what you want from the relationship and how far your actions match those intentions. This moves you away from acting on autopilot — and not only in communication [#35].
4. Impulse control. Impulsivity is a BPD symptom and often leads to risky behaviour and ill-considered decisions. Mindfulness can help develop more control over impulses by observing thoughts and feelings instead of reacting to them immediately with action [#35].
5. Life happens in the present. Mindfulness teaches you to catch the slipping "now", stay in contact with it and get joy from direct contact with life [#35].
6. Less suffering. When we forget mindfulness, the mind wanders between past and future, between worries about what will happen and regrets about what happened. Staying in the "here and now" reduces the amount of suffering in life [#35].

#### Five specifics of mindfulness in DBT
Lena Foer: mindfulness is a key component of DBT but looks somewhat different from other approaches or, for example, Buddhism [#36].
1. Mindfulness is the base for all of DBT. The mindfulness skills are designed so that they later integrate into ways of regulating emotions, skills for acute crisis states and ways of increasing interpersonal effectiveness [#36].
2. "Applied" practices for addictions and destructive urges. DBT has mindfulness practices for working with addictions or any other destructive impulses, because such impulses are not rare in BPD (urge surfing below is one such practice) [#36].
3. Tilted toward emotion regulation. DBT mindfulness skills are "sharpened" a bit more toward emotion regulation [#36].
4. Broken into simple actions. Ordinary mindfulness techniques are split into separate, easier-to-carry-out actions [#36].
5. Mostly informal practice. Many informal practices and few formal ones: you almost never need to sit in meditation, but you often need to observe your everyday life [#36].

#### DBT WHAT skill: observe, describe, participate
One of the first mindfulness skills in DBT. "WHAT" means what to do to be in a state of mindfulness; there are three options: observe, describe, participate [#36].

1. Observe (наблюдать):
- Start by observing your surroundings, thoughts, feelings and any physical sensations without reacting to them [#36].
- Observe without condemnation, without evaluations, without criticism [#36].
- Don't react to thoughts, emotions and urges wherever they push you — just notice them [#36].
- Don't try to hold on to experiences to examine them better or feel them more deeply — let them leave freely [#36].
- Don't try to turn away from discomfort — acknowledge it and be kind to it (and to yourself) [#36].
- Stay alert to the present moment; if you get distracted, bring yourself back without judgment [#36].
- Pay attention to input from your five senses — sight, sound, smell, taste and touch [#36].

2. Describe (описывать):
- Use neutral, non-evaluative words to describe your inner experience and what you see [#36].
- Don't get stuck on the content — call a thought a thought and a feeling a feeling [#36].
- The content of thoughts doesn't always match reality, so it is more fitting to describe them as «Мне пришла в голову мысль, что я ужасный человек» ("The thought came to me that I am a terrible person") instead of «Я ужасный человек, просто ужасный» ("I am a terrible person, just terrible") [#36].

3. Participate (участвовать):
- Participating means being fully immersed in what you are doing. Sometimes it's easy — e.g. snowboarding down a mountain — and sometimes harder, e.g. doing something mundane like washing dishes [#36].
- Participate fully in the experience. If you are taking a shower, participate in that moment: notice how the gel feels on your skin, the water on your body — be in the moment [#36].
- Participate in every experience, whatever it brings you [#36].
- Practise letting go of self-focused thoughts such as «Как я выгляжу» ("How do I look") or «Как у меня дела?» ("How am I doing?"). If they come, notice them and gently bring yourself back to participating [#36].

#### Non-judgmental skill (безоценочность)
Purpose, as framed by Lena Foer: for people who want to be able to support themselves, build relationships in a healthier way, regulate emotions better, and see more often the world as it really is, without "black" or "rose-tinted" glasses [#50].

What it is: noticing your evaluative, criticizing, condemning statements — those that split the world into black and white, good and bad, i.e. statements more related to your interpretation than to facts — and, having noticed them, trying without self-condemnation to rephrase them non-judgmentally [#50].

Examples given (judgmental → non-judgmental description of what is happening) [#50]:
- «Я идиотка, что забыла дома ключи» ("I'm an idiot for forgetting my keys at home") — judgmental.
- «Я забыла дома ключи и очень злюсь, что теперь мне придется лезть домой через форточку» ("I forgot my keys at home and I'm very angry that now I'll have to climb in through the window") — non-judgmental description.
- «Пойти домой по темной улице было глупым решением» ("Walking home down a dark street was a stupid decision") — judgmental.
- «Мне страшно на темной улице и я виню себя за то, что решила по ней пойти» ("I'm scared on the dark street and I blame myself for choosing to walk down it") — non-judgmental description.

The lesson visible in the examples: the non-judgmental version replaces a verdict ("idiot", "stupid") with the facts plus the emotion actually felt (anger, fear, self-blame) [#50].

How to practise [#50]:
1. At any time, simply by observing your own words and thoughts.
2. Through exercises — see *Snowflake non-judgment exercise* below.

#### Snowflake non-judgment exercise
Described by the author as "hardcore": take scissors and a sheet of paper and try to cut out a snowflake, as in childhood for New Year. While cutting, unfolding and looking at the result, try NOT to evaluate it. Her spoiler: «это сложно!» ("it's hard!") [#50].

#### Urge surfing (серфинг желаний)
Purpose: "how not to do what you very much want to do but know will be bad for you" — e.g. checking the news every 10 minutes, overeating, reading social media, abusing alcohol or other substances. Urge surfing is a psychological technique from work with addictions: you don't do what the addiction pushes you toward, while staying aware of it. Rationale: attempts to simply switch away and pretend the craving isn't there make the craving stronger. The technique likens an acute urge to ocean waves, which can be huge but pass quickly; a craving likewise can be terribly strong but doesn't last long [#43].

Steps [#43]:
1. Notice that an urge to do something has appeared. Relate to the urge non-judgmentally and don't condemn yourself for it.
2. Observe the urge without doing what it pushes you to do. Notice how it gets stronger, then weaker. Notice how your thoughts try to find a reasonable excuse to do what you want so much. Watch the temptation arise to do it half-consciously, in passing.
3. Wait until the craving passes. It usually takes 20–30 minutes, after which the urge subsides — the author stresses this figure comes from samples of people with addictions.

What you can do while the craving lasts (complete list) [#43]:
- hold a piece of ice in your hand;
- take a warm shower;
- watch funny animal videos;
- light a scented candle;
- imagine yourself in some very relaxing place.

Limits: if the issue is not news or blogs but hard drugs, relying solely on a mindfulness technique would be naive, in the author's words — "I think that's obvious anyway" [#43].

#### Effectiveness skill (эффективность)
Definition: effectiveness is one of the six DBT mindfulness skills — in the moment we try to relate to our priorities for the future, decide what to do next, and think about the short- and long-term consequences of different actions. The skill's essence is the ability to make such decisions while listening to yourself in the process [#153].

Everyday examples from the author [#153]:
- Not yelling at a partner who is annoying you, because you remember that the relationship matters and you want it to continue.
- Remembering why you went to medical school when, instead of preparing for an exam, you want to watch YouTube all night.

Historical illustration: in an interview, Harald Jähner, author of "Wolfszeit" (Wolf Time) on the rebuilding of post-war Germany, was asked how former Nazis and their victims could work together and build a shared country. His answer (as quoted in Russian): it worked because of something "we despised when we were young — drawing a line beyond which a general amnesty was declared. We simply won't remember and look at who did what in the past"; if old Nazis stopped openly dreaming of Hitler and spreading propaganda, they were accepted. He calls this possibly blindness or making things too convenient, but it led to anti-fascists, former Nazis and persecuted Jews working in one office; "if you are a highly moral person, it's hard to accept that silence helps build a new society. Yet, fatally, that's how it was." Lena Foer calls it hard to imagine a clearer (and also more unpleasant) example of effectiveness: ex-Nazis, anti-fascists and Jews together decided that creating a new society on new principles mattered more to them than retribution for yesterday's crimes and habitual hatred. Her lesson: sometimes, to do something truly important, you have to give up something else — for example, just retribution [#153].

#### Six problems of mindfulness
Lena Foer notes that she, DBT therapists in general and society talk a lot about the positive effects of mindfulness, so "for variety" she lists its problems [#39]:
1. Not a panacea. In severe crisis states it is unlikely to help unless you have many years of experience with long meditations — yet it is very often sold precisely as a panacea [#39].
2. Deceptively straightforward. "Sit and watch your breath, what could go wrong?" In practice different people meet different difficulties: some lack instructions, others lack understanding of what to do with what they find during meditation. Solving these problems is easier with someone to consult — or with a lot of persistence and books [#39].
3. Can become a new reason for self-criticism. If you lose sight of the emphasis on kindness and self-compassion, practice can easily turn into another reason for self-criticism — because perfect mindfulness doesn't exist and a mind drifting into unrelated thoughts is normal [#39].
4. Can be misused for avoidance or forced acceptance. Mindfulness can be used to distract yourself, or conversely to push yourself to accept real problems in the outside world that ought to be solved — for example, a destructive relationship or a dysfunctional work environment [#39].
5. Can indirectly feed social inequality by putting first the approach "everyone is responsible for their own mental well-being". Taking responsibility yourself is not bad, but this way systemic social problems can be overlooked [#39].
6. Risk of encountering something you weren't ready for. When you closely observe internal processes, you may notice something hard to process on your own; for such cases it is good to have a mindfulness trainer or a psychologist [#39].

### Detailed insights

#### Strange sensations during meditation are normal
The (anonymous) channel post reports that during mindfulness practice, skills-training participants often describe strange sensations: becoming lighter or heavier, body proportions changing, a feeling of floating in water. Trainers usually advise opening the eyes and continuing to meditate that way to avoid the sensations. The author's view: this is rather excessive attention to a normal process [#4].

Supporting passage from the book *Mindfulness in Simple English*: people experience all manner of phenomena in meditation — itches, tingling, deep relaxation, lightness, floating, feeling oneself growing, shrinking or rising in the air; beginners often get excited. Mechanism given there: "As relaxation sets in, the nervous system simply begins to pass sensory signals more efficiently. Large amounts of previously blocked sensory data can pour through, giving rise to all kinds of unique sensations." It "does not signify anything in particular. It is just sensation." Instruction: employ the normal technique — watch it come up, watch it pass away, don't get involved [#4].

The author's own condensed Russian rendering of the mechanism differs in emphasis: cut off from its natural volume of sensory data, the nervous system overstates the significance of the specific data that remain — hence the odd sensations. Key point: everything is going normally, you can relax and keep meditating as before; encouraging fears about a natural process is, in the author's words, «ну, такое» ("well, not great") [#4].

## Radical acceptance and validation

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's material on two DBT-related acceptance skills: validation of other people (and of oneself), taught as six levels for supporting loved ones in times of war and disagreement, and radical acceptance / accepting reality, taught through its definition, the situations where it applies, ten practical tips and ten reflection questions. It closes with a Q&A answer by psychologist Lena Foer on how to perceive a life of continuous problems "more lightly" by combining mindfulness, acceptance and self-kindness. The source frames these skills as a response to a painful world: invalidation "destroys relationships", and fighting a reality that cannot be changed keeps painful emotions going [#75, #76, #88, #171].

### Core ideas

- Validation is acknowledgement, not agreement. You can recognise someone's thoughts and feelings as understandable and legitimate while disagreeing with them — which makes validation a way to care for people whose views differ from yours. Full detail in *Six levels of validation* [#75].
- Invalidation damages relationships. Rejecting, ignoring or judging another's experience creates distance and pain, especially in a hard world [#75].
- Fighting reality increases suffering. People tend to fight what causes them physical or emotional pain; the mind doesn't realise this works against it, producing suffering instead of relief and keeping painful emotions alive (citing Linehan, 1993) [#76].
- Acceptance is not approval, giving up, or forgiveness, and it does not remove pain. It reduces suffering and the strength of emotions, takes time, and is a prerequisite for moving on. Full detail in *Ten tips for accepting reality* [#76, #88].
- Radical acceptance is for what cannot be changed — uncontrollable circumstances, the past, other people's behaviour, chronic pain, unexpected changes [#88].
- Life can be full of problems AND valuable. Accepting uncertainty and changeability improves well-being; mindfulness, acceptance ("as if") and self-compassion are ways to get there, while admitting honestly that sometimes life is simply hard [#171].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Six levels of validation
Purpose / when to use: to support loved ones and other people when the world is frightening — the post was written when "войн в мире стало еще больше" (there are even more wars in the world) — and to care for important people even when you disagree or hold different views. Validation means accepting your own or another's experience as understandable and legitimate; the levels are the "how" [#75].

Definition. Validation "не подразумевает согласия или одобрения, а означает признание и принятие мыслей, чувств, ощущений и поведения другого человека как понятных и имеющих место быть" (does not imply agreement or approval, but means acknowledging and accepting another person's thoughts, feelings, sensations and behaviour as understandable and legitimate). Key rule: "Можно признавать и не соглашаться." (You can acknowledge and not agree.) [#75]

What it replaces — emotional invalidation. When emotions run high, in a fight, when we are not ready to listen to each other or ourselves, we fall into emotional invalidation: our thoughts and feelings are rejected, ignored or judged. We may blame, condemn, deny or minimise the importance of another person's experience. The author notes that even if we don't mean it in substance, "по форме все грешим" (in form we all sin). Invalidation destroys relationships and creates distance; it is painful and unpleasant not to be understood or listened to, and it is especially destructive "когда мир такой как сейчас" (when the world is the way it is now) [#75].

The six levels:

1. Being present (Присутствовать). Give someone your full attention and show compassion; be interested and listen actively. Examples from the author: holding someone's hand; listening and doing nothing but listening; listening to 10-minute voice messages to the end; walking at night to a friend's home to sit with her while she cries and tells how hard it is. "Скажем нет многозадачности" (Let's say no to multitasking). Why it works: one common reason people feel uncomfortable around strong emotions is that they don't know what to say — and often the answer is simply presence, just sitting together in silence [#75].
2. Reflecting (Отражение). Summarise what the other person said — the familiar phrases like «правильно ли я понял…» (did I understand correctly…) and retelling what was said. Why: it is an effective way to make sure two people really understand each other and are on the same wavelength. We may perceive events differently, and sometimes understanding — and showing that you understood — "дорогого стоит" (is worth a lot) [#75].
3. Mind-reading (Чтение мыслей). Guess what someone might be feeling or thinking based on the situation. Purpose: to help the person label their emotions accurately, because being able to name feelings is a step toward regulating them. Some people may be unclear about what they feel because they were not allowed to have their feelings or learned to fear them. How: when someone describes a situation, notice their emotional state; then either name the emotions you hear, or guess what the person might be feeling. Example: «Кажется, что тебе страшно и ты злишься из-за происходящего в мире» (It seems you're scared and angry about what's happening in the world) — "это валидация" (this is validation) [#75].
4. Understanding in the context of experience and biology. Behaviour can be understood in the context of the person's history and biology. Experience example: if your loved one emigrated to Israel or Armenia and now there is a new escalation and geopolitical conflict, it may be impossible for them to react to the situation wisely. Validating at this level means understanding their experience: «Я понимаю твою фрустрацию и непонимание что делать, ты уже переживал нечто похожее и знаешь как это тяжело» (I understand your frustration and not knowing what to do — you've already been through something similar and know how hard it is). Biology: highly sensitive people have a biological predisposition to feel some situations more intensely and vividly, so validating a reaction by this feature of their nervous system can also be validation [#75].
5. Normalising (Нормализация). Recognise that emotional reactions are often normal responses to specific situations. Knowing your emotions are normal is useful for everyone; for an emotionally sensitive person, knowing that someone else was also "на эмоциях" (emotional) in that situation is validating. Example: «Конечно, ты беспокоишься. Жить в военное время пугает всех» (Of course you're worried. Living in wartime frightens everyone). Why: it reduces the feeling of isolation [#75].
6. Radical genuineness (Радикальная искренность). Understand someone's emotions by sharing a similar experience — sharing it as an equal. This builds a deep connection. Example: "your worry is normal and war frightens everyone" — and how do I know? I'm terrified too, so I can assume you feel something similar [#75].

#### Ten tips for accepting reality
Source: a post forwarded by Lena Foer from the channel Volnovoy effekt (Ripple Effect), titled «Навык принятия реальности» (The reality-acceptance skill), tagged as a DBT skill / radical acceptance. Rationale given before the tips: as humans we tend to fight what causes us physical or emotional pain; our mind does not realise this works against us, causing suffering rather than relieving pain (Linehan, 1993). When you have problems controlling emotions, this tendency may show up even more strongly, but in fact the tendency to fight reality helps keep painful emotions going [#76].

1. Acceptance does not mean approval. You don't have to love reality, but to reduce your suffering you must accept it [#76].
2. Acceptance also does not mean giving up or stopping trying to change the situation [#76].
3. When frustrated, remember acceptance reduces the strength of emotions. It will take some time — hold on, don't give up [#76].
4. Acceptance won't happen at once. The harder the situation, the harder it is to accept and the more time it will take [#76].
5. Recall past situations you managed to accept. This helps accept reality even when it seems hard [#76].
6. Acceptance is not the same as forgiveness. We work on acceptance for our own sake; it has nothing to do with the other person. Forgiveness is optional; acceptance is necessary to move on [#76].
7. If accepting the whole thing feels too heavy, break the situation into components that can be accepted one by one [#76].
8. Focus on the present; stay away from the future. We cannot accept something that hasn't happened yet [#76].
9. Don't try to accept evaluative judgements — it's impossible. The skill is about accepting reality; judgements are only judgements, not facts [#76].
10. Accepting reality doesn't mean the pain will disappear. It reduces suffering, but pain is a natural part of human life [#76].

#### Five situations for radical acceptance
Definitions first (Katya Klimochkina): acceptance "означает признание и понимание ситуации такой, какая она есть, без необходимости испытывать к ней симпатию или одобрение" (means acknowledging and understanding a situation as it is, without needing to like or approve of it) — acknowledging reality and deciding not to resist or fight things that cannot be changed. Radical acceptance is a DBT concept of complete acceptance of reality, especially when facing situations that are challenging, upsetting or out of your control; it is about acknowledging and accepting the present moment without judgement — "без оценок" (without evaluations) [#88].

When to radically accept:
1. Unchangeable circumstances — situations outside your control that cannot be changed, e.g. the death of a loved one, a natural disaster, and war [#88].
2. Past events — to make peace with things from your past that cannot be changed, including personal mistakes, losses or traumatic experiences [#88].
3. Other people's behaviour — which you cannot control. People have their own choices and actions, and accepting this can reduce unnecessary suffering. The author adds a crossed-out aside that letting others be themselves is "что-то на уровне фантастики" (something on the level of fantasy) [#88].
4. Chronic pain — for people dealing with chronic, constant pain, radical acceptance helps acknowledge limitations and find ways to cope effectively [#88].
5. Unexpected changes — when life takes an unexpected turn, e.g. sudden job loss or an unforeseen change in life circumstances such as emigration; radical acceptance helps adapt to the new reality [#88].

#### Ten reflection questions for accepting what you disagree with
Purpose: help you "принять то, с чем вы не согласны" (accept what you disagree with) so you can move on and stop suffering. Ask yourself [#88]:
1. What exactly is the reality I am currently struggling hard to accept?
2. Am I holding any judgements or evaluations about this situation that cause me suffering?
3. What emotions do I feel facing this reality? Can I name them and acknowledge them without judgement?
4. If I fully accepted this situation as it is, how would it feel in my body? What physical sensations arise?
5. What is the cost of refusing to accept this reality? How does non-acceptance affect my well-being and relationships?
6. What would it mean to give up the need for things to be different?
7. Can I identify any "must" or "that's how it should be" statements I impose on the situation? How do these expectations feed my struggle?
8. What evidence confirms the reality I am resisting? How can I look at the facts objectively, without distortions?
9. How might acceptance open new possibilities for growth and positive change?
10. What would it be like to approach this situation with an attitude of willingness rather than resistance?

Closing reminder: radical acceptance is not about liking or approving; it is acknowledging reality and choosing not to fight it, which can lead to coping with difficulties more effectively and living "эту жизнь полегче" (this life a bit more easily) [#88].

#### Clarifying a vague goal ("take the world more lightly")
Context: reader Masha wrote that she has many problems which she always solves successfully, yet she keeps waiting for the moment when she finishes this and this and this issue and the issues will end — but new difficulties always come. The last two years were a "мясорубка" (meat grinder), and it feels as if the grinding stays with her forever; she sees it as not being able to accept that she lives an adult life, and wants to perceive the world and its challenges more lightly [#171].

Method (Lena Foer): in cognitive-behavioural approaches, the first step with clients is agreeing on the request — the goal you move toward together — and the more detailed the goal, the better. "Perceive the world more lightly" is a great goal, but to move toward it you need to understand [#171]:
- What does "more lightly" mean? How would you think about your problems if you perceived them more lightly? How would you react to new ones?
- What would change in behaviour? What would you start doing differently — take up a hobby, invite friends out more often, go to interesting places? How often?

Answers to these questions help clarify what it makes sense to move toward [#171].

#### Three ideas for accepting life as it is
Claim: research shows that accepting life as it is now — including its uncertainty and changeability — can strongly improve psychological well-being (Lena Foer; no specific study named). Three ideas for moving toward it [#171]:
1. Mindfulness practices help you live in the present moment without fixating on future problems. You can simply sit in an armchair for 15 minutes and observe your thoughts, or build mindfulness into daily life — e.g. while walking, focus on the sensations in your feet or on what you see around you. Important: it is not an instant remedy — to notice anything you need at least a couple of weeks of practice. Thich Nhat Hanh's books add "+500 к вдохновленности процессом" (+500 to inspiration for the process) [#171].
2. Accepting difficulties as part of life can reduce our struggle with them. Acceptance means life can be full of problems AND be valuable and pleasant. It is hard to explain how to practise acceptance in two words; DBT offers a full instruction. First of all, acting "как если бы" (as if) helps: as if you had already accepted your life as it is and decided to do what is valuable and enjoyable right now, without waiting for other tasks to be finished [#171].
3. Self-kindness rules. Research shows self-compassion improves our ability to cope with stress. Saying supportive things to yourself and doing pleasant things works, even if it sounds only moderately convincing at first [#171].

Final note: sometimes life really is very hard and difficult to enjoy. Denying this means slipping into excessive psychologising of everything and ignoring reality. Sometimes the best we can do is admit that it's hard and give ourselves an extra half hour lying on the couch [#171].

### Detailed insights

#### The stream-and-stones story (Jack Kornfield)
In Jack Kornfield's book *A Path with Heart* there is a story of a man who studied meditation with a Tibetan teacher and decided to strive for enlightenment. He went to the mountains and built a small hut by a stream. When he began meditating, he noticed he could not stop his mind — it was flooded with thoughts and memories. It got to the point where he noticed his mind singing in unison with the babbling stream next to his hut. Unable to bear it, he began moving the stones in the stream, trying to make it sing a different song. Lesson: we often do similar things. Kornfield says that when problems arise we project our frustration onto them, as if the rain, the children or the world were the cause of our discomfort; we imagine we can change the world and become happy — but we find happiness and lose illusions "не путем перемещения камней, а путем изменения своего к ним отношения" (not by moving stones, but by changing our attitude toward them) [#76].

#### Framing of acceptance in the channel
Katya Klimochkina opens the radical-acceptance post with Sheldon from *The Big Bang Theory* calling gravity heartless — and says one sometimes wants to say the same about the reality we live in. If that reality cannot be changed, the answer is what "не нравится ни-ко-му" (no-bo-dy likes): accepting it as it is [#88].
