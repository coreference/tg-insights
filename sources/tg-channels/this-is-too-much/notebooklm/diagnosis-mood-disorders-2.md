## Bipolar disorder: routines, caffeine and pregnancy

### What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel taught about managing bipolar affective disorder (БАР, BD) beyond the bare prescription: the Interpersonal and Social Rhythm Therapy (IPSRT) approach and its rationale (circadian rhythms, zeitgebers, relationships), the way the DBT skills training for BD borrows from IPSRT, a systematic review on caffeine as it relates to mania, depression, suicidality, sleep and lithium in BD, and a perinatal psychiatrist's commentary on medication, monitoring and psychotherapy for women with BD around pregnancy. According to the author, these matter because routine disruptions and interpersonal stress are *modifiable* causes of relapse that persist even on mood stabilizers, because common fears (e.g., about a cup of coffee) are more complicated than simple myths or rules, and because untreated or poorly managed BD can affect the course of pregnancy and the child [#220, #151, #154].

This part adds a new sub-theme to the topic: how emotion regulation works in bipolar affective disorder (БАР, BD) and how it differs from other disorders. It comes from a post and its linked Telegraph longread «Регуляция эмоций и БАР» (Emotion regulation and BD), which covers emotional and social intelligence, face-emotion recognition, neurobiological heterogeneity, adaptive vs maladaptive coping strategies, and comparisons of BD with depression and with borderline personality disorder (BPD). The post advertises that the longread also contains "~~оценочными суждениями от меня~~" (*value judgements from me*, struck through), and it does: the author openly criticizes part of the research she summarizes [#100, article #100].

Relation to the existing topic file: the existing file covers IPSRT, routines/zeitgebers, caffeine and pregnancy; it says nothing about emotion regulation, so this part *extends* the topic. One link to existing material: the longread notes that mood cycling and unpredictable behaviour shrink social interaction — consistent with the existing file's point that relationships and BD symptoms affect each other (the *Relationship stress → biological rhythm chain*), though the longread does not draw this connection itself [#100].

### Core ideas

- Medication is necessary but often insufficient in BD. Relapses happen even on mood stabilizers because of disrupted sleep–wake cycles and interpersonal problems; these are modifiable and therefore the target of therapy such as IPSRT [#220].
- Stable routines protect against episodes. Even small disruptions to sleep, energy and eating patterns can precede relapses and phase switches; stabilizing routines makes mood steadier and prolongs remission [#220].
- Relationships drive biological rhythms. Conflicts, grief and role changes knock rhythms off, which then destabilizes mood — so BD therapy addresses relationships, not only schedules [#220].
- Caffeine and BD: a real but contested link. A review reports manic/hypomanic/mixed switches after caffeine and lithium-level rises after caffeine reduction; its findings on depression and suicidality contradict each other; caffeine clearly disrupts sleep. The authors conclude the link is undeniable but the specifics are unknown, and the choice is the reader's [#151; article #151].
- In pregnancy, BD treatment is usually continued, not stopped. Stopping medication risks rapid postpartum manic and mixed states; only lamotrigine among mood stabilizers is considered safe, several antipsychotics are allowed, and lithium is possible under close monitoring [#154].
- Psychotherapy is an important adjunct, not the method of choice, in BD. Medication comes first; IPSRT, CBT or DBT and family work (psychoeducation, crisis plan) add significant value [#154].

- Emotion dysregulation is a transdiagnostic "glue" that binds many mental difficulties; BD shares it with depression, anxiety, eating disorders, personality disorders, ADHD and complex PTSD, but its nature and intensity vary by disorder [#100].
- The BD-specific "flavour" described in the research is impaired emotional/social intelligence and weaker recognition of others' facial emotions, plus emotions hitting with greater intensity — a claim the author herself finds unconvincing [#100].
- Strategy choice has a brain basis. Anyone can choose adaptive or maladaptive strategies, but brain activity predisposes which ones a person reaches for; in BD, neural activation is heterogeneous, so different people with BD favour different strategies [#100].
- People with BD use maladaptive strategies more often than people without, but compared with BPD they use *more* adaptive and fewer negative-focused strategies — which the author reads as proof that "biology is not a sentence" and regulation can be learned [#100].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### IPSRT (Interpersonal and Social Rhythm Therapy) — three functions
The author describes IPSRT as an approach developed specifically for bipolar disorder, a working evidence-based method that, in her view, still few people know about [#220]. In the pregnancy longread it is named, alongside CBT and DBT, among the evidence-based psychotherapies for BD that account for biological, social and psychological factors; besides work on the sleep and activity regime it pays great attention to social aspects: how and with whom the person communicates, who is in their closest circle, and who triggers them [#154]. What it does — three components:
1. Stabilizes circadian rhythms and daily routines — how one sleeps, when one goes to bed and gets up, how one eats, and one's activities. The rationale is the zeitgeber model below: regular routines keep the circadian system stable [#220].
2. Works with interpersonal stress and relationships, which often knock these rhythms off — it helps a person cope with grief, conflicts and changes of life roles. The author's example of a role change: becoming a parent or a manager "может быть приятным, но все таки потрясением и стрессом" (can be pleasant, but is still a shock and a stress) [#220].
3. Combines behavioural, interpersonal and psychoeducational strategies with the aim of lowering the risk of new episodes of hypo/mania or depression [#220].

Why bother when there is pharmacotherapy (the author's answer to «Зачем это всё, если есть фарма?» — *Why all this if there are meds?*): medications are important but often insufficient; even on mood stabilizers relapses occur due to sleep–wake cycle disruptions and interpersonal problems. These factors are changeable, and they are exactly what therapy can influence. Stabilizing routine and lowering stress make mood more stable and help extend remission [#220].

#### Routine disruption → episode pathway (zeitgebers and circadian instability)
Why IPSRT emphasizes normalizing the daily schedule [#220]:
1. Baseline vulnerability. People with BD often have disrupted sleep and wake cycles, energy levels and eating patterns. Even small disruptions can precede relapses and phase switches. (The author struck through "с БАР" — *with BD* — in "У людей с БАР", hinting the point applies to people generally.) [#220]
2. Zeitgebers keep the clock set. A zeitgeber (German "time-giver"; the author jokingly says she has always pronounced it «зайтгибер») is anything that lets us understand what time it is, day or night. For humans these are not only sunrise and sunset but also social signals. The author's full list of examples: neighbours becoming active behind the wall; the work schedule; mealtimes (and a rumbling stomach); craving coffee; meetups with friends; the sounds of phone notifications [#220].
3. When routines collapse, the system destabilizes. Routine-breakers she names: time-zone changes; night shifts; irregular working hours ("привет, трудоголики!" — *hello, workaholics!*); stress. When routines collapse, the circadian system becomes unstable and can trigger a rise or a drop in mood — i.e., hypo/mania or depression [#220].

#### Relationship stress → biological rhythm chain
Why IPSRT focuses on relationships: «"Жизнь" напрямую связана с биологическими ритмами» ("Life" is directly connected to biological rhythms) [#220]. The author gives two chains:
- Conflict chain: a fight with a partner → can't fall asleep because you're caught up in thoughts and worries → sleep is disrupted → mood destabilizes [#220].
- Breakup/grief chain: breaking up with a partner → grief → you stop caring about your daily routine: not washing, not combing your hair, taking sick leave from work → depression begins [#220].
IPSRT addresses these through work with grief, conflicts and role changes (component 2 above) [#220].

#### Caffeine and BD — review findings by domain
The longread retells, by domain, the observations a systematic review (1,678 records screened, 17 studies included; source given as PubMed 32949106) collected on caffeine in people with BD. The headline findings: some people with BD reacted to caffeine in various amounts with a phase switch into a manic, hypomanic or mixed state, and/or their serum lithium concentration rose after they *reduced* caffeine intake [#151; article #151].

A. Mania/hypomania and caffeine [#151]
1. Activation adds to mania. Caffeine's effect on psychomotor activation (fussiness, restlessness) and arousal may contribute to manic symptoms. Caffeine may interact with the hyperdopaminergic state (a lot of dopamine) already present in BD, amplifying mania.
2. Caffeine can mimic mania. Large doses (usually more than 250 mg) can cause caffeine intoxication, whose symptoms pass within 24 hours. Chronic excessive intake produces tolerance, which lowers the risk of intoxication. Symptoms of caffeine intoxication — anxiety, nervousness, rapid speech — resemble feeling manic, and can arise both from the amount and from what the caffeine came in (e.g., an energy drink or cola).
3. Which came first — mania or caffeine? Impulsivity and reduced behavioural control during manic episodes can lead to drinking more caffeine, which can in turn intensify symptoms.
4. Energy drinks are a special risk. They often contain additional stimulants that add to the arousing effect; energy drinks, alone or combined with coffee and cola, raise the likelihood of manic symptoms.
5. Cutting down helps. Reducing or stopping caffeine may ease manic symptoms, which underlines its potential role in worsening episodes.

B. Depression, suicidality and caffeine — the authors flag this section as «все неоднозначно и запутано» (everything is ambiguous and tangled) [#151]:
1. Depression: much coffee or little? Some studies say higher intake of coffee and caffeinated drinks is generally associated with a lower risk of depression. Others describe a specific relationship: about 600 ml of coffee a day (roughly 3–4 cups) is associated with a lower risk of depression, while higher intake does not lower it further.
2. Depressive symptoms and self-medication. Some studies show people in a depressive state may consume more caffeine during an episode, possibly as self-medication against fatigue and low energy. They may also use caffeine to relieve psychomotor retardation (slowness in movements and actions) and loss of energy, or to soften the sedative effect of some medications.
3. Contradictory data on suicidality. Some studies find that coffee drinkers are more than twice as likely to show suicidal behaviour as non-drinkers, with risk rising as the amount grows. Other studies stress that in the general population moderate coffee intake — up to 8 cups a day (the authors add "Карл!" — an exclamation of disbelief) — tends to be associated with a lower suicide risk, but above 8 cups risk may rise, suggesting a complex relationship in which very high caffeine intake could potentially increase suicide risk.

C. Sleep and caffeine — research links high caffeine intake with poor sleep quality [#151]:
1. Caffeine disrupts sleep (see *Caffeine and sleep — four ways*).
2. Vicious circle. People with poor sleep quality are more likely to consume more caffeine — «Порочный круг!» (A vicious circle!).
3. Long sleepers drink more. People classified as "long sleepers" (sleeping more than average) tend to drink more coffee than "normal" sleepers or "short sleepers".
4. Sleep → mood → phase. Sleep problems worsened by caffeine can trigger or speed up bouts of low mood, which can affect phase switches.

D. The authors' conclusion. Everything is ambiguous, contradictory and under-studied. There is no specific knowledge of how caffeine affects BD, but the fact that a relationship exists is undeniable. As usual, we make decisions ourselves, so whether to give up caffeine is the reader's choice [#151].

#### Caffeine and sleep — four ways caffeine disrupts sleep
According to the longread, caffeine is known to disrupt sleep because it [#151]:
1. Increases the time needed to fall asleep.
2. Shortens total sleep time.
3. Lowers sleep efficiency.
4. Worsens the perceived quality of sleep.

#### Pharmacotherapy for BD in pregnancy — decision points
Commentary by psychiatrist Dina Memetova. Starting premise: uncompensated BD (in particular depressive episodes) can affect the course of pregnancy, and whether pharmacotherapy for BD is present in the perinatal period, before delivery, is very important. The question of medication depends on many factors — for example, whether the pregnancy is only being planned or the woman is already pregnant [#154].
1. Already on a regimen when pregnancy occurs → continue it. If a woman with BD becomes pregnant and already has a selected medication regimen, it is important to keep taking the prescribed drugs, because there is a risk of deterioration in the postpartum period. From practice: women who stopped medication during pregnancy faced manic and mixed states in the postpartum period that develop quite quickly [#154].
2. Mood stabilizers: essentially only lamotrigine. The choice is small: the safest and the only one allowed is lamotrigine. The others have a teratogenic effect and are not prescribed. If a woman takes lamotrigine, fairly high doses of folic acid are prescribed starting 3 months before conception and during pregnancy [#154].
3. Antipsychotics: more options. Among the "old" (typical) antipsychotics, haloperidol can be the drug of choice for mixed and manic states. Among the "new" (atypical) ones, many are permitted, including quetiapine and aripiprazole (the source itself ends this list open-ended, without naming further drugs). Watch for side effects: restlessness (неусидчивость), hand tremor, muscle rigidity — the source labels these «нейролептический синдром» (neuroleptic syndrome). Also track weight gain and blood glucose, because of the risk of gestational diabetes (hyperglycaemia, raised blood glucose) [#154].
4. Lithium when it is the only suitable drug — continued, under the monitoring protocol below [#154].
5. Delivery. Usually no drugs are withdrawn around delivery except lithium [#154].

The longread closes with the channel's own addendum: decisions about pharmacotherapy should be discussed with one's own psychiatrist individually in each case [#154].

#### Lithium in pregnancy — monitoring protocol
When lithium is the only suitable drug, it is continued, with close observation of mother and child [#154]:
1. Blood lithium concentration monthly up to week 36.
2. Then weekly from week 36.
3. First-trimester foetal ultrasound to rule out cardiovascular malformations.
4. Stop 24–48 hours before delivery, then resume in the postpartum period.

#### Family psychoeducation and crisis plan for a pregnant woman with BD
Psychotherapy for BD often requires work with the family, so if a woman is in therapy during pregnancy, relationships can be included as a theme. With relatives the therapist [#154]:
1. Runs psychoeducation — explains the phases of bipolar disorder.
2. With the woman's consent, can formulate a crisis plan stating what relatives should do if her condition changes sharply.
3. Answers together the question of what exactly to do to help her cope.
4. Sometimes discusses which medications to give or which specialist to contact.
The goal, in the source's words: «Четкий пошаговый план действий» (A clear step-by-step action plan) [#154].

#### What emotion dysregulation is (the author's description)
Purpose: a working definition before comparing disorders [#100].
- The usual picture of dysregulation: strong intensity of emotions, reactivity and impulsivity, mood swings, slow fading of the emotional reaction, and poorly working strategies to normalize one's state — which together make it hard to function "в мире полном людей и забот" (in a world full of people and worries) [#100].
- Core mechanism: when it is hard to control one's experiences, emotions start to drive behaviour, and "поведение под влиянием эмоции" (behaviour under the influence of emotion) and "целенаправленное поведение" (goal-directed behaviour) are often very different things [#100].
- Metaphor: you are driving, and a friend in the passenger seat who cannot drive grabs the wheel and spins it, so instead of the bright future you planned you end up in a ditch — "Спасибо, друг." (Thanks, friend.) [#100].
- Examples: you want to discuss how your partner is wrong in "reading my mind", but within seconds you start yelling and no conversation happens; you need to call work and ask for a day off on a public holiday, but anxiety paralyses you and the call never happens. Summary line: "Ваши эмоции управляют вами, а не ваши цели." (Your emotions are steering you, not your goals.) [#100].
- Transdiagnostic list: disorders that have emotion regulation impairment as a symptom or significant criterion — depressive, anxiety, eating disorders, personality disorders, ADHD, complex PTSD, and bipolar affective disorder [#100].

#### Three reasons for impaired facial emotion recognition in BD (plus a fourth, neurobiology)
Per the research the author summarizes, BD differs from other disorders in that [#100]:
- Emotional intelligence (a component of social cognition) is often impaired, causing difficulty perceiving, using, understanding and managing emotions.
- Social emotional intelligence matters too: how you read and predict others' emotions plays a role in regulating your own — it complicates regulation, since without "references" to other people's faces a person with BD finds it hard to understand "как мне сейчас" (how I am right now).
- People with BD may find it harder to recognize emotions on others' faces — "эмоциональных детективов со слегка затуманенным увеличительным стеклом" (emotional detectives with a slightly fogged magnifying glass); emotions also hit with greater force, which further hampers an objective view.

Why the recognition and social-intelligence limits arise — the reasons listed [#100]:
1. Mood swings can disrupt consistent processing of emotions, affecting recognition more than in disorders with more stable affective states. "Если жизнь эмоциональные американский горки" (If life is an emotional roller coaster), the skill of understanding emotions is harder to develop.
2. Cycling. Mania or hypomania and the resulting hyperarousal distort emotional perception, reducing accuracy in recognizing and interpreting facial emotions. During depressive episodes there may be a tendency to read faces more negatively — "Мир в сером цвете" (the world in grey), and subtle facial expressions become indistinguishable.
3. Less social practice. Mood fluctuations and unpredictable behaviour make social interactions hard, so there may be less communication than one wants, and less practice leads to difficulty recognizing how the other person feels.
4. Neurobiological factors — see *Neural heterogeneity* below.

#### Emotional "emergency help" — adaptive vs maladaptive strategies
Anyone, not only people with dysregulation, can be overwhelmed in the moment, or long-term stress can make recovery impossible. Then a person, as if on autopilot, resorts to «неотложной помощи» (emergency help) — the ability to monitor, evaluate or correct one's emotional reactions [#100]. It includes:
- Adaptive strategies: cognitive reframing (rethinking the situation); accepting the situation as it is [#100].
- Maladaptive strategies: rumination; worry; focusing on the negative; risky behaviour; avoidance [#100].
Anyone can take either path, but at the level of the brain and its activity there is a predisposition toward which strategies a person will use [#100].

#### BD vs depression and BD vs BPD — comparison of regulation strategies
General finding: studies show people with BD more often use maladaptive coping strategies because of differences in activation of brain areas [#100].

BD vs depressive disorder [#100]:
1. In almost all comparisons there were no significant differences in emotion regulation/dysregulation, but BD showed altered connectivity in specific brain regions tied to the "emergency help" tasks — different parts of the brain perform the "how I regulate myself" function in different disorders. Example: during a depressive episode one person ruminates (replays the same negative thoughts), another avoids any thought content altogether — possible because of variable neural activation in BD: different areas = different strategies.
2. Risky behaviour as a way to regulate one's state was shown more often in BD than in depression. "Risky" means impulsive and without regard to negative consequences for life and health.
3. People with BD showed a significant difference in positive reflection compared with depressed patients, with higher expression of positive emotions — "Радоваться при БАР - получается, ура." (Feeling joy works in BD, hooray.)

BD vs borderline personality disorder (ПРЛ) [#100]:
1. People with BD scored lower on maladaptive negative-focused strategies — self-blame/self-flagellation and catastrophizing — than patients with BPD.
2. People with BD rely more on adaptive "emergency help" strategies than BPD patients.
Author's conclusion: in BD, biological and hereditary predisposition to regulation difficulties is much greater than in BPD, yet adaptive strategies are more likely — "биология вообще не приговор" (biology is not a sentence at all): one can learn to react adaptively by learning emotion regulation [#100].

### Detailed insights

#### DBT skills training for bipolar disorder
While preparing a new run of the DBT skills training group for people with BD, the author concluded that the DBT training for BD is the most different of all DBT skills-training modifications she has seen: part of its skills are the "gold standard" of DBT, and part are based on IPSRT. This is the context in which she explains IPSRT [#220].

#### The fear of coffee in BD
The author notes it is hard to find a person with BD who hasn't been scared off a cup of coffee or an energy drink because of caffeine. Caffeine has long been tied to a "myth" that it worsens the condition (and life) of people with mood disorders; she asks whether it is really a myth. Her answer: prejudices exist, but things are much more complicated — the review's findings are weighty for deciding on "one more cup of coffee", yet they are far from the only ones, and the many observations often contradict each other [#151; article #151].

#### When BD was untreated before pregnancy
- If a woman was not diagnosed or did not take pharmacotherapy for BD before pregnancy — and especially if her condition requires it — this can affect the child's condition [#154].
- There are studies showing an association between untreated maternal mental disorders and later development of ADHD and some other disorders in the child [#154].
- Ethical aspect. A woman may develop mania or a mixed state; then self-criticism (insight) drops and it becomes harder to make decisions for her own safety and the child's health. For example, she may refuse a blood transfusion, an emergency caesarean section or other auxiliary procedures that may be necessary and require a rational approach [#154].

#### Psychotherapy in BD during and before pregnancy
- In the pathogenesis of BD, not only biological factors (genetics) but also psychosocial ones (upbringing, patterns of interaction with close and significant people) play an important role. Several psychotherapy approaches take biological, social and psychological factors into account; among evidence-based ones for BD the commentary singles out IPSRT, CBT and DBT [#154].
- Psychotherapy in BD is not considered the method of choice (as it is, for example, for depression), because BD requires medication support first; still, the therapist's discussion and support play a truly significant role [#154].
- The commentary mentions a "good review on the topic", but the link target is not part of the saved text [#154].
- The post announcing the longread summarises its two parts: medication support (which mood stabilizers and antipsychotics are safe in pregnancy, what matters for young mothers on pharmacotherapy) and what to attend to in psychotherapy for a woman with BD before and during pregnancy. The topic came from readers' requests [#154].

#### Neural heterogeneity in BD
Emotion regulation requires interaction between cortical and subcortical brain areas; changes in these networks have been observed in BD, helping explain regulation difficulties. Neurobiological research points to heterogeneity in BD with different patterns of neural activation: some people show *increased* activation of the amygdala (an emotion-processing area), while others show *decreased* activation in both the amygdala and the prefrontal cortex during emotional experiences. These individual brain differences shape which regulation strategies a person prefers [#100].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The suicidality finding "up to 8 cups" refers to the general population, not specifically people with BD [#151].

## Antisocial personality disorder: signs, myths and therapy

### What this covers

The channel's post continues a series ("our journey through diagnoses connected with emotional dysregulation") with antisocial personality disorder (ASPD, Russian АРЛ), and links a full Telegraph longread by the author, Lena Foer, «Антисоциальное расстройство личности: что это на самом деле» ("Antisocial personality disorder: what it really is"). The longread covers how the diagnosis is made, how it looks in women, myths (serial killers, "people without emotions", "they don't suffer"), the difference between ASPD, psychopathy and sociopathy, and what therapy can do. The author frames the value of the topic as myth-busting: «Чем больше мифов вокруг диагноза - тем больше нового можно узнать, если на пару шагов отойти от поп-культуры и стереотипов» ("The more myths around a diagnosis, the more new things you can learn if you step a couple of steps away from pop culture and stereotypes") [#229, article #229].

### Core ideas

- ASPD is a stable behavioural pattern, like other personality disorders, diagnosed by persistent traits across several life areas plus conduct problems before age 15; full detail in *Diagnostic signs of ASPD* [#229].
- In women ASPD often looks different (self-harm instead of violence toward others, no childhood history, high comorbidity with BPD and anxiety), and because assessment tools were built on male samples, women more often go undiagnosed [#229].
- Most people with ASPD never commit serious crimes; they live ordinary if unstable lives, and their central problems are irresponsibility, impulsivity, conflict and pervasive emptiness and boredom — and they suffer too [#229].
- ASPD, psychopathy and sociopathy are related but different concepts [#229].
- ASPD is treatable, though hard to work with: DBT builds self-control, MBT aims at changing the inner state; the author's conclusion is that ASPD is «сложное расстройство, но не приговор» ("a difficult disorder, but not a life sentence") [#229].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Diagnostic signs of ASPD
Purpose: the key signs by which ASPD is diagnosed, according to the longread [#229].

Core signs (10):
1. Indifference to the rights of others and to the idea of right and wrong [#229].
2. Lying to gain advantage [#229].
3. Insensitivity to other people's feelings, or disrespect for them [#229].
4. Using charm or wit to manipulate others [#229].
5. A sense of one's own superiority [#229].
6. Criminal behaviour [#229].
7. Aggression and threats toward others [#229].
8. Absence of guilt and remorse for one's actions [#229].
9. High-risk behaviour [#229].
10. Irresponsibility and problems keeping commitments — at work and in general [#229].

Rules for applying the list: not all signs have to be present, but the traits must be stable over time and show up not in just one area of life but in several [#229].

Additional required criterion — behaviour problems before age 15 (4 kinds named):
1. Physical violence toward people or animals [#229].
2. Destruction of property / arson [#229].
3. Lying for gain [#229].
4. Theft [#229].

Context the author adds: like other personality disorders, ASPD is a stable behavioural pattern. People with it rarely seek help — which, she suggests with a hedge (perhaps), is why psychologists rarely write about it beyond articles of the type «как спасти себя от психопата» ("how to save yourself from a psychopath"). As with borderline and some other personality disorders, the severity of symptoms decreases over time [#229].

#### Specifics of ASPD in women (7 differences)
Purpose: as with many disorders, ASPD in women may look different from men, and the author lists how [#229].
1. No childhood history may be present. Women may lack the history of typical before-15 conduct problems while having all the other symptoms. The author notes these studies were done on prison samples [#229].
2. Self-harm instead of violence. Impulsivity and aggression in women are often expressed as self-harm rather than physical violence against others [#229].
3. Different item profile on psychopathy questionnaires. On psychopathy questionnaires (psychopathy is not the same as ASPD but close to it), women scored higher on items about financial irresponsibility, impulsivity, lack of remorse and promiscuity — the author adds an ironic aside «(что бы это ни значило)» ("whatever that means") — while men scored higher on irritability, aggression and high-risk behaviour [#229].
4. Less variety in criminal activity among women [#229].
5. Look more adaptive and are harder to recognise. Women with ASPD appear more adaptive and capable of strategy, and are harder to spot because they seem to learn better from consequences — even though everyone with ASPD has some problems ("есть проблемки") with learning from consequences [#229].
6. Comorbid BPD. Again in the prison study, a third of women with ASPD also had borderline personality disorder [#229].
7. Anxiety disorders. One of the most common complaints and problems of women with ASPD is anxiety disorders. Against the popular image of the person with ASPD as «как Ганнибал Лектор, человек без эмоций» ("like Hannibal Lecter, a person without emotions"), anxiety disorders are not only common — they were practically the only thing shared in the adolescent experience of most adult women with this disorder [#229].

Consequence: standard assessment methods were developed on male samples, so women more often remain undiagnosed [#229].

#### ASPD vs psychopathy vs sociopathy
Purpose: answers whether "sociopath, psychopath, antisocial" are the same thing. The author's answer: no, they are different things, though adjacent [#229].
1. Antisocial personality disorder — an official medical diagnosis in the DSM-5 and ICD-11 classifications [#229].
2. Psychopathy — not a diagnosis but a research construct, assessed with a special scale (PCL-R). It includes emotional coldness, lack of empathy and manipulativeness. Not everyone with ASPD is a psychopath. There is also a position according to which "psychopathy" names the most severe segment of the spectrum of antisocial traits [#229].
3. Sociopathy — an outdated term, sometimes used informally; practically not used in scientific literature [#229].

#### What works in therapy for ASPD
Starting point: it used to be believed that people with ASPD cannot be helped; the author states "now we know this is not so" — the disorder responds to therapy, although working with it really is difficult [#229].
1. Dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT, ДБТ) — helps develop skills of emotion regulation and impulse control. Limitation: inner problems such as emptiness and the absence of an inner reward "for the good" («отсутствие внутренней награды “за хорошее”») remain; the gain is that self-control helps the person end up in prison less often [#229].
2. Mentalization-based therapy (MBT, МВТ) — teaches understanding of one's own mental states and those of other people; "in theory" helps develop empathy and improve social functioning. Unlike DBT, it helps change precisely the inner state — but it is more suited to "high-functioning" clients [#229].

Practical barriers: therapy requires time, money and motivation, and with ASPD all of these can be difficult. But those who do reach specialists can significantly improve the quality of their lives and relationships [#229].

### Detailed insights

#### Myth: people with ASPD are serial killers
- Most people with ASPD never commit serious crimes. Fights, drugs, theft and other "everyday" crimes are more frequent among them simply because such crimes are more frequent in general [#229].
- Many people with ASPD live ordinary lives, work (though they may change jobs often) and build relationships (even if unstable) [#229].
- Their problems are most often related to irresponsibility, impulsivity, conflicts and a pervasive feeling of emptiness and boredom [#229].

#### Boredom and the high arousal threshold
Boredom is, per the author, one of the defining features of the disorder, because the arousal threshold in people with ASPD is very high. What pleases and amuses the average person makes no impression on them; to feel something they have to do something very risky, intense or beyond the norm. This is the mechanism the author offers for the risk-seeking behaviour [#229].

#### Myth: only others suffer
It is commonly believed that people with ASPD do not suffer from it — only others do. The author disagrees: everyone suffers, since living with a constant sense of emptiness and breakdowns of control is hard («жить с постоянным чувством пустоты и срывами контроля - ну такое» — "living with a constant feeling of emptiness and lapses of control — not great") [#229].

#### Conclusion of the longread
People with this diagnosis differ; symptoms and their severity can differ "ОЧЕНЬ" — greatly; problems show up in different ways; and they can be helped. Stigma and pop-culture myths only hinder those who really need support and are ready to work on themselves [#229].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The explanation for why psychologists rarely write about ASPD is the author's guess («возможно» — "perhaps") [#229].
