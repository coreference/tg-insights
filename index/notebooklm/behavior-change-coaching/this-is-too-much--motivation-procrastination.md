## Procrastination, motivation and the vacation brain

### What this covers

Three linked questions about why we do or don't do things. First, a practical exercise (by Lena Foer) for "taming" procrastination by finding the function it serves in your life and building a replacement behavior that serves the same function better. Second, Katya Klimochkina's post and linked longread on self-determination theory — intrinsic, identified and introjected motivation, amotivation, the three basic needs, and why an intention can stall for reasons other than "not caring enough" — with self-diagnostic questions. Third, a two-part essay (Klimochkina) on what happens in the brain when habitual work temporarily disappears: stress systems ease off, directed attention gets a rest, novelty engages motivation-related systems, and suddenly you notice you want things. The source treats this as important because procrastination and loss of interest are usually read as laziness, weak will or lack of motivation, whereas the authors argue they are often about function, the kind of motivation, organization of action, or mental space taken up by demands [#44, #247, #248, #266].

### Core ideas

- Procrastination always has a function. Everything we do serves some role; procrastination solves some problem for you, and the job is to discover which one before trying to change it [#44].
- The same behavior can have different causes. Five people watching a series instead of sleeping may do it for five different reasons; equally, SDT says the same outward action can be driven by enjoyment, by valuing the result, or by being unable to allow oneself not to do it [#44, #266].
- Why you act matters, not just how much you want to. SDT replaces the willpower/discipline/laziness explanation with distinct types of motivation that feel and cost different things [#266].
- Guilt-driven productivity looks like motivation but has a cost. Introjected motivation can keep you productive while making rest feel like something to be earned and success feel like mere relief [#266].
- Stalled intention ≠ insufficient motivation. If you know why something matters but can't start, the issue may be task size, avoidance of unpleasant feelings, executive-function difficulties or missing resources — each needing different work [#266, article #266].
- Work may not kill interest in life — it may just fill the "RAM". When the constant system of demands temporarily goes away, other wants become visible again [#248].
- Vacation brain = less stress load + less directed attention + more novelty. Together these make many things around you worth attending to again [#248].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Procrastination function analysis ("Как приручить прокрастинацию" — How to tame procrastination)
Purpose: to find out what concrete problem your procrastination is solving for you, and to replace it with a behavior that serves the same function, ideally better. Premise: "У всего, что мы делаем, есть функция" (Everything we do has a function). The author illustrates with five people who all watch a series instead of going to bed for different reasons: one didn't get enough "time for the soul" after work and wants to make up for it; another feels anxious being alone in the dark with insomnia; a third wants to delay the arrival of tomorrow because of a dentist appointment. So the remedy can't be generic — it depends on the function [#44].

1. Pick one concrete episode. Recall a single specific episode of procrastination and open a text editor. It matters that it is a specific day and a specific occasion, not procrastination "in general" [#44].
2. Write out before–during–after in detail. List what happened before, during and after the procrastination, as finely as you can. Author's example: came home from work → got undressed → made dinner → sat down to eat it at the laptop, continuing the chain as far as it goes [#44].
3. Next to each action, write what you felt and thought at that moment. Author's example:
   - came home from work — strong fatigue, anger, "the sooner I can forget myself and turn on a game, the better";
   - got undressed — even more anger: the apartment is dirty, everything is all over the place;
   - made dinner — even more anger, didn't want to cook but had to;
   - sat down to eat at the laptop — finally could switch to something pleasant, opened the game, felt good [#44].
4. Play detective: find the strongest emotion and the main benefit. Look carefully at the list. Where is the strongest emotion connected with what's happening? What problems does procrastination solve for you? Try to find the main benefit the "тупка" (stall) gives you. In the example, procrastination is a way of distracting oneself from strong fatigue and the irritation that comes on top of it [#44].
5. Evaluate whether this solution is actually good. Ask: is this way of solving the problem effective in the long run, bringing real benefit — or does it only look that way in the moment? If the latter, what would be more effective? What behavior could replace the old one so that it performs the same function as procrastination, maybe even better? [#44]
6. Generate many alternatives. One answer is fine, but don't stop until you have at least 2–3, preferably 5 or more. Then look at them and decide which seems the most right, adequate and effective in the long term for you [#44].
7. Rehearse in imagination. Close your eyes and imagine the same episode you worked with, but this time it ends not with procrastination but with the new behavior. How realistic is this scenario? Does it need fine-tuning? Does something need to be added to make it easy for you to take the new path? [#44]
8. Test in the real world and treat friction as data. Then try to use this imagined model in real life. Things rarely work well on the first attempt, so let the rough spots and difficulties be new inputs for understanding better where to move next [#44].

#### Self-Determination Theory — four motivational positions
In the post, Klimochkina asks why we do some things with interest, a second group because we consider them important, a third out of a sense of duty, and a fourth group we can't start at all although we understand perfectly why they are needed. Self-Determination Theory (SDT) distinguishes several bases of motivation: you can do something because the process itself is enjoyable, because you consider the result important, or "потому что не можешь спокойно позволить себе этого не делать" (because you can't calmly allow yourself not to do it). Outwardly the behavior can look identical — a person completes a task, goes to training, works on a project — but the reasons sustaining these actions can differ [#266].

The linked longread («Почему одни люди берут и делают, а другие не могут начать, даже если понимают, зачем это нужно?» — *Why do some people just go and do it, while others can't start even though they understand why it's needed?*, by Katya Klimochkina and co-author bpdflowers) develops this with a running gym example. One person decided to "взяться за себя" (take themselves in hand) and went to the gym. A second doesn't particularly like sport but trains because they want to be "healthy". A third trains because otherwise they would consider themselves a lazy seal and/or someone who has let themselves go (slang "соскуфишимся"). A fourth understands perfectly what to do, can explain why, and could even draw up a plan for himself, the first, the second and "тому парню" (that guy too) — but somehow never made it to the gym. Everyday explanations of such differences appeal to willpower, discipline or laziness; SDT offers a somewhat different view: what matters is not only how strongly a person wants to do something, but why they do it — and also what happens between "это мне надо, это мне важно" (I need this, this matters to me) and the action, because, as it turns out, these are not the same thing [#266].

The longread walks through four positions, each with its SDT label:

1. "Мне интересно, поэтому я этим занимаюсь" (I'm interested, so I do it) — intrinsic motivation (внутренняя мотивация). The activity is interesting in itself, brings pleasure or satisfies curiosity. Author's examples: she herself went to the climbing gym for years simply because she loves climbing — without particular skill, progress or reasons, just because it's fun; reading about complex PTSD, how the brain works, every kind of terrier (or "подставьте свое" — *insert your own*) because one is genuinely curious how it all works; learning guitar because one likes figuring out music. In such cases you don't have to explain to yourself each time why you do it — the process itself already gives something: "Беру и делаю!" (I just go and do it!). Downside: interest does not cancel fatigue, lack of time and other circumstances; you can adore climbing and still skip sessions because of a billion work tasks or because you slept badly and feel like a vegetable. Intrinsic motivation does not mean a person automatically acts regularly, easily and effortlessly; it means there is something in the activity itself that makes you want to return to it [#266].

2. "Мне не нравится, но я понимаю, зачем мне это" (I don't like it, but I understand why I need it) — autonomous motivation, specifically identified regulation (идентифицированная регуляция). In SDT terminology this is one of the kinds of *extrinsic* ("внешняя") motivation. The word "extrinsic" can be confusing: it does not mean someone is standing over you forcing you (though that happens too) — rather, the activity is needed for the sake of the result, not because the process is enjoyable. Examples: "I don't like training, but I want to be physically stronger"; "I don't like keeping a budget, but I want to understand where the money goes"; "I don't want to fill in session protocols / write reports / force pills into the dogs' mouths, but I consider the task/work important and am ready to do it". You can dislike the action itself yet accept its meaning and understand what you're willing to spend time and effort for. Also, not wanting to do something *right now* is not the same as not wanting to do it at all: you can feel zero enthusiasm about a workout and still go because physical activity matters to you. You don't have to catch fire with the idea every time — sometimes it's enough to consider it worth the effort. The author tags this as behavioral activation ("иф ю ноу - ю ноу" — *if you know, you know*). In SDT this process is linked to internalization (интернализация): gradually a person can absorb external demands and values, recognize their significance and include them in their own system of reference points. The degree of acceptance varies: "I do it because I consider it important" and "I do it because otherwise I'll be scolded" are not the same thing [#266].

3. "Я должна это делать. Иначе буду недовольна собой" (I must do this. Otherwise I'll be displeased with myself) — controlled motivation, specifically introjected regulation (интроецированная регуляция). This is also motivation, and sometimes quite effective — at least if you look only at whether the task got done. Examples: a person overworks because otherwise they'd consider themselves a bad colleague or specialist; never skips a workout because after skipping they feel weak-willed and "not good"; doesn't allow themselves to rest until all tasks are done. External control may no longer exist — nobody demands, checks or punishes — but the demands seem to have moved inside. Now the person monitors their own compliance using guilt, shame, anxiety or the need to prove their own adequacy to themselves — "эти вот критикующие ребята внутри" (those critical guys inside). Task done — you can briefly feel normal; not done — you have to deal with an unpleasant attitude toward yourself. Net result: a person can be very productive not because they like what they do, nor even because they consider it especially important, but because they cannot calmly allow themselves not to do it. Rest starts to feel like something to be earned; refusing to overwork triggers guilt; a completed task brings not so much satisfaction as relief — finally you can stop scolding yourself. "А потом появляется следующая задача" (And then the next task appears). On burnout risk the author says "judge for yourself": introjected motivation really can sustain persistence and achievement striving, but it can also co-occur with psychological ill-being (the 2021 meta-analysis is in *Evidence and numbers*). Takeaway: if a person does a lot, that says nothing yet about what this productivity costs them [#266].

4. "Я всё понимаю, но не делаю" (I understand everything, but I don't do it) — not automatically amotivation. The author singles this out because the explanation "значит, недостаточно хочешь" (so you don't want it enough) — in the style of "some people from the past" — usually clarifies little. A person wants to change jobs, move, start writing a book, keep a blog, take up their health or sort out finances; understands why; can explain in detail why it matters — yet a week, a month, half a year pass and nothing changes. SDT has the concept of amotivation (амотивация): a situation in which the person lacks sufficient intention to act — e.g. the result seems unimportant, the person doesn't believe they can cope, or doesn't see a link between their own effort and what will come out of it. But amotivation is not a universal label for every case of not acting. If I want the result, consider it important and believe I can achieve it, yet still don't start, other mechanisms are worth examining (see the next framework) [#266].

#### Reasons an intention stalls without lack of motivation
The post states that when the intention exists but the action doesn't, this does not necessarily mean the thing isn't important enough to me or that I'm not trying hard enough. The possible problems it lists: (1) how the action is organized; (2) how clear the first step is; (3) what feelings/experiences arise along the way; (4) what resources I am lacking [#266].

The longread spells out the alternative mechanisms for the case "I want it, value it, believe I can — and still don't start" [#266]:
1. The task seems huge and it's unclear what to grab first.
2. Starting is tied to unpleasant experience — boredom, anxiety or the risk of facing failure — so I postpone that moment; the author calls this "база и основа прокрастинации" (the base and foundation of procrastination).
3. Difficulties with planning, switching attention or organization.
4. Simply no time, energy, resources, "событульников" (buddies to do it with) or support needed for the task.

These are different situations, and explaining them all with one word, "motivation", is not a workable scheme. The longread states that procrastination, executive functions, avoidance of unpleasant experiences and self-regulation difficulties are not synonyms for amotivation — they may overlap but describe different things. Therefore the phrases "я не хочу" (I don't want to), "эт не важно" (it's not important), "я боюсь, что не справлюсь" (I'm afraid I won't cope), "я не понимаю, с чего начать" (I don't understand where to start) and "я хочу, но не могу" (I want to but can't) are not interchangeable: different causes may stand behind them, and so they need to be worked with differently [#266].

#### SDT three basic psychological needs
The longread names another important SDT element — three basic psychological needs [#266]:
1. Autonomy (автономия) — the feeling that I act by my own choice, not only because I'm forced or am forcing myself.
2. Competence (компетентность) — the feeling that I can cope with the task and that my efforts can lead to a result.
3. Relatedness (связанность с другими) — the feeling of closeness, support and belonging.

In SDT, satisfying these needs is associated with more autonomous motivation and psychological well-being. The author's caveat: they are not "три волшебные кнопки" (three magic buttons) that, once pressed, make a person suddenly do everything planned, and not an exhaustive explanation of why one person succeeded and another didn't [#266].

#### Four self-diagnostic motivation questions for a stalled task
Dividing people into disciplined and lazy makes no sense, but you can ask yourself a few questions to sort out your motivation around a concrete task [#266]:
1. Do I really want this result? Or do I think I *should* want it?
2. Do I need it myself, or am I trying to avoid guilt, shame and other people's disapproval?
3. Do I believe I can cope? Do I understand what exactly needs to be done?
4. What happens between intention and action? At what moment do I stop — and what gets in the way?

Depending on the answers, completely different things may be needed: understanding one's own goals better, revising one's demands on oneself, breaking the task into doable steps, dealing with avoidance, or organizing external support [#266].

Closing thesis: readiness to act does not necessarily look like strong desire. You can do something because it's interesting; do what you dislike because you understand its point; or fulfil demands because you can't allow yourself to refuse. And you can genuinely want something and still struggle to start. So the question is not only how strongly a person wants to do something, but why they intend to do it, what sustains the intention, and what happens at the moment when it's time to move to action [#266].

#### Attention Restoration Theory (ART)
1. Directed attention is the attention you have to actively manage: focusing and finishing a text, not getting lost among a thousand open tabs, holding an instruction in mind, not going to watch reels too early, waiting while a colleague is "typing". This is fairly energy-consuming work (the author jokes that mothers and grandmothers wouldn't agree) [#247].
2. Not every environment demands it. Some environments let attention work more "softly": staring at water, trees, the street, clouds, walking around an unfamiliar place. These have many stimuli that lightly catch attention on their own without constantly requiring the brain to "СОСРЕДОТОЧЬСЯ" (CONCENTRATE) [#247].
3. Soft fascination. ART calls this *soft fascination* (мягкое привлечение внимания). The idea is that while you're zoning out looking at the sea, part of the directed-attention systems get a break [#247].
4. Evidence, with hedge. A review the author cites found some positive effects of contact with nature on attention measures, particularly working memory and some executive-function tasks. However, the data are heterogeneous, so claiming that "20 minutes in the forest reboots the prefrontal cortex" would be too good to be true [#247].

#### The vacation "wants" formula
Klimochkina's summary of the mechanism: "меньше постоянной стрессовой и привычной нагрузки + меньше необходимости всё время держать направленное внимание + больше новизны = вокруг снова появляется куча вещей, на которые мозгу хочется обратить внимание" (less constant stress and habitual load + less need to hold directed attention all the time + more novelty = a lot of things appear around you again that the brain wants to pay attention to). Each term is explained in *Detailed insights* (stress systems, novelty) and in *Attention Restoration Theory* above [#248].

### Detailed insights

#### The observation that started it: new wants on a "vacation" that isn't restful
The author had been "on vacation" for two weeks, and it hardly looked like one: she was doing a million chores there's no time for on workdays. Still, after two weeks she felt different, and she noticed it by her wants: she wants to do beadwork ("а это… ну не мой обычный вторник" — *and that's… well, not my usual Tuesday*), go kayaking (more predictable), read the dragon erotica flooding reels, finally finish watching *Supernatural*, rock-climb more often, play ping-pong — things that in ordinary life seem completely alien. She knew the vacation research from years ago but decided to check whether things are less obvious, asking what happens to the brain when habitual work drops away for a while [#247]. The lesson she draws: the change shows up not as "finally doing nothing" but as discovering you still want things — even with a non-restful break, and even with just a change of activity ("когда вместо работы копаешь картофель" — *when instead of work you dig potatoes*) [#247, #248].

#### Why workdays crowd out interest
On workdays there may be a lack of interest in life — or simply all the intellectual space is already occupied: enter tasks in the to-do list, answer messages, remember things, switch between Zoom calls, read up on the last client meeting. Constant task-switching, again and again. Squeezed between "need to answer messages" and "need to charge the laptop before leaving", the wish to do beadwork looks like completely pointless nonsense. Then work temporarily disappears, and beads suddenly seem quite cool [#247].

#### Stress systems under chronic load and their easing
Chronic load has a quite bodily side. When we constantly face demands and live in bustle and stress, the "stress systems" are activated — in particular the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, the sympathetic nervous system and the related mechanisms regulating cortisol and noradrenaline. Cortisol and noradrenaline aren't bad in themselves (we really need both), but being constantly in a state of heightened readiness is not great. When at least part of the demands temporarily disappears, the stress systems can slow down somewhat. The author hedges: not in the sense of "cortisol left the brain and went on vacation", but in that "у организма становится меньше причин всё время поддерживать режим повышенной готовности" (the body has fewer reasons to maintain a heightened-readiness mode all the time) [#247].

#### Novelty and motivation-related systems
At home the environment repeats: the same workplace every day, the same route for coffee, the same open Telegram with lots of unread messages, the same to-do list. Nothing wrong with that — predictability actually saves resources. But a vacation brings a sudden amount of new information: a different city, different streets, new food, a kayak, a strange door, beads. Then systems associated with processing novelty, motivation and learning work more actively; in particular, dopaminergic and noradrenergic systems help make new and potentially significant stimuli "noticeable" at all. Suddenly the environment again supplies lots of information you want to look at [#248].

The author explicitly rejects the oversimplification: "Не «дофамин отвечает за интерес» - мозг, к счастью, не настолько примитивен" (It's not "dopamine is responsible for interest" — the brain, fortunately, isn't that primitive). Rather, it is the combination in the vacation formula that produces the effect [#248].

#### What the new wants are — and aren't
The wants that appear are often completely unaccountable — not necessarily "finally realizing your potential". Sometimes you want to do beadwork, or watch a series you haven't watched in 10 years, or write a post in a channel where you haven't written for 10 years, or read dragon porn "because why not". In the author's understanding this isn't about "happiness" or "rest" at all, but about the fact that when the brain doesn't have to serve the same system of demands all the time for a while, other things come into view again — the very ones that were too pointless, optional and silly to break through the queue of "musts" (очередь из «надо») [#248].

Conclusion offered (hedged with "возможно" — *perhaps*): work doesn't always take away our interest in life; sometimes it just occupies so much space in the head that very little RAM (оперативной памяти, *operational memory*) remains for other interest. We're simply too busy to notice what else we want; when habitual tasks disappear even briefly, the brain starts noticing other things [#248].

### Evidence and numbers

- There are data linking the quality of recovery after work with features of the cortisol profile (study not named) [#247].
- A meta-analysis the author cites (PubMed 39836131) shows that vacation is associated with a noticeable improvement in wellbeing; psychological detachment from work and physical activity looked especially beneficial during vacation [#247].
- A review (PubMed 27668460) found some positive effects of nature contact on attention measures, specifically working memory and some executive-function tasks; data are heterogeneous [#247].
- A study linked on the phrase "20 minutes in the forest" (PubMed 41547470) is cited ironically as an example of an overclaim the data don't support [#247].
- The author attaches novelty research (PubMed 26601905) to the claim that novelty engages dopaminergic and noradrenergic systems, and a further paper (PubMed 41412371) to the combined formula; (!) INCOMPLETE: the source does not describe what these studies measured or found [#248].
- A 2021 meta-analysis cited in the longread, pooling 344 samples and 223,209 students, found introjected motivation associated with persistence, with achievement orientation, and with indicators of ill-being. The author stresses it concerned students and statistical associations — not proof that guilt inevitably leads to problems [#266].
- Procrastination research cited in the longread found autonomous motivation associated with less procrastination, and controlled motivation and amotivation with more; results differed depending on which form of delay was studied [#266].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The author repeatedly hedges the neuroscience: stress systems "may" slow down, not that cortisol disappears; nature-attention data are heterogeneous; dopamine is not "the interest chemical" [#247, #248].
- The concluding claim that work occupies mental "RAM" rather than destroying interest is offered with "возможно" (perhaps) and "в моем понимании" (in my understanding) — an opinion, not an established finding [#248].
- The longread hedges SDT itself: the three needs are not magic buttons nor an exhaustive explanation of success vs failure; the 2021 meta-analysis shows associations in students, not causation; procrastination findings varied by form of delay [#266].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the longread does not name the 2021 meta-analysis or the procrastination studies it cites [#266].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the procrastination exercise gives no example of what the replacement behaviors for the fatigue/irritation case could be [#44].
