## Women's health: cycle, pregnancy, check-ups

### What this covers

### Core ideas

- Energy is cyclical, and that is normal. Energy, concentration and productivity are lowest during menstruation, rise in the follicular phase, peak at ovulation and gradually fall in the luteal phase; working with this rhythm rather than against it reduces self-blame (see *Four phases of the menstrual cycle and energy*). [#37]
- The cycle can be "biohacked" by observation, not force. Track the day, notice your own monthly patterns, and shift training intensity, food, rest and demanding tasks to the phase that suits them (see *Six steps to biohack the menstrual cycle*). [#80]
- Check-ups are for prevention, not obsession. The authors say they are against excessive fixation on lab tests and prescribe them to nobody, yet offer a frequency-based checklist from NIH, Mayo Clinic and UCLA Health; Katya adds that tests should be ordered by doctors and symptoms require a specialist. [#122, #346]
- Annual tests answer "am I doing enough?" For Katya, tracking the dynamics of her results silences the inner perfectionist; after years of focusing on lifestyle, "everything is fine" became her new normal, while in 2018–19 she spent a year and a half and lots of money finding out that the problem was lifestyle. [#346]
- Pregnancy well-being is first of all about mentality. Discomfort and mood swings are not only uncontrollable hormones but signs of depleted resources; accepting, letting go, not demanding of oneself and listening to the body are the core skills. [#162]
- Plan the postpartum period in advance. The "4th trimester" lasts up to eight weeks and can be harder than birth itself; a ten-point plan prepares food, help, contacts and expectations (see *Ten-point postpartum plan*). [#180]
- One test is not a verdict. Katya's raised cholesterol turned out to be caused by fatty lamb eaten the day before the test; look at the dynamics, talk to a doctor and prepare for tests properly. [#618]
- AI self-diagnosis is premature and "why" comes before "how". A cited Nature Medicine study shows that people using AI models get correct diagnoses only 34% of the time; and Katya reads Nike's "Why Do It?" as confirmation that motivation must be clarified before action. [#578]

- Everyone adult in Russia can get checked under OMS; only the scope differs. Full диспансеризация is every 3 years before 40 and yearly from 40, and the reduced профилактический осмотр is available every year at any age, so "not 40 yet" or "not my year" is no excuse (see *OMS check-up entitlement by age*). [#711]
- The free check-up is the foundation; fashionable panels are not required. Katya says regularly checking the basics matters more than a one-off spend on a trendy 100-marker panel. [#711]
- The process is easy and app-driven. A Moscow OMS policy, the EMIAS.INFO app, a questionnaire that issues referrals automatically, flexible booking, preparation instructions and in-app queue tickets (see *Six steps to a free check-up via EMIAS.INFO*). [#711]
- Preparation for tests is not optional. Unprepared tests give unpleasant results that then have to be rechecked. [#711]
- Exercise reduces period pain; rest-in-bed is a myth. Regular movement, sleep and balanced food changed Katya's first period day from "survival" to a 13-km hike, and she cites a Cochrane review and US professional guidance in support (see *Two changes that ended Katya's disabling period pain*). [#722]
- Pain to the point of fainting is a medical signal, not a training problem. See a gynecologist first; it can indicate endometriosis or other conditions to rule out. [#722]

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Four phases of the menstrual cycle and energy
Purpose: to understand why energy, productivity and emotional state change during the month. Marina frames it as what "эндокринология" (endocrinology) says: on average every 28 days the body goes through the same hormonal changes. Most women know the PMS symptoms of the last week, but the rest of the month affects them just as much. [#37]

1. Menstrual phase (1st phase). Counted as the start of the cycle; lasts 3 to 7 days. What happens: the endometrium — the mucous lining that would have supported a pregnancy — is shed; levels of estrogen and progesterone fall. Effect (one ⚡ in the post): energy, concentration and productivity are at their lowest in this phase. [#37]
2. Follicular phase (2nd phase). Starts after the bleeding ends and can last about 16 days, "но все очень индивидуально" (but everything is very individual). What happens: the brain signals the release of FSH, which stimulates the ovaries to produce small sacs, follicles, containing immature eggs; hormone levels rise. Effect (two ⚡): energy rises rapidly together with the hormones — creative projects, complex tasks and physical activity all come easier. [#37]
3. Ovulation (3rd phase). Around day 14 of the cycle (±); it usually lasts 24 hours, but because of the peak in hormones it can be felt for 3–4 days. What happens: the ovary releases a mature egg for fertilisation; estrogen and testosterone are at their peak. Effect (three ⚡): "Это самая мощная и активная фаза" (this is the most powerful and active phase) — endurance and stress resilience rise. [#37]
4. Luteal phase (4th phase). Lasts about 10–14 days after ovulation. What happens: the egg is not fertilised and the uterus prepares to lose its lining, the endometrium; progesterone reaches its peak, which has a calming effect on the body and relaxes smooth muscle. Effect (two ⚡): energy and performance gradually fall and endurance declines; it is the ideal time for reflection, routine tasks and self-care. [#37]

Marina's personal application: she has long observed how her body responds in each phase. Her need for food changes with her energy, "от легких салатов до горячих гуляшей" (from light salads to hot goulashes). She takes the maximum from her creative capacities in the early follicular phase without postponing, and does not blame herself when motivation drops at the end of the cycle — she treats herself gently ("отношусь к себе бережно"). [#37]

#### Six steps to biohack the menstrual cycle
Purpose: "Чтобы хорошо себя чувствовать и быть довольной собой в любой день месяца" (to feel good and be satisfied with yourself on any day of the month). Marina builds on the four-phase post and on women (she refers to a Wall Street Journal piece) who have tuned their rituals and schedules to hormonal variability. This helps to understand oneself better, manage emotions and take care of oneself when one is at zero ("когда ноль на балансе"). [#80]

1. Start a cycle tracker. Know your cycle day so you can map it to one of the four phases. Marina uses Fitbit; she says Apple and Oura are also excellent; the best-known apps are Clue and Flo. [#80]
2. Observe how your state changes. Everyone is different, but each woman has her own patterns that repeat month to month. Besides physical changes, Marina tracks how her thinking shifts from "я все могу" (I can do anything) to "я никчемная и не достойная" (I'm worthless and unworthy); now she simply watches the latter thoughts with a smile, because she knows they are a phase. [#80]
3. Distribute strength training according to biorhythms. In the middle of the cycle you can allow yourself to really go hard ("хорошо «угореть»"), and switch to a comfortable mode towards the end of the cycle and in its first days, when energy is lowest. Walking and gentle Pilates are recommended companions for that time. [#80]
4. Choose nourishing food during PMS and the first days of the cycle. More filling dishes, stewed (poached) vegetables, ragouts, natural sugars in the form of sweet potato and pumpkin, warming spices, with particular attention to enough protein. "Чем больше тепла и заботы в тарелке, тем приятнее телу" (the more warmth and care on the plate, the more pleasant for the body). Diagnostic rule: if you strongly crave sweets, ask yourself whether you are getting enough valuable food right now. [#80]
5. Learn to rest. Lack of strength at the end of the cycle is the norm, and coffee will not help. The hardest part is not resting itself but learning to allow yourself to rest. [#80]
6. Revise your schedule according to the cycle. Labelled "Задание для продвинутых юзеров" (a task for advanced users). Difficult meetings, creative projects and new tasks may come much easier in the second and third weeks of the cycle. It is optional, but worth trying to find out whether it holds for you. Marina's own finding so far: during PMS everything is definitely harder, and since that is how it should be, she just needs to leave herself alone and go for a walk. [#80]

Marina's backstory (the lesson behind the method): until 30 she paid no attention to herself or her cycle, suffered terrible pain every month and "забрасывалась таблетками" (dosed herself with pills). Doctors said "родишь — пройдет" (give birth and it will pass); it did not pass. With attentive self-care it became much better. [#80]

#### Women's check-up checklist by frequency (NIH, Mayo Clinic, UCLA Health)
Purpose: a list of tests compiled by the channel's authors from NIH, Mayo Clinic and UCLA Health recommendations that women should take "чтобы предотвратить риски хронических заболеваний и продлить здоровую жизнь" (to prevent the risks of chronic diseases and prolong healthy life). Framing and disclaimers: the authors are against excessive obsession with lab tests and do not prescribe them to anyone; the checklist is for information and is not medical advice; the reason to track indicators is to take measures in time if the goal is to live longer and be more active. The full checklist from the card [#122]:

Twice a year
- Dentist. [#122]

Once a year
- Gynecologist. [#122]
- Complete blood count with ESR (клинический анализ крови с СОЭ). [#122]
- Lipid profile: total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, triglycerides. [#122]
- Biochemical blood test: glucose, ALT, AST, bilirubin, protein, albumin, creatinine, sodium, potassium. [#122]
- C-reactive protein. [#122]
- Ferritin. [#122]
- TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) — present on the card image, dropped by the OCR transcription. [#122]
- Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c). [#122]
- Vitamin D. [#122]
- General urine test. [#122]
- (after 45) Fecal occult blood test. [#122]
- (if you smoke) Chest CT / X-ray. [#122]

Once every 2 years
- Ophthalmologist. [#122]
- (after 40) Mammography. [#122]

Less often
- (once every 3–5 years) Pap test for cervical cancer. [#122]
- (once every 3–5 years) HPV test. [#122]
- (from 45, once every 10 years) Colonoscopy. [#122]

Once in a lifetime
- (after 18) Hepatitis test. [#122]

#### Katya's annual check-up routine
Katya Strelnikova does this set every year (she notes the channel's women's checklist above as the broader reference). Why: "Мне интересно и важно отслеживать динамику, держать руку на пульсе" (it is interesting and important for me to track the dynamics, keep my finger on the pulse), and it answers the question "делаю ли я достаточно, чтобы быть здоровой?" (am I doing enough to be healthy?) — her answer is yes, which counters the inner perfectionist that is always waiting to say where she urgently needs to improve. [#346]

1. Dentist — cleaning plus treatment if needed (no treatment was needed this year). [#346]
2. Gynecologist — examination plus routine tests; this year a Pap test and ferritin. [#346]
3. Blood test panel — complete blood count, cholesterol, glucose, TSH, calcium and similar; this year she also added vitamin D to make sure it was normal — it was, and so was everything else. [#346]
4. New this year: the state preventive examination (диспансеризация) — she finally registered at her local polyclinic, which she had not got round to since 2018, and went through it; the doctor joked that she should be sent to space and would surely live to 100. [#346]

Lesson from her history: in 2018–19 she spent about a year and a half and "тонны денег" (tons of money) trying to find out "что со мной не так" (what is wrong with me); "Спойлер: образ жизни" (spoiler: lifestyle). After concentrating on her well-being a few years ago, hearing "у тебя все хорошо" (everything is fine with you) at the doctor's became the new normal, which she calls an incredible feeling. Her stance: tests should be ordered by doctors, and with symptoms one should see a specialist; doctor-assembled test bundles by symptom or body system are only for general interest. [#346]

#### Ten-point postpartum (4th trimester) plan
Context: at week 35 of her second pregnancy Marina wrote that after her first birth she expected all her body's adventures to end on discharge from the maternity hospital on day 3, followed by a wonderful romantic life with the baby. Instead the postpartum period turned out much harder for her than the birth itself, and nobody had really talked about it. The second time she wants to have a plan in advance — everything can still go out of control at any moment, but holding on to these points helps her take better care of herself. [#180]

1. Acknowledge that the postpartum period exists. Pregnancy and birth do not end with discharge on day 3. It takes up to eight weeks to truly return to the world, and that is normal. Action: talk about it with the husband and the parents who will be around so everyone understands she needs time. [#180]
2. Don't forget quality, valuable food. Broths, ragouts, porridges — more warm and nourishing food. Buy a multicooker. Prepare a plan and recipes: ones she can cook herself and ones her mother will cook when she comes to help. [#180]
3. Accept mess as part of well-being. Marina calls herself an absolute "neat freak" but will set her cleaning mania aside for a while: "Чистота не сделает меня счастливой, а вот внутренний покой — да" (cleanliness won't make me happy, but inner peace will). [#180]
4. Try to sleep with the baby during the day. She does not yet believe it is possible but will at least try. [#180]
5. Collect the contacts of needed specialists in advance. A pediatrician and breastfeeding consultants, so as not to panic in difficult moments. [#180]
6. Ask for help and don't hide that it is hard. No heroic efforts to pretend everything is fine; if it is hard, that is normal, and asking for help is not shameful. [#180]
7. Buy everything necessary in advance. Better to have everything at hand at once so as not to spend time and nerves on shopping afterwards. [#180]
8. Make no expectations or plans for the first eight weeks. No plans and no big goals; just live in the moment and accept everything as it is. [#180]
9. Disconnect from work if it doesn't give energy. If work brings no joy or satisfaction, she will switch off from it in the first weeks; the main task is care of herself and the family. [#180]
10. Find joy in this difficult month. The first month is hard but full of moments that will never repeat; afterwards you rewatch gigabytes of photos and videos from those days all your life. [#180]

#### Five principles of inner stability in the last week of pregnancy
Context: Marina describes the last week before birth as "сильный кризис" (a strong crisis): a contrast between already heavy physical fatigue and the wish to stay a little longer in calm before the changes; total uncertainty (contractions or waters can start any minute); the need to be rested yet ready; wanting to relax while to-do lists flash in the head; anxiety, because you know how much work and pain lie ahead. These are the principles she returns to for inner balance. [#213]

1. Tolerance of uncertainty. She cannot control most of what is happening, but she can develop tolerance of that uncertainty. In practice it means permitting herself to be in the present and constant checking in: "«А как я сейчас? Что мне нужно прямо сейчас?»" ("How am I now? What do I need right now?"). [#213]
2. Trust as a support. When everything feels shaky, trust — in people, in one's body, in circumstances — becomes the main support; it helps to feel safe and lowers emotional tension. She notes that the Huberman podcast talks a lot about the importance of trust for mental health. [#213]
3. Self-compassion. When tired, it is easy to feel resentment towards loved ones — it seems others don't care enough or pay attention. Marina says Olya Polishchuk talked about this in an interview and she agrees. As soon as you find strength to support yourself with your own inner voice, you no longer want to blame others. Definition: "Самосострадание — это способность бережно относиться к себе, просить о помощи и принимать ее" (self-compassion is the ability to treat yourself with care, to ask for help and to accept it). [#213]
4. Meeting basic needs. In this state simple things gain special value: sleep, full meals, fresh air, movement and contact with loved ones are "якоря" (anchors) on which the feeling of stability is built in any situation. [#213]
5. A sense of self-sufficiency. Being self-sufficient means accepting yourself even when you have no strength for anything and temporarily can't show up the way you are used to; "моя ценность не зависит от того, что я делаю, а определяется тем, что я есть" (my worth doesn't depend on what I do but is defined by what I am). [#213]

Her hedge: all of this is very hard, "Но никто и не говорил, что будет легко" (but nobody said it would be easy). [#213]

#### Four energy restorers after a sleepless night with a baby
Context: as a mother for the second time, Marina again faced sleepless nights; Huberman's healthy-sleep protocols went to the back shelf for a long time, but some useful, supportive things remained. [#219]

1. Nutrition. She deliberately does not tell herself to avoid fast carbs and sugar: "Зачем в этой ситуации добавлять еще один источник стресса?" (why add one more source of stress in this situation?). Instead she focuses on the volume of quality food during the day: full meals with protein, whole grains and a variety of vegetables, especially stewed or baked for nourishment — "Наедаться!" (eat your fill!). This matters for her energy and for supporting lactation; "Сытый малыш спит лучше" (a well-fed baby sleeps better). [#219]
2. Rest and pauses. The common advice is to sleep with the baby during the day; it is hard and deep daytime sleep often doesn't happen. But you can top up with short pauses and rest practices — a Power Nap, NSDR. What helps her: simply putting on bilateral music and closing her eyes for 20 minutes. [#219]
3. Circadian rhythms. Catching 10 minutes of sunlight in the morning is "бесценно" (priceless): daylight ("morning light exposure по Хуберману") gives an energy boost and helps the baby figure out where day and night are. In the evening, after 20:00 — no phone, only dim yellow light, blackout curtains and an aired room. [#219]
4. Give yourself a break. Being a mother is big work 24/7, and if self-care is not prioritised you can burn out. So no super-productivity, only the most important things — "Пишу пост — и иду в кровать" (I write the post and go to bed). [#219]

#### Three conclusions from the cholesterol story
The story (Katya Strelnikova, May 2026): the headline "how to get rid of raised cholesterol in 2 weeks" is a deliberate parody of clickbait — "Спойлер: никак" (spoiler: no way). In April she did her annual blood test and for the first time in her life got a value flagged red: raised cholesterol, two days after turning 40. She immediately started an "investigation": food from the VkusVill store? More dairy? Early perimenopause? Her husband, who eats even more VkusVill food and about five times more sweets than she does, got tested too — and everything was fine for him. Her "inner health coach" opened a new chat with Claude to discuss her diet and pick the least cholesterol-heavy options, and she switched to 10% sour cream; she calls this a useful exercise because it confirmed that she eats normally. Her lifestyle data: 8 hours of sleep, vegetables at every meal, 14,000 steps, an average of 750 calories burned (in activity), sport almost every day, no alcohol, no smoking, sweets 2–3 times a week. She soon got to the gynecologist she had been meaning to visit for three months, who said "«Екатерина, я уважаю ваш перфекционизм, но обсуждать это смешно»" ("Ekaterina, I respect your perfectionism, but discussing this is ridiculous"); they reviewed three years of her cycle — no perimenopause — and she was told to retake the cholesterol test. Two weeks later the retest showed no elevation. The real cause: she had eaten fatty lamb at a market food court and in restaurants the day before the cholesterol test, although she knows tests require preparation. She still sees some movement in cholesterol and will keep paying attention to some products (dairy, meat). Her stated personal lesson: learning to bear the lack of control over the body wisely and with humility "мне еще только предстоит" (is still ahead of me). [#618]

The before/after lab values from the screenshot (mmol/L, first test 22.04.26 → retest two weeks later) [#618]:
- Triglycerides: 0.35 → 0.49. [#618]
- Total cholesterol: 5.14 (flagged red) → 4.99. [#618]

- Non-HDL cholesterol: 3.31 → 3.14. [#618]

The three conclusions, as she states them:
1. If you live a healthy lifestyle, there is a high chance that at 40 you are completely healthy — even if you started at around 35. [#618]
2. "Один анализ — не приговор и не диагноз" (one test is not a verdict and not a diagnosis). Look at the dynamics, talk to a doctor, and do not google "early perimenopause". [#618]
3. Read the preparation recommendations for tests and prepare — if you want to save a couple of thousand roubles and your own stress. [#618]

#### Why Do It before Just Do It
Katya's take from the Femtech Force Jam 2026 conference recordings: in 2025 Nike launched the campaign "Why Do It?", rethinking its legendary Just Do It for a new generation — Just Do It stays, but a question now stands in front of it. Her explanation of why Just Do It often fails and irritates: the command "just do it" assumes the person already knows why, already wants it, already decided, and only has to start; but most people get stuck not at the action stage but much earlier. "Why Do It?" returns to where everything really begins, to the questions we skip in pursuit of results: Why do I need this? What do I want to get from it? What will change if I start? What will I lose if I don't? This is "не слабость и не прокрастинация" (not weakness and not procrastination) but necessary inner work without which any "how" falls apart at the first difficulty. She anchors it in the Nietzsche line "Если человек знает зачем, он сможет любое как." (If a person knows why, they can manage any how.). [#578]

#### OMS check-up entitlement by age
Purpose: to know which free check-up you can claim this year. Katya's rule: "провериться по ОМС может каждый взрослый — вопрос только в объеме услуг" (every adult can get checked under OMS — the only question is the scope of services). [#711]

1. Full диспансеризация, under 40 — once every 3 years. [#711]
2. Full диспансеризация, from 40 — every year. Katya turned 40 this year, which is why she could "пуститься во все тяжкие по анализам" (go all out on tests). [#711]
3. Профилактический осмотр (preventive examination) — can be done every year at any age, even when the year is "not yours" for full диспансеризация. It is a reduced version, but the base is there: blood tests, fluorography (chest X-ray screening), ECG, an oncology examination and a gynecologist. [#711]

Conclusion she draws: "«мне еще не 40» или «год не тот» — не повод проходить мимо" ("I'm not 40 yet" or "it's not the right year" is no reason to pass it by). [#711]

#### Six steps to a free check-up via EMIAS.INFO (Moscow)
Purpose: to do the state check-up in Moscow even if, like Katya, you are not registered (прописаны) there. Context: she does not live in Moscow and every summer on arrival does a set of tests; she still pays private labs (she names Invitro, Fomin, Frauklinik) but asks why refuse what is "буквально впаривают на всех автобусных остановках" (literally pushed at you at every bus stop) — the city ads are what reminded her of free диспансеризация a couple of days after arriving in Moscow in July. She frames the steps as "пошагово, что для этого нужно сделать и почему это не страшно" (step by step, what you need to do and why it isn't scary). [#711]

1. Get a Moscow OMS policy. Check which region your policy belongs to. Hers was from Sergiev Posad, where she is registered. A couple of years ago she went to the nearest insurance company in Moscow and asked them to issue a Moscow policy; it took about 10 minutes. Why: the Moscow services described here are accessed with a Moscow policy. [#711]
2. Download the EMIAS.INFO app and register. After getting the new policy she installed the app and found the word «Диспансеризация» in two clicks. This year a «Московского чекапа» (Moscow check-up) banner was even shown right on the first screen — she jokes she does not know whether that is only for "forty-year-old" her or for everyone. [#711]
3. Fill in the questionnaire. It asks about health status, symptoms, heredity and lifestyle. On that basis referrals are issued automatically, without having to go anywhere, and you can book the needed examinations at a convenient time. [#711]
4. Book. Waiting time and convenience depend on where you book. Her maximum wait was about two weeks. For bioimpedance she had to go to a district "Госуслуги" (state services) centre, and for CT and mammography to another polyclinic, but everything was within 30 minutes of travel; you may be lucky and have everything "all inclusive" in your own branch. Tips: (a) if you cannot book or there are no slots, check at 7–8 a.m. — usually that is when you can catch the slot you need; (b) referrals are valid until the end of the year, so you need not do everything at once and can spread the tests over several visits — "особенно, если вы не эмигрант, как я" (especially if you're not an emigrant like me). [#711]
5. Prepare. Tests require preparation. She offers herself as the cautionary example — the "creator" of the situations «нажраться жирного ягненка перед анализом на холестерин» (stuffing yourself with fatty lamb before a cholesterol test) and «попить черного чая перед ЭКГ» (drinking black tea before an ECG). In the app every examination has a section «Как подготовиться» (How to prepare); read it so you do not get results that upset you and have to be rechecked. [#711]
6. Show up. She was always put off by queues at terminals for a ticket; this time she never stood in one, because the ticket is now issued in the app at the press of a button. Everything was orderly, relatively polite and quick. Her one reservation: it is still more pleasant when "тетя из ИНВИТРО" (the lady at Invitro) draws your blood for money; but she has no real complaints. [#711]

Her disclaimer on scope: she does not judge the quality of the doctors' work, because she did not consult them; but the examinations and tests themselves — especially if, like her, nothing bothers you — can definitely be done this way. Her bottom line: what would have cost almost 40,000 roubles privately cost her zero; perhaps she spent a little more time, but in her view it was worth it. [#711]

#### Two changes that ended Katya's disabling period pain
Her story: the first day of her period was always "днем выживания" (a day of survival); she sometimes fainted from pain, and colleagues sometimes offered to call an ambulance. This Sunday she went on a 5.5-hour, 13-km mountain hike — on exactly the day her period started (she notes, with her husband as witness, that her period always "waits" for an important event, a long-awaited hike, a run with a friend or a flight to the other side of the world). In recent years she has had almost no discomfort and does not cancel plans because of her cycle. What changed: [#722]

1. A healthy lifestyle. She sleeps a lot and regularly, moves a lot and consistently, eats a balanced diet. She admits that when you read this in a textbook (and she reads such textbooks) it is hard to believe — and then it simply works ("А потом херак, и вот мы здесь", *and then, bam, here we are*). Evidence she cites: a Cochrane review of 12 studies showed that regular exercise can noticeably reduce menstrual pain with almost no side effects; the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists recommends movement as part of treating painful periods (her link goes to an American Family Physician article); and there is no evidence that sport on those days does harm. Hence her conclusion that "you must lie down during your period" is a cultural myth rather than a medical fact. Caveat in the post itself: if the pain makes you lose consciousness, go to a gynecologist first, not to the gym — severe pain can be a sign of endometriosis or other conditions that should be ruled out. [#722]
2. Period products that help rather than get in the way. Her combination: a menstrual cup plus period underwear (brand Onest). She has loved the cup since 2018 because she cannot stand the feeling of something leaking out of her; she has worn the underwear since 2024. Her objections to pads: endless waste, the smell, and the adhesive that sticks permanently to her favourite Uniqlo AIRism underwear (she admits the material of the underwear is probably to blame but "chooses to demonise pads"). Her result: "надела и забыла" (put it on and forgot) — nothing sweats, chafes or slips, confirmed by the 13-km hike. The mechanism is described under *How period underwear work* below. [#722]

### Detailed insights

#### Well-being during pregnancy (Marina's diary, month 7)
After seven full months of her second pregnancy, Marina reports that the discomforts and mood swings are not only tied to hormonal changes that seem uncontrollable; they are also "признаки истощения ресурсов организма" (signs of depletion of the body's resources) that you don't manage to react to in time — and that is completely natural. [#162]

Why it is hard: the body demands more sleep, but getting enough becomes harder — habits and routine haven't adapted, pillows have become uncomfortable, the baby is active in the belly at night. Nutritional quality needs change. Her favourite sport became unavailable, and a new routine has not yet formed. All these inputs arrive at once and keep changing. [#162]

Hence her thesis: "беременный well-being — это, в первую очередь, про ментальность" (pregnant well-being is, first of all, about mentality) — the ability to accept, let go, not demand of oneself, show compassion. She keeps learning to listen to her body and be in the moment; meditation, which she started out of necessity, helps, and she greatly enjoys the Calm app. [#162]

Constant connection with oneself 24/7: if something hurts where it shouldn't, if a depressed mood appears for no visible reason, or you just want to cry — "это не норма" (this is not normal). A cause can always be found if you are attentive: it may be lack of sleep, or constipation, which is not rare in pregnancy. Her pillars ("киты") of good well-being at that stage: osteopathy, massage, Pilates — plus a comfortable mattress, a pregnancy pillow and fresh air. [#162]

Her closing view: the body constantly needs support, but in return it gives a feeling of the force of life, connection with nature, inner power and pride in being a woman — sensations she says are incomparable. [#162]

#### Femtech and AI self-diagnosis
Katya praises the Russian-language Femtech Force Jam 2026 conference (focused on Health Tech, many smart women and ideas) as a rare conference at which she didn't fall asleep in the second minute. Still, she remains a skeptic about the speed of health digitalisation. She addresses readers who take their tests and symptoms to ChatGPT and then either assume the worst or, conversely, miss real problems. Her practice-based observation: the real people she meets daily lack not information, trackers and the like, but "контакта с собой" (contact with themselves). [#578]

The study she cites (her summary): in February 2026 Nature Medicine published a randomised study with 1,298 participants and three models (GPT-4o, Llama 3, Command R+). On their own the models identified the correct diagnosis in almost 95% of cases, but when real people used the same models, accuracy fell to 34% — no better than simply googling. People asked imprecise questions, misinterpreted answers and ignored correct recommendations even when they were stated plainly. The authors' conclusion as she reports it: a high score on a medical exam does not equal a useful tool for the patient. [#578]

#### How period underwear work and why they don't smell
The explanation given in the post (an advertisement for the brand): the gusset has three layers of hypoallergenic fabric with a membrane coating. Layer 1 absorbs and dries thanks to air circulation; layer 2 retains moisture; layer 3 lets neither moisture nor dust through but does let air through. The specific period smell comes not from the blood itself but from bacteria multiplying; in a disposable pad it is warm, moist and stuffy for them, whereas in the underwear blood is simply absorbed and dries, so a feeling of freshness lasts all day. The one condition stated: "Главное, правильно стирать" (the main thing is to wash them correctly) — the source does not say how. (!) INCOMPLETE: washing method not given. Katya says she uses the "Классика" (Classic) model for everyday wear and "Сон" (Sleep) for sleeping, and that the brand also sells a menstrual disc. [#722]

#### Free baseline vs fashionable panels
Katya observes that in recent years "велнес-люди" (wellness people) chase expensive check-ups, trendy tests and biohacker panels, which can create the impression that these are necessary to feel good and live long. Her answer is no: free диспансеризация covers most of what is important and is, in her words, "тот самый фундамент, с которого стоит начать" (the very foundation to start from). This fits her earlier stance (part 1, #346) that tests should be ordered by doctors and that she tracks dynamics yearly — here she adds that the regularity of basic checks, not the size of a panel, is what matters. [#711]

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The cycle-phase durations are given as averages with explicit hedges ("В среднем, каждые 28 дней"; follicular "около 16 дней, но все очень индивидуально"). Note the stated ranges (menstruation 3–7, follicular ~16, ovulation ~1, luteal 10–14) do not add up exactly to 28 days; the source does not reconcile this. (!) UNCLEAR [#37]
- Scheduling by cycle phase is presented as optional and experimental ("Это необязательно, но стоит попробовать"); Marina's only firm finding is that PMS makes everything harder. [#80]
- The check-up checklist is explicitly "для ознакомления" (for information) and not medical advice; the OCR of the card omitted TSH, which is visible on the image itself. [#122]
- Conflict of interest: the Здоровье.ру description comes from a paid advertisement post; Katya nonetheless adds that tests should be prescribed by doctors and symptoms require a specialist. [#346]
- The cholesterol story is a single personal case. Katya's husband half-jokingly said the second test "могут быть неточными" (may be inaccurate); she keeps the result she likes — a self-ironic remark, not a resolved question. She also still sees "динамика" (a trend) in her cholesterol and keeps watching dairy and meat. The "750" figure is given as "Средний расход калорий 750" without stating the time frame (presumably daily active calories). (!) UNCLEAR [#618]
- The AI-diagnosis findings are Katya's own summary of the Nature Medicine study, not the paper itself. [#578]
- The pregnancy and postpartum material is first-person experience ("Личный опыт"), not medical guidance; point 4 of the postpartum plan (daytime co-sleeping) is something Marina doubts is even possible, and in #219 she confirms deep daytime sleep often doesn't happen. [#180, #219]
- Marina's postpartum plan treats eight weeks as the recovery horizon; the source gives no further basis for the figure. [#180]

- Katya explicitly does not judge the quality of the state doctors' work — she did not consult them; her endorsement covers the tests and examinations only, and especially for people with no complaints. [#711]
- The procedure is Moscow-specific (EMIAS.INFO, a Moscow OMS policy, the Moscow check-up banner); the source does not say how this works in other regions beyond the general OMS entitlement rules. The Invitro prices are her figures at the time of writing. [#711]
- The test list is what the questionnaire assigned to her at 40, based on her answers about health, symptoms, heredity and lifestyle; it is not presented as a universal list, and she skipped four referrals for reasons of convenience. The difference from the #122 checklist (CT/X-ray only for smokers; fecal occult blood after 45) is not discussed in the source. (!) UNCLEAR [#711]
- Conflict of interest: #722 is marked as an advertisement (Реклама, with an advertiser registration code) for the period-underwear brand; the mechanism description of the underwear comes from that ad. The promo code and discount are logistics and are dropped. [#722]
- The evidence summaries (Cochrane review of 12 studies; ACOG recommending movement) are Katya's own paraphrase, not the studies themselves; "may noticeably reduce" is her hedge. Her personal improvement is attributed jointly to lifestyle and products; the source does not separate their effects. [#722]
- Red flag stated by the author herself: fainting from period pain warrants a gynecologist before any exercise; endometriosis or other conditions should be ruled out. Notably, her own past fainting is not followed in the post by any account of a diagnosis. (!) UNCLEAR [#722]
