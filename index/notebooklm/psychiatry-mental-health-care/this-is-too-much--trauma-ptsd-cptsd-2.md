## Coping with trauma, ongoing threat and post-traumatic growth

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's practical material on what happens after (and during) trauma: a list of coping strategies that help a person get through a traumatic experience without developing PTSD; a WHO/NHS-based guide for supporting a loved one after a traumatic event (the author frames it around a terrorist attack "or another terrible event"); the concept of post-traumatic growth (PTG), its five domains and the coping styles that foster or block it, told through Aimee's story in *Sex Education*; and two posts (written in 2026, under shelling and night evacuations) on how to live and keep working while a real, unpredictable threat continues. According to the author, social support plays the decisive role in recovery, and it is possible to work on quality of life and daily functioning even before the external threat has ended [#78, #111, #112, #258, #263].

This part adds one post (#77, October 2023, by Lena Foer, psychologist and CBT therapist) with her longread for Kosa.Media, "Я тяжело воспринимаю новости об убийствах и жестокости. Как помочь себе?" (I take news about killings and cruelty hard. How can I help myself?). It covers how heavy news and doomscrolling affect the psyche, why the urge to keep looking is so strong, whether news can cause PTSD, how to tell that news consumption is harming you, and how to care for yourself: an information diet, the "urge surfing" technique and focusing on positive experience. The author's reason for writing it: nowadays it is hard to open a news feed, messenger or social network without coming across yet another mention of killings and cruelty; some people read news for work, others need to know what is going on, but excessive consumption of such content can seriously harm one's state [#77, article #77].

Relation to the existing topic file: the existing file mentions doomscrolling only briefly — msg #111 says its coping advice also applies to people exposed through doomscrolling and that seeing videos or photos is not the same as being a participant but can leave a very strong imprint, and msg #263 advises separating information that helps you act from information that only scares you. Post #77 extends this with a full account of the effects, a PTSD-risk answer, warning signs and concrete methods. Its PTSD answer is consistent with and sharpens #111: full PTSD from news should not develop without prior PTSD or risk factors, but it can when you see a loved one in the news or are on the scene yourself. Its information-diet advice parallels idea 4 of msg #263 and adds specifics: once a day, text instead of video, neutral wire agencies [#77].

### Core ideas

- PTSD can be prevented to a degree by predictability and meaning-making. PTSD can only be spoken of some time after the trauma; what helps a person not develop it is creating a predictable environment around themselves and making sense of the traumatic event (full strategy list in *Coping strategies…* below) [#111].
- Indirect exposure counts. The coping advice applies both to people who took part in a traumatic event and to those exposed through doomscrolling news; seeing videos or photos is not the same as being a participant, but it can leave a very strong imprint [#111].
- Social support is decisive for recovery, and its effect depends on how it interacts with the person's coping style; severe PTSD itself can reduce perceived positive social support 3–12 months after trauma [#78].
- Post-traumatic growth is real but does not cancel suffering. PTG is positive personal change after trauma; the author repeats twice that no upsides cancel the suffering of living with trauma [#78, #81].
- Ongoing threat is hard because it is real, unpredictable and mostly not happening right now — you cannot flee from a danger that is absent at the moment, but you cannot consider yourself safe either [#258].
- You don't have to wait for safety to live. Research on psychological help amid continuing violence shows that quality of life and functioning can be worked on while the threat persists; the task is to acknowledge danger without automatically handing it all your time, attention and remaining life [#258, #263].

- Heavy news has measurable effects beyond "feeling bad". People usually say they feel "плохо" (bad); the author proposes unpacking this, noting there is now a lot of research on how consuming negative news and doomscrolling affects people. It can raise symptoms of anxiety, depression and distress, worsen existing mental disorders and, in people at risk, trigger individual PTSD symptoms (full list in *Five kinds of effects…*) [#77].
- Doomscrolling is an unintentional misuse of an ancestral survival system. We look because the brain treats threats as something to watch (see *Why we keep looking* below) [#77].
- News alone should not cause full PTSD in people without a history or risk factors, though individual symptoms can appear. Exceptions: seeing a loved one in the news or on video, or being at the scene yourself [#77].
- Wanting information and being upset by it are normal. The issue is when it seriously affects your state. Then you should treat yourself with more care and take action (see the six signs) [#77].
- Self-help works in three layers: limit and reshape intake (information diet), ride out the urge to go back (urge surfing), and actively build up positive experience, because positive emotions make us more resistant to distress as well as emotionally steadier [#77].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Coping strategies for getting through trauma without developing PTSD (11 strategies)
Purpose: to help oneself pass through a traumatic experience (as a participant or via news/doomscrolling) and lower the risk of developing PTSD. The author first lists the symptoms of PTSD to watch for: intrusive memories and flashbacks, nightmares, avoidance of reminders of the trauma, negative changes in thoughts and mood, and heightened arousal or reactivity. The strategies [#111]:

1. Seek support ("Обращайтесь за поддержкой" (Reach out for support)). Turn to friends, family and anyone who can empathise with you, validate you and guide you through difficult times. Join support groups or volunteering, where you can talk with other people who have been through something similar [#111].
2. Focus on what you can control. Shift attention from frightening, anxious thoughts about the catastrophe to what you can actually do to bring a sense of control and safety back into your life [#111].
3. Practise self-care. Prioritise what is in your control — namely, caring for yourself: full sleep, regular physical exercise, healthy eating and ways to relax your body. Do things that bring joy and relaxation *even if you do not feel them in the moment* — e.g. time in nature, music, hobbies. The author's comment: "это банально, но это необходимо" (it's banal, but it's necessary) [#111].
4. Give yourself structure. Create a daily routine and a system in your life to provide a sense of stability and predictability; this can weaken the feeling of chaos or unpredictability that trauma can produce [#111].
5. Set realistic goals. Break larger tasks or goals into smaller, doable steps so you don't feel overloaded and gain more control. Mark your progress and achievements along the way, however insignificant they seem in the moment. "Baby steps лучше их отсутствия" (Baby steps are better than none). The author adds that, strange as it sounds, the point is not the goals themselves but what achieving them gives you [#111].
6. Use relaxation techniques — progressive muscle relaxation, yoga or stretching — to reduce stress and promote bodily relaxation. They can calm the nervous system and ease anxiety or hyperarousal symptoms, the author hedges, "хотя бы на время" (at least for a while) [#111].
7. Make sense of the event. Allow yourself to acknowledge and express emotions about the traumatic event if you want to talk about it. Avoid suppressing or denying feelings — you may feel many things and it is worth expressing them. Tell people close to you or specialists what you think and feel [#111].
8. Distinguish the present from the past. When you fall into memories or images of the frightening event, help yourself return to the current moment, where you are safe. Ground yourself through the outside world: describe where you are, lean against the back of the chair, or look at the date/time [#111].
9. Practise patience and self-compassion. Recovery from a traumatic event takes time; give yourself as much of it as you need [#111].
10. Psychoeducation. Anxiety, intrusive thoughts and images, rumination, worry, panic attacks, disturbed sleep, a flattened or fluctuating emotional background "и много чего еще" (and much else) are a normal reaction to severe stress. Understanding what is happening to you and how to help yourself "дорогого стоит" (is worth a lot) [#111].
11. Seek professional help if needed. If you are trying with all your might to cope with the aftermath and it is still hard, consider turning to a specialist [#111].

Closing thesis: by applying these strategies you can help yourself recover and reduce the risk of PTSD; asking for help is normal, and it is possible to get through it given time, support and self-care [#111].

#### Supporting a loved one after a traumatic event (10 WHO/NHS-based recommendations)
Purpose: what family and friends can do for someone who has survived a traumatic event (the author mentions a terrorist attack or another terrible event). The author says the tips are based on recommendations of the World Health Organization and the UK National Health Service. Family and friends can play a decisive role through support, help and respect for boundaries [#112].

1. Stay in touch. Show that you are there. Don't wait for them to call — call yourself. Ask what you can do for them and give concrete examples of household tasks (calling other people, cooking, walking the dog, picking the children up from school). Encourage them to connect with friends and family members who can give additional support [#112].
2. Give emotional support. *Listen without judgment* and let them express their feelings openly. *Acknowledge their feelings* — fear, anger, sadness or anxiety — as normal reactions to a traumatic event; show they are not alone in this. *Express empathy*: speak in a soft, calm tone, keep eye contact, respect silence [#112].
3. Help them stay in the present. If a feeling of unreality and detachment appears, ask them to stamp their feet, press their feet into the floor or their back into the chair back, count all the brown objects in the room, or focus on breathing. Remind them that "человек уже в безопасности" (the person is already safe) [#112].
4. Encourage self-care. Emphasise the importance of rest, healthy food and regular exercise for recovery. Offer to accompany them to medical check-ups if needed [#112].
5. Create a safe environment. Help them feel safe by making home comfortable — this can be a night light, or your offer to live together for a while or for them to move in with you. *Minimise triggers*: keep potential triggers in mind (loud noises, reminders of the event) and offer support in avoiding or managing them — e.g. you will be the one who goes to the shopping mall for groceries [#112].
6. Offer practical help. Doing the same household duties, errands, care for children/parents/animals; cleaning, grocery shopping — make sure basic needs are met [#112].
7. Respect autonomy. *Respect their decisions* about how to recover, including whether or not to seek professional help. *Avoid pressure*: don't force them to tell about their experience or take part in activities that are unpleasant to them. *Respect silence* and passivity just as you respect determination and the wish to do something [#112].
8. Psychoeducate yourself. Learn about the psychological consequences of trauma and PTSD to better understand what the survivor may be experiencing; this makes it easier not to feel helpless when faced with intense emotions [#112].
9. Encourage professional help. Recommend a mental-health specialist experienced with trauma and PTSD — especially if you notice that it is not getting easier over time. Help them find a comfortable form of support: support groups, hotlines or clinics that work with trauma survivors [#112].
10. Take care of yourself. Supporting a traumatised person is painful, frightening and hard, so turn to your own close people who can look after you. Take breaks, rest, don't exhaust yourself [#112].

#### Five domains of post-traumatic growth
Purpose: to recognise how PTG shows up. The author lists five manifestations, each illustrated with Aimee from *Sex Education* [#81]:

1. Personal strength. Survivors often discover inner reserves of strength they did not suspect; they become more resilient and better prepared to overcome adversity [#81].
2. Improved relationships. Some find their relationships deepen and become more meaningful after trauma; they may develop heightened compassion, empathy and greater appreciation for loved ones. Example: Aimee as a "master" of help and support for others, especially Maeve and Isaac — she will always drive Maeve, even to search for her sister in another city instead of going to class, bake cupcakes and offer to climb a very tall ladder to the second floor looking for keys [#81].
3. Greater appreciation of life. Trauma can lead people to re-evaluate priorities and value life more; they may learn to enjoy the present moment and find more joy in everyday experiences. Example: the playful montage in episode 1 of season 4 where Aimee "researches" the joys of everyday experiences [#81].
4. Spiritual or existential growth. Some experience a deepening of spiritual or existential beliefs and may find greater meaning or purpose in life [#81].
5. New possibilities. PTG can bring increased creativity and openness to new possibilities; survivors may seek new interests, take on hard tasks or change the direction of their lives [#81].

Related finding: an active personal stance (activism) against sexualised violence correlates positively with PTG among survivors of violence, because it fosters interpersonal relationships and gives a sense of purpose (a ResearchGate study on sexual violence, coping self-efficacy, self-blame, shame, activism and feminism) [#81].

#### Three coping styles and their relation to post-traumatic growth
Per the research the author cites [#78]:
1. Adaptive coping — associated with PTG: different points of view within one's social circles help change the negative impact of intrusive trauma-related thoughts.
2. Avoidant coping — avoiding thinking about the trauma; it *hinders* PTG because it prevents survivors from sharing their experience and accessing social support.
3. Emotional coping — re-living the traumatic events; it has a *less significant* effect on PTG.

#### Why ongoing unpredictable threat is hard for the psyche (threat mechanism)
Context: the author describes the strange state of not being physically in a combat zone, yet hearing and seeing explosions, reading incident reports, evacuating in the middle of the night — and then morning comes and one has to work, cook, answer work chats, walk the dogs "Как ни в чем не бывало?" (As if nothing happened?). The danger hasn't gone, and ordinary life is possible — a dissonance [#258].
The mechanism, step by step [#258]:
1. The psyche struggles with a threat that is simultaneously real, unpredictable and (at least for now) not directly happening part of the time.
2. Mobilisation: when danger becomes current, the body mobilises — attention switches to the threat, the body prepares to act, usual tasks recede. This is useful when you urgently need to take shelter or evacuate.
3. No full relaxation: if you cannot tell when danger will return, relaxing fully is hard; attention keeps scanning the environment — what's that sound, what's that light in the window, what are they writing, has anything changed.
4. Uncertainty–anxiety–checking loop: uncertainty sustains anxiety, and anxiety pushes you to search for information again, sometimes even when a new check no longer changes anything (the author jokes that finding anti-shatter window film on sale at 3 a.m. is still better than nothing).
5. The resulting trap: most of the time you cannot flee from the immediate danger, because it is not there right now, but you also cannot consider yourself safe — and you still have to live.
6. The nervous system is not a fire alarm that is either on or off. Stress intensity varies; between episodes of direct threat there are periods when you can eat, sleep, work, socialise and even (the author's ironic "ха") enjoy yourself. This does not necessarily mean you have forgotten the danger or aren't taking it seriously enough.
7. Conclusion from research: studies of psychological help under conditions of continuing violence show that quality of life and everyday functioning can be worked on while the external threat is not over; you don't have to wait for the world to become safe again. The hardest part is acknowledging the danger exists while not automatically giving it all your time, attention and remaining life — not "pretending nothing is happening", but learning to live in the gaps between what is happening and what might happen. "Звучит дико" (Sounds wild) [#258].

#### How to keep living amid ongoing threat (6 research-based ideas)
Premise: we cannot change what is happening and cannot get a guarantee that it will be safe to sleep tonight or tomorrow ("Не ковид, так чума" — *if not covid, then the plague*); hiding is not an option either, since one has to eat, earn, fulfil obligations, walk the dogs — and there may be no immediate threat right now [#263].

1. Separate preparing for danger from constantly expecting danger. Preparing means understanding the situation: where to check official information, where to physically go during a threat, what to take with you and how to contact loved ones. During covid we bought masks and sanitizer; here it is something similar — with protective film for windows. Constantly replaying possible scenarios in your head is *not* the same: it doesn't necessarily make you better prepared, but it can drain the strength needed for everyday life [#263].
2. Don't wait for anxiety to pass before doing things. Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) has the idea of *psychological flexibility*: noticing unpleasant thoughts and feelings without first demanding to get rid of them, and continuing to act in line with what matters. You can worry and go to work; miss your old life and go to work; not know what will happen in a month and still go to work — or meet friends, go to training, anything. This is not positive thinking: we don't convince ourselves everything will be fine; we admit we don't know how things will turn out and decide what to do in the circumstances that exist now [#263].
3. Keep at least part of your usual life. During the pandemic, disrupted daily routines (the author's example: woke at 9:59, poured coffee, sat down to work, worked until 23:59 and went to bed) were associated with more pronounced symptoms of anxiety, depression and psychological distress. The author's hedge: this doesn't prove that a schedule by itself treats stress, but it supports the idea that a stable daily routine can be one of the genuinely meaningful supports. You don't need to restore all of your former life — that may simply be impossible; it can be enough to eat and sleep regularly, leave the house, talk to someone and leave room for activities that give interest or pleasure, even a drop [#263].
4. Figure out which information helps you act and which just scares you again. Following warnings about real threat is important, but endlessly scrolling reels, refreshing the feed and checking Telegram is not the same as increasing your safety. Keep access to sources that help you make decisions and limit the flow of material after which you know no more but are more anxious [#263].
5. Don't try to cope alone. Social support: sometimes it's a person you can text at night a joke like "я как в сериале про подростков, которые в заложниках в школе" (I'm like in a series about teenagers held hostage at school); sometimes a shared dinner, help with chores, or the chance to be next to someone who understands the context [#263].
6. Don't turn adaptation into another exam. Being tired, irritable or unable to enjoy things as before doesn't mean you are coping wrong. Ongoing threat is a real load, not a personal-effectiveness task. And if your state interferes with sleeping, eating, working or maintaining relationships for a long time, seeking help is not a sign of insufficient resilience [#263].

Main thought: the point is neither learning not to be afraid nor that daily tasks will magically save you. First — necessary protective actions; in the gaps between them — everything else that makes life worth living. You don't have to feel safe to take the next step; sometimes it's enough to know what you will do if the situation changes, and to allow yourself to get on with life until it does [#263].

#### Five kinds of effects of negative news and doomscrolling on the psyche
What can happen when a person reads about something terrible or sees frightening photos, according to the author's summary of research [#77]:

1. Anxiety symptoms intensify. There are two types:
   - *Cognitive*: worry about what you've read and growing general anxiety.
   - *Somatic*: muscle tension, insomnia, pain in different parts of the body, a racing heartbeat, tightness in the chest, digestive problems and other manifestations [#77].
2. Depression symptoms intensify. Examples: general pessimism, lower mood, problems with concentration and capacity to work, lower libido, fatigue, self-blame, and seeing the outside world as a hopelessly bad place where nothing can be fixed any more [#77].
3. Distress symptoms appear (distress = negative stress). Examples: endlessly replaying the news in your head, excessive worry about it, and obsessive thoughts about where all this will lead. Threshold: if this state lasts more than a month and seriously affects your ability to function, it makes sense to see a psychiatrist. If such symptoms are pronounced and persistent, they may indicate adjustment disorder. This is a disorder related to PTSD that sometimes arises with significant life changes and other stressors, for example fighting going on near where the person lives [#77].
4. Existing conditions get worse. If a person has a mental disorder, heavy news or doomscrolling can cause it to flare up [#77].
5. PTSD symptoms. If a person has risk factors for PTSD (childhood abuse is one) or has had PTSD in the past, individual symptoms may now develop. These include avoidance of thoughts, people, objects or significant activities; re-living the trauma in flashbacks, intrusions (intrusive memories) or nightmares; and a sense of constant threat [#77].

The author notes these are only the most common consequences; consuming content about cruelty can have many other negative effects [#77].

#### Six signs that news consumption is harming you
Signs that it may be time to reconsider your content-consumption habits [#77]:

1. You decided to stop scrolling the news but find it hard to stop.
2. However much you switch to something else, your thoughts keep returning to the news stories and you cannot get out of them.
3. Everyday events that didn't trigger such a reaction before now set off anxiety that keeps building.
4. You have symptoms of depression and they are getting stronger. The author suggests checking them, for example, with the Beck test ("по тесту Бека" — *with the Beck test*).
5. It becomes hard to function and handle everyday tasks such as brushing your teeth, cooking or working.
6. You develop somatic symptoms: muscle tension, insomnia, pain in different parts of the body, a racing heartbeat, chest tightness, digestive problems and other manifestations [#77].

#### Information diet (3 rules)
The author's first recommendation for when the news makes you feel bad [#77]:

1. Limit the time you spend on news, for example by checking it once a day. According to the author, that is usually enough to stay informed, and refreshing the feed every hour is unlikely to help. Exception and adjustment: if your job involves reading news (for example, journalism), you probably can't do this, but you should at least take breaks from news outside working hours rather than keep refreshing feeds long after the workday has ended [#77].
2. Replace video content with text. The author's reasoning is that texts affect us less than video [#77].
3. Replace emotional media and news channels with more neutral ones. For example, read the summaries of international news agencies, where information is presented fairly dryly and without escalation ("без нагнетаний" — *without hype/escalation*) [#77].

#### Urge surfing (серфинг желаний) for the craving to return to the news
Purpose / when to use. On the information diet you may feel a very strong urge to go back to your usual news-reading routine. For this the author recommends a technique used in addiction work, urge surfing, which helps people become aware of a dependency and learn not to obey it [#77].

Mechanism. In addictions (for example, to nicotine or alcohol) there is *craving* (тяга): an obsessive, hard-to-overcome desire to use the substance. Trying to switch to something else and pretend the craving isn't there only makes it stronger. In urge surfing, the craving is compared to waves in the ocean: they can be huge, but they pass quickly. Craving works the same way: it can be terribly strong, but it doesn't last long [#77].

Steps:
1. Notice the craving. When the urge appears (here, the urge to go back to the news), notice it. Treat it without judgment and don't condemn yourself for having it [#77].
2. Observe the urge without doing what it pushes you to do. Notice how it becomes stronger, then weaker. Pay attention to how your thoughts try to find a reasonable justification for doing what you badly want to do. Notice the temptation to do it in passing, half-consciously [#77].
   - *Grounding while the craving lasts:* hold a piece of ice in your hand, take a warm shower, watch funny animal videos, light a scented candle, or imagine you are in a pleasant, relaxing place. According to the author, such actions help you ground yourself [#77].
3. Wait for the craving to pass. It usually lasts 20–30 minutes, after which the desire subsides for a while [#77].

Common mistake built into the method: trying to distract yourself and act as if the craving doesn't exist. The author says this makes it stronger [#77].

#### Focusing on positive experience
Another way to help yourself when reading the news is hard is to concentrate on positive experience. The author's reasoning: positive emotions make us more resistant to distress as well as emotionally steadier [#77]. How:
1. Notice all the pleasant things that happen to you during the day, however small they are [#77].
2. To make it easier to keep your attention on them, keep a diary of pleasant moments [#77].
3. Plan activities that will bring you pleasure in advance. The author says it is best to plan the whole week at once [#77].

### Detailed insights

#### Aimee's story as a case study of trauma and recovery (Sex Education)
The author uses Netflix's *Sex Education* (spoilers for season 4). Context: in season 2 Aimee boards a bus to school carrying a cake she made for Maeve; a man stands close behind her, and after a moment of confusion she realises he is masturbating on her. She shouts, but nobody on the bus reacts. She gets off at the wrong stop and walks to school. When Maeve asks about the cake, Aimee explains a man ejaculated on her leg and she smeared the cake in shock — then asks whether the stains will come out because she likes those jeans. When Maeve tells her to report it, Aimee brushes it off, stressing how cheap the jeans were; Maeve says "На тебя напали" (You were assaulted); Aimee replies the perpetrator was probably "просто одинок. Ничего особенного. Это глупо. Я в порядке" (just lonely. Nothing special. It's stupid. I'm fine) [#78].
The lesson: over the series it becomes clear Aimee is not fine — the minimisation is followed by trauma symptoms. The next week, trying to board the bus, she sees her attacker's face; she walks to school instead of taking the bus (avoidance); she doesn't want her boyfriend to touch her; and for a long time (two seasons) she doesn't understand herself and tells no one what is going on inside her. The author praises the depiction as realistic: every day brings a new "trick" from the mind [#78].
In the season 4 finale Aimee makes a performance and a photo shoot with the jeans; before that she photographs garden gnomes and yells at people for catcalling. The author reads Aimee re-thinking her experience and taking steps against it as exposure and as post-traumatic growth [#78]. In the follow-up post, the author (explicitly "на мой субъективный взгляд" — *in my subjective view*) says the activism side of PTG is shown most vividly: Aimee is proactive and shares her experience with loved ones "(и не только)" (and not only them); her black-and-white photos with the jeans are something personal, about her story and her newfound strength "без джинс" (without the jeans); and the bus-stop performance of burning the jeans shows existential growth, opening of new possibilities and personal strength [#81].
Real-life origin: per the author, Aimee's story is the real story of the series' creator Laurie Nunn, which happened to her five years before *Sex Education* while she was riding a bus to college. For the screenwriter, the series itself is her PTG — about working on herself and a meaningful, loud conversation that shows the problem to the world in a form that is hard not to understand or hear [#81].

#### Social support and PTSD
Severe PTSD can lead to lower perceived positive social support between 3 and 12 months after the trauma, yet social support plays a decisive role in recovery; its effectiveness depends on the interplay between social support and coping styles (see *Three coping styles…*) [#78].

#### Why we keep looking: the ancestral threat-monitoring mechanism
The author explains that the urge to consume such content is very strong largely because of mechanisms inherited from our ancestors. They survived by closely watching phenomena or creatures that might be dangerous. So when we meet something the brain perceives as a threat, we become absorbed in watching it, and the brain actively works out frightening scenarios that might threaten us. Meanwhile the body produces cortisol and adrenaline [#77].

In this sense, she writes, doomscrolling is a (unintentional) misuse of a system that helped people survive danger for thousands of years. Even if nothing terrible is happening to the person at that moment, reading the news creates a sense of danger as if it threatened them right here and now, rather than being at some distance. This creates the feeling that you must not look away under any circumstances [#77]. (This complements the threat-scanning and uncertainty–anxiety–checking loop described in msg #258 of the existing file.)

#### Can reading news and watching videos cause PTSD?
The author's answer: individual symptoms can appear, but full PTSD should not develop, at least if you have not had it before and have no risk factors. As examples of risk factors she lists life-threatening traumatic events and sexualised violence. However, PTSD can develop in a person who saw someone close to them in the news or in a video, or in someone who is at the scene of events themselves [#77].

#### Normalisation before action
The author stresses that wanting to consume information is normal, and being upset by harsh or sad content is completely natural. Action is warranted when it starts to seriously affect your state; then it is worth treating yourself with more care and taking measures [#77].

## Dissociation, depersonalization and out-of-body experiences

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's explanations of dissociation: what it is, its two main forms (depersonalization and derealization), its causes and triggers, why it is part of borderline personality disorder (BPD), how it can become a habitual avoidance strategy that keeps a person in "continuous crisis", what out-of-body experiences (OBE) are and how they relate to complex PTSD, and practical ways to cope — above all grounding, including the 5-4-3-2-1 technique. According to the authors, dissociation matters because it is a defense that can become a source of new danger, and because it "is not something you should try to ignore" — frequent dissociation is a reason to seek help [#26, #45, #105, #192, #193].

### Core ideas

- Dissociation is a separation of the self from experience. Thoughts, emotions and identity feel as if they exist apart from the person; it shows up as detachment from the body, memory loss or "falling out" of reality. See *Definitions* below [#26, #105].
- Trauma is the most significant factor. Dissociation is a BPD symptom appearing under high stress; psychological trauma is considered the main driver of dissociative symptoms [#26, #105].
- A defense becomes avoidance. Dissociation protects during unbearable events, but once learned it can be used for any fright, which reduces awareness of real dangers and feeds a "continuous crisis" — see *Dissociation as defense turned avoidance* [#45].
- Mild, transient dissociation after stress is normal; persistent dissociation is a disorder feature. Feelings of unreality can last a few days after a shocking event; as part of a mental disorder it can be a constant experience [#105].
- Out-of-body experiences are a particular version of dissociation. They are a regulation mechanism under extreme stress and danger and have specific links to cPTSD [#192, #193].
- Grounding shifts attention from inner to outer world. Techniques like 5-4-3-2-1 do not solve problems but buy time and the possibility of a conscious next step [#146].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Four signs of dissociation (recognition checklist)
Lena Foer notes that dissociation can be hard to recognize because symptoms can be barely noticeable and vary from person to person. Common signs [#26]:
1. A feeling of detachment or disconnection from oneself or from the surroundings [#26].
2. A feeling of being in a dream or not fully present in reality [#26].
3. Memory loss or gaps in memory [#26].
4. A feeling of watching what is happening as if from the side [#26].

#### Four steps when you feel dissociation
Lena Foer's sequence of helpful steps for the moment of dissociation [#26]:
1. Practice mindfulness and grounding techniques. For example, concentrate on your breathing, on the sensation of your feet on the ground, or on the sounds around you. Why: this helps you return to the present moment and reduces the sense of detachment [#26].
2. Do a self-care procedure. This could be taking a bath, a walk, or doing something you love. Why: caring for yourself reduces stress and anxiety, which contribute to dissociation [#26].
3. Seek professional help. A therapist can give support in the moment and help understand the causes of the symptom, and can help develop strategies for overcoming dissociation [#26].
4. Keep a journal. Writing down your experiences helps reveal patterns and triggers, and helps you reflect on your experience and on how you cope with it [#26].
The post closes with the rule that dissociation should not be ignored: «Важно помнить, что диссоциация - это не то, что вы должны пытаться игнорировать.» (It is important to remember that dissociation is not something you should try to ignore.) If you often notice dissociation in yourself, it is important to seek help and support [#26].

#### Dissociation as defense turned avoidance (the "continuous crisis" mechanism)
The phenomenon. Lena Foer describes a frequent trait of people with BPD: a state of "continuous crisis" — a chain of hard events where as soon as one disaster ends, the next begins. Her illustration: if you didn't catch a cold, you left your phone in a taxi; if you didn't leave the phone, you ended up at night in a rough neighbourhood in the middle of a construction site — with the series continuing without end, all the time [#45].

How it works, step by step [#45]:
1. Dissociation is a psychological defense. The person begins to perceive a hard situation as if it were not happening to them, as if they are not here at all — «и никакого тут, может, и вообще нету» (and maybe there is no "here" at all). It can look very different: some people see what is happening from the side, others just slightly "switch off" when scared and may not even notice it themselves [#45].
2. It arises in response to trauma. It is easier to survive, for example, violence if you are "a little not here" and it is not happening to you [#45].
3. It becomes a habit. Once it has appeared, dissociation can become the habitual way of coping with fright, even small fright. «Так механизм психологической защиты становится механизмом психологического избегания.» (Thus a mechanism of psychological defense becomes a mechanism of psychological avoidance.) [#45]
4. Avoidance raises real risk. Example: Masha survived a car accident; now she may dissociate every time she is in a car again. If she is driving, dissociation affects her ability to drive — it is hard to notice dangers in time while trying to abstract from the outside world — so by dissociating she risks another accident. The author adds that this is not the only risk factor: there are always fools on the road, and one can end up in a crash (in an abusive situation, in any other trauma) in many ways. But «диссоциация - это движение с закрытыми глазами по дороге с дураками» (dissociation is driving with your eyes closed on a road full of fools), so the risks rise very substantially [#45].
5. Conclusion. «Именно сильное желание отвернуться от неприятного служит топливом непрерывного кризиса и поддерживает его.» (It is precisely the strong wish to turn away from the unpleasant that fuels the continuous crisis and sustains it.) [#45]

What follows — two practices [#45]:
- Look for avoidance moments. Notice moments when you want to "not think about it, let it somehow resolve itself", when you zone out into series or games or otherwise avoid being present in the outside world, or when it is hard to notice your body and the passage of time and everything feels as if through a fog [#45].
- Find out in advance what you avoid. Answer ahead of time: what are you avoiding, what are you trying not to think about, what are you trying not to face, where are you trying not to end up. These are the zones where it is critically important to switch on awareness [#45].
- Rationale. We can move on autopilot or think through and make sense of each step. Conscious movement costs more than autopilot, but in situations associated with elevated risk for you, these costs are justified [#45].

#### Depersonalization symptom list
Depersonalization is a feeling of detachment, distance or disconnection from oneself, one's body or mind — a feeling that what is happening around you is not happening to you. A person experiencing it is cut off from their thoughts, feelings, sensations and actions. Symptoms listed by Katya Klimochkina [#105]:
1. Changes in perception [#105].
2. A distorted sense of time and space [#105].
3. An unreal, unstable or absent "self" [#105].
4. Emotional or physical numbness [#105].
5. A feeling of watching a film about your own life [#105].
6. Feeling cut off from parts of your body or your emotions [#105].
7. Uncertainty about the boundaries between yourself and other people [#105].

#### Derealization symptom list
Derealization is a feeling of unreality connected with other people, places or objects: what is happening in the world around you seems unreal. With derealization the surroundings feel [#105]:
1. Unreal [#105].
2. Foggy [#105].
3. Dream-like [#105].
4. Lifeless, static [#105].
5. Visually distorted or blurred [#105].
6. Objects change shape, size or colour [#105].
7. A feeling of detaching oneself from the surrounding world [#105].
8. A feeling of looking at the world through a window pane [#105].
9. Feeling as if other people are robots (even if you know they are not) [#105].

#### Four causes of dissociation
Per Katya Klimochkina [#105]:
1. A natural reaction to uncontrollable trauma. For many people, dissociation is a natural reaction to a trauma they cannot control, and develops as a way of coping with trauma (one-time or ongoing), especially prolonged childhood trauma or abuse [#105].
2. Coping with very stressful experiences [#105].
3. Cultural or religious practices — some people may dissociate within certain such practices [#105].
4. Alcohol or medication — dissociation can be a side effect of using alcohol or some medications, or of stopping them [#105].
Dissociation can be a symptom of PTSD, depression, anxiety, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder or borderline personality disorder [#105].

#### Five coping measures for dissociation
Katya Klimochkina's "how to cope" list [#105]:
1. Basics first — «как не банально» (however banal it sounds): sleep routine, healthy eating, physical activity [#105].
2. Do not use alcohol or psychoactive substances [#105].
3. Know your triggers (see *Triggers* below) [#105].
4. Use grounding techniques — focus on the external surroundings, e.g. count all the red and blue objects around you [#105].
5. Mindful breathing — breathe "in a rectangle" (по прямоугольнику), and make the inhale a little shorter than the exhale [#105].

#### 5-4-3-2-1 grounding technique
Purpose. Lena Foer calls 54321 «универсальный ключ к эмоциональной регуляции» (a universal key to emotional regulation); the accompanying card describes it as a technique that connects you with the present moment by exploring the five senses [#146, img #146].

When to use it — the 10 indications [#146]:
1. You are dissociating or have simply "fallen out" of reality.
2. You are stuck in frightening, anxious thoughts or self-criticism.
3. You can't stop ruminating.
4. You are drowning in procrastination.
5. You have an acute urge to do something you decided never to do again.
6. You feel the ground slipping from under your feet and find it hard to control yourself.
7. You are lost and don't know what to do next.
8. You have gone through severe stress.
9. You want to distract yourself and switch attention.
10. You are stuck in daydreaming.
Because it is very simple, it is accessible from many difficult states [#146].

The steps (text of the post plus the card) [#146, img #146]:
1. 5 — see: count five visual objects of one colour or shape that you see.
2. 4 — hear: four (different) sounds you can hear.
3. 3 — feel in the body: three tactile / bodily sensations you can feel.
4. 2 — smell: two smells you can catch.
5. 1 — taste: one taste you can feel.
(The card's OCR text is column-scrambled; the pairing above follows the post text "five visual objects of some colour or shape, four sounds, three tactile sensations, two smells and one taste" [#146, img #146].)

Why it works. While you are counting, the focus of your attention shifts from the inner world to the outer world, which makes it easier to make a conscious decision about how to act next [#146].

Limits. «54321 не решит все ваши проблемы, не выведет вас из стресса и не поменяет ваше мышление. Но он даст вам время и возможность для того, чтобы сделать первый шаг ко всему этому.» (54321 will not solve all your problems, will not take you out of stress and will not change your thinking. But it will give you time and the opportunity to take the first step toward all of that.) [#146]

#### How an out-of-body experience feels
Katya Klimochkina defines OBE as a regulation mechanism in response to very strong stress and a sense of danger. It feels like [#192]:
1. Seeing oneself from outside, e.g. from above or from a corner of the room [#192].
2. A sense of detachment from the physical body, as if "hovering" in the air [#192].
3. Distorted perception: time seems slowed down or unreal [#192].
4. A feeling of being as if in a dream, where the body moves *automatically* [#192].

#### Causes of out-of-body experiences
Most often [#193]:
1. Trauma and stress — a strong emotional shock, e.g. during violence or an accident, can provoke OBE as a dissociative mechanism allowing one to avoid fear or pain [#193].
2. Sleep disturbances — OBE are often linked to sleep paralysis or states at the border of sleep and wakefulness [#193].
3. Neurological causes — temporal lobe epilepsy, migraines or brain injuries, especially if the temporo-parietal area of the brain is affected [#193].
4. Psychoactive substance abuse [#193].
5. Meditation or deep relaxation [#193].

#### Five links between out-of-body experiences and cPTSD
Katya Klimochkina says she was especially interested in how OBE connects to cPTSD — the "spoiler" being that OBE is a particular version of dissociation [#193]:
1. Reducing emotional intensity. During strong stress or a traumatic moment the brain may "switch off" to reduce the intensity of emotions; OBE then feels like a mental "stepping away" from oneself to escape unbearable experiences [#193].
2. Regaining safety or control. When faced with a reminder of past trauma, a person may "switch off" in order to feel safe or regain control [#193].
3. Amplifying non-belonging. Since cPTSD is often accompanied by shame, self-blame or a *loss of connection with oneself*, OBE can strengthen the feeling of "not belonging" to one's body or to reality [#193].
4. Flashbacks. During flashbacks a person may "relive" the trauma in the first person while simultaneously feeling separated from their body [#193].
5. Helplessness memories. OBE can arise when recalling events in which the person felt helpless, frozen or overwhelmed [#193].

### Detailed insights

#### Definitions of dissociation
Lena Foer's definition: dissociation is a psychological state in which a person's thoughts, emotions and sense of identity seem to exist separately from them. It takes various forms — a feeling of separation from one's body, memory loss, or a feeling of falling out of reality. Dissociative symptoms can be provoked by traumatic or stressful experiences, but can also arise without visible reasons [#26].

Katya Klimochkina's definition: dissociation is disconnection or detachment from one's sense of self (depersonalization) or from one's surroundings (derealization). Its symptoms can affect all aspects of mental functioning — memory, identity, emotions, perception and behaviour [#105].

#### Dissociation in BPD
Dissociation is one of the symptoms of BPD and can appear in situations of high stress. The experience of psychological trauma is considered the most significant factor in developing dissociative symptoms [#26]. Lena Foer cites a Springer article (Borderline Personality Disorder and Emotion Dysregulation) alongside this explanation [#26].

#### Normal versus disordered dissociation
You may have mild dissociative symptoms after a stressful or traumatic event. For example, if you witnessed an accident, what you saw may seem unreal; these feelings of "unreality" can last several days until what happened is "digested". When it is part of a mental disorder, dissociation can be a constant experience affecting a person's daily life [#105].

#### Triggers
A trigger is a reminder of something traumatic from the past that can cause you to dissociate. It can be something you hear, see, taste, smell or touch; it can also be a specific situation or a particular way your body moves. Triggers can be very diverse things [#105].

#### Frida Kahlo's "Accident" as an illustration of OBE
In 1925 Frida Kahlo was in a serious bus accident that caused severe injuries, multiple fractures and chronic pain. In her work *"The Accident"* («Авария») she depicted herself watching her injured body from above — a vivid image of what is often called an out-of-body experience. Katya Klimochkina reads the drawing as reflecting not only the psychological shock of the event but also how «разум может инстинктивно «покинуть» тело, когда реальность становится слишком сложной для восприятия» (the mind can instinctively "leave" the body when reality becomes too hard to perceive). The attached image is the drawing; its legible inscription reads roughly "…de 1926 – Frida Kahlo (Accidente)" [#192, img #192].

#### Further features of OBE
- A sensation of "floating" outside the body and watching oneself from the side; this state is sometimes *confused* with dreams of flying [#193].
- The experience may be accompanied by a sense of calm, but sometimes causes anxiety or confusion [#193].
- Some people feel they cannot return to their body until the state ends on its own [#193].
