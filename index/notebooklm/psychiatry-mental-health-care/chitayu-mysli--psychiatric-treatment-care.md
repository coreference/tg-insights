## Psychiatric medication: ADHD drugs and antidepressants

### What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel says about psychiatric medication: drug treatment for adult ADHD (atomoxetine and stimulants), a complete atomoxetine memo forwarded from the Psycho_pharmacologist channel, the problem that effective ADHD drugs are not sold in Russia, why ADHD medication is worth trying at least once, what to do when treating ADHD worsens autistic symptoms, why the wish to lower an antidepressant dose is usually stigma, how to taper an antidepressant without withdrawal, and the author's own experience of finding the right antidepressant. It also includes a Q&A session (#58) about ASD/ADHD diagnosis and support that opens with the medication question. According to the source, it matters because untreated ADHD can cost people work, relationships and even lives, and because ADHD medication "может очень (ОЧЕНЬ) сильно облегчиться" life [#58, #208, #615].

### Core ideas

- Adults with ADHD do have drug options: atomoxetine and stimulants — the latter allowed only in some countries. The author's position: people with ADHD need medication [#58].
- ADHD medication should be tried at least once. It may not suit, may not help, or side effects may outweigh benefits — then it is dropped — but the potential relief is very large (see *Three answers on whether ADHD medication is needed*) [#615, #696].
- There are no pills for autism. Medication targets ADHD; antidepressants are indicated only if there is depression or anxiety [#696].
- In autism + ADHD, ADHD is treated first, because it is considered the complicating factor; worsening autistic symptoms during ADHD treatment are a common problem to be lived through carefully rather than a reason to stop (see *Treatment order in autism + ADHD*) [#692].
- Wanting to reduce a working psychiatric dose is "pure" stigma — in the rest of medicine you take the dose that works (see *Self-check for medication stigma*) [#78].
- Antidepressant tapering is paced only by the individual's reaction — if half-tablet steps cause withdrawal, they are too fast for that person (see *Individual antidepressant taper*) [#853].
- In Russia, ADHD treatment suffers from two problems: outdated doctors who misdiagnose ADHD as bipolar II or BPD, and the absence of effective drugs such as bupropion and methylphenidate (forwarded opinion from NO STIGMA) [#208].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Self-check for medication stigma
Purpose: a "life hack" the Psycho_pharmacologist author offers to clients who ask "может мне стоит снизить дозу антидепрессантов, мне же хорошо?" ("maybe I should lower my antidepressant dose, I'm feeling fine?") [#78].

The base principle: "в медицине необходимо принимать ту дозу, которая работает" ("in medicine you need to take the dose that works") — this applies to all of medicine, not only psychiatry. Because psychiatry is stigmatized, psychiatrists get these questions far more often (the author admits she never worked in somatic medicine and does not know how often somatic doctors hear them) [#78].

Argument 1 — analogies with somatic medicine:
- *Thyroid:* a person with reduced thyroid function still has problems on a dose of 50 and the problems go away on 100. Does it make sense to go back to 50 once 100 solved the problems? [#78]
- *Antibiotics:* the prescription is 1000 for 7 days; after 3 days you feel better — would you cut it to 500 or even 250? [#78]
These feel illogical, yet in psychiatry the same reasoning seems "logical" — "Да, вот так и выглядит стигматизация области" ("Yes, this is what the stigmatization of the field looks like") [#78].

Argument 2 — the "liver" objection, answered with paracetamol: the usual justification for lowering a dose is "воздействием на печень" ("the effect on the liver"). Paracetamol is one of the drugs that affects the liver most strongly and can cause liver dysfunction when overused. The maximum daily dose for an adult is 4 g; one sachet of Fervex contains 500 mg, so it takes 8 sachets a day to reach the maximum — which does not look frightening to most people. Yet even a couple of sachets of Fervex with paracetamol make the liver work harder than taking an SSRI such as sertraline [#78].

Conclusion and the test: "желание снизить психиатрические препараты является чистой воды результатом стигматизации" ("the desire to reduce psychiatric drugs is purely a result of stigmatization"). The self-test: ask yourself "А если бы это был препарат от какого-то физического недуга, я бы стремился его снизить/отменить/не пить вообще и тп" ("If this were a drug for some physical ailment, would I try to lower it / stop it / not take it at all, or the like?"). If the answer is NO, the urge is stigma [#78].

#### Atomoxetine prescriber memo
A memo made by the Psycho_pharmacologist author from *Stahl's Essential Psychopharmacology. Prescriber's Guide (7th edition)*, published as four image cards [#90, #91, #92, #93].

Drug class: selective norepinephrine (noradrenaline) reuptake inhibitor [#90].

Indications:
1. ADHD in adults and children from 6 years old (FDA-approved) [#90].
2. Treatment-resistant depression [#90].

Mechanism of action:
- Raises the level of the neurotransmitter norepinephrine, and may also increase dopamine in the prefrontal cortex [#90].
- Blocks norepinephrine reuptake pumps (norepinephrine transporters), presumably increasing noradrenergic neurotransmission [#90].
- Because in the frontal cortex dopamine is inactivated by norepinephrine reuptake (dopamine transporters are mostly absent there), atomoxetine can also boost dopamine neurotransmission in that part of the brain [#90].

How long it takes to work:
- The onset of therapeutic effect in ADHD can be seen as early as the first day of taking it [#90].
- The therapeutic effect can keep building for 8–12 weeks [#90].
- If it does not work within 6–8 weeks, it may not work at all [#90].

Goal and duration of treatment: the goal is to reduce symptoms of inattention, motor hyperactivity and/or impulsivity that disrupt social, school and/or occupational functioning. Continue treatment until all symptoms are under control or improvement is stable, then continue indefinitely, periodically reassessing whether treatment is still needed. ADHD treatment started in childhood may need to continue into adolescence and adulthood if clinical benefit is confirmed [#90].

#### Atomoxetine — what to do if it does not work
Five steps from the memo card [#91]:
1. Consider adjusting the dose or switching to another drug [#91].
2. Consider behavioral therapy [#91].
3. Consider whether the patient is not taking the medication as prescribed (adherence problems) [#91].
4. Consider evaluation for another diagnosis or a comorbid condition — for example bipolar disorder, substance misuse, or a somatic illness [#91].
5. Some patients show a clear lack of benefit because they have bipolar disorder. They need additional assessment for bipolar disorder, discontinuation of atomoxetine, and a switch to mood stabilizers [#91].

#### Atomoxetine — partial response or comorbidity
Three scenarios from the same card [#91]:
1. Treatment-resistant depression together with ADHD: try a different monotherapy for ADHD before adding an SSRI, SNRI or mirtazapine. Combine antidepressants with atomoxetine with caution, because theoretically this can activate bipolar disorder and suicidal thoughts [#91].
2. Co-occurring bipolar disorder: consider mood stabilizers or atypical antipsychotics approved by the FDA for bipolar disorder [#91].
3. Partial response in ADHD: atomoxetine can be combined with modafinil and methylphenidate [#91].

#### Atomoxetine — safety profile
Warning: not recommended for patients without a verified ADHD diagnosis [#92].

Monitoring: it may be reasonable to monitor blood pressure and pulse at the start of treatment and while therapeutic doses are being increased [#92].

Why side effects happen: norepinephrine rises not only in the target brain areas but also in the body and at receptors other than those producing the therapeutic effect (for example norepinephrine's action on acetylcholine release). This causes reduced appetite, increased heart rate and blood pressure, dry mouth, urinary retention and similar effects. Side effects usually appear acutely but often pass with time [#92].

Life-threatening or dangerous side effects (5):
1. Rapid heartbeat and hypertension [#92].
2. Orthostatic hypotension [#92].
3. Severe liver damage (rare) [#92].
4. Hypomania and, theoretically, induction of mania [#92].
5. Activation of suicidal thoughts and behavior (rare) [#92].

Abuse potential: atomoxetine does not boost dopamine activity in the brain's limbic areas, which theoretically explains why it has no abuse potential (does not form dependence) [#92].

Do not use (5 contraindications):
1. If the patient is taking an MAOI [#93].
2. If the patient has a current pheochromocytoma or a history of one [#93].
3. If the patient has a severe cardiovascular disease that could worsen with a clinically significant rise in heart rate and blood pressure [#93].
4. If the patient has angle-closure glaucoma [#93].
5. If there is a proven allergy to atomoxetine [#93].

Pregnancy and breastfeeding:
1. No controlled studies in pregnant women have been done; some animal studies showed adverse effects. Use in pregnancy requires weighing potential benefit to the mother against potential risk to the fetus. Women of childbearing age should usually stop atomoxetine before a planned pregnancy [#93].
2. It is unknown whether atomoxetine passes into human breast milk, but all psychotropic substances are assumed to. The recommendation is either to stop the drug or to bottle-feed [#93].

#### Three answers on whether ADHD medication is needed
A subscriber with several diagnoses including ADHD came to the psychiatrist about the other diagnoses; she said her ADHD symptoms do not really bother her and she is fine being who she is. The psychiatrist replied that she is fine with herself but the people around her are not, so medication is needed. She asked whether psychiatry, unlike psychotherapy, touches what doesn't bother the patient, or whether this was unethical. The author calls it a great question that opens several areas at once [#615]:
1. Do we always need medication for ADHD? "Желательно да" ("Preferably yes"). It may not always suit or help, and side effects may outweigh the benefit — then of course the drugs are dropped. "Но всё таки хотя бы раз надо попробовать, потому что ваша жизнь может очень (ОЧЕНЬ) сильно облегчиться" ("But you should still try at least once, because your life can become very (VERY) much easier") [#615].
2. Can we treat one condition and ignore another? In principle yes, but neurodivergence and ADHD affect all areas of life and affect how other disorders run, so leaving ADHD untreated is unlikely to work. The author adds that the doctor's wording ("you're fine, others aren't") was not very correct [#615].
3. Working with doctors: earlier the paternalistic model dominated — the doctor is the expert and the patient "obeys". Now the partnership model is preferred: doctor and patient decide together what to do and what suits or doesn't — though the doctor naturally knows more of the nuances [#615].

#### Treatment order in autism + ADHD
Situation: a subscriber writes that when she starts treating ADHD her autistic symptoms intensify, and when she stops, life overall gets worse; her doctor says pills won't help autistic rigidity, and she has no depression or anxiety [#692].

The author's answer, step by step:
1. Know it is common: in combined autism + ADHD this is a very widespread problem [#692].
2. Treat ADHD first: at present ADHD is considered the complicating factor in this combination, so clinicians first try to "tame" it [#692].
3. Don't rush yourself: when autistic traits intensify during ADHD treatment, observe what exactly is intensifying [#692].
4. Keep the ADHD treatment and live through the period as gently as possible — "не бросая СДВГ" ("without dropping the ADHD [treatment]"). In theory it should eventually get easier [#692].
The author adds that this is "наша борьба всю жизнь" ("our lifelong struggle"), speaking as someone who shares it [#692].

#### Individual antidepressant taper
Situation: a subscriber took antidepressants for 4 years and is coming off them slowly and painfully. Two psychiatrists recommended fast reduction (half a tablet at a time) and she felt withdrawal; she left the second doctor and now splits the tablet into thirds herself, planning quarters, but fears her jewelry scale can't weigh such small amounts. She read that other countries reduce by 10% every 1.5–2 months, which needs liquid forms, and asked whether those exist in Russia. She is also tired of doctors telling her "СДВГ или РАС у вас быть не может, вы просто тревожная" ("you can't have ADHD or autism, you're just anxious") and prescribing anxiety drugs that leave her half-asleep for months [#853].

The author's principles:
1. Difficulty is normal: coming off an antidepressant can be hard for everyone — neurodivergent and neurotypical alike [#853].
2. Pace by individual reaction only: "Мы тут ориентируемся ТОЛЬКО на индивидуальную реакцию. Если вам слишком быстро по 1/2т , то значит вам слишком быстро" ("Here we go ONLY by the individual reaction. If half a tablet at a time is too fast for you, then it is too fast for you") [#853].
3. Choose an individually comfortable reduction step and observe how you respond [#853].
4. Technique for small doses: crush the tablet, buy empty capsules, weigh the powder and fill the capsules with the needed dose [#853].
(!) UNCLEAR: the author does not answer whether liquid forms are available in Russia, nor comment on the 10%-every-1.5–2-months schedule the subscriber read about [#853].

### Detailed insights

#### Should I take pills? Matching medication to symptoms
A subscriber with diagnosed ADHD and (not yet diagnosed but obvious to her) autism was told by her psychologist — a general one without specialization — to try pills. She is afraid, believes her difficulties come from not having rested for several years, and fears pills will burn her out completely [#696]. The author's answer:
- First understand which pills, for what purpose, and for which symptoms [#696].
- There are no pills for autism; for ADHD it is always worth trying, because it can make life much easier [#696].
- If the pills are antidepressants and you have neither depression nor anxiety, there is no indication for them [#696].
- A psychologist cannot make decisions about medication. If the psychologist is effectively sending you to a psychiatrist, that is never superfluous — and a visit to a psychiatrist does not mean you will necessarily be prescribed drugs [#696].
- Burnout is quite possible, and then antidepressants clearly won't help — but first you need to figure out what is actually happening to you [#696].

#### ADHD treatment problems in Russia (forwarded from NO STIGMA)
The forwarded post names two key current problems [#208]:
1. Insufficient qualification and outdated knowledge of doctors: many doctors take in new knowledge about ADHD pharmacotherapy poorly and often diagnose other disorders — bipolar disorder type II and borderline personality disorder — instead of ADHD. This leads to overdiagnosis and wrong treatment, which worsens patients' condition [#208].
2. No available medication: effective ADHD drugs such as bupropion and methylphenidate are not permitted for sale in Russia. As a result many people suffer, lose jobs and relationships, and sometimes even die by suicide because of untreated ADHD. Instead of solving this, the post says, doctors focus on creating new courses and renaming the disorder to profit from a supposed "rethinking" of the diagnosis — "Кринж" ("Cringe") [#208].

The post's position: psychiatry should focus on diagnosis and prescribing, while psychotherapists do therapy; a skew toward psychotherapy at the expense of pharmacotherapy and correct diagnosis makes things worse. Its three conclusions [#208]:
- Doctors should keep their knowledge up to date and diagnose more accurately [#208].
- Effective ADHD drugs should be legalized [#208].
- Each specialist should do their own job: psychiatrists — medication and diagnosis; psychotherapists — psychotherapy [#208].
It claims these measures would substantially improve the quality of life of people with ADHD [#208].

#### Q&A on autism/ADHD diagnosis and support (Part 2 of a Q&A)
The author's answers to subscriber questions [#58]:
- Medication for adult ADHD: yes — atomoxetine preparations and stimulants (stimulants are allowed only in some countries) [#58].
- Working with a psychologist when you have ASD/ADHD: the author states a "достаточно жесткое мнение" ("fairly tough opinion"): (a) psychologists must be good at diagnosis; (b) they must understand ASD/ADHD, because that lets therapy be adapted to your traits. So it is best to look for a CBT psychologist who works with neurodivergence [#58].
- Differentiating complex PTSD + skill deficits from ADHD: a big, complex question needing careful differential diagnosis, especially when they co-occur. Unfortunately the probability of trauma is high in neurodivergent people, and even higher in women [#58].
- Should all clients be screened for ASD and ADHD, even socially adapted ones? Is medication needed in adulthood? For ADHD — no; for ASD it is best done preventively, because masking can be very deceptive about how socially adapted someone is. Medication is needed by people with ADHD [#58].
- Can an autistic person be disorganized, or is that ADHD? Both. Autistic people do like rules and organization, but with executive dysfunction, or if they have no rule about order, there can be disorganization; it can also be an ADHD manifestation [#58].
- Literature for a 21-year-old newly diagnosed with ASD: *Полное руководство по синдрому Аспергера* (The Complete Guide to Asperger's Syndrome), which the author calls the most adequate on the Russian-language market so far [#58].
- How to read books with ADHD: "Вопрос - боль)" ("The question is a pain"). Little by little — plan it and read 1–2 pages at a time without forcing yourself; and when it's interesting, you can read in hyperfocus [#58].
- Is it primarily a social problem (e.g., women face very high demands and are criticized even when they mention their diagnosis)? There is a social part, but it is unlikely to be the main one, since difficulties show up in families and in people without high demands; the modern social context often aggravates the problem [#58].
- Differential diagnosis of ADHD + ASD vs bipolar disorder: you must distinguish depressive and manic phases from periods of hyperactivity / special interests and from exhaustion in neurodivergent people [#58].
- Is difficulty starting or finishing tasks a reason to suspect a diagnosis? You can always get checked — it won't hurt — but it is only one manifestation and may be unrelated to ASD or ADHD; depending on the cause there are different methods of self-help with procrastination [#58].
- Age of diagnosis: ASD can be diagnosed from 18 months (1.5 years), ADHD from 3 years [#58].

#### The author's own experience: the right antidepressant
Asked whether she feels satisfied with her life and how she copes with her neurodivergence, the author answers honestly [#854]:
- All her life she has felt that just living requires constant, unrealistic effort. She finished medical school under an unreal level of stress, with no understanding of her condition at the time [#854].
- Her first psychologist, whom she saw in her 6th year, helped her a lot, especially with anxiety, but could not understand this and told her not to dramatize [#854].
- In the 10 years since, her life has improved greatly and she has learned to feel satisfaction with some aspects of it. "Сильно облегчил жизнь подходящий антидепрессант и отсутствие регулярных депрессий" ("A suitable antidepressant and the absence of regular depressions made life much easier") [#854].
- The next important step was understanding her neurodivergence, accepting her traits and adapting to some of them [#854].
- Life is still hard: work is hard at times, working the way she likes and is used to has been very hard lately, food annoys her, and socializing outside work is extremely difficult. Would she trade her life for another? "Не уверена)" ("Not sure") [#854].
Lesson carried by the story: the right medication and understanding one's neurodivergence improved life substantially but did not remove the difficulty; and a helper who doesn't understand neurodivergence can still help with some things (anxiety) while dismissing the core struggle [#854].

## Choosing specialists and evidence-based mental health care

### What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel teaches about getting into mental health care and getting the *right* care: practical strategies for finding a psychiatrist versus a psychologist, the legal limits and real uses of an online psychiatric "second opinion", the economics behind private psychiatric prices in Moscow, the argument that psychotherapy for psychiatric disorders is medical treatment and must follow clinical guidelines, a comparison of specialists for fear of death and grief (including the death doula), and two warnings about how medicine treats psychiatric symptoms — somatic doctors fearing them, and psychiatrists failing to trust patients' reports. According to the source, this matters because people with psychiatric disorders get sick "and sometimes die" in this field, because the choice of specialist is currently a heavy burden placed on patients, and because people with psychiatric symptoms are "такие же люди, как и все остальные" (the same people as everyone else) [#4, #219, #350].

### Core ideas

- Finding care is hard, and that is reality, not a failure. A psychiatrist is best found through trusted clinics; a psychologist is the hardest choice because several independent conditions must coincide. Expect not to find the right one the first time [#600].
- Assessing professionalism should not fall on the patient. Forwarded author Masha argues the field puts too much responsibility for choosing a specialist on clients; the solution is a more consolidated professional community that maintains quality [#219].
- An online psychiatric consultation is consultative, not medical in Russia: no prescriptions, certificates or diagnosis reports — but it can give a second view of diagnosis and treatment, psychoeducation and a plan [#242].
- Private psychiatry is expensive for structural reasons: long appointments, scarcity of psychiatrists, and the costs of private clinics; state evidence-based psychiatry is improving as an alternative [#575].
- Working with a psychiatric disorder moves psychotherapy from psychology into medicine, where it is a "non-drug method of treatment" and must follow clinical guidelines; this requires a basic education in psychology or medicine [#350].
- For death-related fears and grief there are three kinds of specialists — psychiatrist, psychotherapist and death doula — differing in education, methods and focus [#519].
- Psychiatric symptoms are just symptoms. Somatic medicine's fear of psychiatric patients is unjustified, hallucinations are not an automatic reason for antipsychotics, and patients' reports should be believed and interpreted in context [#4, #767].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Author's algorithm for finding a psychiatrist
Purpose: answering the frequent question "how to find a good psychotherapist/psychiatrist/psychologist among so much choice, preferably not at a huge price". The author admits "я всегда точно так же мучаюсь" (I always struggle in exactly the same way) [#600].
1. Keep a list of clinics you trust. The author has a set of clinics she trusts and books a random specialist there — the clinic's quality control substitutes for evaluating the individual doctor [#600].
2. Or ask colleagues for a specific name. She pesters colleagues to recommend a specific person — but notes this is one of the few privileges of being a doctor, so it is not available to most readers [#600].
3. General rule for patients: "идите в проверенные места" (go to proven places), accepting that it will never be cheap [#600].
4. Why it is worth paying for a good one: a psychiatrist is a relatively one-off specialist — you do not see them every week — so it is better to go to a good one [#600].

#### Four factors that must coincide when choosing a psychologist
The author calls choosing a psychologist "САМЫЙ сложный процесс" (THE most difficult process), because success depends on all of the following at once [#600]:
1. The specialist is good (professionally competent).
2. They work in the right approach (therapeutic modality) for you.
3. They work with your specific problem.
4. They have openings now and you click personally — the personal match needed to establish a therapeutic relationship (availability and personal fit are given together in the source).
Why it takes so long even for professionals: after a consultation, the author often spends a lot of time on recommendations, because even for a patient she has seen and understands, matching a specific specialist and arranging it with them "требует очень много сил и ресурсов" (takes a great deal of effort and resources) [#600].
Practical consequences: searching on your own is always difficult and long; tune in to the fact that you may not find the right person the first time — "Это не баг, это реальность" (This is not a bug, it's reality). Aggregator platforms are "достаточно сложно" (quite problematic), so it is better to go to centers or use friends' recommendations — "Но и это не гарант" (But that is no guarantee either) [#600].

#### Online psychiatric second opinion — what it is and when to take it
Source: post forwarded from the Psycho_pharmacologist channel, written because people kept emailing "какую услугу выбрать" (which service to choose) [#242].

Legal status. In Russia the law on medical activity still states that prescribing drugs, writing prescriptions and providing medical services in general happen at an in-person appointment in a clinic holding a medical license. Therefore any online consultation with a psychiatrist is not a medical service but has a consultative character. No certificates, diagnostic reports or prescriptions are issued; for any documents you must see a doctor in person in a clinic [#242].

What the service can give (six items) [#242]:
1. An additional opinion from another psychiatrist on the diagnosis.
2. An assessment of the current treatment the client is receiving for their psycho-emotional difficulties.
3. Answers to all the questions you have that, for some reason, your treating doctor cannot answer.
4. Psychoeducation and support on the difficult road to recovery.
5. Help building a plan of further treatment steps, including taking your place of residence into account.
6. Information on the most effective psychotherapy approaches for the specific case, plus a list of psychotherapists who work with that problem.

When to take it (four situations) [#242]:
1. Relatives and loved ones of people with psychiatric disorders — e.g., to understand what to do next when the loved one is currently in a hospital or rehabilitation center.
2. You live in another country and want an opinion on your condition in your native language — but you will still have to see a local doctor to get prescriptions.
3. You live in another Russian city and doubt your diagnosis, treatment or your doctor's competence — caveat: you may be asked to come in person to the city where the second-opinion doctor practises, or to take the new information to your local doctor.
4. You do not understand what is happening to you or where to go with your complaints — no idea where to start: psychologist or psychiatrist, whether pills are needed or you can manage without for now, and if a psychologist, which approach.

#### Three reasons private psychiatry is expensive
Context: questions to Karina about "premium" prices at the center and future clinic. The author notes she is both an owner and specialist and also a patient — she sees psychiatrists, has had a therapist weekly for 3 years and has taken antidepressants regularly for about 3 years, "так что знаю эту сферу со всех сторон" (so I know this field from all sides) [#575].
The price problem: private psychiatric visits in Moscow cost 10, 13, 15 thousand rubles and upward, "и хочется всплакнуть" (and you want to cry). When the clinic Чайка raised her price to 13 thousand, she inwardly cried too, because it is hard to accept that people must pay that much for her consultation. For that reason she tries to see everyone within one hour, although many initial psychiatric consultations need 1.5 hours (at МНС she always did 1.5-hour first visits) — 1.5 hours would be "совсем космическая сумма" (a truly astronomical sum) for many people. Other doctors charge 3–5–7 thousand, which makes people think psychiatrists have gone crazy [#575].
1. Time. A psychiatrist's appointment lasts an hour, while many other specialties do initial visits in 20–30 minutes. Recalculated per hour, the other doctor costs the same — they just do not need an hour [#575].
2. Scarcity — supply and demand. There are not many psychiatrists. Their number grows every year, but when the author began practice and entered residency, psychiatry was still quite stigmatized both in society and among doctors, so there was no big competition for places and no large graduating cohorts. Meanwhile many people need help, so psychiatrists are in an advantageous position: departments in Чайка and other multi-specialty clinics try to hire psychiatrists and expand, and practically fight for specialists. "Поэтому вступает в игру рынок, спрос и предложение" (So the market, supply and demand come into play) [#575].
3. Private medicine costs. To reach even minimal break-even rather than working at zero or a loss, clinics — especially non-chain ones — have to set a price of about 10 thousand rubles in Moscow, because rent, taxes, administrators' pay and other expenses are practically not covered at a lower price [#575].
Conclusion of the author: private psychiatry is an expensive pleasure, especially when combined help (psychotherapy + pharmacology) is needed, and it is sad that more vulnerable social groups need such costly treatment programs. The positive side: in recent years not only private but also state evidence-based psychiatry has been developing actively — psychotherapy departments and centers at hospitals are appearing, there are many good young doctors in ПНД (psychoneurological dispensaries), and many new expensive medicines are available there. So there is an alternative; "частная медицина никогда не сможет быть супер бюджетной, доступной и качественной одновременно" (private medicine can never be super-budget, accessible and high-quality at the same time) [#575].

#### Psychiatrist vs psychotherapist vs death doula — whom to see about death and grief
Purpose: in mental health, death comes up often — fear of one's own death, death of loved ones, living through loss, and thoughts of ending one's life. The post compares three specialists who work with this [#519].
1. Psychiatrist — a doctor specializing in diagnosing and treating psychiatric disorders. *Education:* medical — must have a medical diploma and a specialization in psychiatry. *Methods:* can prescribe medication, apply psychotherapy or other medical interventions. *Focus:* treating psychiatric disorders [#519].
2. Psychotherapist — a specialist working with mental and emotional problems through talk therapy. *Education:* may be a psychologist or a doctor, but not necessarily; the main requirement is professional training in a psychotherapeutic approach. *Methods:* talk therapy, self-regulation techniques, awareness, working through trauma. Cannot prescribe drugs but can refer to a psychiatrist when needed. *Focus:* emotional and psychological support, help changing behavior, overcoming crises, searching for meaning [#519].
3. Death doula — a non-clinical specialist who accompanies a person at the end of life and supports loved ones through the dying process, and also supports people in other losses. *Education:* no single standard; doulas train in specialized courses (the source names INELDA, in English, and Death Foundation, in Russian). *Methods:* emotional, spiritual and practical support — helping prepare for a loss, helping with grieving, creating rituals, farewells, helping talk about death and other losses. Can work with one person or the whole family system. Cannot prescribe drugs but can refer to a psychiatrist if necessary. *Focus:* helping people live through loss. A loss is not always someone's death: it is "любое событие, включающее утрату привычного уклада жизни" (any event involving the loss of one's habitual way of life). Grieving is "переход из жизни до потери в жизнь после неё" (the transition from life before the loss to life after it) [#519].

#### Nine types of loss a death doula works with
The full list from the source [#519]:
1. An impending or already-happened death of a loved one.
2. One's own terminal diagnosis and preparation for one's own death.
3. Perinatal losses — miscarriages, stillbirth, infertility.
4. Death of pets.
5. Divorce — loss of family wholeness and of the role of husband/wife.
6. Moving — loss of a habitual way of life.
7. First motherhood — loss of the former free way of life.
8. Loss of faith, of a sense of safety, of meanings.
9. Any other losses — "когда старый мир рухнул, а в новом ещё не ясно, как теперь жить" (when the old world has collapsed and in the new one it is not yet clear how to live now).
The promotional part of the post adds, as description of who the training suits, that a death doula course is aimed at people who want to professionally accompany people and their loved ones at the end of life and in grieving (graduates choose which losses to work with), at specialists already working with end-of-life — psychologists, psychotherapists, psychiatrists, nurses and doctors, palliative and funeral-sector workers — and at people who want to understand their own attitude to death and loss and gain inner stability. Dates and link are dropped as logistics [#519].

#### Two main problems of the psychology field
Trigger: after Natasha's post about recruiting for the center, a comment storm mixed together claims that higher education is unnecessary for a psychologist, treating psychoses with psychoanalysis, and that the evidence-based approach is wrong because evidence base supposedly translates differently. The author names, in her opinion, the two main problems of the field [#350]:
1. Absence of regulation of activity in psychology.
2. Specialists not understanding the difference between relative norm and pathology.
The reasoning: counseling — helping with family problems, existential questions and similar — is one thing, and there many other methods "работают отлично" (work excellently). But once you work with a client who has a psychiatric disorder, you are no longer in the field of psychology but in the field of MEDICINE, where psychotherapy is a non-drug method of treatment — "ЛЕЧЕНИЯ! Чувствуете разницу?" (TREATMENT! Feel the difference?) [#350].
Medicine is strict: every doctor of every specialty has written clinical guidelines based on international research; consequences of deviating from them differ by country, but globally nobody is praised for it. Being part of medicine, psychotherapy likewise must follow clinical guidelines — unfortunately not yet at the legislative level — because research has already been done and written up on what treats the patient/client more effectively according to studies and international experience [#350].
Why it matters: in psychotherapy we work with the fact that people get sick, and sometimes die. Therefore working with eating disorders (РПП), suicidal risk, depression and similar conditions without a clinical basis — without being able to recognize these states and do differential diagnosis — and without following what works most effectively is a very big risk for both client and therapist. Hence the importance of both a basic education in psychology or medicine and following clinical guidelines [#350].
The author pre-empts objections with her credentials: 8 years of medical school, residency in psychiatry, retraining in clinical psychology, and study of psychoanalysis and other approaches. She acknowledges the post may seem harsh compared with her usual ones, but "медицина не то место, где мы можем позволить себе вольности, отвечая за жизнь других людей" (medicine is not the place where we can afford liberties when we are responsible for other people's lives) [#350].

### Detailed insights

#### The burden of choice should be lifted from patients (forwarded post by Masha)
Masha (channel Маша does therapy) took part, with colleague Liza Fedorova, in an episode of the project "Справиться проще" with Natasha and Karina, discussing difficulties in the field: the state of diagnostics, problems in training specialists, pricing of these services. She notes each of these topics could be discussed separately because of its complexity, ambiguity and the huge room for interpreting answers, and that naming problems does not mean there is a quick solution [#219].
Her main point, addressed to people seeking help: too much attention is currently given to the idea that the client is responsible for choosing a specialist. She grants that fit matters — psychotherapy is a special kind of interaction, and even a good specialist (by professional criteria) will not suit everyone. But evaluating professionalism criteria "это большая нагрузка на клиентов" (is a big burden on clients) [#219].
She sees it as important to lift this burden of difficult choice from clients and patients. People with pathological doubts often write to her about how hard choosing a specialist is — a specialist is not always easy to find, and the choice adds an extra aggravating factor. Colleagues have already done a lot to ease this choice: associations are being created and good clinics with quality control are opening. But in her view the real solution is a more consolidated professional community whose task is to create an environment where specialists can maintain the quality of their services, develop together and develop the field, in order to help clients and patients [#219].
As illustration of this conviction she describes her own path: she noticed an interest in therapists' professional development and training and recognized her core value of belonging and tribe (with a nod to RO DBT); she developed an educational project at the МНС clinic, where a community of colleagues was "главным бустом моего развития" (the main boost of my development) as a specialist; after leaving that clinic she and Liza began the professional community project "ПРАКТИК" for specialists' development and support. (The invitation to follow the project is dropped as promo.) [#219]

#### Somatic medicine's fear of psychiatric symptoms
At a case-based medical conference (covering medicine, ethics and management, with interactive elements), the author was angered by a case where a patient had hallucinations and the doctors wanted to immediately treat them with antipsychotics or even hospitalize them in a psychiatric inpatient unit. Her view: "огромная часть врачей боится психиатрической симптоматике [sic] и наших клиентов как огня" (a huge share of doctors fear psychiatric symptoms and our clients like fire) — a big problem of somatic medicine [#4].
Concrete examples she and colleagues regularly meet in the clinic: people with depression, bipolar disorder (БАР) and other disorders are refused surgery or a caesarean section without certificates (справки) from a psychiatrist; once a certificate was even requested for laser hair removal [#4].
Her reasoning: the prevalence of psychiatric disorders is huge, and most people do not even suspect they have them because they do not seek help — and they go to somatic doctors just the same. "Это такие же люди, как и все остальные. И галлюцинации - не повод сразу давать нейролептик" (These are the same people as everyone else. And hallucinations are not a reason to immediately give an antipsychotic.) She draws no further conclusion than that psychiatric symptoms are just symptoms, people with them are just people, and we should treat each other as people who are simply having a hard time, as with other disorders [#4].
A side note from the same post: the author says she generally dislikes conferences because her concentration lasts only part of a talk, but a case-based, interactive format kept her engaged [#4].

#### Trust patients and take a context-aware history — the "pink dog" story
On 10 October the author told her favorite story from residency: a patient in a psychiatric hospital complained that he saw a pink dog. He was treated this way and that, the antipsychotic dose was increased, but the dog remained — until a doctor looked out of the window and saw a dog covered in pink paint, because a nearby building was being painted. The moral, in her words: "верьте пациентам и внимательно собирайте анамнез, с учётом контекста" (believe patients and collect the history carefully, taking context into account) [#767].

## Health policy and healthcare systems

### What this covers

In autumn 2024 the author, a practicing psychiatrist, took a course on health policy at MSU (МГУ) and posted what she learned in the lectures. The posts cover: whether health can be measured; the history of insurance medicine in Russia and why infectious epidemics sank it; the link between public drug spending, healthcare budgets and life expectancy, with a lecture slide; and two later posts that apply health-system thinking to current debates. One debate is whether letting GPs prescribe antidepressants would make psychiatrists cheaper. The other is the "mentoring" law for medical graduates. She notes that parts of the course make her sad ("местами на меня навевает грусть это всё" — "in places all this makes me sad") and that she finds it "Очень конечно все интересно, но запутанно" ("all very interesting, of course, but confusing"). For her, the topic matters because it shapes how psychiatric care is measured, financed, priced and staffed. [#336, #342, #343, #349, #355, #578, #769]

### Core ideas

- Health cannot be measured directly. Everyone tries to count health indicators, yet "health" has several competing models and cannot be computed, so it is unclear what world medicine is actually measuring. [#336]
- Russians have a right to health protection, not to health. This is one of many contradictions in health policy. [#336]
- Infections killed early insurance medicine in Russia. Insurance coverage reached people who were already getting care and missed the poor, where epidemics start. Russia therefore used the state Semashko model until the 1990s. [#342, #343]
- Public drug and health spending tracks outcomes. Countries that supply more outpatient medicines at public expense have lower mortality from basic diseases and higher life expectancy. Russia (with Bulgaria) spent the least on such drugs. [#349, #355]
- Market logic in psychiatry depends on the health-system model. In centralized Russia, letting GPs prescribe antidepressants would not lower psychiatrists' prices the way the market does in the decentralized US. Russian GPs already prescribe them anyway. [#578]
- The mentoring law will probably make the workforce crisis worse. The author expects fewer medical applicants, fewer specialists and no better primary care. [#769]

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Three types of healthcare system
The author gives this classification as background for why market competition sets medical prices in the US but not in Russia. Historically, healthcare systems have fallen into three types: [#578]
1. Centralized (центральная). Examples: Russia, France and others. The state covers and organizes most of the population's healthcare needs. As a result, market competition has relatively little effect on how medical services are priced. [#578]
2. Decentralized (децентрализированная). Examples: the US, Germany and others. The private sector covers and organizes most needs. Market and competition therefore drive pricing more than in Russia. For the same reason, in the US "без страховки, ты платишь безумные деньги, т.к. государство практически не покрывает стоимость" ("without insurance you pay insane money, because the state covers practically none of the cost"). [#578]
3. Mixed (смешанная). Named as the third type. (!) INCOMPLETE: the source does not describe how the mixed type works and gives no examples. [#578]

#### Three nuances against "let GPs prescribe antidepressants so the market lowers psychiatrists' prices"
The proposal. In a comment-thread discussion, someone put forward this idea: "если антидепрессанты начнут назначать врачи терапевты, то поток к психиатрам снизится и они начнут снижать цены. Условно рынок порешает, как в США" ("if GP physicians start prescribing antidepressants, the flow of patients to psychiatrists will fall and they will start lowering prices. Roughly, the market will sort it out, as in the USA"). The author grants that at first glance the idea seems very sensible, especially from a commercial and competitive point of view. "Но это медицина и тут есть нюансы" ("But this is medicine, and there are nuances here"). [#578]
1. Nuance 1 — this already happens in Russia. In Russia you can already go to a GP and ask them to extend your prescription if you can't get to a psychiatrist in time or can't get an appointment at all. GPs also prescribe antidepressants and anti-anxiety drugs themselves. They do this especially when a patient is very ("ОЧЕНЬ") afraid of going to a psychiatrist and the GP can see that the patient really needs treatment. Her conclusion: "Так что так можно" ("So that is possible"). The proposed change would therefore not create a new channel of access. [#578]
2. Nuance 2 — a GP lacks psychiatric expertise. A GP deals mainly with other kinds of disease and physically cannot know the nuances of psychiatric disorders or do good differential diagnosis of them. In her words, a doctor's value is not in being "машиной для выписки рецепта" ("a prescription-writing machine"). It is in understanding what is going on and choosing treatment methods to match, taking the person's individual characteristics into account. [#578]
3. Nuance 3 — Russia and the US have different health-system models (the one she finds most interesting). Russia is centralized and the US is decentralized (see *Three types of healthcare system*). That is why market competition shapes medical prices much more in the US than in Russia. Importing the US logic ("the market will sort it out") ignores this difference in structure. [#578]

The author ends with "Такие ~~пирожки~~ круассаны🥐 Что думаете?" — a joke, roughly "Such are the ~~pies~~ croissants — what do you think?". It invites discussion and draws no further conclusion. [#578]

#### Three expected consequences of the medical-graduate "mentoring" law
The law, as the author describes it. The new law on "mentoring" (наставничество) for graduates of medical universities works like this. After finishing medical school and before getting accreditation, the graduate — whom she mockingly calls "личинку врача" ("a doctor larva") — is sent to compulsory work ("на отработку") somewhere. Only after that can they take accreditation and then enter residency (ординатура) or go to work in an outpatient clinic (поликлиника). [#769]

Her view of the law itself. She feels very sorry for young specialists. She thinks they will most likely not work as doctors and build medical experience. Instead, in reality they will mostly stand in for nurses and feldshers (paramedics). What frightens her more than the bill is how people outside medicine react to it: they rejoice and even gloat, "как будто это решение решит какие-то их проблемы" ("as if this decision will solve some of their problems"). [#769]

The next round of problems she expects: [#769]
1. Fewer medical students, and so fewer doctors. Probably fewer people will choose to enter medical universities in the first place, which means even fewer doctors in the end. [#769]
2. An even bigger shortage of narrow specialists. Specialists are already in short supply, and there will be fewer still. Not everyone will get the chance to complete residency, and the age at which people finish residency will rise sharply. [#769]
3. No better primary care. The quality of primary (therapeutic/GP-level) care will not improve, "особенно в разрезе принудительности" ("especially given its compulsory nature"). [#769]

Bottom line. She concludes that medicine most likely won't get easier in the coming years, "т.к. в целом подход к переработкам и дефициту кадров не меняется" ("because, overall, the approach to overwork and staff shortages isn't changing"). [#769]

### Detailed insights

#### Measuring health: what are we actually counting?
The author's main takeaway from a lecture: everyone tries to calculate health indicators, but nobody can say what health is. There are completely different models of health, and the indicator itself cannot be computed. So what are "we" — meaning world medicine — counting? The lecture left it unclear which exact indicator would be a good measure. [#336]

A related contradiction: Russians have a social right to the protection of health ("право на охрану здоровья"), but not to health itself. She says there are many such contradictions in health policy. [#336]

She applies this to her own field. What would health indicators in psychiatry even be? How could that factor be fully understood, and how might it affect the way care is delivered? She says plainly that she has no big conclusion, "пока во всяком случае" ("at least for now"). She is sharing the questions because they interest her. [#336]

#### History of insurance medicine in Russia and why infections sank it
The sequence, according to the lecture. Insurance medicine was first attempted in Russia by the Bolsheviks. The author is unsure whether to call the state the Russian Empire or the USSR at that point ("Рос.Империи? СССР?"). The attempt failed for two reasons: it broke down on infectious diseases, and it could not make medicine accessible. Russia then moved to the state Semashko model and kept it until the 1990s. [#342]

Before insurance. Zemstvo (земское) healthcare was for a time replaced by factory healthcare, in which factories and plants paid for their own workers' treatment. Insurance medicine was then tried as the next step. [#343]

Infections were the dominant problem. Epidemics and outbreaks — typhus, tuberculosis and cholera, "я уже не говорю про сифилис и остальные типа гриппа" ("not to mention syphilis and others like flu") — were the scourge of the 19th and early 20th centuries. The First World War, the growth of cities and other factors of the time made outbreaks harder to bring under control. Infections were therefore one of the global problems of healthcare. [#343]

Why insurance failed. When Russia tried to move from factory healthcare to insurance medicine, a problem appeared. People who could pay insurance contributions were already getting fairly good treatment. People who could not pay ended up with even less access to medical help. [#343]

The epidemiological mechanism. Infectious outbreaks usually start in poor, crowded districts. People there eat poorly and work hard, so their immunity is low. From there, infections often spread to all layers of society. An insurance model that left out exactly these people could not contain epidemics. [#343]

Conclusion. In the author's summary, infections kept insurance medicine from developing in Russia for almost another century, until 1990. [#343]

She adds jokingly that her nerdiness about history ("душность с историей") and her passion for infectious diseases had finally come in useful. [#343]

#### Public spending on outpatient drugs, mortality and life expectancy
Statistic from the lecture. The more a country spends on medicines for outpatient treatment — drugs the state hands out to citizens, as opposed to drugs people buy for themselves — the lower its mortality from basic disorders (cardiovascular and similar) and the higher its life expectancy. [#349]

Russia's position. On what she calls a sad chart, Russia ("мы") and Bulgaria spent the least of all countries on such drugs. [#349]
