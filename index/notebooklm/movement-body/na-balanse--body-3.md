## Eating disorder recovery, weight genetics and body acceptance

### What this covers

Three long first-person posts by health coach Katya Strelnikova: the story of how she lived with an eating disorder (ED, РПП) for about ten years without recognizing it, the free EAT-26 screening questionnaire she recommends, her explanation of why she "will never be thin" (genetics, metabolic adaptation after years of dieting, and post-ED syndrome), and how a genetic test she took in 2018 turned from a painful verdict into a practical map for eating, movement and sleep. According to the source, this matters because an ED can look like "normal life" for years — recognizing that you need help is the main step — and because chasing thinness against one's biology causes lifelong suffering, whereas energy, strength, sleep and calm are more reliable goals [#683, #717, #741].

### Core ideas

- An ED can be invisible because it looks like the norm. Katya did not know she had an ED for about ten years, because diets were culturally normal, everyone around her lived this way, and she was ashamed to admit something was wrong. The key step is realizing you need help; an ED is treatable [#683].
- An ED resembles an addiction. For some people a crash diet is a one-off; for others it is the start of decades of struggle, with both biological and psychological predispositions (see *Stages of Katya's eating disorder*) [#683].
- Thinness is genetically easy for only a few. Only about 1.9% of people are constitutionally thin, yet their build was made the standard everyone else must meet — by starving or by Ozempic [#717].
- A dieting history makes the body resist weight loss. Metabolic adaptation, hormonal shifts (more ghrelin, less leptin) and fat-cell epigenetic memory pull weight back; this is not a "broken metabolism", but it is objectively harder [#717].
- Post-ED syndrome changes the head, not only the body. Echoes of restriction (anxiety around "forbidden" food, eating fast, endless inner negotiations) remain long after recovery [#717].
- Stop making thinness the goal. Better anchors are energy, strength, sleep, calm, resilience and satisfaction with oneself and life [#717].
- A genetic test is a map, not a verdict. Seeing reality honestly — "my body is not broken, it is just like this" — allowed Katya to stop fighting her body and "negotiate" with it through specific habits [#741].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Stages of Katya's eating disorder
Katya's narrative is a sequence of phases that readers can use to recognize their own pattern [#683].

1. Cultural soil and onset. Her first diet was done together with her mother; in the 2000s this was normal. Magazines wrote only about losing weight for summer; in cartoons non-thin girls were at best the ugly sisters and at worst witches. At university a friend she went to the gym with shared a diet: one week eat whatever you want, one week only apples and kefir — and this seemed perfectly fine. The words "eating disorder" simply did not exist in her world [#683].
2. Secrecy and self-blame. She told no one how she felt and was sure she was the only one failing: everyone else copes, so she must try harder and restrict even more [#683].
3. Predispositions. She describes an ED as close to addiction and notes she had "a whole bouquet of addictions". Biological predisposition: her father and his parents were alcoholics. Psychological predisposition: she always felt herself to be «недо» ("not enough", literally "under-") [#683].
4. The hook — control. Her first diet was Dukan; she still remembers the taste of unsalted chicken breast. Watching the weight drop she felt something new: control — "at least something in my life I control" — and wanted more, and then more [#683].
5. Escalation (acute phase). When weight stopped falling as fast as she wanted, she began shrinking portions, refusing food and "working it off": running in circles around her room for an hour (on the 6th floor — "poor neighbours"), compulsive workouts, fasting days, fasts; at the peak, 6 nuts a day. She was angry all the time (later apologized to all her friends) yet sure this was how it should be [#683].
6. Physical warning signs. Hair started falling out and her periods stopped. A boy she liked met her by chance, did not recognize her and asked whether she was ill. Her mother was frantic but thought Katya was just "messing around"; Katya herself did not understand what was happening. This lasted about six months [#683].
7. Rebound, not recovery. She exited the acute phase on her own, but stresses that "stopped" is the wrong word — nothing ended. First she blew up and began to binge; she writes the word in capitals, «ЖРАТЬ» (to devour), saying she cannot call it anything else, then cried and asked her mother to put a lock on the fridge, and quickly regained the weight plus about 10 kg more [#683].
8. Hidden phase (10 years). Endless weight swings; skipping breakfast because she overate at dinner; demonizing foods; detoxes; photoshopping and "tuning" herself in every photo; drowning self-hatred in alcohol "and not only". All of it, too, felt normal: "everyone lives like this" [#683].
9. Recognition trigger. Her husband helped the second time: she finally told him what she thought of herself, and he answered: "Do you really think that about yourself? I think you need help." For the first time she considered that maybe not everyone lives like this. Around then an interview with Yulia Lapina came out on Wonderzine (she says "I think"), she bought Lapina's book, and her path to healing began [#683].
10. Lesson. She concludes she could have given herself many more years of free life had she known earlier; the main thing is to understand that you need help, and a good way to do that is to take the EAT-26 questionnaire today. Her own current score is 0 out of 78, which she reports "with pride and surprise" [#683].

#### Three reasons why I will never be thin
Purpose: to explain why some people find it easier to be thin than others, and to argue against making thinness the goal. Katya frames it against two internet camps — one says getting thin is simple ("you just need to…"), the other says thin equals sick ("abs are not about health") — and says the truth is "not so clear-cut" [#717].

1. Genetics. Genes determine a great deal — where fat is stored, how easily muscle grows, how the body handles glucose. For some it is genetically easier to stay lean and muscular, for others to be plump and soft: "this is not an excuse, it is physiology". Her genetic test showed four factors:
   - *Late satiety.* The satiety signal comes with a delay: when another person has already put down the fork, her brain has not received the message yet. She always compared herself to the man with a black hole in his stomach from the film "Route 60"; the test explained a lot.
   - *Saturated fats are stored rather than used.* Her cells respond to fatty acids so that saturated fat from food is preferentially stored in adipose tissue rather than burned for energy.
   - *"Thrifty" metabolism.* Her body is genetically set to conserve resources — an evolutionary advantage under scarcity and a direct difficulty today, when food is plentiful and moving is not really necessary.
   - *Estrogen receptors work at half strength.* Estrogen regulates where the body stores fat and how active metabolism is; her receptors for it are genetically less sensitive.
   Each factor alone is small; together they fully explain why maintaining thinness requires colossal effort. Her conclusion: this is biology to be taken into account, not fought blindly. Her test also lists a "predisposition to increased BMI and obesity", and she calls herself a large person "with wide bones" [#717].
2. The dieter's metabolism («метаболизм „худельщицы“»). If you have done restrictive diets, fasted or otherwise "tormented yourself", your body now remembers it.
   - *Metabolic adaptation:* during weight loss metabolism drops more than the lost kilograms would predict. The best-known example she cites is The Biggest Loser contestants: their metabolism fell by about 500 kcal/day below the expected level, and for many the adaptation persisted for years.
   - *Hormones are the main mechanism pulling weight back:* after weight loss ghrelin (hunger hormone) rises and leptin (satiety hormone) falls, so the body literally wants more food than it needs and lives in constant hunger. Add genetically delayed satiety and it becomes an "explosive mix".
   - *Fat-cell memory:* she cites fresh data that fat cells keep an epigenetic "memory" and return to fat storage faster after weight loss.
   - *Caveat she adds "so as not to scare":* this does not mean metabolism is "broken forever"; it means the body of someone with a dieting history objectively resists weight loss more than the body of someone who never dieted — "it really is harder for us" [#717].
3. Post-ED syndrome. After restrictive eating not only the body but the head changes, and she calls this perhaps the hardest part. Although her ED is long past, "echoes of war" still slip through: sudden anxiety about "forbidden" food with related breakdowns and overeating; a habit of eating fast as if the food will be taken away; endless negotiations in the head. A person without this experience simply eats when hungry and stops when full; people with it must rebuild what should be innate. This does not make them broken, but it makes the road to calm relations with food longer (possibly lifelong), and that is worth acknowledging [#717].

Other factors she acknowledges but does not develop: hormonal background, age, sleep, stress level, family weight history — "but these three are the main ones for me" [#717].

#### Reliable anchors instead of thinness
Katya writes that she lists the reasons not to justify why she has not lost weight, but to ask whether it is time to stop making thinness the goal and the motivation for self-care. Her list of "far more reliable supports": (1) energy, (2) strength, (3) sleep, (4) calm, (5) resilience (устойчивость), (6) satisfaction with oneself and life. These are what actually make life better — unlike minus five kilograms for which you would have to suffer for the rest of your life [#717].

#### Gene-informed eating adjustments (problem → habit mapping)
Purpose: turn each genetic tendency into a concrete counter-habit — "negotiating" with the body rather than fighting it. Katya's mapping [#741]:

1. Poor satiety (leptin and fullness signals reach the brain badly) → a salad at every meal (she learned to batch-prepare), plus "normal food" with protein and complex carbohydrates.
2. Cannot stop once she starts eating sweets → eat sweets only after a meal, never on an empty stomach, where they trigger a chain reaction.
3. Tendency to snack often → eat regularly and filling meals.
She closes the list with an open-ended «И т.д.» — (!) INCOMPLETE: further mappings are not given.

Beyond eating, she introduced home workouts, runs and "those 14,000 steps in any weather"; to make that possible she had to learn to sleep and give up other harmful habits. It took her "only some 8–10 years" (said jokingly) [#741].

### Detailed insights

#### Katya's genetic profile as she describes it
In #741 she summarizes the 2018 test (taken at Genotek): "in terms of thinness my genes are against me". Items: a predisposition to increased BMI; leptin and satiety that poorly tell the brain she is full; a craving for sweets, a tendency to snack often and to gain weight from fatty food; high estradiol, which slows muscle gain; and two copies each of the two main "obesity genes", MC4R and FTO. One of the test pages photographed in #717 (Metabolic diseases section) shows obesity risk at 20% against an 18% average risk, defining obesity as abnormal and excessive fat deposits that may harm health [#741] [#717].

#### Body composition vs BMI
A bioimpedance analysis a couple of weeks before #717 showed her BMI above norm, but the report said that "in your case BMI does not reflect the real picture": skeletal muscle mass was above normal, while fat and visceral fat were normal. The photographed report reads (paraphrased from the image): high muscle mass — to identify obesity look at body-fat percentage; if there is excess weight or obesity, consult an endocrinologist/dietitian to normalize body mass while preserving muscle. Her lesson: she constantly has to remind herself she is fine; what is not fine is diet mentality, which says that if she is not thin she is defective — "that is exactly why I was treated for an ED for 20 years" [#717].

#### The cultural thin standard
Katya argues it is strange that the build of the roughly 1.9% of constitutionally thin people was made the standard for everyone else, who must literally starve or inject Ozempic to match; she says the whole capitalist consumer culture — "and patriarchy too" — is built on this. She does not elaborate and points instead to Naomi Wolf and the Nagoski sisters (see Tools). She herself will not be thin "unless I start injecting Ozempic — I won't" [#717].

#### From painful verdict to luck: reading the genetic test
Reading "kiddo, you're destined to be fat" (her paraphrase of the test) was unpleasant, even painful, in 2018, when her self-acceptance was rudimentary and she had lived over ten years with an ED and fantasies of being thinner, lighter, slighter in order to finally like herself, be happy, succeed. Every diet was a promise: "a bit more and I'll become *that* girl". The test said: no, never. It also explained that it was no accident she could not stop eating sweets, was always hungry and never full, and that weight returned after every diet — "this is not weakness, not lack of discipline, not lack of willpower", for which she had hated herself for years; "it's just me". This was not what she wanted to read, but it let her look at herself honestly for the first time. She tells clients that to change you first need to see and admit reality. Conclusion: the body is not broken, war with it is pointless, you can only negotiate with it [#741].

#### Murakami's reframe: a tendency to gain weight as a gift
In "What I Talk About When I Talk About Running", Haruki Murakami writes that he gains weight easily while his wife eats what she wants, does no sport and does not gain. At first this seemed unfair; then he calls the tendency to plumpness a gift of fate: those who gain easily must think about movement and nutrition early, while those who "get away with everything" have no reason to, and with age their bodies start to fail them. He says much of what he can do as a writer — endurance, patience, keeping the distance — he learned from daily running; the tendency to gain led him to running, and running helped make him the Murakami we know. This motivated Katya: "since I won't be thin, I wonder what I can be if I sort out eating and movement" — and she did [#741].

#### Outcome and side benefits
She did not become Kate Moss but became a version of herself she sincerely admires: slim, sporty, active, with no complaints about her body, health, energy or lifestyle, and she completely stopped wanting to be someone else. Bonus: movement, normal food and sleep also lower her other risks — hypertension (which her grandmother and mother have), depression and mood swings, and vascular problems [#741].

#### How to use a genetic test
Her advice when recommending such a test: read the results as a map, not a verdict, and be sure to consult a doctor [#741].
