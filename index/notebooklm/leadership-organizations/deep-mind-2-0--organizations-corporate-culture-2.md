## Corporate culture change

### What this covers

This part collects what the deep mind channel (the author runs "deep mind consulting", an organizational-consulting practice working on employee stress, communication and state) taught in 2019–2020 about how companies change their culture: why there are no ready-made transformation recipes, Robert Kegan's "organizational grooves", a typology of companies by attitude to culture work, the five key problems of companies, evidence on leader stress and mindfulness, a first approach to state management, strategic questions under hyper-competition, human-centred management lessons from Diana Kodoeva, the reasons behind the D&I-culture "hype", and the Andersons' Conscious Change Leadership. According to the author, a large restructuring of corporate cultures is under way: companies that see it early capture the best talent and future leadership positions, while those that ignore it fall behind [#75, #13].

This part covers five posts: a full Russian translation of Dean Anderson and Linda Ackerman Anderson's paper on moving beyond change management to Conscious Change Leadership (shared as a PDF), a post on Deep mind's corporate retreats, a report from a culture workshop in a top IT company with the team's flip-chart "vector" from what they dislike to what they want, a starter reading list on modern corporate cultures, and the author's argument that corporate culture "will not build itself" in large companies. According to the source, culture is the collective expression of individual mindsets, it changes only through discomfort and through personal (inner) change of leaders and staff, and in large companies it has become the main field of top-management effort because culture — not strategy or structure — is why people burn out and leave. [#287, msg #416, #476]

This part covers deep mind's posts from December 2021 to June 2025 on how companies adapt and change their culture: what it takes to run corporate retreats aimed at culture change, how an employee can find a company that genuinely cares about people (criteria and a reader-sourced list), a 5-stage model of how companies of different maturity care for employees, how prolonged collective stress tests a culture, how body movement exposes a company's culture and power relations, and the concept of "visionary procrastination" in change initiatives. According to the source, the task companies face now is "адаптировать свою культуру к новой реальности" (to adapt their culture to the new reality), and companies that invest in wellbeing, communication and culture are "магнит для самых сочных кадров" (a magnet for the juiciest talent) [#542, #562].

### Core ideas

- Business is becoming more conscious, ethical and horizontal — and therefore far more effective. The author calls this a "new wave" and a genuinely new business paradigm; see *No ready-made solutions* for why it cannot be bought off the shelf [#13].
- Lasting change comes from regular collective practices ("grooves"), not one-off decisions. Results come from daily actions; habits are strong, so the organization needs regular practices that rewire them — co-created with employees (see *Organizational grooves*) [#41].
- Companies differ sharply in how they treat culture work — from dismissive "lagging" companies to "biohackers" who measure stress and invest in culture (see *Three types of companies*) [#75].
- Fear and distrust are expensive. Hiding mistakes, constant distrust and control cycles drain organizational energy and kill initiative (see *Five key problems* and *Diana Kodoeva's principles*) [#178, #190].
- Culture, not process, is where ethics, engagement and adaptability live — which is why companies rush toward D&I culture (see *Three reasons companies build D&I culture*) [#237].
- Classic project/change management and best practices fail in today's fast, unpredictable transformations; leaders need flexibility, awareness and integrality (see *Conscious Change Leadership*) [#286].
- Adaptation to the new economy requires a new inner capacity — seeing one's own thinking models and behaviour patterns and consciously changing them [#133].

- Transformation is a different kind of change. The Andersons distinguish three types of change; project and change management support only "development" and "transition", while transformation — where the future is undefined and a new mindset is required — needs Conscious Change Leadership. [#287]
- Mindset is the cause of everything. In the paper's thesis, leaders' mindset determines what dynamics they see, how they interpret them, their leadership style, strategy and results; so transformation must start with leaders' own mindset. [#287]
- Culture is the organization's mindset. Culture is the collective manifestation of each individual's mindset; if culture does not fit the transformation, in nine cases out of ten change slows or fails. [#287]
- Culture changes through discomfort. Because culture is "the water we swim in", rebuilding the habitual is unpleasant; people feel relief and discomfort at once. [#416]
- Deep mind's approach moves from teaching skills to transforming culture. Intensive formats such as retreats work through attention to people's state, modelling of team role models and almost invisible practice rather than lecture-style knowledge transfer. [#332]
- Above ~200 people culture must be built deliberately. Small companies integrate through personal contact; large ones through goals, values and communication skills (or, on average, process administration). Culture is "the new super battlefield of managers". [#476]

- Culture change needs a rare combination of skills. Changing a corporate culture (not just running a meditation or stress-reduction training) requires simultaneous top-level mindfulness/facilitation expertise and first-hand knowledge of top-level corporate leadership; the author has found almost no one in Russia who has both (see *Two lenses*) [#542].
- Talent leaves companies that do not care. The Great Resignation in the US is driven by burnout and the search for caring culture, and the same trends are visible in Russia; bosses who do not see the value of culture lose their best people (see *Why culture matters for talent*) [#562].
- Real care can be recognised by three criteria concerning the founders/CEO, the HR function and team-interaction work (see *Three criteria*) [#562].
- Companies develop through 5 stages of employee care, from organisations outside the modern context to ones where the internal product dominates the external one [#676].
- Suppressed feelings do not disappear — under prolonged stress they return later as addiction, depression and outbursts of anger, so a culture must be able to pass a "stress test" [#658].
- The body reveals the culture. How employees move during a short movement session diagnoses the culture and power relations faster than multi-page surveys [#754].
- Good intentions stall. Visionary procrastination — sharing values but postponing action at obstacles — applies to any change in companies [#1412].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Organizational grooves (Robert Kegan)
Purpose: answers the question "how to achieve sustainable change in an organization". The author's reasoning: our everyday actions determine the results we get; to improve results we have to do something differently every day — but the power of habit is very great. Harvard professor Robert Kegan (whom the author calls "one of the best minds of our time") introduced the concept of the "организационная колея" (organizational groove/rut) [#41].

Definition: "Колея - это набор практик в компании" (A groove is a set of practices in the company): regular actions at the level of the whole organization that, day after day, lead to a change in habits and the development of company culture [#41].

Examples of grooves (all six from the source):
1. Open-dialogue practices in the team — regular formats where team members speak openly [#41].
2. Minutes of silence before every meeting, for attunement — a short pause so participants tune in before working together [#41].
3. Joint meditations — meditating together as a team/organization [#41].
4. Regular checking of decisions against the company's purpose and values — making alignment with purpose a recurring step rather than an occasional reflection [#41].
5. Business processes that encourage openness and working with shortcomings and mistakes — for example, a mistakes log, or making all meetings accessible to the whole staff [#41].
6. Regular co-creation of a shared vision and goals — vision and goals are built together repeatedly, not handed down once [#41].

Key rule / pitfall: it is important that the groove is created together with employees, and not simply "спущена сверху начальством или консультантами" (handed down from above by bosses or consultants) [#41].

#### Three types of companies by attitude to culture work
Origin and purpose: the author's classification of all companies his team talked to while selling organizational-consulting services during 2019. It frames who is ready for culture work. The opening thesis: "Зарабатывает тот, кто успевает действовать раньше остальных" (The one who earns is the one who manages to act before others) — not the only way to make money, but a fundamental one. A big restructuring of corporate cultures is under way; while many do not see it at all, some are already taking the best people on the market. Each type is illustrated with a typical client response and a medical metaphor [#75].

1. Lagging and — the author presumes — dying companies. Typical reaction: "Вы серьёзно пытаетесь нам продать работу со стрессом, коммуникацией и состоянием сотрудников? Хахахаха, идите ищите дураков дальше." (Are you seriously trying to sell us work with stress, communication and employee state? Hahaha, go look for other fools.) Metaphor: the patient coughs blood but is used to it and does not understand it is time to change lifestyle. "Некоторые отстали так надолго, что думают будто идут первыми." (Some have lagged for so long that they think they are in the lead.) [#75]
2. "Normal" companies. Typical reaction: they understand they cannot fix some problems for years despite having reworked all business processes; they ask whether it is possible to work with people directly; they train employees and are ready to invest in culture, "Но пока не понимаем, как делать это системно" (But we don't yet understand how to do it systematically). Metaphor: such patients may suddenly fall ill and die — they exercise periodically but never go for check-ups until something hurts badly [#75].
3. "Biohackers". Their self-description: they long ago realized the importance — perhaps even the priority — of the internal product over the external one; how their people feel and interact fully determines market results. Their practices: constantly improving corporate culture; measuring employee satisfaction and stress levels; having internal facilitators and coaches; not punishing mistakes; developing honesty, transparency and direct dialogue; encouraging psychotherapy, coaching and meditation for employees; having a purpose genuinely higher than profit alone and constantly checking actions against it and the team's values. They say: everything is already good, and that is exactly why we recognize the importance of continuous work with culture, communication and team state — do an outside audit and tell us what to improve. The author: these are the savviest; they already profit from their speed and are building future leadership positions [#75].
   - Pitfall of biohackers: they often think they are already smarter than everyone. The author's warning: reading a couple of books on the brain and meditation, doing psychotherapy and going on a retreat does not mean deeply understanding the processes of human and relationship development — but while there is a lot of ignorance in the field, "кажется, что вы уже бог" (it seems you are already a god) [#75].

Author's practical conclusion: his clients are types 2 and 3. Type 1 can also become clients, but mostly they talk from a "prove it" position, and the author says he is increasingly tired of proving and wants to stop doing so entirely [#75].

#### Five key problems of companies (deep mind consulting card)
A diagnostic list from a deep mind consulting slide [#178]:
1. Companies are built around external goals and processes; the internal dimension is almost completely ignored — people's inner state and relations are left out of how the company is designed [#178].
2. Employees do a "second job" — hiding mistakes and personal imperfections — effort goes into concealment rather than the work [#178].
3. The organization loses energy from constant distrust and the need to hide shortcomings [#178].
4. Poor-quality decisions because of disintegration ("дезинтегрированности") — fragmentation leads to bad decisions [#178].
5. Most people are busy building personal safety and increasing their own resources and status — rather than serving the common goal [#178].

#### First approach to state management (I / We matrix)
A deep mind consulting card titled "Первый подход к управлению состоянием" (A first approach to state management), organized in two rows — "Я" (I) and "МЫ" (We) — with two actions each [#181]:

#### Diana Kodoeva's human-centred management principles
Context: the author's dialogue with Diana Kodoeva, ex-top manager of Gett and author of the book "Gett — сервис со смыслом" (Gett — a service with meaning). She introduced a human-centred approach at Gett and got outstanding results in the customer-support service. Headline: "Страх обходится компании слишком дорого" (Fear costs the company too much). The author shares the key points of their conversation about modern management [#190]:

1. Change begins with understanding that most processes in a company are people. If you solve the people question, everything starts working. People determine the success of a business; good management is first of all management of people [#190].
2. Meaningful, human-centred organizations can show super-results. People are motivated by living goals, trust and basic freedom. Active, capable people want to do something interesting, be heard and be able to take initiative. The old management paradigm has big problems with this because there "человек-это функция" (a person is a function): he must do his job, not talk. Such management is like an elephant in a china shop when it comes to feelings and meaning in work [#190].
3. Poor delegation often stems from simple self-doubt; out of fear and distrust management tries to control everything. This creates a "cycle of control that requires control". Control costs money and kills initiative. Self-check questions suggested: "чего я боюсь, что не могу делегировать? Почему я должен бояться других людей? Почему я считаю их ненадежными, глупыми, неспособными?" (What am I afraid of that I can't delegate? Why should I fear other people? Why do I consider them unreliable, stupid, incapable?) Today scientific and empirical facts let us say that management's fear is too expensive for the company. The author's own addition: the same holds for states, where society pays with unfulfilment for the fears of the authorities with their absurd control and restrictions [#190].
4. A subtle, sensitive culture of trust needs a reliable entry filter that screens out candidates with different values [#190].
5. Nine tips for building a company in the new paradigm (Diana's summary):
   1. Inspire — create conditions in which people reveal their talents.
   2. Eliminate root causes instead of fighting consequences.
   3. See people as winners and give them a chance.
   4. Choose people who are motivated from the start.
   5. Remove unnecessary control and meaningless hierarchy.
   6. Know where you want to get to.
   7. Work with your own fears.
   8. Quickly remove people who act like a "virus" destroying the culture.
   9. Find people close in values and do not get in their way. [#190]

Author's addition: working in this paradigm leads to "magic" that bureaucrats cannot get into their heads — employees take on responsibility and show super-results [#190].

#### Three reasons companies build D&I culture
The author's team says it helps companies build a culture of openness and transparency; colleague Ksenia Babat calls this D&I culture — D for diversity, I for "вовлеченность" (inclusion/involvement). Why companies are "running" to build it [#237]:
1. The social environment is changing. Discrimination and any form of violence, including communicative violence, are becoming a problem. The need to work more ethically grows, which forces culture change, because "этику невозможно реализовать на уровне лишь бизнес-процессов или структуры" (ethics cannot be implemented only at the level of business processes or structure) [#237].
2. Companies strive for maximum effectiveness in cutting-edge fields and highly competitive environments. Cultures with good engagement, diversity and openness give an advantage because they are (per the author) proven to influence: innovativeness; profit; resilience/sustainability; speed of decision-making [#237].
3. Companies and their leaders reach new levels of values. With inevitable evolutionary development people acquire higher-order, non-material values. This leads to new forms of organization and to transformational goals and values in the company — and from that moment directive, hierarchical management no longer works, so one has to manage through culture and involve employees. Scientists the author names as having proved and described different levels of values and consciousness: Robert Kegan (Harvard), Susanne Cook-Greuter (Harvard), Bill Torbert (Yale) [#237].

#### Conscious Change Leadership (Dean and Linda Anderson, Being First)
Why it is needed (the highway metaphor, quoted in the post): imagine driving in rush hour on a Los Angeles highway with sun visors down and eyes closed — you will hardly arrive safely and will likely cause an accident. Being unable to see all signals and obstacles in a difficult, chaotic setting makes the trip impossible. On a straight empty road you could drive some distance with minimal damage, but a high-speed highway is completely different. High speed, unpredictability and lack of control are absolutely characteristic of today's organizational transformations; managing them with classic project or change management is as hard as driving with eyes closed. Good and best practices stop working: they were formed in completely different conditions and became "good and best" precisely for that past environment [#286].

Thesis: "Сегодня ни у кого нет точного ответа на вопрос – что делать?" (Today nobody has an exact answer to the question — what to do?) The environment thus forms new requirements for leaders of new generations [#286].

Three leader qualities in the Andersons' approach:
1. Гибкость — flexibility.
2. Осознанность — awareness/consciousness.
3. Интегральность — integrality (integral perspective). [#286]

#### Conscious Change Leadership (Anderson & Ackerman Anderson) — overview
Source: a PDF titled, in translation, "Don't sleep at the wheel: moving beyond change management to Conscious Change Leadership", by Linda Ackerman Anderson and Dean Anderson, translated from English by Maxim Nechay, © 2020 Being First, Inc. The channel shared it without its own commentary; everything below is the authors' position. [#287]

The driving metaphor. Imagine driving at rush hour on a Los Angeles freeway with sun visors down and eyes closed. On a straight empty road you might cover some distance with minimal damage, but on a freeway — high speed, unpredictability, lack of control — you will almost certainly crash. Leading a transformation today with modern project or change management approaches is, the authors say, an equally hard task: leaders cannot see all the signals and obstacles in a chaotic environment. [#287]

History of change management. Before change management existed, leaders saw their job as setting the direction of change and delegated the "driving" to others, usually a project team. That worked while directions were clear and roads wide with bright markings. When leaders hit problems of adoption and failure to reach goals, they realized they had to pay attention to what they had previously not noticed, not understood or undervalued — above all the human factor. This created, in the 1990s, the field of change management. It answered two breakthroughs in leaders' understanding of change: [#287]
1. The "human" aspect. It gave a tool to deal with the finally recognized human side of change that showed up as insurmountable resistance when leaders' demands met the organization. Besides overcoming resistance through stakeholder work, change management improved leaders' communication plans and identified staff growth areas needed to implement change. [#287]
2. The "process" aspect. It responded to leaders' wish to improve implementation itself, increasing attention to proactive change planning, or at least to how people accept change. By offering planning tools and approaches close to project management, it raised leaders' chances of reaching targets. [#287]

Why it is no longer enough. The popularity of change management shows leaders recognized what was missing. Yet research, per the paper, shows 60% to 70% of implemented changes fail. Neither leaders nor change-management practitioners have advanced far since, especially given today's "freeway" reality; the same applies to Six Sigma, organizational development and continuous improvement. There are still too many "change accidents", too much "road rage" and too much obstruction in leaders' field of view. The authors call on leaders and practitioners to clear the obstructions, "fully take the wheel" and step beyond project and change management to the next evolutionary stage — conscious change leadership — because conscious change leaders are the ones able to deliver a real breakthrough. [#287]

#### Three types of change
The first task of change leadership is to understand the change environment and how it has evolved. There are three types of change, each requiring its own leadership strategy — "Универсального решения нет!" ("There is no universal solution!"). [#287]
1. Development (развитие) — supported by project and change management. [#287]
2. Transition (переход) — also supported by project and change management. [#287]
3. Transformation (трансформация) — the "today's generation of complex change"; project and change management are insufficient for it. Most organizations today, the authors say, are in a "storm of transformation". [#287]

(!) INCOMPLETE: the paper names development and transition but does not define them; the page titled "3 Типа Изменений" ("3 Types of Change") contained a diagram whose content was not transcribed.

Why transformation is unique (two reasons): [#287]
1. The future is undefined at the start. The only way to create it is to move forward with a firm intention to discover it. Without a clear goal, leaders must move into the unknown relying on expanded sources of information and support to formulate and show the new future. Since the target state is unclear, the road is unclear too; a detailed calendarized plan is impossible — this is where project management fails. Because transformation cannot be "managed" in the classic sense, a new way of conducting change is needed. [#287]
2. The target state is so different from the current one that a qualitatively new level of mindset is required to make the change and anchor the new state. This deeply affects people and corporate culture: leaders and employees must fundamentally change their approaches, behaviour and work processes, and cultural norms must change to create an environment supporting the new reality. People will have to change much of what they do, but mainly how they think, how they work with adjacent units and how much they invest in what truly matters to the company. The main change strategy must therefore focus on securing the needed level of personal transformation of everyone, including executives, from the early stages — this is the foundation for a breakthrough result. [#287]

These two features make the dynamics of process and people far more unpredictable and unmanageable than in development and transition; classic change-management strategies help but are "absolutely insufficient". [#287]

Four competence areas of Conscious Change Leadership. The approach, accompanying transformation at all levels of the system, requires new competences of leaders and practitioners in: (1) creating an all-encompassing, holistic, integral change strategy addressing all aspects of process and people dynamics; (2) a qualitative change of leaders' and practitioners' views and approaches matching the new requirements of the environment and of transformation itself; (3) defining the culture and behaviour that fit the new state; and (4) a conscious approach to designing, implementing and timely correcting the transformation process from launch to successful result. Each is developed below. (!) CONTRADICTION: the text lists four areas, then says "we will consider each of these three fields", and later calls process dynamics "the fourth most important aspect". [#287]

#### Cornerstone 1 — Developing an integral change strategy
Executives understand their central role in developing a new business strategy, but simply announcing it is not enough to implement it. It needs a change strategy that delivers the organizational and cultural change matching the new business strategy, especially if the company aims at a breakthrough. When leaders do not recognize the type of change and the field in which it manifests, they cannot build the right change strategy and miss their targets. Correctly identifying the type of change is the first step to a correct change strategy. [#287]

Who must build it. In development and transition changes, executives usually do not develop a change strategy: they state expectations and delegate to a project team of "technical" experts, which immediately sets "goals, schedule and budget" and plans implementation with classic project/change-management approaches. In large transformational change this delegation is not allowed: it requires the thinking level of the top executive. Because of large business impact, complex "human" issues and many interdependencies, executives are obliged to develop it themselves. [#287]

Seven responsibilities — the change strategy executives build must: [#287]
1. Clearly articulate the image of the future and the importance of the "breakthrough result" tactic for reaching it.
2. Correctly distribute effort among the main priority directions.
3. Identify the main levers on which to concentrate effort toward the target state.
4. Set up the necessary participation of all stakeholders in designing and reaching the target state.
5. Clarify roles, decision points and the supporting change structure for managing information flows, communication and timely corrective actions.
6. Create an effective strategy and conditions to accelerate the process.
7. Establish a realistic pace of change.

#### Three components of a change strategy: content, people, process
A comprehensive strategy for transformational change has three equally important components. [#287]
1. Content. Most executives concentrate here, mainly on business strategy. Content includes the new business direction and the resulting structural, systems, product and technical changes. Before change management appeared, leaders concentrated exclusively on the technical aspect, which is still the most understandable and comfortable part for them. [#287]
2. People (the human factor). Change management opened the door to this second component. It focuses on three things: overcoming resistance, improving communications and training. The authors' critique: these three strategies keep executives' attention on the surface, external level — employees' behaviour, skills and actions — and try to change people "from above", from outside. They do not reach deep enough for real transformation — to mindset, approaches and worldview. Such shifts are possible only from the inside out: "Разговорами по душам тут ситуацию не изменишь" ("You won't change the situation with heart-to-heart talks"). Only when people decide internally to change is their personal transformation real and sustainable, with minimal resistance. [#287]
3. Process. How to conduct change so that the organization understands and accepts it, reaches business results, changes its approaches and establishes a new culture. Project and change management focus mainly on analysis and implementation; when change is "rolled out", it tends to produce "human factor" problems caused by people's refusal to engage at early stages. Executives must create a strategy that launches a process of searching for and developing the best solution, in which people are not only maximally effective but sincerely want the project to succeed. When all conditions are included in a single strategy, breakthrough becomes possible. [#287]

#### Cornerstone 2 — Transforming mindset; Command-and-Control vs Co-creative
The second cornerstone is transforming the mindset of leaders and rank-and-file staff, which requires a deeper understanding of the human dynamics of change. Because most leaders need a personal inner shift to a qualitatively new level just to grasp the complexity of transformation dynamics, the authors are convinced transformation must begin with leaders' mindset — what change is and what they must do. [#287]

Lineage. The popular topic of mindset in organizations was, per the paper, first raised by Joel Barker's videos on thinking paradigms and then taken up by Peter Senge's research on "mental models" (1990). Both brought awareness into executives' field of view; ways of vertical growth of executives' mindset are necessary and the most important condition for a breakthrough. [#287]

Core thesis: "образ мышления – причина всего" ("mindset is the cause of everything"). Mindset affects behaviour, decisions and, most importantly, results. How leaders perceive people, the organization and change determines: (1) which human, cultural and process dynamics of transformation they see or do not see; (2) their inner interpretation of the seen and unseen dynamics; (3) their leadership style, chosen strategy and ultimately the result. [#287]

Command-and-Control mindset (the prevailing one) collects various obstructions that prevent leaders from seeing the true dynamics of transformation and that exclude required personal change. It perceives all change as a set of separate predictable and controllable events manageable by external influence — a mechanistic approach that excludes the power of awareness as an organizational resource. Co-creative mindset, in contrast, sees different types of change and recognizes transformation as a self-organizing, emergent process best supported by positively aligning the interaction of change dynamics at individual and organizational levels; it understands transformation as driven first of all by the growth of people's awareness. In today's business this growth of awareness is reflected in the move to e-business, globalization, customer-oriented product development and unified software solutions across the structure, all of which "dismantle" hierarchy in communication and decision-making. [#287]

Comparison table (Command-and-Control → Co-creative): [#287]
1. Power over others → Relationships with others.
2. Total control of the process, the scope of change, the schedule and the budget → A process designed around the real scope, schedule and budget; everything is adjusted as the project goes.
3. A project plan strictly following deadlines and stages → A three-month planning horizon implying constant analysis and correction within the period when needed.
4. Everything is provable, understandable, controlled and predictable → Readiness to take in incoming information and ask key questions to determine needed actions; readiness for uncertainty and opportunity.
5. The organization consists of separate pieces, each can be improved separately → The organization is a complex system where all parts interact and affect the success of the larger system.
6. Project plans are a set of predefined activities → Project plans are dynamic; executives and stakeholders are involved as needed.
7. Every choice is within the project plan → Constantly track signals of the need to adjust the process and understand what lies behind them.
8. Top-down messages, instructions and demands → External and internal communication whose style recognizes the importance of employees' role, thus deeply involving them.
9. "I told you" communication; the stakeholder is a passive object → Two-way communication that elicits employees' maximum contribution and builds partnership with leaders.
10. Firm belief that resources are finite and insufficient → Belief that resources will appear as the project goes for its successful realization.
11. Management knows best → Employees with different points of view find very valuable solutions.
12. No need to change → Personal change of both leaders and rank-and-file staff is a mandatory condition of successful change.
13. Do what is best for the project and the unit implementing change → Do what is best for the whole organization in the long term.

#### Five mistaken conclusions of Command-and-Control leaders
Leaders with this mindset "logically" arrived at the erroneous conclusions that: [#287]
1. Transformation can happen in numerous unconnected processes and integration is optional.
2. People will "swallow" whatever they are told to do, and nothing much needs explaining.
3. Resistance is a "necessary evil" to be delegated to the HR department.
4. Project and change management will handle control of the process and planned schedule perfectly well.
5. Finding the right solution is the best recipe for success and the only thing a leader must do.

Behind these, the most important limitation is that Command-and-Control blinds leaders to the full variety of human, cultural and process dynamics actually occurring — with deep effects on people, communication, relationships and culture. If leaders do not understand these processes, they cannot lead the changes taking place. [#287]

#### Reactive vs conscious approach to transformation
There are two opposite approaches to transformational change. [#287]
- Reactive — used by leaders seeing through the Command-and-Control lens. They move on autopilot, doing what they did before as if transformation were no different from operational management. They orient first to "external" indicators without checking whether their personal assumptions and "entrenched" approaches still work; they treat each change like any other, with minimal self-analysis of what works and what doesn't; being "right" matters to them. Their Command-and-Control "blinkers" and lack of introspection leave them ignorant of transformation's real human and process demands. They spend precious crisis time on problems they did not notice, and value project and change management because these help them climb out of difficulties caused by weak processes. [#287]
- Conscious — leaders who "do not sleep at the wheel" understand the real dynamics and act consciously. They attend to both external and internal aspects, understanding the equal importance of the content of change and of the influence their own state of mind has on the process; they go beyond old conditioned habits and follow the principle of co-creation. They see the impact of people and process dynamics on the whole organization because they look for it, knowing it is the key. They deliberately raise their own awareness of how the organization and people, including themselves, are changing; with an "open mind" and close observation they navigate the difficult route, leading proactively and avoiding failures. The key is not just leadership in change but *conscious* leadership of change. [#287]

#### Developing conscious change leaders (method and case)
Conscious Change Leadership starts with fully understanding the power of mindset to govern perception and results; once leaders grasp this, they more readily look inward to examine their own. Leaders must do this individual work either before the change or at its very earliest stages — the first serious test given today's prevalence of Command-and-Control. [#287]

The most frequent question the authors hear from leaders and consultants is "Как мне заставить руководителей посмотреть в зеркало…?" ("How do I make executives look in the mirror…?") — i.e., realize that the company's future depends on their thinking, behaviour and leadership. There is no simple answer, but leaders see sooner when they see tangible evidence of how their mindset affects the organization, the change process and ultimately their lives. Consultants can help them see this influence, positive and negative, on change results. [#287]

Conditions for inner transformation of a leader: it is most likely when it happens through personal experience, outside the office, with professional support by trained facilitators, in a safe environment conducive to self-work. The goal is not personal growth for its own sake but improving the results of organizational change through the leader's personal transformation; done alongside achieving change results, it becomes a powerful component of the transformation strategy. In the authors' consulting, all clients go through this "breakthrough" programme at the start of change: once leaders begin changing themselves, they can support such deep change in the organization. [#287]

Case — CEO in a deregulating industry. Coaching, feedback and participation in the "Breakthrough Leadership" programme helped him see that his controlling, directive mindset prevented the company from implementing the new business strategies required by the new environment of free competition. He also discovered his "defensive" strategy was about survival, not large-scale growth; this fear-based thinking limited the company's future options. His personal insights became breakthroughs for himself, the change strategy and the company. He then sent 1,200 managers to the "inner breakthrough training" and organized a conference on shaping the company's future vision for 300 key leaders — something that had never happened in the company's long history. Conveying his inner change, he opened the event by inviting everyone to take part in shaping the company's future without limiting themselves. Lesson per the authors: when leaders design a change strategy that "awakens" most people in the organization, implies a new mindset and accounts for process and people dynamics, transformation goes easier, faster, cheaper and with greater success. [#287]

#### Cornerstone 3 — Accounting for the cultural factor
"Культура для организации – то же самое, что майндсет для человека" ("Culture is to an organization what mindset is to a person"): culture is the collective manifestation of each individual's mindset. Therefore the change strategy must include adapting culture to the requirements of new business approaches. [#287]
1. Match current culture to the target state as soon as the target state emerges. If organizational culture does not fit the transformation, in nine cases out of ten change slows down or fails completely. [#287]
2. Create an image of the fitting culture. This can be a very important and inspiring process if employees are involved in it. [#287]
3. Find what blocks it now. Once you know which culture you aim for, you can detect current manifestations that prevent it — norms, work processes, leaders' influence, the motivation system and similar elements (the source itself leaves this list open with "и так далее"). These must be revised and new approaches adopted. [#287]
4. Put culture work into the change plan. The culture-change work must be reflected in the change plan. [#287]

One of the most successful culture-change strategies, per the authors, is personal change of each person first and only then revision of approaches and the culture as a whole; used in the CEO case, it requires high competence in the fourth aspect, process dynamics. [#287]

#### Cornerstone 4 — Four process-thinking principles of transformation
Transformational change requires shifting focus from a "project management" approach to a "process" one. Several basic principles, each equally important, define process thinking: [#287]

1. Transformation cannot be "managed". The flaw of project and change management for transformation lies in their names ("management"). This is a "verdict" on Command-and-Control leaders and must be accepted by those responsible for change; otherwise, constantly anxious about uncertainty, afraid of not having the planned result by a specific date, or "fighting" the natural chaos of processes, they will never lead transformation consciously — they will only "react". Leaders must stop rigidly controlling the process itself while having a good "navigation system": the authors use The Change Leader's Roadmap (CLR). [#287]
2. Emergent processes and outcomes. Even with the CLR, transformational processes live their own life: processes and results "emerge" along the way. Quoting Webster's dictionary, "emergent" means appearing unexpectedly, or arising as a new or improved state; becoming visible or known. The outcome is emergent because change must start before the end goal is clear: you will have an image of where you want to go but no exact understanding of how. Like circus trapeze artists, you must let go of one trapeze while flying to the next; you catch it before falling only if your strategy allows it. The key to success is high engagement: since at the start nobody knows the best solution, leaders must engage the company's "collective intelligence" in defining the goal, designing, testing and implementing until the best solution emerges and can be implemented and anchored. The process emerges from the flows of the organization, market and people: new quality and performance requirements, resource requests, competitors overtaking you with new technology. "Who would have thought?" is a common phrase of transformation. Hence the conscious change leader's mindset: "Планируй как можно тщательнее, работай с тем, что приходит в процессе" ("Plan as thoroughly as possible, work with what comes up in the process"). [#287]
3. Learning and course correction. The most important aspect of transformation is the ability to learn during change and immediately adjust processes and outcomes. Simple in words, it demands a monumental shift in mindset and culture for most organizations. To truly adopt it, the change strategy must include abandoning Command-and-Control cultural norms such as: "Kill the messenger with bad news", "Make a mistake and you're dead", "Don't rock the boat", "First prove viability, then implement". "Learning and correction" is both a mindset and a way of acting: it must be built into culture, and the system and processes prepared to match. For example, leaders must encourage feedback and the flow of new information, doubts and signs of problems, build two-way communication, and communicate where this information should go and how it will affect process and result. In traditional organizations leaders allow no deviation from announced plans; in consciously led organizations leaders are open to constant route changes as the environment demands, to improve processes and reach the best possible result. [#287]
4. Creating critical mass by engaging the whole system. Transformation happens only if a critical mass of employees moves to the new mindset. The fastest way to reach it, and shared "inner ownership" of success, is to involve the whole system: all groups affected must take part in designing the future state and the processes leading to it. Involve employees in designing the vision, project requirements, detailed analysis of the change's impact on operations and submitting improvement ideas — they face various decisions daily and may have invaluable ideas. Large-group methods have come a long way: "Future Search" (Weisbord, 1995), "Real-Time Strategic Change" (Jacobs, 1994), "Search conferences" (Axelrod, 1992), "Whole-Scale Change" (Dannemiller, 2000) and "Open Space Technology" (Owen, 1997) are, per the authors, an excellent way to form "collective intelligence" solutions on all aspects of change, raise ownership and enthusiasm, and develop both employees' mindset and organizational culture. Such group sessions are key strategies to accelerate each phase, doing more work in less time. [#287]

#### Conditions for successful change (secured by sponsors)

#### Two lenses needed for corporate retreats
Purpose: explains why corporate retreats aimed at culture change are, in the author's words, the hardest thing a trainer can do in a company, and what profile a facilitator needs. The author (head of deep mind) wrote this after his team ran the second annual retreat for Qiwi without him — he had been focused on delegating all programmes that year but found it hard to believe that corporate retreats could be handed over: "Сегодня это самое сложно, что может делать тренер в компании, если честно" (Today this is the hardest [sic] thing a trainer in a company can do, honestly). The two lenses that must be switched on simultaneously and at a high level ("хрен в реальности найдешь" — which in reality you damn well won't find together) [#542]:
1. Expertise in mindfulness and facilitation ("экспертизу в осознанности и фасилитации"). - The author notes a growing number of people launching mindfulness-in-business projects who have decent experience in various companies; he finds this understandable ("escaping" from often flat office tasks into authoritative work with people sounds tempting) and expects much to come of it — but without top-position experience something will still be missing.

The author's metaphor: the skill set is "такой редкий суповой набор, что его мало кто может собрать" (such a rare soup kit that few can assemble it) — a soup everyone needs, like Tom Yum in Moscow about three years earlier, and everywhere not quite made right. #### Movement-based culture diagnosis
Purpose: a quick way to diagnose a company's culture, employee health and power relations — deep mind added a dance practice to one stream of its corporate resilience programme and found that "По тому, как сотрудники двигаются можно быстрее и лучше диагностировать культуру в компании, чем через многостраничные анкеты и отчеты" (By how employees move you can diagnose the culture in a company faster and better than through multi-page questionnaires and reports) [#754].

How it works and why, per the author [#754]:
1. Offer a short, safe movement session. About half an hour; small, safe exercises, explicitly not "грязные танцы" (dirty dancing). An ethically conducted half-hour movement session can positively change team dynamics for the whole day, making employees more stress-resilient and happier — which in turn, the author claims, directly affects business results.
2. Why it helps. Movement and dance release us emotionally and help us feel real, living contact; everyone wants this in some way, even if afraid to admit it.
3. Why it is hard — trauma. The body and movement are charged with emotion: people fear contact with their own body because of accumulated emotional and physical pain that mostly they don't even feel; traumatic past experience has bodily correlates that are triggered by movement, making it unwanted.
4. Why it is hard — power. Stiffness is also created by the power relations we are embedded in. Collectives are saturated with signals about how one may and may not behave, who one must be, before whom to shrink or loosen up: "Мы всегда воссоздаем структуру властных отношений собственными телами" (We always recreate the structure of power relations with our own bodies).
5. Observe. Watching a group for just half an hour reveals much about the problems and state of employees and about the culture of the founders and management. The author says that having seen reactions to counter-cultural ("strange" for the mainstream) practices in 100+ companies, overall employee health and cultural maturity are visible instantly when you propose to move together or talk about feelings.
6. Read the extremes. In some companies people don't turn cameras on even for a conversation, and only "marginals" would move or simply sit with eyes closed; in others you see engaged people with whom it is so enjoyable that you want to meet in person and keep being friends. The author asks which case will have better coordination, ability to retain talent and crisis response — "Думаю, ответ очевиден" (I think the answer is obvious).
7. Use it on yourself as a leader. The author dances and moves with his own team and community; this shows the degree of the collective's inner freedom and simultaneously gives him feedback on the clarity and ethics of the power relations between them.

Expected reception: even in this safe format, movement will seem strange to half of companies and be entirely inaccessible to the other half. The author expects that as more companies evolve a step further, moving in them (and for them as organisations) will become easier [#754].

The diagnostic question to ask about your own company: how comfortable are people in the team going into "strange" experiences that are in essence utterly normal — observing the breath, moving, talking about one's feelings — and why has it turned out this way? [#754]

### Detailed insights

#### No ready-made solutions for transformation
At a meeting with Russian practitioners in Italy who do embodiment work and the transformation of a large company, the author discussed the "new wave": business becoming more conscious, ethical, horizontal — and many times more effective. Yet few understand how to actually use modern transformation models: there are many and they are complex. One example the author flags as worth diving into: the Integral Agile Transformation Framework, the practical application of Ken Wilber's theory in business [#13].

The author is very sceptical of people who say "we know how to do everything, give us your business and we'll transform it". His reasons: many large companies are switching to a short strategic-planning cycle of only 3 months because everything changes very fast; frameworks work differently in different cultures; so "Готовые решения в этой ситуации - хрень, если честно" (Ready-made solutions in this situation are crap, honestly). Each time you have to reinvent the bicycle in a sense. The new paradigm is genuinely new, and we are at the point where all stakeholders must be brought together for dialogue to jointly figure out how to fundamentally change companies. (The same post records the decision to launch a mindful-leadership community in Russia as the vehicle for that dialogue) [#13].

#### Technology changed business models (Gref on Uberization)
The author quotes Herman Gref: the world has changed categorically; technologies changed everything, including what business fears most — the business model. Gref recalls a conversation with the president of Volkswagen when Uber appeared: VW was not afraid of self-driving cars ("our engineers are no worse"; as soon as there is demand for electric cars, they will make one better than Tesla), but feared new business models they did not understand — "мы боимся Uber, потому что у нас под это мозги не заточены" (we fear Uber because our brains are not tuned for it) — engineering brains can make the best hardware but do not understand such a business model, so VW invested in several taxi companies just in case. Lesson per Gref: entrepreneurs are often not frightened by challenges they have already faced and learned to answer, but by unknown challenges they are not ready for; for all old companies the Uberization of the economy is a big challenge [#133].

The author's commentary: the global economy did not exist 15 years ago — it is a completely new phenomenon. There has been a world economy and world trade since the Middle Ages, but never such a speed of movement of capital, labour and information. To adapt we will have to discover a new quality in ourselves: "видеть собственные модели мышления и паттерны поведения и сознательно их изменять" (to see one's own thinking models and behaviour patterns and consciously change them) [#133].

#### Why culture cannot be left to build itself in large companies
- Small and mid-size companies. Startups and mid-size companies integrate through personal contacts. Even when the founder at some point realizes he does not know the employees in the corridor, his behaviour and "vibe" are still strongly felt in chats and offices. In this situation it can really seem that no special culture work is needed: culture is formed by your *actual* behaviour ("и никода иначе" — "and never otherwise" [sic]) and that of the people you invited to work with you — "Нормально веди себя – нормально будет" ("Behave decently — it'll be fine"). [#476]
- The ~200-person threshold. The situation differs seriously in large companies on competitive markets. Above 200 people a company becomes hard to integrate through regular personal communication. It then integrates or keeps its integrity at best through goals, values and communication skills, and in the average case through good administration of processes. [#476]
- Requests are symptoms of culture. Over 3 years of consulting large leading companies, the author's team noticed that practically all requests they receive turn out on inspection to be symptoms of problems or tasks in culture. These companies, not always consciously, have already started working on changing their culture — which, the author notes, will become permanent. [#476]
- Stress and burnout as a symptom. One manifest symptom (the author says it is more complex but simplifies) is an epidemic of stress and burnout. People burn out and leave not because of the company's strategy or structure but because of its culture. [#476]

#### Four cultural reasons people leave or become apathetic
Per the author, people leave or fall into apathy because of: [#476]
1. The company's attitude to employee well-being.
2. Accepted norms about work, people and relationships.
3. The company's goals and values.
4. The quality of communication with colleagues and bosses.

Without purposeful culture work from the biggest bosses down to everyone else, nothing will change — a little, pretending, or extremely slowly, yes. Without change at the level of the problem, in the long term the company either loses staff or — as is already clearly visible in large companies with good material working conditions — loses productivity through employee demotivation. [#476]

#### Culture as the new battlefield of top management
In an era of total remote work and growing international competition for talent, it is "невозможно не работать на культурой" ("impossible not to work on culture" [sic]) for large modern companies. The author goes further: "корпкультура – новая главная точка приложения услилий менеджмента самого высокого уровня. Это новое супер поле для битвы управленцев" ("corporate culture is the new main point of application of top-level management effort. It is the new super battlefield for managers"). His reasoning: in a world of exponential growth and digital, success is determined not by КФУ (key success factors) but by the ability to learn faster than others, adapt to crises and *sense* the future ("yes, big business is also a place for feeling!"). For this everyone badly needs the best people and excellent interaction between them, which only a right and dynamically developing corporate culture can provide. [#476]

#### Why culture matters for talent: the Great Resignation and Russia
In the US a phenomenon sociologists have called the Great Resignation is underway: people voluntarily leave companies en masse; the key reasons named are burnout and the search for organisations with a caring corporate culture. The author argues that, however much some would like Russia to follow its "special path", the same tendencies are visible even in its economy: over two years of the pandemic the labour market changed dramatically, and all countries have become participants in an international talent market drawn into global social processes [#562].

Many companies — "точнее, очень конкретных боссов" (more precisely, very specific bosses) — do not understand the value of corporate culture and do not see how fast they need to run to raise the culture of communication and general mindfulness. This dismays the best employees: they face meaninglessness and lack of prospects and decide to look for a new job. Companies that do invest are today "магнит для самых сочных кадров" (a magnet for the juiciest talent). The author states that all deep mind clients are already somewhere on the way to this magnetism, since other companies are not ready to invest in real work with stress, communication and "new-wave" leadership; he also notes deep mind is often bound by NDAs and sees only part of the market [#562].

#### Corporate culture under a stress test (May 2022)
The author describes the Russian situation in 2022: a two-year COVID period that was hard on the psyche and physiology is overlaid with a layer of uncertainty and violence that multiplies stress and traumatises people. He sees two failure patterns: some "fell apart" at once and continue to live as a shadow of their potential; others discarded all feelings and try to overwork all their tasks so as not to see the unpleasant reality [#658].

Mechanism: feelings do not go anywhere — "Как по законам физики, они никуда не убывают" (As by the laws of physics, they do not diminish) — but overwhelm people later as addiction, depression, outbursts of anger and other negative states, and this is already catching up with many. The questions the article raises: do leaders close their eyes to the human problem in the company, and when does a corporate culture become genuinely adaptive to the challenges of modern times [#658].

#### Visionary procrastination in change initiatives (June 2025)
Visionary procrastination is a key concept introduced in research by Tina Sendlhofer from the Netherlands: "когда сотрудники искренне разделяют ценности устойчивого развития, но откладывают действия, сталкиваясь с препятствиями" (when employees sincerely share the values of sustainable development but postpone action when they encounter obstacles). The author wrote a Kommersant article on it ("Ловушка благих намерений" — The trap of good intentions; subtitle: sources and tools for mitigating visionary procrastination in responsible companies) and says that in essence it is about any changes in companies, a topic he considers more relevant than ever for the coming years [#1412].

Context given by the author: he contrasts the current St Petersburg International Economic Forum with its past, saying the words "international" and even "investment" could now be dropped from its name, and quotes Gref: "Предприятия остановили инвестиционный цикл" (Enterprises have stopped the investment cycle) — the largest bank financing investment projects in the country had, since the start of the year, not financed a single new investment project, only completing old ones [#1412].
