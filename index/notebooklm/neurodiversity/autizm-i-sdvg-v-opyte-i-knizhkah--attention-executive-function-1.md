## Executive function, autistic inertia, time perception and object permanence

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's explanations of executive function and its breakdown in autism and ADHD, the components of attention and time perception that suffer in ADHD, the not-yet-official concept of sluggish cognitive tempo, autistic inertia (trouble starting, stopping and switching) and what autistic people themselves say helps, object permanence in relationships and in conflicts, and a study-based argument that rest alone does not repair self-organisation. According to the authors, this matters because these difficulties are often misread as laziness, indifference or insufficient effort; knowing the mechanisms lets people "немного меньше грызть себя" (bite themselves a bit less) [#25, #263, #284].

### Core ideas

- Executive function is the capacity to be one's own manager, and up to 80% of autistic people have impaired executive function; it is also impaired in other neurodevelopmental conditions such as ADHD [#25].
- Sluggish cognitive tempo describes an "internalised", passive, foggy inattention with slow processing and low energy, overlapping with ADHD and autism but without hyperactivity or impulsivity [#29].
- Time perception in ADHD is impaired in at least three components (estimation, sense/monitoring, motor timing), it is not in the diagnostic criteria, and there are no universal behavioural fixes [#86].
- ADHD attention is impaired unevenly: sustained and divided attention suffer more than selective attention, and all three neural attention mechanisms work inefficiently [#224].
- Autistic inertia is the tendency to stay in the current state — unable to start, unable to stop, and treating switching itself as a separate task; it is not laziness or depression [#263].
- Emotional object permanence problems in ADHD make absent people "drop out" emotionally; a related dimension — keeping the value of a person in mind during conflict — can be trained [#216, #217].
- "Can't get organised" is several different processes, and rest restores energy but does not teach planning, organisation or impulse control [#284].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Nine skills of executive function
Lena Foer defines executive function as all the skills that let us manage different aspects of life — "наша способность быть себе менеджерами" (our ability to be our own managers): arriving on time for a meeting, putting the keys in their place when coming home, and a million other everyday things. The skills listed [#25]:
1. Planning.
2. Organising oneself, others and space.
3. Taking on tasks and doing them.
4. Allocating time.
5. Self-control.
6. Applying consistent effort.
7. Showing flexibility.
8. Reflecting on how our own thinking works (metacognitive processes).
9. Prioritising.

#### Seven types of self-regulation linked to executive function
Lena Foer gives seven main types of self-regulation tied to executive function [#25]:
1. Awareness of what is happening — managing one's own attention.
2. Self-control — restraining oneself when one does not want to but has to.
3. Non-verbal working memory — holding information in memory to navigate plans and tasks.
4. Verbal working memory — remembering inner speech.
5. Emotions — using inner dialogue and images to regulate one's emotional state.
6. Self-motivation — prompting oneself to act when there are no external stimuli.
7. Planning and problem solving — finding new approaches and solutions.

How executive function problems can look [#25]:
- "временнАя слепота" (time blindness) — inability to plan and keep future events in mind;
- difficulty building chains of actions toward long-term goals;
- difficulty organising work and making schedules;
- difficulty controlling emotions and impulses;
- difficulty analysing and processing information.

Because of such impairment, even household tasks like clearing a table can look — and actually be — very hard [#25]. (!) INCOMPLETE: the post promises follow-up posts on how executive function works, how it fails and what to do; those posts are not part of this input.

#### Characteristics of sluggish cognitive tempo (SCT)
Katya Klimochkina learned about SCT at a workshop by Masha Sergeeva on ADHD diagnosis strategies and problems. SCT (ВКТ, "вялый когнитивный темп") is a concept describing a set of attention-related symptoms; it is not yet recognised as a separate disorder. Its characteristics [#29]:
1. Daydreaming ("мечтательность наяву") — absorption in one's own thoughts (about everything) that detaches the person from the surrounding world; the mind wanders.
2. Slow processing — more time may be needed to process information, answer questions or make decisions.
3. Easily confused — people with SCT may become confused or disoriented, especially with complex or fast-changing information; changing plans are genuinely hard.
4. Inattention — resembles ADHD, but in SCT inattention is more internal, marked by clouded consciousness rather than restlessness; "При ВКТ внимание «пассивно»" (in SCT attention is "passive").
5. Low energy — frequently reported physical and mental tiredness and lack of motivation, which may not improve after rest.
6. Social withdrawal — preference for solitary activities and visible social isolation.

What SCT, autism (ASD) and ADHD share [#29]:
- *Concentration difficulties*: in SCT — internalised inattention, daydreaming, mental sluggishness; in ADHD — external inattention, distractibility and trouble sustaining concentration; in ASD — variable, possibly problems with selective attention or switching attention between tasks.
- *Executive function impairment*: planning, organisation, working memory and task initiation can be impaired in all three.
- *Processing speed*: slowed cognitive processing is linked to SCT, can be seen in some people with ADHD (especially on tasks requiring sustained attention), and processing-speed differences can occur in ASD.
- *Working memory problems*: a working memory deficit is a common feature of neurodivergence; working memory is temporary storage and use of information, and it affects tasks needing memory and attention — "а это - вся наша жизнь" (and that is our whole life).
- *Attention-related symptoms*: forgetfulness, absent-mindedness and mistakes happen to everyone, but are much more frequent in SCT, ASD and ADHD.

What distinguishes SCT [#29]:
- *Sensory processing*: sensory sensitivity is common in ASD, while SCT may involve a sensitivity related more to mental processing and attention than to sensory input.
- *Less impulsivity*: unlike ADHD, SCT is usually not associated with impulsive behaviour or rash acts.
- *No hyperactivity*: SCT does not involve the hyperactivity or restlessness that mark the hyperactive-impulsive subtype of ADHD.

#### Three problem components of time perception in ADHD
Time perception is defined in the post signed by Alina Safi as the subjective experience of time passing and organising one's life by an internal clock. Its peculiarity is one of the ADHD symptoms not included in the diagnostic criteria, yet it occurs in most people with attention deficit. A study published in 2021 identified three problem components [#86]:
1. Time estimation — estimating the time needed to complete a task or reach a goal.
2. Time sense — monitoring time: how much has passed and how much is left. Research on time sense showed that children with ADHD relied on an internal subjective feeling of time without checking the clock, and misjudged how much had already passed.
3. Motor time — the ability to estimate and control the duration of one's movements and actions; people with ADHD often predict the time of their own actions inaccurately.

The author suspects there may be more than three characteristics. There are currently no universal behavioural strategies to improve time sense; calendars, to-do lists and timers are effective apps but cannot fully solve the time-perception problem [#86]. The attached image is a clock face with numbers only and carries no additional content [#86].

#### Attention classification in ADHD
Alina Safi's post and its diagram describe attention as a complex system of several components, with different types impaired to different degrees in children with ADHD [#224, img #224].

I. By mode of control [#224]:
- *Exogenous attention* — activated by external stimuli, happens automatically and involuntarily. In ADHD: excessive reaction to external stimuli; difficulty ignoring irrelevant stimuli; quick switching to any new stimulus.
- *Endogenous attention* — controlled voluntarily, requires effort and intention. In ADHD: difficulty concentrating on long tasks; difficulty directing attention to the needed objects; getting stuck on one task or, conversely, excessive switching.

II. By functional type [#224, img #224]:
1. *Selective attention* — focusing on relevant information while ignoring distractors. Impaired moderately in ADHD: easy distractibility, difficulty concentrating in noisy surroundings, inability to filter out irrelevant information.
2. *Sustained attention* — maintaining concentration on a task over time. Impaired much more strongly: quick fatigue from tasks, falling performance over time, missing important signals. The post's example: a child can play video games for hours but cannot do 10 minutes of homework.
3. *Divided attention* — processing several information streams at once. Works especially poorly: cannot do several things at once, overload from multiple stimuli, problems with prioritising; children cannot listen to the teacher and take notes simultaneously and forget the previous task after switching to a new one.
4. *Attention switching* — flexibly moving focus between tasks or stimuli. Also impaired: getting stuck on tasks (e.g. on an interesting activity), difficulty starting new tasks, chaotic aimless switching between tasks.

III. Mechanisms of attention (per the Perceptual Template Model) [#224, img #224]:
1. *Stimulus enhancement* — increasing the "brightness" of the target stimulus; improves perception when the signal is weak.
2. *External noise exclusion* — filtering and blocking irrelevant information; improves perception amid strong interference.
3. *Internal noise reduction* — lowering "noise" in the nervous system itself; improves signal-processing accuracy at all levels.

In ADHD all three mechanisms work inefficiently: important signals are weakly amplified, interference is poorly filtered, and internal noise is raised. According to the post this explains why attention problems appear in any conditions — in silence as well as with distractions [#224].

#### Autistic inertia — starting, stopping, switching, and what helps
The channel author (who says she knows the state from her own experience) describes autistic inertia: you have a task, you know how to do it, you have the time, and still cannot move, "будто между "я хочу" и "я делаю" кто-то вынул мостик" (as if someone removed the bridge between "I want" and "I do"); or the reverse — you are absorbed and cannot stop though hungry and it is time to sleep. Both are autistic inertia. The term was coined by autistic people themselves by analogy with school physics: a body at rest tends to stay at rest and a body in motion keeps moving until an external force acts; for people it concerns states — hard to start from rest, hard to stop on the go, hard to switch [#263].

The source study: a team led by Elizabeth Pellicano (Communications Psychology), including autistic co-authors, analysed 501 Reddit posts on the topic with almost ten thousand comments, aiming to understand how people experience the state. The author calls it the very first step, since academia previously did not know or speak about autistic inertia [#263].

1. Not being able to start. Many posters had taken their trouble starting tasks for laziness. People sometimes cannot "найти кнопку запуска" (find the start button) even for the simplest task — from washing the floor to a beloved hobby — regardless of whether it is boring or desired. Inside it feels like a barrier, as if "примерз" (frozen) or "заперт в клетке" (locked in a cage), while fully aware, which makes one even angrier. Hardest are multi-step tasks like cooking: unclear where to begin, the brain is flooded with information, and a stupor sets in [#263].
2. Not being able to stop. Not necessarily suffering: often it is a flow state where everything goes by itself, described as uplifting and healing, a "море кайфа" (sea of bliss). But the same inability turns against people: they could not break off to eat or sleep and were absorbed in one activity for days [#263].
3. Switching is a separate task. One participant put it: you cannot go straight "от мытья посуды к стирке" (from washing dishes to laundry) — you must first go "от мытья посуды к переключению задач" (from washing dishes to switching tasks) and only then to laundry. Switching itself is a task that must be started. That is why abrupt interruption hurts autistic people so much; being yanked out of a state is described as "вылететь через лобовое стекло" (flying out through the windshield) [#263].
4. It is not laziness. Laziness is a tempting simple answer but unconvincing: procrastination does not explain why one cannot start even something genuinely interesting. Nor does it reduce to depression — flow-state absorption looks nothing like it. Inertia strikes regardless of effort: one can want badly to start and still get stuck dead [#263].

What helps, according to the people themselves [#263]:
- *External push*: body doubling (someone simply nearby while you do a hard task), reminders, a sense of being accountable to someone.
- *Structure*: rigid routines, schedules, breaking a big task into small steps.
- *Time for switching*: set a timer with a margin, to have time to crawl out of one state and into another.

There is no universal recipe, but knowing the phenomenon is described and somewhat studied lets one blame oneself a little less [#263].

#### Emotional object permanence in ADHD — manifestations and causes
Katya Klimochkina defines object permanence as the ability to understand that people, objects and relationships continue to exist and matter even when physically absent or not interacting with you. It forms in infancy: "вижу маму - мама есть" (I see mum — mum exists), and if I don't see mum she "hopefully" still exists. In ADHD there may be difficulties with emotional object permanence, especially in relationships: when a close person is out of sight, the brain struggles to hold their emotional presence, so it is as if they no longer exist [#216].

How it manifests [#216]:
1. Out of sight, out of mind — a friend you saw regularly moves away and you "forget" them; not from rejection or actual forgetting, but because their emotional presence leaves your focus; you "stop feeling" closeness due to physical distance and feel emotionally detached when the person is not right before you.
2. Emotions on/off depending on contact — lots of emotion and connection in person, but once apart the feeling of connection melts. This can confuse a partner, swinging 180 degrees from involvement to detachment.
3. Dates, signs of attention, ordinary requests fly out of the head — not from indifference but because ADHD working memory does not hold such things long.
4. Long-distance relationships — if meetings are rare and everything rests on messaging, it is probably "не ваш варик" (not your option); without physical presence keeping the emotional connection is hard, you forget to write, and relationships may gradually fade.
5. Need for frequent confirmation of feelings — without constant assurance of being loved or remembered (since you don't always remember someone is there), and without regular tokens (the author jokes: 100 peonies or a couple of Lego sets), anxiety may arise; even in stable relationships one wants to hear it again and again. Confirmation here is about restoring emotional clarity.

Why it happens [#216]:
1. Working memory problems — working memory is like a "ментальный стикер" (mental sticky note) holding important information briefly until crumpled; the brain doesn't hold what it doesn't see now, so loved ones "fall out" of attention without "reminders" — not because you don't care.
2. Context-dependent attention — the ADHD brain scans only what's in front of it; everything else "collapses" like hundred-year-old browser tabs.
3. Impaired time perception — you may think it's been "only a couple of days" since you wrote, but a week has passed; past and future feel vague and distant, the brain lives "here and now".
4. Dopamine-driven motivation — due to dopamine imbalance the brain struggles to "switch on" without novelty or urgency; even if you want to write, it may not feel important enough to act.
5. Emotional vulnerability and fear of rejection — when too much time has passed, shame, guilt or anxiety that the person is angry appear, and contact is postponed further. A closed loop: "Я забыла написать, теперь мне стыдно, и я продолжаю это о т к л а д ы в а т ь." (I forgot to write, now I'm ashamed, and I keep postponing it.) A small slip (not replying on time) causes guilt that can become big GUILT and lead to avoidance; the more time passes, the harder the step to restore contact, though the desire hasn't gone.

The author says this hit close to home and that she would try to record a video message to friends to break the loop [#216].

#### Object permanence in conflict — four strategies
Continuing Katya's post, the channel author adds another dimension: the ability to remember what a person means to us and why we value them even when angry or in conflict — holding an integrated image of the person with both positive and negative sides, which she calls critically important for healthy relationships. Impaired object permanence occurs not only in ADHD but also, e.g., in borderline personality disorder and some traumatic experiences [#217].

What people with impaired object permanence experience during fights [#217]:
- A feeling that the close person was always terrible, nothing good ever happened, nothing unites you — even after 10 years together in harmony. Psychologists call this "эмоциональной амнезией" (emotional amnesia): literally forgetting all the good amid acute negative emotion.
- Conviction that there is, was and will be no relationship at all — complete devaluation of the bond and shared history, as if it were an illusion.
- Disproportionately strong emotions — every fight perceived as a potential end of the relationship.

Why it matters: when the information that we value the person and share a history is emotionally available, reconciling is easier and conflict hurts less; a realistic view holds that conflicts are a normal part of relationships. When the relationship seems to cease existing during a fight, it is exhausting, each conflict looks like a tragedy, and a person may act impulsively and regret it — breaking up or saying cruel things that cannot be taken back [#217].

The four strategies [#217]:
1. Regulate emotions ("первый совет ДБТ-терапевта на все случаи жизни" — the DBT therapist's first advice for every occasion). Use whatever in-the-moment distress-reduction techniques help you, to ease your state and not worsen the conflict: leaving the situation ("time-out"), slow calm breathing, going for a run, retreating into a favourite book — anything that lets you exhale and lower the emotional temperature.
2. Create an "emotional reminder". If in conflicts it's hard to remember why the person matters, prepare beforehand: write in a note exactly what will likely become inaccessible in a fight — concrete moments you're grateful for, qualities you value, shared plans and dreams. You may not fully believe it mid-conflict, but a seed of doubt in your negative perception is already good.
3. Practise mentalisation. Try even in conflict to keep the ability to see the other's perspective — e.g. mid-fight ask yourself what your opponent thinks and feels (it will be hard). This cognitive "bridge" helps not to see the loved one as an absolute enemy.
4. Discuss it with your partner in a calm moment. Explain that during fights it is sometimes hard for you to hold an integrated image of the relationship, and agree on "signals" or phrases that help both of you remember the value of the relationship.

The author's claim: object permanence "это не магия, а навык" (is not magic but a skill) that improves with practice; each time you keep the value of the relationship in view during conflict, neural pathways strengthen and it gets easier over time [#217].

#### Self-organisation difficulties as separate processes (why rest is not enough)
Katya Klimochkina challenges the idea (often held by older generations about younger ones, or by zealous perfectionist control-seekers) that someone who constantly fails at daily tasks isn't trying hard enough, and that if tired they should just rest and try again. She bases her argument on a study of cognitive rehabilitation in adults with ADHD: a program targeting attention, motivation, organisation, impulsivity and anger management, with participants randomly assigned to rehabilitation or a control group (no intervention) [#284].

The finding as she presents it: what looks from outside like "не могу самоорганизоваться" (can't organise myself) may consist of several different processes [#284]:
1. You may understand perfectly what needs doing but lose the thread of the task.
2. You may start a task but switch to something else.
3. You may make a plan but fail to hold it in mind at the needed moment.
4. Sometimes impulsive decisions and emotional reactions add on, leaving even fewer resources for ordinary life.

A universal "rest" does not necessarily solve all of these: sleep and reduced load can restore strength, but do not by themselves teach planning, organising activity or managing impulsivity. This doesn't mean rest is useless or that a person with ADHD must urgently train all skills at once; rather, it is important to distinguish exhaustion from difficulties that persist even when there is more energy. The takeaway: if ordinary life demands disproportionate effort, the question may be not only how much you rest but which specific tasks are hard for you and what help each needs [#284].

## Hyperfocus, special interests and monotropism

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's posts on autistic special interests, on hyperfocus and flow (and how they look differently in ADHD and in autism), on monotropism as a theory of autistic attention, on a lab study that tried to measure hyperfocus in autistic teenagers, and on how autistic adults themselves tell special interests and stims apart from OCD. According to the source, this matters because special interests improve quality of life and are a lever for teaching and therapy, because attention differences are often misread as a matter of choice, and because confusing a stim with a compulsion (or the reverse) leads to the wrong help [#13, #109, #283].

### Core ideas

- Special interests are not fixed for life. Contrary to a supervisor's claim, an autistic person can have any number of special interests, and they can stay the same for life or change at any frequency; autistic people often say they do not choose them [#13].
- Special interests help rather than harm. The older view that interests should be restricted has been replaced by the view that they raise quality of life, help communication, emotional coping, confidence and sometimes employment [#13].
- Hyperfocus is not the same as flow. Flow is chosen and trainable, deliberate attention plus deliberate action; hyperfocus results from difficulty shifting attention and is not necessarily goal-directed. Both look different in ADHD (spontaneous, irregular) and autism (predictable, tied to interests and routine) [#89].
- Monotropism is a broader theory than hyperfocus. It describes a whole cognitive style of narrow, powerful attention on interests (and explains the drive for predictability), whereas hyperfocus is a state one "falls into" from time to time [#96, #280].
- Lab instruments may not capture hyperfocus. In a small study autistic teens reported more hyperfocus on questionnaires but showed no EEG difference; the authors argue a lab cannot supply a person's own interest [#280].
- Stims/interests and OCD feel different from the inside. Autistic adults distinguish them by ownership, presence of a thought, time cost, emotions, effect on anxiety, controllability and reaction to disrupted routine — and OCD can hijack an existing interest [#283].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Hyperfocus vs flow in ADHD and autism (four-cell comparison)
Purpose: to separate two kinds of attention and engagement that are often conflated, and to show how each appears in ADHD and in autism (РАС = autism spectrum disorder) [#89].

Definitions.
- Hyperfocus is an umbrella concept: complete absorption in some task and intense concentration on it, when the whole world "outside the task" disappears. The author's illustration: while writing the post she completely forgot that cat-feeding time had passed 30 minutes earlier, and did not hear the cats yelling at her, absorbed in the text [#89].
- Flow is complete immersion in an activity with a surge of energy, full engagement and enjoyment. The author links it to the DBT mindfulness skill "participate" ("участие") [#89].
- The difference: "Поток - про выбор и тренировку" (Flow is about choice and training) — it is not only deliberate attention but also deliberate action. Hyperfocus is a consequence of difficulties in switching attention; it is not necessarily about purposeful action, and sometimes the opposite — about difficulty starting to do something [#89].

The four cells:
1. Hyperfocus in ADHD. People with ADHD often concentrate on activities that are especially interesting or satisfying — video games, reels, something creative (e.g. getting stuck drawing even lines for an illustration or finishing a game level). Falling into hyperfocus happens spontaneously and can stop the person switching to other tasks even when something more important "had to" be done. Example: she started cleaning her room and found herself many hours later watching a many-hour drama series, with 5 batches of freshly baked cupcakes. ADHD hyperfocus is irregular and can interfere with daily activities because of the difficulty switching attention [#89].
2. Hyperfocus in autism. Concentration is tied to special interests. It is more predictable and is linked to structured activities or a daily routine that give a sense of comfort and control (e.g. you are always very engaged when drawing or studying languages). Hyperfocus as "сосредоточенность на запланированной деятельности" (concentration on a planned activity) provides stability and increases performance, but can make it harder to switch flexibly between tasks when that is needed [#89].
3. Flow in ADHD. Reaching flow can be hard because of difficulties with sustained attention and impulsivity — but if the task is interesting, flow happens. In flow, people with ADHD can temporarily "overcome" attention deficit and distractibility; productivity and creativity come much more easily. The downside: the state is hard to maintain, because external stimuli easily break it [#89].
4. Flow in autism. Autistic people can reach flow more easily during special-interest activities and while staying in their usual daily routine. Constant activities support a stable flow state, giving high engagement and satisfaction — the example: every day, in any weather and any mood, at 18:00 she weaves rugs and enjoys the time. The author calls it a bold claim: "аутист + специальный интерес = высоковероятное состояние потока" (autistic person + special interest = a highly likely flow state) [#89].

#### Monotropism (attention as a narrow beam) vs polytropism
Purpose: a theory of autism that explains intense focus on interests, difficulty switching, and the drive for predictability [#96, #280].

- Definition. Monotropism is a cognitive style, a way of processing information, characterised by strong concentration of attention on what is interesting [#96]. Origin: proposed in the early 2000s by autistic researchers Dinah Murray, Wenn Lawson and Mike Lesser. The core: autistic attention converges narrowly and powerfully on a small number of interests, and because attention is finite, little is left for everything else; this also explains the difficulty switching familiar from autistic inertia [#280].
- The beam metaphor. Imagine attention as a beam of light in the dark: where you point it is your focus. Some people's beam is diffuse and lights a wide field; for autistic people the beam is very narrow, but precisely because of that it is powerful and allows very deep concentration. Whatever does not fall into it stays invisible — which is how attention concentrates on special interests while everything else, the surrounding world and people, is forgotten [#96].
- Monotropism vs hyperfocus. They are close. Hyperfocus is an umbrella term for difficulty switching attention and concentrating in the moment — a state a person "falls into" from time to time. Monotropism explains one-directional focused attention on one's interests in life generally; as a feature of how attention is built, it also explains the striving for predictability and familiarity [#96]. The later post says hyperfocus "fits perfectly" into this theory, making it immediately clear how it could work [#280].
- Polytropism vs monotropism (the "multiplicity vs one-directionality" dilemma most articles discuss). Polytropism is being able to do several tasks/interests at once, to multitask, thanks to breadth of attention. Monotropism is the opposite: one cannot multitask or switch quickly between tasks. Multitasking is usually seen as a great skill, especially at work (answering messages, sitting in a meeting, ordering lunch and doing your work at once), while monotropism is usually seen as a deficit. The author questions this: intense focus can mean spending hours studying a topic, collecting and organising information about one's interest — and if your work is your special interest, monotropism can be very useful [#96].
- Inertia and executive functioning. Executive dysfunction is impairment of the mental processes for planning, organisation, memory and starting and finishing tasks; it is common in autism. Autistic inertia is "more than" executive dysfunction: it is tied to difficulty shifting attention, so starting or finishing a task or moving from one to another is very hard. "Монотропные умы" (monotropic minds) switch with difficulty, so it is hard for them to focus on tasks unrelated to their current interests. The author suggests it is likely that autistic people show executive dysfunction because of a lack of motivation or interest in what supposedly needs doing [#96].
- Takeaway as stated: monotropism is a cognitive style specific to autism, marked by intense focus on interests and difficulty switching to something less engaging [#96].

#### Four common signs of hyperfocus
The ADHD post defines hyperfocus as a state in which a person is so absorbed in something that they ignore everything else; it is usually discussed in connection with autism, schizophrenia and ADHD, but there is little research on its effect on the brain and thinking. Although it shows up differently in everyone, four general signs exist at present [#109]:
1. Intense concentration of attention.
2. External stimuli are, as a rule, not consciously perceived.
3. The task must be engaging for hyperfocus to arise.
4. Task productivity increases in the state of hyperfocus.
[#109]

#### Pros and cons of hyperfocus (table)
From the table image attached to the ADHD post (the author invites readers to add their own observations) [#109]:
- Pros:
  1. Allows achieving results in work, study and creative work.
  2. Getting many tasks done in a short time.
  3. "Повышает самооценку" (raises self-esteem) and the sense of competence.
- Cons:
  1. Exhaustion after many hours of concentration.
  2. Hard to tear oneself away and shift attention.
  3. Can be directed at useless tasks.
[#109]

#### Seven ways autistic adults tell stims and interests from OCD
Source: an interview study by Hannah Long and colleagues with 15 autistic adults living with both autism and OCD — described as the first work asking about the differences those who know them first-hand. From outside the two look alike; from inside, the post says, they differ quite distinctly [#283]:
1. Whose is it. Stims and interests are described as part of oneself: "когда я стимлю, это как будто подтверждает, кто я" (when I stim, it's as if it confirms who I am). OCD comes "from the head" and feels alien, even though the person fully understands it comes from themselves. One participant: "аутизм - это арена, а ОКР - лев. Ты и так на арене, тебе и так страшно, а ОКР - это лев, который уже случился" (autism is the arena and OCD is the lion. You are already in the arena, you are already scared, and OCD is the lion that has already happened) [#283].
2. Is there a thought. Autistic repetitions come without a thought — "просто делаю, и все" (I just do it, that's all). Behind a compulsion stands a thought with "вывернутой логикой" (twisted logic), whose irrationality the person recognises, yet it still has to be done [#283].
3. How much it takes. People do not count their stims and interests as time spent: "это просто то, как я живу" (this is just how I live). OCD demands constant active management [#283].
4. What emotions. Stims and interests come with the whole spectrum — joyful ones, anxious ones, depending on the situation. About OCD not one of the fifteen named a single positive emotion — only fear, panic and stress; one said: "куда меня ни помести, тревога останется" (wherever you put me, the anxiety will stay) [#283].
5. What happens to anxiety. Stims and interests lower anxiety and help regulate. OCD feeds it — anxious both before and during [#283].
6. Control. A person can stop their own repetitions, even when they don't want to. A compulsion cannot be stopped, though they badly want to [#283].
7. If routine is disrupted. Disruption of a purely autistic routine feels "неуютно", "странно", "что-то не так" (uncomfortable, strange, something's wrong). Disruption of a routine that OCD has crept into leads to a meltdown — one participant put it as "разверзается ад" (hell breaks loose) [#283].

### Detailed insights

#### Special interests: what they are and how they work
- Definition and diagnosis. Special interests are very intense, focused passions in a specific subject that a person can pursue a lot, for long and with enviable enthusiasm. Many autistic people have them; having special interests is one of the diagnostic criteria for autism [#13].
- Number and change. A person can have any number of special interests; according to research cited by the author, children with autism have 8 on average. Interests can stay unchanged for life or change at any frequency. Lena Foer opens the post by recounting that her supervisor said "если xxxx аутист, и yyyyy его специальный интерес, то это на всю жизнь, такие вещи не меняются" (if xxxx is autistic and yyyyy is his special interest, that's for life, such things don't change); she says she already told the supervisor she was wrong [#13].
- Not chosen. Autistic people very often write that they do not choose their interests: intense passion for a topic seems to start and fade regardless of their wish [#13].
- Specific or popular; gender. An interest can be super-specific or quite popular. As a rule, women's special interests more often fit social expectations — for example, an interest in animals [#13].
- "Not magic." If someone digs into a topic a lot they will probably understand it well, but that does not mean they must become a genius in the field. The author adds this because for a long time she thought her special interests were not "real" since she had not become an outstanding expert in them — "Больше стараться надо было (сарказм)" (*Should have tried harder (sarcasm)*) [#13].
- From harmful to helpful. Doctors and teachers used to believe special interests were harmful and autistic people should be shielded from them. Today they are considered to raise quality of life, help communication, help cope with emotions, feel more confident and sometimes find work [#13].
- An analogy for non-autistic people. To understand what special interests are for autistic people, think of what personal relationships are for you: when you are motivated to better understand someone you like, relate to their emotions, read their facial expression, want to know them better and better, make progress yet still want to improve — and it feels like a very natural drive, a self-rewarding process — "то это вот оно" (then that's it) [#13].
- Neuroscience: no single explanation. There is no one convincing neuroscience explanation of why the autistic brain focuses so strongly on its interests. One hypothesis: capacity that in neurotypical people is occupied by processing social signals goes to it. Another: it involves the brain region responsible for directing attention to an object (the salience network), which in autistic people engaged in a favourite activity is much more active than in neurotypicals [#13].
- Use in teaching and therapy. Special interests greatly help teachers in teaching autistic people and psychologists in therapy. To lower tension in the office, talk about them. When you need to figure out where to find motivation, how to talk to people, how to work with depression or where to go to study, interests point to obvious directions worth considering [#13].

#### Hyperfocus in ADHD (the concluding post of the series)
- The post opens by asking readers to recall being so absorbed they couldn't tear away, forgetting important tasks or even bodily needs, and the frustration of explaining to people that the ability to concentrate on some things and not others "не зависит от вашего выбора" (does not depend on your choice) [#109].
- Although DSM-5 does not name hyperfocus as a direct ADHD symptom, it is widely mentioned in academic literature on ADHD. Research has shown people with ADHD experience hyperfocus more often than people in general. Despite its apparent incompatibility with other ADHD traits such as inattention and impulsivity, hyperfocus is often seen as a positive state in ADHD, because tasks are done more efficiently and for longer in it. It also has downsides (see *Pros and cons of hyperfocus*) [#109].

#### The EEG/questionnaire study of hyperfocus in autistic teens
- Framing. Hyperfocus is "when you found something interesting and four hours vanished, your back won't straighten, the tea went cold and maybe the food is still in the microwave". It is easy to recognise from description but harder to capture with instruments [#280].
- Design. Patrick Dwyer (also an autistic researcher) and colleagues at the University of California, Davis studied how hyperfocus might look in lab experiments. 18 autistic and 22 neurotypical teenagers filled in hyperfocus scales (first themselves, then separately their parents), then did psychoacoustic tasks, a visual working-memory test, a sound-distraction task and EEG, where hyperfocus is captured via the N2pc and Pd components — waves reflecting pulling attention to an object and suppressing a distracting signal [#280].
- Results:
  1. On questionnaires, autistic participants had more hyperfocus, across all four types at once: everyday, school, hobby and screen time.
  2. Autistic participants' visual working memory was smaller and their reactions on average slower.
  3. On EEG the groups did not differ at all; sound distracted autistic participants exactly as much as controls.
  4. Most importantly, the different types of measurement were almost unrelated to one another: the questionnaire score said nothing about what the EEG would show.
  [#280]
- Who describes the experience. The group difference on questionnaires was most pronounced in parents' reports: parents of autistic teens noticed more hyperfocus than the teens reported about themselves, and parents expected their children to enjoy hyperfocus more than they actually did. The teens described their hyperfocus as rather positive, though with mixed feelings — and this matched the parents' view. So teen and parent reports agree on "good or bad" but diverge on amount and on the expected delight [#280].
- Why questionnaires and lab diverged (authors' explanation). Monotropism is from the start about personal interest, while a lab task is built the opposite way: you are given the instruction "look here, ignore that", everything extra is removed from the room, and you are asked to try hard. The lab cannot supply your own passion, and measuring hyperfocus without it is "все равно что проверять любовь к плаванию в ванне" (like testing a love of swimming in a bathtub). Also, following instructions in a quiet room says little about how a person handles distractions in noisy life under load [#280].
- Catch. The sample was very small (18 and 22), so the absence of an EEG difference may simply reflect lack of statistical power. The authors themselves write that other approaches are needed — from eye-tracking to collecting data in the course of ordinary life [#280].
- Conclusion drawn by the channel. The experience of hyperfocus does not go away because of this, and monotropism remains a working frame for viewing it. What diverges is the instruments — worth remembering when a headline says some autistic trait "не подтвердилось приборами" (was not confirmed by instruments): "Иногда это значит, что прибор измеряет не то" (Sometimes it means the instrument measures the wrong thing) [#280].

#### Special interest or OCD: background and nuances
- Why they get confused. Autistic repetitive behaviours and OCD revolve around the same things: certainty, control, routine. Both involve discomfort with the unexpected, and in both people build rituals. They also often co-occur: per a meta-analysis cited, OCD is present in 11.6% of autistic children and adolescents, and autism in 9.5% of children with OCD. So the question "is it one or the other" often turns out to be "both" [#283].
- OCD can hijack an interest (the least obvious finding). Participants described OCD as something that "подкрадывается" (creeps up) and "вцепляется" (latches on) to existing routines and passions. A special interest can become material for obsession: one participant's interest in medicine and wish to harm no one turned into intrusive thoughts precisely about causing harm. Another described how an interest that touched a strong emotion slid into fixation: "про особые интересы обычно говорят, что это просто хорошо, но у них есть и темная сторона" (people usually say special interests are simply good, but they have a dark side too). So the same object of passion can be a resource and a trap at different periods [#283].
- Why the distinction matters. It is needed to know what to do next. Stims and interests are handled in the direction of "дайте им место" (give them room) — they replenish resources and regulate. OCD is treated with exposure and ritual (response) prevention; if that is mistakenly applied to stimming, the person loses a working self-regulation tool. The opposite error is costly too: obsessions get written off as autism and the person is left without help. Sorting this out on oneself is hard, and this is a case for going to a specialist who knows about both conditions [#283].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- (!) UNCLEAR: the research behind "children with autism have 8 special interests on average" and the "stunning study" asking autistic people about their interests (more eye contact, richer intonation, more complex sentences, self-initiated conversation when talking about favourite trains and dinosaurs) are not identified in the source [#13]. The latter finding: when talking about their interests, autistic people looked others in the eyes more, intoned speech more richly, used more complex sentences and started conversations on their own [#13].
- The neuroscience of special-interest focus is unresolved; both explanations (redirected social-processing capacity, salience network) are presented only as hypotheses [#13].
- The flow-in-autism equation is self-described as "Смелое заявление" (a bold claim); and the same post, previewing monotropism, calls that concept "все интересно и неоднозначно" (interesting and ambiguous) [#89]. The link between autistic executive dysfunction and lack of motivation is hedged as "Вероятно" (probably) [#96].
- Hyperfocus research is scarce: "исследований о его влиянии на мозг и мышление пока немного" (there is little research on its effect on the brain and thinking yet); hyperfocus is not a DSM-5 symptom of ADHD [#109].
- (!) CONTRADICTION (in framing, not resolved by the source): #89 describes hyperfocus as not necessarily purposeful action and frames flow as the productive, chosen state, while #109 lists increased productivity as one of the four signs of hyperfocus [#89, #109].
- The EEG study had only 18 + 22 participants; the null EEG result may reflect low statistical power, and questionnaire and lab measures barely correlated [#280].
- The OCD interview study is 15 people — "скорее набор цитат конкретных людей, как преддверие будущих исследований" (more a set of quotes from specific people, a prelude to future research). A systematic review of 31 studies found that repetitive behaviour in the two groups overlaps noticeably in intensity and content, and targeted comparisons are few (total scale scores are compared more often): "В учебнике различение выглядит чище, чем в данных" (In the textbook the distinction looks cleaner than in the data) [#283].
