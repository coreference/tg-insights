## Executive dysfunction, environment and work

### What this covers

This topic gathers what the author (a specialist who runs ADHD trainings and wrote a book about ADHD) teaches about executive dysfunction: what the term means, the four brain circuits behind it, the related autistic phenomenon of "inertia", and the everyday consequences — the "ADHD tax" and unrealistic planning — illustrated with her own stories. It also covers why neurodivergent people often have an unstable sense of self (brain networks, especially the DMN), why changing environment and context is a key self-management skill, and her basic criteria for choosing a profession and work format with ADHD and/or ASD. According to the author, environment can either help overcome executive dysfunction or worsen it, so understanding the mechanism and shaping the context are practical necessities, not luxuries [#64, #332].

### Core ideas

- Executive dysfunction is the default state in ADHD/ASD, only its intensity varies. In general it is a state where working memory, motivation, emotional regulation and will "leave" you; in ADHD and ASD they leave "by default", sometimes more, sometimes less. See *Four neural circuits* for the mechanism [#64].
- Symptom profiles differ because the four circuits are damaged to different degrees. Some people have more working-memory deficits, others more emotional-regulation or time-estimation problems [#66].
- Autistic inertia is a difficulty with starting AND stopping, not laziness or stubbornness [#425].
- ADHD inevitably costs you something ("ADHD tax"), even with strong hypercontrol, but planning reduces it [#60].
- Long-term planning of time and energy fails in ADHD; the author's own overbooked weeks and her book written to the wrong outline are cases in point [#54, #204].
- The sense of self is less stable in ADHD/ASD for neurological reasons — the DMN is not properly suppressed or runs in parallel [#733].
- Environment and context are levers: leaving home, removing the bed and endless chores from view, makes concentration much easier [#332, #534].
- There is no single right profession for ADHD/ASD; only basic criteria (flexibility or clear rules, people load, special interest, no overwork/night shifts) plus routine, food, sleep and planned rest [#544, #695].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### What executive dysfunction is
The author defines executive dysfunction as a state in which your "normal" cognitive functions have left you, listing:
- working memory (рабочая память);
- motivation (мотивация);
- emotional regulation (эмоциональная регуляция);
- will (воля) — the author ends the list with «и т.д.», so it is open-ended in the source.

In the general population this happens occasionally; in ADHD and ASD these functions leave "by default — just sometimes more, sometimes less" («при СДВГ и РАС, они покидают тебя по умолчанию»). She notes there is a lot of confusion around the term and how it relates to ADHD: everything depends on what we mean by "executive functions" and how they relate to self-regulation. As a result, when people use the term today they often describe an extremely pronounced state in which "your prefrontal cortex refuses to do its job" [#64].

Her illustration: you wake up in the morning and realise you literally cannot do anything. Yesterday you were jumping around, having fun and briskly doing your work; today — "рррраз и всё" (bam, and that's it). There will be no work. You simply cannot get out of bed, because you need to "pull yourself together" and organise the process of getting up, having breakfast and the rest of the morning — and you can't. "What work can there be if you can't manage to get out of bed and brush your teeth" [#64].

She attributes this to "incorrect" functioning of four neural circuits, joking that the list — "What", "When", "Why", "Who" — sounds like the truly Russian question «кто виноват и что делать» (who is to blame and what is to be done) [#64].

#### Four neural circuits of executive dysfunction ("What", "When", "Why", "Who")
Purpose: to understand where ADHD symptoms come from and why they differ between people [#64, #66].

1. The What circuit (Контур «Что»). Runs from the frontal lobe to the basal ganglia, in particular to a structure called the striatum (полосатое тело). It is tied to working memory: it is in this circuit that what we think starts to determine our actions — especially when it comes to plans, goals and the future. A deficit here shows up as working-memory problems [#66].
2. The When circuit (Контур «Когда»). Runs from the same prefrontal area back to the cerebellum, at the very back of the head. It is the brain's timing scheme: it coordinates not only how smooth behaviour is and its sequence, but also the timeliness of your actions and when certain actions are performed. A malfunctioning "When" circuit in a person with ADHD explains why we so often have trouble managing time [#66].
3. The Why circuit (Контур «Почему»). Also starts in the frontal lobe and passes through the central part of the brain (the anterior cingulate gyrus, передняя поясная извилина) to the amygdala — the gateway to the limbic system. It is often called the "hot" circuit because it is connected to emotions: here what we think controls what we feel, and vice versa. It is the final decision-maker in all our plans: when we are considering several things we could do, this circuit ultimately picks one based on how we feel about them and on their emotional and motivational properties [#66].
4. The Who circuit (Контур «Кто»). Runs from the frontal lobe to the very back of the hemisphere. This is where self-awareness happens — where we become aware of what we are doing, how we feel (both inside and outside) and what is happening to us [#66].

How to use the model: looking at ADHD through these four circuits lets you understand where symptoms come from. Depending on which circuits are most and which are least affected, each person shows different symptoms: some have a larger working-memory deficit; others have more problems with emotion regulation or with estimating time, but fewer with everything else. "But they are all connected to these neural circuits" [#66].

#### Three brain networks behind self-identification (DMN, Salience, Executive)
Definition first. The author defines self-identification as «осознанное внутреннее ощущение себя, основанное на переживании собственной уникальности, принадлежности и личного опыта» (a conscious inner sense of oneself based on experiencing one's own uniqueness, belonging and personal experience). It is not only "who I am" but also "how I understand myself", "what I relate myself to" and "how this influences my choices, feelings and behaviour" [#733].

Why it matters for neurodivergent people: self-identification can become an important support, especially given late diagnosis, being visibly different from others, and repeated experiences of being misunderstood. Through self-identification we can find words to describe our experience, build a stable connection with ourselves, rethink our path and learn gentle acceptance of our traits [#733].

Why it is harder: the process is not just a psychological construct but is conditioned by how the brain is built and works — which is why people with ASD and ADHD have problems with self-identification more often than neurotypical people. In the conventional "norm" several functional brain networks interact [#733]:

1. Default Mode Network (DMN) — the network of the brain's passive mode. Activates when a person is not focused on external tasks but turned inward: thinking about themselves, the past, the future, other people. It plays the central role in forming the sense of "I" and the continuity of self over time [#733].
2. Salience Network — the network of significance. Helps determine which stimuli are important and switches attention between internal and external tasks. It participates in deciding which aspects of experience a person includes in their identity and which they filter out [#733].
3. Executive Network — the ventral prefrontal network. Takes part in self-reflection and conscious control, including making sense of one's behaviour, goals and values [#733].

What goes differently in ADHD/ASD: in neurodivergent people these networks work in their own way, especially the DMN. The author repeats what she says she has explained before: in ADHD the DMN is not inhibited as it should be when the other networks activate, or even works in parallel with them. Because of this, our experience, the sense of self and of wholeness can be disrupted, provoking exactly the problems with forming a stable self-identification [#733].

#### Environment management
The author calls environment management (управление средой) a skill that neurodivergent people need extremely, whether they have ASD or ADHD: environment can either help us overcome executive dysfunction or make it worse. On her ADHD training this is taught directly [#332]:

1. Learn to notice what in your environment destabilises you — what distracts you, what drains you, what takes your strength away («отвлекает/истощает/забирает ваши силы») [#332].
2. Learn how to change the environment to suit yourself [#332].
3. Prioritise the workplace — the author says it is especially important [#332].
4. Start with small steps, not with "declutter the whole apartment". Do not set yourself the task «разобрать всю квартиру» (sort out the whole flat); begin with your physical desk or the desktop on your computer [#332].

Example from abroad: in Western countries, for instance the UK, there are separate specialists who visit families with children with ADHD, inspect their rooms and write recommendations for parents on changing the space so that the child with ADHD can both study and rest more effectively. The author stresses that for adults this is no less relevant than for children [#332].

Her own case: one day she, Liza and Masha went to work and write books at a coworking space in Peredelkino. Result: the book plan was written, a chapter for a new anthology was drafted, everything was sent to the editor, and other work tasks were partly done. She is sure that had she stayed home that day, in that weather, she would have spent most of the time sleeping or procrastinating [#332].

#### Basic criteria for choosing a profession and work format with ADHD/ASD
Context: a reader asked how to tell which profession suits someone with ASD+ADHD, what criteria it should meet, and whether it should be tied to a special interest to live in balance without sliding into hyperfocus and fixating only on that field. The author answers that there is no right or obvious answer: neurodivergent people differ — different symptoms, predispositions, the conditions they grew up and live in (which also affect career choice) and different life values. So, in her view, only basic things can be offered [#544]:

1. Potential flexibility of the job — with the caveat that many people with ASD do well with work that has a clear schedule and clear regulations [#544].
2. Check how many people tire you. Your option may be remote or hybrid work so you are not in the office every day. "Опенспейс - ад для всех" (open space is hell for everyone) [#544].
3. A job built around your special interest would be ideal if you find one [#544].
4. No overtime and no night or 24-hour shifts — "you simply won't have time to recover" [#544].

The overriding rule, whatever job you look for or find: schedule, food, sleep and planned rest — plus tracking your individual sensory profile and overload from people [#544].

On office vs remote: when a reader wrote that productivity drops in the office but at home it takes enormous effort to make oneself work, the author answered that it is important to pick what fits your particular presentation of neurodivergence — some find it easier in the office ("oh god, such people exist"), some only remotely, some need hybrid. The question is more about the field of work and the team atmosphere combined with the workload [#695].

### Detailed insights

#### Autistic inertia
A series of comic cards from watamota.com, reposted by the author, defines autistic inertia (аутичная инерция) as «ЗАТРУДНЁННАЯ СПОСОБНОСТЬ НАЧАТЬ ИЛИ ПРЕКРАТИТЬ КАКОЕ-ЛИБО ДЕЛО» (an impaired ability to start or to stop any activity). It is "a feature of perception and behaviour, and not a person's 'whim', stubbornness or laziness" [#425]. The card's character says «А Я УЖЕ ВСЁ? ТОЛЬКО НАЧАЛ!» (Am I already done? I only just started!) — showing the stopping side [#425].

The cards show the paired situations where inertia appears — each a transition that is hard in both directions:
- Waking up / going to bed — with the character stalling with "just a sec, just a sec, one second..." (ЩАС, ЩАС, СЕКУНДОЧКУ) [#424];
- Taking a break / returning to the task — on returning, the character says «ЭТО О ЧЁМ ВООБЩЕ? НИЧЕГО НЕ ПОНЯТНО» (what is this even about? I don't understand anything) [#426];
- Going into the shower / getting out of the shower [#427];
- Leaving the house / coming back [#428];
- Starting work / finishing work — at the start "I don't want to! I just sat down to rest!", at the end "I don't want to! I just got into the work!" [#429].

The lesson of the series: both starting and stopping are hard; once switched into a state (rest, shower, home, work), the person resists leaving it [#424, #425, #426, #427, #428, #429].

#### The "ADHD tax"
The author describes the "ADHD tax" (налог на СДВГ): with ADHD, inevitably — even if you have very strong hypercontrol on top — you start losing or forgetting things or failing to cope. Examples she lists: lost things, abandoned higher-education programmes that were already paid for, plane tickets for flights you were late for, "до бесконечности" (endlessly). Unfortunately, this is the "payment" for having ADHD and the overload that comes with it. Her own examples: she "paid" for a trip to a congress with her wonderful glasses, and for a conference in St Petersburg with her watch — and she has no idea where she lost them [#60].

Remedy: a well-planned schedule, a rest schedule and a list of items you check can greatly reduce "our tax" [#60].

#### Failure of long-term planning (the author's cases)
Overbooked conference trip. The author describes what the inability to plan long-term in ADHD looks like: you travel to a conference in St Petersburg; at the same time you have a talk at another conference on Saturday, and the deadlines for submitting recorded lectures for two other courses are burning. You still put the talks and workshops of the conference you plan to attend into your calendar and think: fine, I'll write one lecture on the Sapsan train, finish the rest later, and do "100500" other things in parallel. Outcome: from 8 a.m. she went out only for 15 minutes to a grocery store and back to eat something, spent the whole day writing, giving, writing and recording lectures, still had two left — and did not attend the conference at all. Her closing line: «Мозг, а можно мне НОРМАЛЬНО рассчитывать время и силы?» (Brain, can I please estimate time and energy NORMALLY?) [#54].

Writing a book to the wrong plan. She calls this her "most ADHD story" of recent years. She was offered a book deal with a very short deadline (about 3 months) and wrote all summer in all her free time from two management jobs and clients — after which burnout hit her, which she says she is still fighting. When she finished the first draft by September and sent it to the editor, it turned out she had been writing to the wrong plan the whole time: her brain had fixated on the first plan, not the final one — although the correct plan sat at the very top of the document she was writing in. "But who are we to look at it and check" [#204].

What saved her: the book's original concept — any chapter can be opened and read, not necessarily in order — so the book was quickly reassembled and the needed parts were added. The lesson she draws: a modular structure made the planning error recoverable [#204].

Then problems with the publisher and a change of editor stalled the process; when it resumed she began procrastinating desperately: "there was everything" — ADHD combined with already being burned out, shame, impostor syndrome. In the end she completely rewrote the third part of the book, which is how an important (to her) part about women's specifics appeared. She says she still does not quite believe the book exists — in her head it is still somewhere between the wrongly written plan and the long procrastination — and shares the story so readers find it easier to believe that a long project like this can be completed [#204].

#### Context and leaving home: lessons from a writing trip
During a writing trip the author shared progress (two chapters done, slower and less efficiently than she wanted) and her conclusions [#534]:
- Context matters enormously, especially with ADHD. Leaving and removing the familiar home setting — with its endless household chores you get distracted by anyway, plus the attractive bed always waiting for you — is a great idea; concentrating becomes much easier [#534].
- Clearing only client hours is not enough to write nonstop and fast. The rest of the work constantly distracts — both the work itself and thoughts about it — and other tasks that also require writing throw you off badly. As a consequence, she missed a class with students because she forgot to put it into her schedule [#534].
- Half-jokingly: there is little oxygen in Moscow, and a runny-nose virus also spoils things [#534].

Later, answering a reader who remembered she also found it hard to work during the nature trip, she said that at least back then she was actively writing and made very good progress — whereas now she is not writing it [#695].

### Practical playbook

- Reduce the ADHD tax with three tools: a well-planned schedule, a rest schedule, and a checklist of items to verify (see *The "ADHD tax"*) [#60].
- Use a modular structure (chapters readable in any order) for long projects so a planning mistake can be fixed by reassembly [#204].
- When you need concentrated output, take a dedicated block of time away from home and clear not only client hours but other work obligations too; still put every commitment into the schedule [#534].

## ADHD skills training, coaching and organization tools

### What this covers

This topic covers the practical help formats for adult ADHD the author works with: the group CBT skills training (Young-Bramham protocol) she runs with Maria Novikova, how it is organized, what the pre-group interview is for, and why there are no combined ASD+ADHD groups; ADHD coaching as a separate format; everyday organization tools (capture lists, paper "genius ideas" sheet, a planner and the Singularity app); her own route back to reading; and her repeated message that ADHD skills fade and must be relearned. According to the author, generic time-management methods fail people with ADHD because they are used piecemeal and not adapted to neurodivergence, so the point of training is to build skills into a personal system and to keep returning to them [#840, #675].

### Core ideas

- Capture everything outside your head. With ADHD, ideas come "on the go" and are then forgotten; a phone to-do list that is always with you plus a paper sheet for ideas during work reduce forgetting (see *Two-tool capture system*) [#220].
- There is no universal planning system. What matters is finding what fits you; the one firm rule is that calendar and to-do list are always with you [#252].
- Skills training builds a system, not a collection of tricks. Methods fail when used separately and unadapted; the training teaches how to assemble skills into one individual system, plus peer exchange of life hacks [#840].
- The training format itself teaches time sense. No recordings are made, because a person with ADHD given a recording will not watch it; live attendance is part of the method [#570].
- ADHD skills are not installed for life. They depend on context, fall away after illness, moves or emotional swings, and must be relearned — even by the author, who teaches them constantly [#675, #694].
- Groups need homogeneous deficits. ADHD is homogeneous enough for a single protocol; ASD is not, and ASD+ADHD even less so, so no combined groups exist [#803].
- Skills without changing the environment rarely help. If the environment exhausts you, knowing skills alone is unlikely to be enough [#700].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Two-tool capture system
Purpose: fewer "forgettings" and a way to organize life with ADHD. The author introduces it by describing the typical ADHD cycle around writing posts: you come up with a great topic on the go, then naturally forget it, then remember it but are too lazy to write, then start suffering and postpone even more — "стандартная СДВГшная жизнь" (standard ADHD life). She notes that she intended to write about something else entirely — itself an illustration [#220].

1. A notebook or to-do list where you write everything. Ideally, in current conditions, it should be electronic and convenient, so that when you are walking down the street and suddenly come up with a post idea or remember something important, you can quickly open the phone and immediately write it into your to-do app — or dictate it by voice [#220].
2. "Листок гениальных идей" (sheet of genius ideas). Put a single sheet of paper or a tear-off notepad next to your workplace. When your "incredibly genius ideas" come to mind while working, write them there *without getting distracted by them* and go back to work. When you finish work, look at the sheet, cross out the absolute nonsense, and move the genuinely good ideas and things you will do into the to-do list. Then return to step 1 and plan when exactly you will do that item [#220].

Why it works (author's logic): the paper sheet lets you park an idea without breaking focus, and the later sorting step separates impulses from real tasks before they enter the plan [#220].

#### Five ways back to reading with ADHD
Context: the author and friends discussed what to do about compulsive phone scrolling ("залипанием в телефон"), which "не даёт ни отдыха, ни чувства прожитой жизни" (gives neither rest nor a sense of a life lived). Reading is one of her ways to cope, but "#СДВГ и чтение это так слоооожнооо" (ADHD and reading is sooo haaard): although books have been her special interest since childhood, in recent years she could not read at all, "прям до слёз" (to the point of tears). Five things helped her return to reading this year instead of scrolling [#289]:

1. A small e-reader. She loves paper books, but besides threatening to push her out of her home, they are hard and heavy to carry. A small e-reader goes anywhere, even on walks with the dogs. Pitfall: reading on a phone or tablet is "провальная история" (a failed story) — there are a million options to get distracted by everything else on the device [#289].
2. Start with something light. She came back to reading through fantasy. Her inner critic protested ("мы в 1 классе прочитали хоббита… какое ромфентэзи в 30 😱" — *we read The Hobbit in first grade… what's with romantasy at 30*), but she reminds herself what it is for: (a) to *start* reading — which is also a skill of decomposition and reinforcement, so anything that grips you works; (b) the goal is not to scroll but to rest, and serious books often do NOT suit rest [#289].
3. Audiobooks — see *Audiobook ladder* below [#289].
4. Schedule. Allocate time in your schedule specifically for reading. It does not have to be one, two or three hours — 10 minutes, 15, half an hour. The key is to stop if hyperfocus begins [#289].
5. Praise yourself [#289].

#### Audiobook ladder
A three-rung rule from the reading post [#289]:
1. "если не читается - слушай" (if it doesn't read — listen).
2. "если не слушается - делай с этим что-то впараллель" (if it doesn't listen — do something in parallel with it).
3. "если прям нужно вникнуть - читай и слушай параллельно одну и ту же книгу" (if you really need to get into it — read and listen to the same book in parallel).

#### Structure of the 8-session ADHD skills training (Young-Bramham protocol)
The CBT skills group the author runs with Maria Novikova follows the Young-Bramham protocol [#758, #840]. According to the author, all current ADHD skills-training protocols look alike: 8 meetings, once a week, 2 hours each [#570, #599]. Content by session, as she describes it [#570]:
1. Sessions 1–3: organization and planning, and working with time blindness.
2. Sessions 4–6: executive dysfunction — working with emotions, inhibition skills, self-motivation and self-reinforcement.
3. Sessions 7–8: organizing space and relapse prevention.

Each session covers a new group of skills [#570].

No recordings: the author never records meetings. Reason one: it contradicts the setting of ADHD groups — "дайте человеку с СДВГ запись чего-либо и он не придет и не посмотрит" (give a person with ADHD a recording of anything and they won't come and won't watch it); the task of her and all ADHD trainers is to teach a sense of time and the assimilation of skills. Reason two: confidentiality [#570].

#### Format of a single 2-hour skills-group session
Answering a reader who asked how a session runs (lecture, questions, stretch breaks), the author lists the components [#599, #570]:
1. First hour — homework review. Who succeeded at what, what did not work and why; consolidating the week's skills. Part of the discussion happens in small groups where participants themselves discuss and share successes — this helps them immediately start using skills independently [#599, #570].
2. 10-minute break [#599].
3. Second hour — new skills. Mostly a lecture part, but in some sessions there are exercises to practice the skills [#599, #570].
4. Then practice at home until the next week [#599].
5. Movement allowed. Participants are always allowed to walk around and move during the training [#599].

#### Three tasks of the pre-group interview
Source: cards forwarded from the NeuroDive channel. The word "собеседование" (interview) makes many people anxious, because it is associated with job hiring or project selection ("Я должен себя проявить, чтобы меня взяли, как на работу?" — *Must I prove myself to be taken, like for a job?*). The post sets out to explain what the interview is, why it is needed and why not to fear it [#758, img #758].

Definition: the interview before forming a group is a dialogue in which the trainers get to know potential participants: "Мы хотим узнать вас, выслушать жалобы и подсказать, насколько наша работа может быть вам полезна" (We want to get to know you, hear your complaints and advise how useful our work can be for you) [#759].

1. Learn needs and goals. The trainers want to make sure the group will suit you and help solve your problems. You share your complaints, and they tell you whether the training will be useful specifically for you — so as not to waste money [#760].
2. Screen out contraindications. There are reasons why a person will not be able to do the skills-training tasks needed for effective therapy, for example severe depression, the active phase of an addiction, psychosis. In that case the recommendation is first to relieve the acute state and then return to the group [#761].
3. Assess motivation. The trainers need to understand how ready you are for the training, again to avoid unnecessary financial and time costs. So at the interview they explain the format of the sessions and how the process will run, and answer all questions [#762].

Closing points: the interview "ЭТО НЕ ПОСТАНОВКА ДИАГНОЗА" (is not diagnosis) — it is a conversation to ensure comfortable and effective interaction for every group member. After the dialogue you can be enrolled if your difficulties match the training topic — "Даже если у вас нет подтверждённого диагноза СДВГ" (even if you have no confirmed ADHD diagnosis) [#763].

#### Three properties of ADHD skills
From the post "Навыки при СДВГ не вбиваются навсегда — и это нормально" (Skills with ADHD are not hammered in forever — and that's normal). Clients often say: "Я прошёл(а) тренинг, было лучше, но теперь всё опять развалилось. Наверное, со мной что-то не так" (I did the training, it was better, but now everything has fallen apart again. Something must be wrong with me), or that if skills "fall off", maybe they don't work at all. The author's "little secret": skills with ADHD (and in neurodivergent people generally) are not consolidated forever, even if they helped perfectly before [#675]. Skills:
1. "живут не в голове, а в связке с контекстом" (live not in the head but bound to context) — when the context changes, the skill may not fire [#675].
2. "требуют переобучения после сбоев" (require relearning after disruptions) [#675].
3. "не закрепляются на “всю жизнь”" (are not fixed "for life"), especially if you are ill, moving house or caught in emotional swings [#675].

What to do: it does not mean skills are useless — it means "вы живой человек с живой нервной системой, которая не всегда может удерживать структуру" (you are a living person with a living nervous system that cannot always hold structure). If everything that worked has "fallen off", it is not your fault and not a "breakdown", but a natural consequence of changes. Don't be ashamed or angry — just start again, and not with a big task but with a small act of self-care. Maintaining skills is a separate skill. For people with ADHD skills are "не задача, не этап, это путь" (not a task, not a stage, but a path) [#675].

### Detailed insights

#### The author's own skill relapses
- After surgery, during two weeks of recovery, the author dropped out of all her habits: food, water, cleaning, self-care. Not because she "stopped wanting to live mindfully", but because the body switched to survival and the brain to "эконом-режим" (economy mode). Each time this happens she has to remind herself that maintaining skills is a separate skill — even though she knows the skills by heart, "хоть ночью разбуди" (wake me at night and I'll recite them) [#675].
- Responding to a reader's feeling that with ADHD and ASD you have to keep *learning* all your life — you understood, read and memorized everything, then after a while it is as if erased and you relearn — the author "comforts" readers with her own record: about 8 ADHD trainings per year for about 5 years, many ADHD clients in therapy earlier, a written book, constant articles/posts/podcasts on the topic, and doing the skills herself for about 6 years. "И ВСЕ РАВНО ОНИ ВЫПАДАЮТ И К НИМ ПОСТОЯННО НАДО ЗАНОВО ВОЗВРАЩАТЬСЯ" (AND STILL THEY DROP OUT AND YOU CONSTANTLY HAVE TO COME BACK TO THEM). Motto: "У нас нет цели - только путь" (We have no goal — only the path) [#694].

#### Why generic methods fail and what training adds
Many people with ADHD, by adulthood, have tried many time-management variants and other common aids, especially from business culture, are quickly disappointed, and conclude "я все попробовал, но ничего не работает" (I tried everything, but nothing works). The author's explanation: it is not that nothing works, but that these methods are (1) usually applied piecemeal and (2) not adapted to neurodivergence, so no common framework and structure for using helpful skills forms. In the skills training they teach not only separate skills but how to build them into a single system unique to each person with ADHD, and participants constantly exchange life hacks, which many later name as extremely useful [#840].

#### ADHD coaching
- What it is: the author calls coaching for people with ADHD one of the excellent and proven ways of helping. The format differs from psychotherapy and from skills training: the coach helps step by step to build the life of a person with ADHD, often acting in parallel as a buddy ("бадди"). Besides techniques and skills, you can sit down with the coach and plan your week, set priorities. The coach is in principle on call and constantly checks what you did and didn't do — "работает кнутом и пряником" (works with stick and carrot) [#559].
- When it fits: when a person at some point in life cannot cope with daily life and organization on their own [#559].
- Why it is scarce: it requires specific skills and deep immersion in ADHD and is a heavy load on the specialist. So ADHD coaching is in high demand worldwide, yet such specialists are very few. In the Russian-speaking space a search returns many people, but it is very hard to verify whether they really understand ADHD, have coaching education *and* specifically ADHD-coaching education, which has many particular nuances [#559].
- Training is costly: according to the author, essentially only one institute (ADDCA) teaches ADHD coaching; she and Maria Novikova have long looked at its programs, but the cost is 8 thousand dollars per level and two levels are needed. Few can afford it, so certified ADHD coaches are also expensive — "такая грустная история с коучингом СДВГ" (such a sad story with ADHD coaching) [#559].

#### Why there are no ASD+ADHD skills groups
- A reader asked whether separate support groups for ASD+ADHD would appear, noting that groups and even tests exist separately for ASD and for ADHD. The author: there are no AuDHD trainings and it is not certain there ever will be. ADHD is more homogeneous, so a single protocol can be made. With ASD it is harder: even at the same support level it is extremely difficult to assemble a group with similar deficits. The ASD+ADHD combination would most likely not be very effective as a group. She leaves it open: "посмотрим, что как сложится дальше" (we'll see how things develop) [#803].
- To a mother with AuDHD struggling with sensory overload while caring for a 3-year-old (asking for help already used 100% and exhausted; several days a week together 24 hours, with meltdowns and shutdowns), the author answered that there are no protocols as such for adult ASD skills training, but she and Maria Novikova recently made an ASD self-help training for "Справиться Проще". Her caveat: if you do not change the environment that exhausts you, mere knowledge of skills is unlikely to help [#700].

## Emotional dysregulation, rejection sensitivity and relationships

### What this covers

This topic gathers the author's posts and Q&A answers about the emotional side of neurodivergence: what "rejection sensitive dysphoria" (RSD) is claimed to be and why the author doubts it is a separate condition; why people with autism, ADHD, trauma or high sensory sensitivity often feel emotions very intensely and what helps; why it is worth learning to notice emotions even when one barely feels them (autism with emotional hyposensitivity); and two relationship questions — a partner's anger outbursts and a man who blamed his infidelity on ADHD. Across these, the author keeps one line: emotional differences are real and not a weakness, but building regulation skills and making choices stays each person's own responsibility [#280, #681, #698, #707, #866].

### Core ideas

- RSD adds nothing new. Its signs are already explained by the CBT model of ADHD and the bio-psycho-social model; research is almost absent; the remedy is the same as for emotional dysregulation in general (see *Author's three objections*) [#280].
- Neurodivergent emotional sensitivity is neurophysiological, not "character". Dysregulation is common in ADHD and autism, and it is a feature of sensory and emotional regulation that can be learned to recognise and support (see *What helps*) [#681].
- Emotions are a signal system, like pain sensitivity. Both a muted and an over-weighted signal are harmful, so we learn to tell which emotions mislead and which report something important (see *Emotions as a signal system*) [#707].
- Regulation is the person's own job. A partner can show love, but learning anger control belongs to the person with ADHD; likewise ADHD does not decide whether someone cheats — the person does [#698, #866].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Proposed signs of RSD (8-item list)
The author opens by defining RSD: «Дисфория чувствительности к отвержению (RSD) — это когда вы испытываете сильную эмоциональную боль из-за неудачи или чувства отверженности» (*Rejection sensitive dysphoria (RSD) is when you experience intense emotional pain from failure or a feeling of being rejected*). It is most often linked to ADHD, and experts suspect it arises from differences in brain structure. The author then asks «есть она всё-таки или нет?» (does it actually exist or not?) and lists the signs that are offered as describing what people with RSD face [#280]:
1. They can very easily feel embarrassed or awkward [#280].
2. They show signs of low self-esteem and problems believing in themselves [#280].
3. They find it hard to contain their emotions when they feel rejected; this is often visible in children and teenagers with the condition [#280].
4. Some may react with sudden displays of anger or rage, while others may burst into tears [#280].
5. Instead of losing control of their emotions outwardly, some people with RSD may direct their feelings inward [#280].
6. They often "please people" («угождают людям») and try very hard to avoid others' disapproval [#280].
7. They may avoid starting projects, tasks or pursuing goals where failure is possible [#280].
8. They compensate for the fear of failure or rejection by giving their all or striving for perfectionism; the downside is that they often feel strong anxiety and cannot easily make self-care or downtime a priority [#280].

#### Author's three objections to RSD as a separate condition
The author says several things personally bother her («Меня лично смущают несколько вещей», *a few things personally bother me*) [#280]:
1. Existing models already explain every sign. All these signs are explained by the CBT model of ADHD and by the bio-psycho-social model. These have a biological part (high impulsivity and difficulties with emotional regulation) and a part tied to experience of upbringing and failures, through which we acquire core beliefs («глубинные убеждения») about ourselves and the world. So no new entity is needed to account for them [#280].
2. There is practically no research. According to the author, PubMed returns no studies on this topic at all, other sources give vague references, and there is essentially one doctor, William Dodson, who has been pushing («педалирует») the topic since 1990 [#280].
3. What is the function of singling it out? The author grants that, most often, our experience of failure and rejection is extremely painful because of this, and that many people have emotional dysregulation problems — but far from all people with ADHD. It again depends on experience, upbringing and the combination of individual ADHD traits, so labelling it as a distinct condition does not add explanatory value [#280].

Conclusion: «ничего нового и революционного не происходит, расходимся» (nothing new or revolutionary is happening, let's disperse). What helps is still the same: individual therapy, emotional regulation skills, and accepting that people may reject and not understand us [#280].

#### What helps with neurodivergent emotional sensitivity (4 supports)
Context: many people with autism, ADHD, traumatic experience or heightened sensory sensitivity are emotionally sensitive, «Иногда — очень» (sometimes very) — contrary to the myth that autistic people have no emotions [#681]. The author lists what helps:
1. Normalisation — telling oneself: «мне не нужно объяснять, почему мне сейчас тяжело — я просто так устроен(а)» (I don't need to explain why it's hard for me right now — I'm just built this way) [#681].
2. Regular "safety pauses" («паузы безопасности») throughout the day [#681].
3. Support from loved ones that does not demand explanations [#681].
4. Self-regulation skills — breathing, movement, simple sensory markers («простые сенсорные маркеры») [#681].
(!) INCOMPLETE: the source names the supports but does not describe how long safety pauses last or what counts as a "sensory marker".

The guiding idea: «Эмоциональная чувствительность — не ошибка, а сигнал» (emotional sensitivity is not a mistake but a signal); if you learn to interact with it rather than suppress it, it becomes not an enemy but a navigator. Personally, the author says what helps her recover is dogs — contact with them, their unconditional love and joy in the world — and of course walks [#681].

#### Emotions as a signal system (pain-sensitivity analogy)
Answer to an autistic reader (diagnosed ASD, apparently emotionally hyposensitive) whose psychotherapist advised tracking emotions; she felt resistance because she usually feels «ровно-спокойно» (even and calm) and asked whether one needs to dig into emotions and why [#707].
1. Starting fact: people with ASD indeed most often have much lower understanding of their own emotions than non-autistic people [#707].
2. Against the pop-psychology cult: in modern psychological pop culture emotions are sometimes raised to the skies — everything must be felt, lived through and expressed, "and God forbid you don't" [#707].
3. The body's view: emotions are «просто одна из сигнальных систем нашего тела. Не больше и не меньше» (just one of our body's signal systems. No more, no less). Evolutionarily it does not pay to think everything through cognitively — "you'd be eaten while thinking" — and emotions let us react fast. They also have a social function, which the author says she will cover separately [#707].
4. The analogy: emotions are like pain sensitivity. It is bad when it is muted — you can get burned; it is bad when too much weight is given to it — it can seem to hurt and sting where everything is fine [#707].
5. Purpose of tracking: that is why we learn to distinguish emotions — to understand where they lie to us («подвирают») and where they report something important [#707].

### Detailed insights

#### How neurodivergent emotional sensitivity shows up
The author cites three points as "what science says" [#681]:
- Emotional dysregulation (difficulties managing emotions) occurs in 30–70% of adults with ADHD [#681].
- Autistic people often show both heightened emotional intensity and a delay in becoming aware of their emotions [#681].
- Sensory overload directly affects emotional reactions, especially combined with anxiety or fatigue [#681].
Hence, she says, it is not "character" and not "winding oneself up" («накрутил себя») but a neurophysiological feature of emotion processing [#681].

Manifestations listed [#681]:
- A person may flare up sharply over a trifle — and not understand why themselves.
- Emotions "stick": the situation is over, but inside it is still noisy.
- It can be hard to put into words what exactly is being felt.
- Or the opposite: the feeling comes down like an avalanche, as if everything at once.
The author stresses there is no weakness in this: it is not "drama", "manipulation" or "infantilism" but a feature of sensory and emotional regulation that can be learned to recognise and support [#681].

#### A partner's anger outbursts in ADHD
A reader wrote that her boyfriend with ADHD has anger problems: they fight often because he dislikes something in her words or actions, he is often irritated, and in fights he fixates on one topic and cannot move off it even when the discussion goes in circles. She walks "as if on a minefield", fearing the next outburst, though she knows he loves her and is ashamed of it. She asked whether this is normal for ADHD and how to behave and help [#698].
The author's answer: emotional dysregulation in ADHD is fairly common, and most often it shows exactly as bursts of irritability and anger. But fighting dysregulation is the person's own task: the partner can show that she loves him in any case, while «освоение навыков контроля гнева ответственность вашего партнёра» (mastering anger-control skills is your partner's responsibility) [#698].

#### ADHD does not excuse infidelity
A reader saw a post in which a man justified cheating by having ADHD and asked whether it is really impossible with ADHD to stay faithful and build stable, lasting relationships [#866]. The author's answer:
- Cases like this are why many specialists and lay people start either devaluing or getting angry at people with ADHD [#866].
- "Of course not": even if you are impulsive and have regulation problems, «все равно только ты выбираешь как тебе поступать» (it is still only you who chooses how to act) [#866].
- Second, there are skills that help you build your life [#866].
- Her ironic close: «Так то удобно спихнуть измены на СДВГ» (it's convenient, of course, to dump infidelity on ADHD). She notes she gave a whole interview on this subject on another channel [#866].
