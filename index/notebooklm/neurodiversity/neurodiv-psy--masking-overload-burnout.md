## Masking, the CAT-Q questionnaire and unmasking

### What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel taught about autistic masking (camouflaging): the hidden cost of masking for the masker, a complete Russian-language self-report tool for measuring it (the CAT-Q, translated and adapted by the channel author Юлия Ан with editor Фрэнки Друг), and the practice of unmasking — showing neurodivergent traits more openly, and accepting that some people will then dislike you. According to the source, masking is a survival mechanism learned especially by people diagnosed late or not at all, it is associated with psychological stress and problems in functioning, and the lower one's masking score the better [#52, #192, #258, #259].

### Core ideas

- Invisible autism is not mild autism. When others say "you don't look autistic", it is because the person has spent most of their life minimising autism's impact on others — for others' sake — while feeling it twice as strongly themselves [#52].
- Masking can be measured. The CAT-Q estimates how much a person over 16 masks and/or compensates for autistic traits in social interaction; it is not a diagnostic tool and its Russian version is not standardised, so results need caution [#189, file #192].
- More masking is worse, not better. In the questionnaire's interpretation, higher scores reflect "better" masking but are linked to psychological stress and functional problems, so ideally one's score should be as low as possible [#192].
- Masking was learned for survival. Undiagnosed autistic people quickly learned to adjust their personality to others to fit in, because their behaviour was treated as unacceptable; as adults they keep believing they must mask to be liked [#258, #259].
- Unmasking means accepting being disliked. One of the hardest parts of unmasking is learning not to be liked; being disliked is not your responsibility and does not mean you are wrong or guilty [#257, #260, #261].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### CAT-Q — Camouflaging Autistic Traits Questionnaire (Russian version)
What it is and who it is for. «Опросник камуфляжа аутичных черт (Camouflaging Autistic Traits Questionnaire, CAT-Q)» (Camouflaging Autistic Traits Questionnaire) is a questionnaire about camouflaging — masking — in the social behaviour of people over 16. It helps assess how strongly you mask and/or compensate for your autistic traits during social interactions. The author notes it is especially relevant for women and nonbinary people. In the questionnaire's own description it is a self-report method used to assess strategies of compensating for and masking autistic traits during social interactions. Original authors: Laura Hull et al.; translation and adaptation by Юлия Ан; editor Фрэнки Друг [#189, file #192].

What it is not. «Опросник не служит для диагностики аутизма.» ("The questionnaire is not for diagnosing autism.") It helps one explore one's individual autistic features. The author stresses that results should be treated with caution because «опросник не был апробирован и стандартизирован на русскоязычной выборке!» ("the questionnaire has not been tested and standardised on a Russian-speaking sample!") [#189].

How to fill it in. Read each statement and choose the answer that best describes your experience during social interactions, marking the matching cell with a tick or a cross. The form has fields for name (or pseudonym) and date of completion [#192]. The 7-point answer scale:
1. Strongly disagree («Совершенно не согласен») — 1 point
2. Disagree — 2 points
3. Partly disagree — 3 points
4. Can neither agree nor disagree — 4 points
5. Partly agree — 5 points
6. Agree — 6 points
7. Fully agree («Полностью согласен») — 7 points
[#192]

The 25 statements (English rendering of the Russian items; reverse-scored items marked *, factor in brackets: C = Compensation, M = Masking, A = Assimilation) [#192]:
1. When I talk to someone, I consciously copy their body language or facial expression. [C]
2. I monitor my body language and facial expression to seem relaxed. [M]
3. I rarely feel the need to pretend (play a role) in order to get through a social situation.* [A]
4. I have developed a script that I follow in social situations. [C]
5. I repeat phrases I have heard from others in exactly the form I first heard them. [C]
6. I adjust my body language and facial expression so as to look interested in the person I am talking to. [M]
7. In social situations I feel as if I am "playing a role" rather than being myself. [A]
8. During social interactions I use behaviour patterns I learned by watching other people interact. [C]
9. I always think about the impression I make on other people. [M]
10. I need other people's support in order to take part in communication. [A]
11. I practise my facial expressions and body language so that they look natural. [C]
12. I do not feel the need to look other people in the eyes if I do not want to.* [M]
13. I have to force myself to interact with people when I am in social situations. [A]
14. I have tried to improve my understanding of social skills by watching other people. [C]
15. I monitor my body language and/or facial expression to look interested in the person I am talking to. [M]
16. In social situations I try to find ways to avoid interacting with other people. [A]
17. I have studied the rules of social interaction to improve my social skills. [C]
18. I am always aware of the impression I make on other people. [M]
19. I feel I can be myself when I am with other people.* [A]
20. I study the facial expressions and movements/gestures of people interacting with each other while watching TV, films or reading fiction. [C]
21. I adjust my body language and facial expressions so as to look relaxed. [M]
22. When I talk with people, I feel the conversation flows naturally.* [A]
23. I have spent some time learning social skills from TV series and films, and I try to apply them in communication. [C]
24. In social situations I do not pay attention to my gestures/movements and facial expression.* [M]
25. In social situations I feel as if I am pretending to be "normal". [A]

#### CAT-Q scoring and interpretation
1. Score each answer 1–7, where 1 = "Strongly disagree" and 7 = "Fully agree" [#192].
2. Reverse-score the starred items 3, 12, 19, 22 and 24 (7 down to 1): for these, 7 = "Strongly disagree" and 1 = "Fully agree". These are the statements phrased in the non-masking direction (e.g. feeling one can be oneself, conversation flowing naturally), so agreeing with them means less camouflaging [#192].
3. Add up all 25 items. The total ranges from 25 to 175; a higher score reflects «лучшую маскировку» ("better masking") [#192].
4. Apply the threshold: «Общий балл 100 или выше указывает на то, что вы маскируете аутичные черты.» ("A total score of 100 or higher indicates that you mask autistic traits.") [#192]
5. Read the meaning of a high score correctly: higher scores are associated with psychological stress and problems in functioning, «поэтому в идеале вам нужно, чтобы ваш балл был как можно ниже» ("so ideally you want your score to be as low as possible") [#192].
6. Interpret with caution, because the questionnaire is not standardised for a Russian-speaking sample [#192].
7. Optionally compute the three factor subscores (next framework) and compare them, and the total, with the English-sample means (see *Evidence and numbers*) [#192].

#### CAT-Q three-factor structure
The 25 items split into three factors, each describing a different kind of camouflaging strategy [#192]:
1. Compensation («Компенсация») — items 1, 4, 5, 8, 11, 14, 17, 20 and 23 (9 items). Strategies used to actively compensate for difficulties in social situations. Examples given: copying body language and facial expressions, learning social cues from films and books [#192].
2. Masking («Маскировка») — items 2, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21 and 24 (8 items). Strategies used to hide autistic characteristics or to portray a non-autistic persona. Examples given: changing the face and body so they look confident and/or relaxed, forced eye contact [#192].
3. Assimilation («Ассимиляция») — items 3, 7, 10, 13, 16, 19, 22 and 25 (8 items). Strategies used to try to fit in with others in social situations. Examples given: pretending, avoiding interaction, or forcing oneself to interact with others [#192].

#### Unmasking — what it is and what it involves
Definition. The author defines unmasking: «анмаскинг (или размаскировка) - это процесс, при котором мы стараемся меньше скрывать свои нейроотличные черты в общении» ("unmasking is the process in which we try to hide our neurodivergent traits less in communication") [#257].

Concrete unmasking behaviours named by the author [#257]:
1. Stimming more («больше стиммим»).
2. Putting in earplugs when it is noisy.
3. Asking again when something is unclear.

The inner critic that persists. The author reports noticing that she masks less in many situations, yet a voice in her head still demands: «"Смотри в глаза!", "Следи за лицом", "Убери фиджет в рюкзак, а то что люди подумают!"» ("Look them in the eyes!", "Watch your face", "Put the fidget in your backpack, or what will people think!"). The lesson: reducing masking behaviourally does not immediately silence the internalised demands to mask — the same demands that CAT-Q items about eye contact and facial monitoring measure [#257].

Unmasking is a choice of priorities. The author asks readers whether they are trying to mask less, whether they currently have other priorities, or whether masking is for them «вопрос выживания» ("a matter of survival") — i.e., she frames unmasking as optional and context-dependent, not an obligation [#257].

The "learning not to be liked" sequence (carousel by @elenacarrOll, translated by the author) [#257, #258, #259, #260, #261]:
1. The hardest part: «Одна из самых непростых вещей в анмаскинге - это научиться не нравиться людям» ("One of the most difficult things in unmasking is learning not to be liked by people") — rendered here from the card text spread over lines [#257].
2. Where masking came from: those of us whose autism went undiagnosed for a long time (or still is undiagnosed) very quickly learned to adjust our personality to those around us in order to fit in; masking became a survival mechanism [#258].
3. Why it mattered: this survival mechanism can be very important for autistic people because we learn that our behaviour is often considered unacceptable in society [#259].
4. The belief that outlives its usefulness: when we grow up, we often keep holding the belief that we need to mask in order to be liked by people [#259].
5. Why the first unmasked steps feel unsafe: we may feel especially unsafe the first time we risk showing our real self and not being liked; for some this means dropping a "pleasant person" mask that has been taking root for 20–30 years [#260].
6. The reframe: although unmasking may seem terribly frightening and uncomfortable, being disliked by someone is not your responsibility; nor does it mean you are wrong or guilty in some way. It is part of life, and you are doing the right thing by unmasking [#261].

### Detailed insights

#### The hidden cost of "not looking autistic"
A translated comic by Nicole (Sensory Stories) captures the core mechanism of masking's cost. To "Они: Ты не выглядишь аутичной!" ("Them: You don't look autistic!"), the speaker answers that you don't see it because her autism mainly affects *her*: she spent most of her life minimising its impact on others — «ради них, не ради себя» ("for their sake, not for mine") — and so now the other person doesn't see it, «но я ощущаю в два раза сильнее» ("but I feel it twice as strongly"). The lesson: the invisibility of autistic traits is produced by effort that is shifted onto the autistic person, and the cost to them grows rather than disappears. The channel posted it under the tags #поддержка (support) and #аутичныемемы (autistic memes) [#52].

#### Who masks more, according to the CAT-Q materials
The author states that measuring camouflaging is especially relevant for women and nonbinary people [#189]. The English-sample norms reproduced in the questionnaire (see *Evidence and numbers*) show autistic women and nonbinary people with the highest total scores [#192].

## Meltdowns, shutdowns and autistic social hangover

### What this covers

This topic covers two linked phenomena of overload in autistic people. First, meltdowns and shutdowns: what they are, what they are not, how the outward meltdown differs from the inward shutdown, and the early distress signals that a meltdown is near. Second, "social hangover" (социальное похмелье): the day-after exhaustion that follows socializing in groups, its autistic-specific symptoms, its causes (more interactions, social anxiety, masking, sensory overload) and five strategies for preparing and recovering. According to the source, it matters because these reactions are common for autistic people, are often misread as tantrums or manipulation, and are hard to see coming; understanding one's own traits and defending boundaries is presented as the main help [#128, #129, #167].

### Core ideas

- Meltdowns and shutdowns are stress reactions of an overloaded psyche, not panic attacks, sulking, hysterics or manipulation; they pursue no goal and are nothing shameful or frightening [#128].
- A meltdown is directed outward and is easy to notice; a shutdown is directed inward, the person closes off from the world. Both are triggered by sensory and/or emotional overload and/or abrupt changes [#128].
- Autistic people often find it hard to notice an approaching meltdown, but there are recognizable distress signs that a breakdown is close [#129].
- Group socializing can produce a "social hangover" the next day; in autistic people its symptoms differ somewhat from the introvert version and its causes include more separate interactions, social anxiety, the effort of masking autistic traits, and sensory overload [#163, #164, #167].
- What helps with social hangover is, "as always", understanding one's own traits and defending one's boundaries, made concrete in five strategies [#167].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Meltdown vs shutdown comparison
Purpose: to distinguish the two forms an autistic stress reaction can take. The channel's card defines both as "реакции аутичного мозга на сенсорную, эмоциональную перегрузку и/ или резкие изменения" (reactions of the autistic brain to sensory, emotional overload and/or abrupt changes) [#128].

1. Meltdown (мелтдаун, срыв) — "Реакция направленная наружу, легко заметить" (a reaction directed outward, easy to notice). It may show as: aggression; shouting; throwing things around; tears; attempts to push the other person away; self-harm (attempts to hurt oneself); repeating the same thing over and over; running away [#128].
2. Shutdown (шатдаун) — a reaction directed inward ("внутрь"). The person seems to close themselves off from the world, may become nonverbal, stop answering, and feel exhausted and sleepy [#128].

The accompanying post adds that both occur in children and in adults, and that although they are typical of autistic people, they also happen in non-autistic people, only more rarely. They are "просто реакции перегруженной психики на стресс" (simply reactions of an overloaded psyche to stress) [#128].

#### Early signs of an autistic meltdown (8-item checklist)
Purpose: autistic people often struggle to recognize that a meltdown is approaching; these distress signs help to track that "срыв близко" (a breakdown is near). The card is by Thomas Henley (@ThomasHenleyUK), translated by the channel [#129, img #129]. The signs:

1. Increasing dissociation or brain fog ("туман в голове") [#129].
2. Becoming nonverbal — a temporary loss of speech [#129].
3. Emotional overload [#129].
4. Reduced executive functions — e.g. attention control, behavioural inhibition, and others [#129].
5. Confusion / feeling lost ("растерянность") [#129].
6. Heightened sensitivity to sensory triggers [#129].
7. Paranoia, a feeling of being judged by the people around you [#129].
8. You feel that the breakdown is approaching [#129].

#### Symptoms of social hangover in autistic people (8-item list)
The author says the autistic version of social hangover can look somewhat different from the introvert definition. Symptoms listed (card and post give the same list) [#165, msg #167]:

1. Unpleasant physical sensations, for example headaches or stomach aches [#165, msg #167].
2. Increased or decreased sensory sensitivity [#165, msg #167].
3. A feeling of strong fatigue [#165, msg #167].
4. Difficulties communicating, loss of social skills [#165, msg #167].
5. A need for time alone [#165, msg #167].
6. Repetitive negative thoughts [#165, msg #167].
7. An (increased) need for stimming — the card says "Повышенная потребность в стимминге" (increased need for stimming), the post "Потребность в стимминге" (need for stimming) [#165, msg #167].
8. Irritability — the list ends with "и др." (and others), i.e. the source itself presents it as non-exhaustive [#165, msg #167].

#### Causes of social hangover in autistic people
Why a group gathering costs more than one-on-one contact, per the card and post [#164, msg #167]:

1. More separate interactions / more information to process. With many people to talk to there are more individual interactions, in each of which you must listen carefully and come up with answers; the card frames it as more information to process than in one-on-one communication [#164, msg #167].
2. More social anxiety — worry about saying something stupid and not fitting in [#167].
3. Masking before each person. The need to adjust to each individual person and to hide one's autistic traits ("скрывать свои аутичные черты") in front of each one separately takes more effort [#164, msg #167].
4. Sensory overload. Group socializing often comes with a heavy sensory load: noise, unfamiliar lighting, smells, plus handshakes and hugs [#164, msg #167].

#### How to help yourself with social hangover (5 strategies)
The author frames the help as understanding your traits and defending your boundaries. Each strategy appears on the card in short form and in the post in expanded form [#166, msg #167]:

1. Find out as much as possible about the upcoming event. What: gather information beforehand. Why/option: you are allowed not to go to events where you know nobody, do not understand what will happen, and the situation looks maximally unpredictable [#166, msg #167].
2. Don't schedule parties/meetings too often; give yourself time to rest and recover. Also better not to plan noisy, crowded meetings after an already hard working or study day [#166, msg #167].
3. Think about sensory accommodations. Questions to ask yourself: Can you meet in a place that is comfortable (not overwhelming) for you? Can you wear noise-cancelling headphones to filter out part of the noise? Is it possible to take sensory breaks (for example, stepping outside) every so often? Do the people around you know about your traits? [#166, msg #167]
4. Plan in advance how much time you are ready to spend with the group before you become uncomfortable. The author's own example: she knows she can spend about 1–1.5 hours in company relatively comfortably; after that she starts planning how to escape home quickly [#166, msg #167].
5. Take care of yourself afterwards. The next day, let yourself rest properly and spend time alone (or with a comfortable person or a pet) and with your own interests [#166, msg #167].

### Detailed insights

#### What meltdowns and shutdowns are not
The channel stresses that meltdowns and shutdowns are "не панические атаки, не обиды, не истерики и не манипуляции" (not panic attacks, not taking offence, not hysterics and not manipulation). They do not pursue any goal, occur in both children and adults, and are simply the overloaded psyche's reactions to stress — "в них нет ничего стыдного и страшного" (there is nothing shameful or frightening in them) [#128].

(!) Note on scope: the post says these reactions happen to autistic people often and to non-autistic people too, though more rarely, whereas the card speaks of "реакции аутичного мозга" (reactions of the autistic brain) [#128, img #128].

#### Definition of social hangover
The title card of the carousel is "Аутизм и социальное похмелье" (Autism and social hangover) [#162]. Social hangover is a term originally used for introverts, describing "усталость, раздражительность и недомогание" (fatigue, irritability and malaise) on the day after a person spent all their energy socializing in company — a state similar to a hangover, but without alcohol [#163]. In the post the same original introvert definition is rendered as "усталость, раздражительность, туман в голове" (fatigue, irritability, brain fog) [#167].

#### The author's own case
The author describes herself as an autistic person with ADHD who loves socializing. She usually prefers one-on-one interaction or small groups in a sensorily comfortable setting (for example, at home), but sometimes does not want to miss café gatherings or parties — especially "now", when people are leaving the country and every moment feels especially precious. She noticed that the day after gatherings in large groups (more than 4 people) she experienced something like a hangover: headaches, fatigue, brain fog, low mood — really like the morning after drinking too much, although she had not drunk at these meetings. Searching online whether autistic people can have something like a hangover after socializing, she was surprised to find a lot of information, which became the basis of the symptom, cause and strategy lists above. Lesson: a hangover-like state after group socializing can be a recognizable autistic overload pattern, with a threshold the author places at groups larger than 4 people for herself [#167].

## Neurodivergent burnout and chronic fatigue

### What this covers

This topic gathers three card series posted in the channel: a forwarded explainer from the channel Nerdivergent on burnout in neurodivergent people (where it comes from, how to recognise it, what to do), a translated series by @autisticrose on why many autistic people are tired all the time, and the channel author's (@ia_psy) own account of constant fatigue as an autistic person with ADHD and GAD (generalised anxiety disorder), with lists of what does and does not help her. The source treats burnout as something neurodivergent people often meet simply by living as students, office workers or parents in environments not adapted to them, and treats constant lack of energy as a central, painful symptom — the author calls it "самый грустный симптом для меня" (the saddest symptom for me) [#108, #234, #293].

### Core ideas

- Burnout is built into neurodivergent life in social contexts. Anyone can burn out at any time for many reasons, but for neurodivergent people burnout is often an inseparable part of existing in social settings, because spaces are unadapted and "unusual" traits are not accepted [#108].
- Masking is the most frequent cause; overload is the second; missing resources and accommodations add to it (see *Causes of neurodivergent burnout*) [#109].
- Burnout is recognised by three indicators: chronic fatigue not explained by other conditions, lowered tolerance to stimuli, loss of executive function [#110].
- Recovery starts with root causes (sensory load, masking, boundaries) and is supported by self-care, routine and neurodiversity-informed therapy [#111, #112].
- Autistic tiredness has several distinct sources: chronic illness, different sensory processing, less REM sleep, and the cost of being around people who do not understand you [#235, #236, #237, #238].
- Techniques work only when practised consistently. Physical activity helps when regular and gentle; a one-off intense effort when already exhausted backfires [#293].
- Individual variation. Both the Nerdivergent series and the author stress that not everyone's burnout or fatigue looks like what is described [#107, #293].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Causes of neurodivergent burnout
Purpose: to explain where burnout comes from in neurodivergent people (card "ОТКУДА ЭТО ВООБЩЕ?" — *Where does this even come from?*), from the Nerdivergent series [#109].

1. Masking (most frequent factor). Masking is defined as "сокрытие своих проявлений, чтобы соответствовать ожиданиям" (hiding one's manifestations in order to meet the expectations) of society, i.e. of some abstract "normality". It is named "Самыми частым фактором выгорания" (the most frequent factor of burnout) [#109].
2. Overload (second cause). Because a neurodivergent person must hide their natural behaviour *at the same time* as sustaining executive functions — concentration, productivity, punctuality, following plans — they get overloaded quickly, and this overload can itself be a second cause of burnout. The mechanism is the double load: suppressing oneself plus performing demanding executive tasks [#109].
3. Lack of resources and accommodations. The absence or shortage of necessary resources and accommodations can also contribute to the development of burnout [#109].

Context from the preceding card: the background condition is that many spaces are not adapted, and "unusual" manifestations are rejected both by society and by neurodivergent people themselves; this harms those who are forced to suppress those manifestations and the needs connected with them. The stress tied to one's place (school, office, home as a parent) and/or surroundings is what pushes people into burnout [#108].

#### Three indicators of neurodivergent burnout
Purpose: to help a person tell whether they are in neurodivergent burnout (card titled "How to tell?"; OCR reads the heading as "KAK пОНЯТЬ?") [#110].

1. Chronic fatigue not linked to other syndromes and conditions. Signs you may notice: sleep disturbances, loss of strength and energy, headaches, apathy, and other similar signs. The key qualifier is that the fatigue is not explained by another condition [#110].
2. Reduced tolerance to stimuli. You may get overloaded more often and faster; it may become harder to be in social situations, to talk, and similar [#110].
3. Loss of executive function. It becomes harder to concentrate, memory worsens, and everyday tasks such as cleaning or work become harder to do [#110].

#### Burnout recovery, part 1: working with root causes
Purpose: what to do first if you suspect burnout (card "ЧТО ДЕЛАТЬ?" — *What to do?*). The series advises working "в первую очередь … с первоисточниками" — first of all with the original sources of the burnout [#111].

1. Reduce sensory load. Where possible, avoid overloading places, wear more comfortable clothing, and use sensory protection: earplugs, headphones, dark glasses [#111].
2. "Take off the mask" ("Снять маску"). Try more often to behave in the way most natural for you [#111].
3. Set boundaries and remind people of them. The card acknowledges that saying "no" can be genuinely hard, but stresses that it is important for recovery [#111].

#### Burnout recovery, part 2: self-care and therapy
Purpose: additional supports (card "ЧТО ЕЩЁ ДЕЛАТЬ?" — *What else to do?*); self-care "в самых разных её проявлениях" (in its most varied forms) can help with burnout [#112].

1. Do comfortable things. Set aside time for your special interests and hyperfixations, favourite films and series, and eat comfort food [#112].
2. Maintain routine. "However banal it sounds", going to bed on time, eating several times a day and keeping up hygiene as far as possible really can help you cope [#112].
3. Therapy — with a condition. Therapy can also help with burnout, *provided* the therapist knows about your neurodivergence and about methods of working with it [#112].

#### Four reasons autistic people are often tired (@autisticrose, translated by @ia_psy)
Purpose: to answer "Почему многие аутичные люди все время уставшие?" (Why are many autistic people tired all the time?) [#234].

1. Chronic illnesses ("Хронические заболевания"). Many autistic people have chronic illnesses, many of which can cause fatigue; constantly living with pain and other symptoms can be very exhausting [#235].
2. Sensory information ("Сенсорная информация"). Autistic brains process information differently from non-autistic brains, and therefore, the card says, "we" get tired faster [#236].
3. Non-restorative sleep (Russian: невосстанавливающий сон). Autistic people spend less time in REM sleep than non-autistic people — 15% compared with 25% (no study is named on the card) [#237].
4. Other people and communication ("Другие люди и общение"). "Неважно, маскируемся мы или нет," (It doesn't matter whether we mask or not,) being around people who do not understand us is very tiring [#238].

#### Personal fatigue map (@ia_psy, "Работаю из кровати: нейроотличия и усталость")
Purpose: the author's own structured account of constant tiredness ("Working from bed: neurodivergence and fatigue"), posted after her talk at the conference "Нейроотличники". She explicitly frames it as what happens *specifically to her* as an autistic person with ADHD and GAD; readers need not have the same problems. It is organised in four parts that can serve as a template for mapping one's own fatigue [#293].

A. The problem (what it looks like) [#294]:
1. She is a low-energy person, both physically and morally (emotionally).
2. She has no desire to do anything, even pleasant things.
3. She tends to spend all her free time lying down.
4. She gets worn out quickly when she leaves the house.
5. Sometimes she wakes up with brain fog ("туманом в голове").
6. She almost never has spontaneous bursts of energy.
7. "Я даже работаю из кровати." (I even work from bed.)

B. Why it happens (her causes) [#295]:
1. Sleep problems.
2. Constant overload — from internal and external stimuli.
3. Body pain.
4. Anxiety.
5. Social situations and their unpredictability.
6. Low physical activity.
7. Difficulty planning work and rest.
8. Delayed information processing ("Отложенная обработка информации").
9. Masking.

C. What does not help her [#296]:
1. Coffee.
2. Short, intense physical activity.
3. Doing breathing techniques once (a single, one-off session).
4. Loud sounds and bright light.
5. Pretending she has no problems and loading herself heavily.

D. What helps her [#297]:
1. Knowing what tires her most.
2. Control over how much she works and rests — keeping a balance.
3. Rest without obligations ("Отдых без обязательств!").
4. Antidepressants and therapy.
5. Attention to her body and emotional state.
6. Relieving pain ("Купировать боли").
7. Not spending much time in places where she is sensorily uncomfortable.
8. Crawling out outside and moving more — "(в долгосрочной перспективе)" (in the long term).
9. Unmasking where possible and safe.
10. Accepting her limitations.

### Detailed insights

#### Why physical activity both helps and does not help
The author explains the apparent contradiction between "low physical activity" being a cause of her fatigue and "short intense physical activity" being on her does-not-help list. According to research (she does not name the studies), physical activity improves mental health if it is done *consistently* — even if it is just walking. She also feels better when she does undemanding physical activity *several times a week*. But if she is tired and, after a week of sitting at home, tries to perk herself up with a *single intense workout*, she simply collapses ("вырублюсь") afterwards. Her conclusion, delivered ironically: "Техники работают, если делать их стабильно (кто бы мог подумать)" (*Techniques work if you do them consistently (who would have thought)*). The same logic explains why a one-off breathing exercise is on the does-not-help list and why moving more is qualified as helpful "in the long term" [#293, #296, #297].

#### Burnout is common, but its presentation varies
The Nerdivergent series opens by framing spring as a time to notice one's burnout "and do something about it (if you want, of course)", and closes with a caveat: of course not everyone's burnout shows up exactly as described, but the authors hope the advice can help [#107].

#### Masking as a thread through both burnout and fatigue
Masking appears in every series: as the most frequent cause of burnout [#109], with unmasking as a first-line remedy [#111]; in @autisticrose's cards as not the whole story — even without masking, being among people who do not understand you is exhausting [#238]; and in the author's personal map as one of nine causes, with unmasking listed as helpful only "там, где возможно и безопасно" (where possible and safe) [#295, #297].
