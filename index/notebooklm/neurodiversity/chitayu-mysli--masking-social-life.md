## Masking, compensation and unmasking

### What this covers

How neurodivergent people (ADHD, autism spectrum — РАС) learn masking, skills and adaptation while growing up, why that adaptation costs a great deal of energy and breaks down (decompensation) with age, load and illness, and a published model of what motivates masking, what forms it takes and what it costs. It also covers how the childhood instruction to endure (терпи) becomes a pillar of masking that hides one's own sensory profile, and what unmasking looks like in concrete, everyday terms. The author's position is that masking is rather bad than good and lowers quality of life, so reducing it wherever it is currently possible should be actively reinforced [#96, #144, #145, #277, #777].

### Core ideas

- Adaptation is expensive. Growing up, neurodivergent people learn masking, skills and adaptation, but this takes an enormous amount of energy, especially if they ignore their traits and do not rest enough; see *Decompensation mechanism* [#96].
- Symptoms "worsening" usually means compensation is failing. When all neurodivergent manifestations intensify, look for what changed in load or health; age 30+ makes compensation harder if one neglects special needs [#96].
- Masking is more harmful than helpful. It has identifiable motives, forms and consequences (energy depletion, anxiety, loss of self, others' stereotypes); see *Model of masking* [#144, #145].
- "Endure it" is a pillar of masking. Children with sensory sensitivity who were taught to tolerate discomfort grow into adults who no longer notice what overloads them [#277].
- Less masking, less fatigue. In a safe, familiar setting where masking can be "turned down to the minimum", even a large gathering can be not tiring but warm [#777].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Decompensation mechanism (why symptoms seem to appear or worsen later)
Purpose: answers a question patients often bring to the author — "Почему раньше я так не ощущал симптомы СДВГ/РАС?" (Why didn't I feel ADHD/ASD symptoms like this before?). Typical versions: "at university I was distracted, but not this much, and now I can't concentrate at all", or "my sleep used to be rock-solid, and now I can't stop my hyperactivity if I've worked to the limit" [#96].
1. Learning to compensate. As they grow up, neurodivergent people learn masking, skills and general adaptation [#96].
2. The cost. This requires a huge amount of energy, especially if one does not take one's traits into account and does not give oneself enough rest [#96].
3. Youth hides the cost. At a young age most people have more strength and bodily resources, so adaptation comes much easier [#96].
4. Capacity falls. With age, and with growing load, more complex social situations and physical illnesses, the organism's ability to compensate decreases [#96].
5. Decompensation. As a result, all symptoms become sharper; the author calls this decompensation (декомпенсация) [#96].
6. What to do. If you feel all your neurodivergent manifestations have intensified, look for what changed in your load or your health. And age 30+ — "как бы мы не шутили" (however much we joke about it) — is when, if you pay little attention to your special needs, the organism finds it hard to compensate well [#96].

Author's own example: against a background of heavy load, including mental load, not only her ADHD but all her autistic symptoms — which she normally notices much less — flared up: she could hardly be outside without noise-cancelling headphones, was enraged by overhead light at home, and was exhausted by any dog's yapping [#96].

#### Model of masking: motivation → forms → consequences
Source: a diagram the author translated into Russian and presented at a conference talk on how masking forms, which models exist and why masking is rather bad than good; credited to "Putting on My Best Normal: Social Camouflaging in Adults with Autism Spectrum Conditions", August 2017, Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders 47(4). The author calls it "прекрасная модель маскинга и его последствий" (a wonderful model of masking and its consequences) that visually shows the causes leading to masking, masking itself and its consequences [#144, #145, img #145]. The diagram gives only labels; the explanations below stay at the level of those labels.

A. Motivation for masking (Мотивация для маскинга) — two clusters feeding into masking [#145]:
1. Assimilation (Ассимиляция), fed by:
   - *Действующий член общества* — being a functioning member of society;
   - *Вопросы безопасности* — safety concerns;
   - *Достаточная норма* — being "normal enough".
2. Being in society and being accepted (Быть в обществе и быть принятым), fed by:
   - *Принятие, отсутствие отвержения* — acceptance, absence of rejection;
   - *Снижение стресса* — stress reduction.

B. What masking is (Что такое маскинг?) — two forms branching from masking [#145]:
3. Compensation (Компенсация):
   - *Правильные виды выражений* — using the "correct" kinds of expressions;
   - *Планирование каждого разговора* — planning every conversation.
4. Camouflage (Маскировка):
   - *Избегание быть слишком аутичным* (label shown with the last two words in guillemets) — avoiding being too autistic;
   - *Играть соответствующую роль* — playing the appropriate role.

C. Consequences (Последствия) — three outcomes [#145]:
5. Other people's stereotypes (Стереотипы у других людей): *Ты не можешь быть аутистом* (you can't be autistic) and *Слишком хорошо скрываются трудности* (difficulties are hidden too well).
6. I'm falling apart (Я разваливаюсь на части; split across lines in the diagram): *Истощение энергии* (energy depletion) and *Тревога сделать что-то неправильно* (anxiety about doing something wrong).
7. Not my true self (Я не мое истинное я): *Кто же я?* (who am I, then?), *Обман людей* (deceiving people), and *Предательство себя* (self-betrayal).

Author's takeaway from preparing the talk: she re-thought how much masking can lower quality of life and concluded it is very important to reinforce (подкреплять) the reduction of masking wherever it can be removed at the present moment [#144].

#### Learning your sensory profile after "endure it" upbringing
Purpose: for adults (especially autistic) who were trained in childhood to tolerate sensory discomfort and now live in diffuse, unexplained discomfort and fatigue [#277].
1. Understand the origin. Children with sensory sensitivity — especially those who grew up in the scarcity of the 1990s — were often taught to "endure" and tolerate discomfort. The author grants that the skill of tolerating discomfort is extremely important ("а то мы превращаемся в снежинок" — otherwise we turn into snowflakes), but says it does not work with sensory sensitivity. Such children, most often autistic, learn not to show that they dislike something or are uncomfortable — until a meltdown or shutdown [#277].
2. Recognize the adult result. Later, when nobody says "ешь что дали" (eat what you're given) or pulls a scratchy sweater onto you anymore, these adults no longer even think about what brings discomfort and overload into their lives — often because life as a whole is one big discomfort, plus a constant tiredness "for some reason" [#277].
3. Study yourself gradually. One needs to learn, little by little, to explore oneself and one's sensory profile [#277].
4. Permit accommodations. Examples given: refuse food that is unpleasant in texture, choose comfortable clothing, wear sunglasses even indoors [#277].
5. Expect discoveries. Once sensations are no longer suppressed, specific sources become visible; before, "все сливается в один общий" (everything merges into one general "painful-uncomfortable" stream of stimuli) [#277].
6. Know the diagnosis. This is why it matters for people with level 1 support needs, and even those in the broader phenotype, to understand and know their diagnosis: then you start to understand what to do with yourself — and to study your sensory profile [#277].

Author's example: after realizing she is on the spectrum, she very cautiously began allowing herself such life hacks. She found that walking in winter is more pleasant in sunglasses, and that towels irritate her, so she switched to a waffle bathrobe. She now knows that sun getting into her eye is physically painful and that loud sounds which don't bother others genuinely hurt her ears. Were these there before? Yes — but when you endure and don't pay attention, you don't know it [#277].

### Detailed insights

#### What unmasking looks like in practice
- At the conference where she spoke about masking, the author's "unmasking for today" was sitting in noise-cancelling headphones because another speaker was presenting very loudly [#144].
- On a trip to Sochi for her sister's birthday — 10 people and 2 infants — she was surprised to feel almost no fatigue and instead warmth and a sense of home comfort, which is very unusual for her in large groups. Her tentative conclusion ("Пока я пришла к выводу" — so far I've concluded) is that this resulted from reduced masking and reduced anxiety about being in company and having to adjust to others [#777].
- Concretely she: left when she needed to; stayed silent at the table if she didn't want to talk; spent the whole drive to Abkhazia in the car in headphones. Her sister later called her and her sister's husband (who is "the same") "asocial", but lovingly and without hostility [#777].
- Lesson: in a setting where you can turn your masking down to the minimum, there is much less fatigue. Her caveat: the whole group was people she knew. She also half-jokingly adds that the golden autumn scenery may have helped — she was constantly looking at the beauty around and not noticing people [#777].

## Autistic communication, belonging and being misread

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel author's posts on how autistic people communicate and belong: the double empathy problem (communication breaks down on both sides, not just because of an autistic "deficit"), the mismatch between inner feelings and outward nonverbal signals that leads to being misread, why a trusted person makes public places manageable, why a small safe "tribe" is a real need, and how to tell social anxiety from autistic limited social energy when work demands a lot of talking. The author treats these as painful, everyday issues for people with ASD: constantly thinking about how one is perceived is described as "большое напряжение" (a great strain) [#867], and rejection is described as felt by the brain almost like death [#431].

### Core ideas

- Autistic people often enjoy solitude but still want company — liking a lot of time alone does not mean not wanting time with others [#212].
- Communication problems are two-sided (double empathy problem): autistic and neurotypical people misunderstand each other; the theory challenges the view that all communication problems are an autistic deficit [#382].
- The need for a tribe is universal and evolutionary, and neurodivergent people are more likely to face rejection and fail to find their people — yet they need a tribe no less [#431].
- Inner experience and nonverbal expression often do not match in autism — the author calls this one of the official criteria — so close people should ask rather than interpret [#867].
- Autistic social energy is limited regardless of practice, unlike social anxiety, which can be worked on; this forces real choices about work and life [#852].
- Nonverbal-heavy content can be harder to process — the author's hypothesis for why she dislikes sitcoms and romance but enjoys detectives and any book [#790].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Double empathy problem — six research findings
Definition: the double empathy problem is a theory that when autistic and non-autistic people communicate, understanding and skills are lacking on both sides. The usual framing is that people with ASD have difficulty understanding context and other people (verbally and nonverbally); the theory asks whether the other side of the communication also has difficulties. According to the author, it is increasingly discussed, and scientific data are appearing showing the problems are bidirectional: autistic people misunderstand neurotypicals just as neurotypicals do not understand autistic people, "И коммуникация не ладится от слова совсем" (And communication simply doesn't work at all) [#382].

Caveat stated by the author: far from all scientists and specialists support the theory; it largely challenges the view that all communication problems of people with ASD come from their deficit in communication [#382].

What studies conducted so far show, as listed by the author (no specific studies or authors are named — (!) INCOMPLETE: no citations given):
1. Facial expressions are misread both ways. Autistic people misread neurotypicals' facial expressions, and neurotypicals likewise misread and misinterpret autistic people's facial expressions [#382].
2. Hasty, wrong judgments by non-autistic people. Non-autistic people tend to make quick (and incorrect) judgments about autistic people that interrupt or spoil communication with them; they also more often suspect them of insincerity or deception [#382].
3. Difficulties shrink among autistic peers. The social and communication problems of autistic people are less noticeable or significant when they communicate with other autistic people [#382].
4. More openness among autistic peers. People with autism are more open and tell more about their lives when talking with each other [#382].
5. Similar trait scores → closest friendships. People with similar scores on an autistic-traits test rate their friendship as the closest [#382].
6. Implications for diagnosis and therapy. The double empathy problem may explain shortcomings of diagnostic tools and therapy methods [#382].

Author's view: she likes that communication problems are now examined from both sides, because any communication is a two-way process and we need to learn to find common ground from each side. Her own observation: it is sometimes much harder to talk with neurotypical people because she often has to explain why she behaves one way or another, whereas with friends who are also on the spectrum this happens "гораздо гораздо меньше" (much, much less) [#382].

#### Two reasons a trusted companion helps in public places
Context: a common belief is that a person with ASD is better off alone. Many autistic people do say they like spending a lot of time by themselves, but this does not mean they don't want to spend time with others. It especially matters for going out to public places or parties with little-known people: in such cases many people with ASD would rather stay home unless someone from their closest circle goes with them [#212].

Why it is much easier to be in such places with a partner or friend:
1. The companion takes over part of the communication, especially the necessary bits — for example buying a coffee or asking/finding something out [#212].
2. The environment feels less unpredictable and unsafe. A crowd or just a public space will not feel as unpredictable and unsafe, because a more predictable person is right there [#212].

Recommendations:
- If you are autistic and need to go somewhere you don't want to go alone, try to take someone you feel good with [#212].
- If you are a friend or close person of someone with ASD, don't refuse to go with them, even to places that seem the strangest to you [#212].

#### Social anxiety vs autistic limited social energy at work
Question from a reader: how to cope with a lot of communication at work as an introvert, when one often needs to initiate discussions and negotiations but does not want to talk to anyone more than necessary [#852].

The author's answer, in order:
1. First decide what your "introversion" is linked to. The label hides different causes, and the remedy depends on which one it is [#852].
2. If it is social anxiety — this is something one learns to face and to build up different experience with; i.e. it can be worked on through exposure to new experience [#852].
3. If it is autism — understand that you have a limited reserve of energy for social communication in any case. The options are then:
   - choose a job where you can roughly "вывозить" (handle, cope with) the amount of communication;
   - or put together a combination of different jobs, or reduce your working hours/rate (уменьшать ставку);
   - or give up other communication outside work [#852].
4. Accept that it is a hard choice. The author says this is unfortunately often a very difficult choice, but at some point we will have to make it anyway [#852].

#### Two-context approach to being misread (work vs close people)
Question (#867): a subscriber diagnosed last year with ASD, ADHD and GAD asks how to deal with being misunderstood — her words counted as rude or interpreted wrongly, with other emotions read into them. Her examples: since childhood her mother has said she is being rude (she says she just becomes too emotional when worried); that she "бычу глаза" (glares) — a phrase still painful for her to even write; her mother trained her to look in the eyes, although she felt more comfortable listening without looking, because the mother believed she wasn't listening if she didn't look. Recently, while they were baking kulich together, she told her mother that an analysis had found a pathology in her colon, and the mother said she said it too cheerfully — while she was simply focused on something else and of course was not glad about the illness. She asks whether she can work on this or explain that it is her trait, when the person likes to say she "нести флаг" (waves the flag of her diagnosis too much), and worries that strangers at work or study may perceive her the same way and form a bad impression [#867].

The author's answer: she calls it a very painful and relevant question for people with ASD, and first offers moral support — living with this and constantly thinking about how you are perceived is a great strain. Then she splits what to do by situation [#867]:
1. Work-type situations (strangers, colleagues, study). Here one learns — most often individually in therapy — how to synchronize inner experience with outer expression and how to give people social signals correctly. Adjusting is treated as a skill to learn in these settings [#867].
2. Situations with close people. Constant adjustment here is an excessive load. Ideally, talk to close people and explain that your inner state and your nonverbal expression often may not match, since this is one of the manifestations of autism (the author states it is even one of the official criteria). Let them instead clarify in the moment what exactly you are feeling — so you can at least relax a little, not analyse everything constantly, and simply be yourself [#867].

### Detailed insights

#### Belonging: the need for a tribe
The idea comes from the author's therapy group on overcontrol (hypercontrol), which discussed people's need for a tribe. In short: in evolutionary terms people could not survive alone, so we are social animals, and that is why rejection by society is so painful for us — for our brain (which is "ещё там далеко в прошлом", still far back in the past), rejection by the tribe is practically equal to death [#431].

Being neurodivergent makes it much more likely to face rejection and not find one's "tribe"; with ASD it can be even harder. The author acknowledges there are autistic people who are not interested in society and suffer little from lack of communication and belonging — but she says such people are far fewer in the ASD population than those who suffer because they cannot find "their" people [#431].

Her conclusion, "грустная, но правда жизни" (sad but the truth of life): it is harder for us to find our tribe, but we need it no less than other people. So it is important to keep looking for people with whom you feel good, calm and safe — "даже 2 человека - это уже племя!" (even 2 people are already a tribe!) [#431]. (!) UNCLEAR: the source says "продолжать людей" — a word (likely "искать", to look for) appears to be missing [#431].

#### Nonverbal communication and media preferences (author's personal hypothesis)
The author describes a heated argument with her husband about why she loves watching thrillers and shows like "Очень странные дела" (Stranger Things); he dislikes thrillers, she has loved them since early childhood. Their hypotheses — "это все твоя травма" (it's all your trauma), watching detectives with her grandmother from infancy, having graduated from medical school — led nowhere. She can hardly watch sitcoms, comedies and especially very romantic content: unrealistic happy endings irritate her, she gets stressed by embarrassing situations, gets emotionally overloaded, or it is simply boring [#790].

Her later hypothesis — simpler than trauma or adrenaline-seeking (which she still doesn't see as a problem anyway):
- In detectives, thrillers and mysticism the focus is on what is happening and on finding the truth/logic/explanation of events [#790].
- Even in sitcoms, without the heavy emotional load of a romantic drama, everything is built around relationships between characters — awkward situations, sad ones, and frequently played-out misunderstandings (she thinks Turkish and Korean series are built only on these) — and all of it through largely *nonverbal* communication, which is harder to perceive [#790].
- Books do not have this problem: she can read anything, all genres are fine, because books have almost no nonverbal communication — everything has to be written out, so everything is clear at once [#790].

She hedges: she doesn't know how far this is the ultimate truth, but there is clearly a grain of truth in it; and in any case her "dark and sad soul" loves watching and reading gloomy, sad things [#790].

- The double empathy problem is not universally accepted: the author notes that far from all scientists and specialists support it; the six findings are listed without naming the studies. (!) INCOMPLETE: no sources cited [#382].

- The nonverbal-content explanation of media preferences is explicitly a personal hypothesis with "a grain of truth", not an established finding [#790].
- The author acknowledges a minority of autistic people genuinely uninterested in social contact; the tribe argument applies to the majority who suffer without "their" people [#431].
- (!) UNCLEAR: "важно продолжать людей" in #431 appears to miss a verb (probably "look for") [#431].
