## Regulating the neurodivergent nervous system

### What this covers

This topic gathers everything the channel taught about how the neurodivergent (ND) nervous system works and how to regulate it: the window of tolerance, hyperarousal and hypoarousal, dorsal vagal shutdown, the vagus nerve and vagal tone, heart rate variability, and an extensive toolbox of practices that either lower activation (calming) or raise it (energising), including bilateral stimulation through 8D audio and in-the-moment somatic techniques from the author's own therapy. Most of the material is translated from the clinical psychologist Megan Anna Neff (Neurodivergent Insights). According to the source it matters because ND nervous systems tend to get stuck in fight/flight or freeze, which undermines rest, recovery from burnout, sleep and everyday coping — and because, in the author's words, our difficulties are not "all in our head" [#35, #578, #34].

### Core ideas

- The ND nervous system is physiologically different, not imagined. The author likes the idea that "наша нейроотличность не означает, что все проблемы в нашей голове" (our neurodivergence does not mean all problems are in our head): many daily difficulties really are tied to how our nervous system works, and we deserve to know this and to be taken seriously by doctors [#35].
- Regulation is the key to physical and emotional balance. The ND nervous system often gets stuck in either over- or under-activation; the goal is a "golden middle" inside the window of tolerance where we feel comfortable and can handle daily tasks [#103].
- The window can be widened. There are natural baseline differences (including neurodivergence), but by observing one's own nervous system and doing regulating exercises one can, over time, build capacity to handle stress and change [#159, msg #56].
- Each deliberate shift builds vagal tone. Every time we notice a stress state and move it in a more regulated direction, we increase vagal tone (nervous-system flexibility) and regain control over the body [#578].
- Rest without regulation may not restore. If the system stays stuck in fight/flight or freeze after the "danger" has passed, rest time does not deliver its full restorative effect — which harms burnout recovery [#578].
- Regulated does not mean emotionless. In a regulated state the author still feels all the emotions, but without the sensation that the nervous system is doing "5000 оборотов в секунду" (5000 revolutions per second), so the emotions no longer overload her [#729].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Window of tolerance
Purpose: a model of our capacity to self-regulate ("Наша способность саморегулироваться"), used to recognise what state we are in and choose a response [#159].

- Definition. The window of tolerance is the optimal state of the nervous system in which we are attentive, adaptive and engaged. In it we cope effectively with stress and change; the sympathetic ("fight or flight") and parasympathetic ("rest and digest") systems work in harmony; we adapt easily to our surroundings. It is also called a "regulated nervous system". It is the zone in which we can healthily handle strong emotions and stress, with a wide range of coping options and resilience to the demands of daily life. Characteristic feelings: calm, stable, engaged in the present. The window is described as "ваш уровень стрессоустойчивости" (your level of stress resilience) [#159].
- Width matters. The wider the window, the more we can bear without harm to mental health, the more effectively we handle stress and the more easily we recover after overload [#159, msg #56]. The channel's framing post asks: do you struggle with anxiety or depression, often feel detached, sluggish and tired, or conversely regularly feel heightened anxiety, overexcitement and confusion, and find it hard to stay engaged and focused through the day? If yes, the cause may be a narrow window of tolerance that limits your inner potential [#56].
- Leaving the window. Inside the window, gas and brakes work together as in a car and we respond calmly to challenges. If stress is too strong, we leave the window, lose self-regulation and fall into a stress state — either hyperarousal or hypoarousal. Either the sympathetic or the parasympathetic system takes control to protect us and help us cope, "даже если это означает поведение, которое может быть нам не на пользу" (even if this means behaviour that may not be good for us) [#159].
- Hyperarousal (гиперактивация). Sympathetic system dominates. Feelings: anxiety, aggression, fear. Physiological signs: rapid heartbeat, fast breathing, raised blood pressure. #103 adds the states it causes: anxiety, irritability, restlessness, tension [#159, msg #103].
- Hypoarousal (гипоактивация). Parasympathetic system dominates. Feelings: fatigue, apathy, detachment/alienation. Physiological signs: slowed heartbeat, slow breathing, digestive problems. #103 adds: low mood or depression, low energy, sleepiness, lack of motivation [#159, msg #103].
- Up- vs down-regulation as the response. Raising activation helps get out of a depressed/shut-down state; lowering activation helps calm down when overexcited or anxious [#103].

#### Car metaphor of the autonomic nervous system
The presentation asks the reader to imagine: the sympathetic nervous system is the gas pedal, the parasympathetic system is the brakes, and the vagus nerve is the driver who controls both. The vagus nerve is a cranial nerve governing many automatic physiological processes (heart rate, breathing, blood pressure, digestion, reproductive functions). It is part of the parasympathetic system: it activates it while suppressing the sympathetic system, slowing heart rate, breathing and pressure, enhancing digestion and relaxing the body [#159].

Vagal tone. Low tone → difficulty adapting to stress and a narrow window of tolerance. High tone → easy adaptation and a wide window. Conclusion stated on the slide: the higher the vagal tone, the better we cope with stress [#159]. Elsewhere vagal tone is glossed as "гибкость нервной системы" (flexibility of the nervous system) [#578]. #### Why the window is narrower for some people
Factors that narrow the window, per the presentation [#159]:
1. Traumatic experiences — people who grew up in a traumatic environment experience overload more often.
2. Neurodivergence — people with OCD, ADHD and autism are more sensitive to stress and have an initially less flexible nervous system. This means it is hard for us to adapt to changes and easier to lose control over emotions. ND people often have an amplified stress response: more stress hormones and harder recovery.

#### Two-step nervous-system mapping (worksheet)
Purpose: learn to understand your own window before trying to widen it. The presentation first instructs: observe your nervous system — notice how you feel (breathing, tension, thoughts) and identify which state you are in (hypo-, hyperarousal or window); track your reactions — write down what causes you stress and understand how you react to it [#159].

1. Step 1 — Map your nervous system ("Картография НС"). The first step toward control over your body is becoming aware of the current state of your nervous system and being able to identify it at any moment. For each state, describe sensation, thoughts and behaviour. The worked example:
   - *Hyperarousal* — sensation: irritable, angry, anxious, agitated; thoughts: "Я просто хочу убраться отсюда" (I just want to get out of here), "пусть это прекратится" (make it stop); behaviour: likely to shout or react sharply.
   - *Window of tolerance* — sensation: energetic, creative, calm, happy, peaceful; thoughts: "Я справлюсь с этим" (I can handle this), lots of ideas; behaviour: more sociable, often starts a new creative project.
   - *Hypoarousal* — sensation: foggy mind, feeling disconnected, exhaustion, heaviness; thoughts: "Я хочу спать" (I want to sleep), "Я чувствую себя отстраненным" (I feel detached); behaviour: withdrawal, half-speech, disengagement, indifference [#159].
2. Step 2 — Map your triggers and helpers. Once you can tell where you "are", learn to identify your usual triggers (and the helpers that keep you inside your window), sorted as physical / emotional / interpersonal. The worked example:
   - *Hyperarousal triggers* — physical: heat, discomfort, being surrounded by irritating sounds; emotional: anger, irritation, shame; interpersonal: when I feel used or abandoned/rejected.
   - *Window helpers* — physical: calming, repetitive movements; sitting in a comfortable position, wearing comfortable clothes; interpersonal: talking with a friend at a deep level.
   - *Hypoarousal triggers* — physical: too much going on around me (too many people, sounds); emotional: sadness, overwhelm, shame; interpersonal: emotionally tense conversations — when people are angry [#159].

#### Ways to widen the window of tolerance (10 practices)
From the presentation slides "Увеличение вашего Окна толерантности" [#159]:
1. Breathing practices. Slow, deep breathing activates the vagus nerve and hence the parasympathetic system; it helps relieve anxiety, focus and strengthen connection with the body. Conversely, fast, shallow breathing activates the sympathetic system and creates activation when the body is in a frozen/numb state — so breathing can be used in both directions.
2. Cold exposure. Studies (per the slide) show cold exposure increases vagal activity and HRV and reduces sympathetic responses. Options: adding 30 seconds of cold water at the end of a shower, immersing the face in a bowl of ice water, or putting ice on the back of the neck. Cold on the neck was found especially effective.
3. Exercise and movement. Exercise was shown to increase HRV by stimulating and toning the vagus nerve. Vigorous movement (long walks, running), weightlifting and somatic movement are named as excellent ways to raise vagal tone. Weightlifting has the extra benefit of deep pressure on the joints, which many ND people find relaxing. Somatic movement is especially useful for treating trauma and relaxing the body mindfully. Dr. Sam Zoranovich is recommended as a source of ND-friendly somatic movement exercises.
4. Mindfulness and meditation. Contemplative practices have many mental-health benefits; meditation is linked to reduced brain inflammation, better memory and better ability to absorb new information, and to increased HRV and vagal tone. Mindfulness in particular can activate the vagus nerve, especially when breathing exercises are included.
5. Probiotics and omega-3. The vagus nerve is deeply connected with the gut (it is responsible for digestion), so when the gut is stressed, the vagus nerve is stressed too. Caring for the gut with probiotics and omega-3 in turn cares for the nervous system.
6. Laughter. Studies (per the slide) show laughter stimulates the vagus nerve by inducing diaphragmatic breathing; about ten minutes of laughter a day has been shown to have many health benefits. Suggestion: add more humour to life or try targeted laughter meditations.
7. Vibration, singing and humming. Very powerful ways to activate the vagus nerve. One study reportedly showed that chanting "om" can deactivate the limbic centre of the brain responsible for threats and emotions. The vagus nerve is connected with the vocal cords and the muscles at the back of the throat, so using the vocal cords mindfully activates it directly.
8. Massage. Mindful person-to-person touch stimulates the vagus nerve and increases oxytocin, the bonding hormone. It can come from another person, but simple self-massage also works; a video by Suki Baker is cited as a good example.
9. Havening. A form of self-soothing that creates a sense of safety through comforting, therapeutic touch; the idea is that soothing touch increases serotonin, which calms. Specific movements: cupping the cheeks with the palms, holding a hand on the chest, or crossing the arms (as if hugging yourself) and stroking the shoulders and upper arms.
10. Relaxation exercises. These act directly on the vagus nerve to calm the body and bring it out of dysregulation. They can be used before sleep, to strengthen oneself in the morning, and to recover after stressful events; they include various breathing techniques, progressive muscle relaxation (PMR) and meditations.

#### Nervous-system state shifting (смена состояния нервной системы)
Purpose: make rest actually restorative and widen the window by switching the nervous system into a mode of safety. Source: the author's translation of Neurodivergent Insights material [#578, file #578].

- Definition. When we are in a stress state and can (A) identify it and (B) do something to lower or raise activation and enter a more regulated zone, we are doing nervous-system state shifting. It helps handle stress, and "как бонус" (as a bonus) each time we catch ourselves in stress and correct it to a regulated level, we increase vagal tone [#578].
- Step 1 — notice the state. Pay attention to which state your nervous system is in [#578].
- Step 2a — if mobilised (sympathetic dominance, too much excitation, need to calm down): do down-regulating activities, i.e. ones that reduce excitation and activation of the body. The post says excessive sympathetic activity must be balanced with relaxation methods [#578, msg #578].
- Step 2b — if immobilised (parasympathetic dominance — numbness, sluggishness): do up-regulating activities, i.e. ones that increase the body's energy and wakefulness. Up-regulation is useful when you need a surge of energy to stay awake for driving, an exam, work or other activities. The post names activating breathing, movement and sensory activities that "wake up" the body [#578, msg #578].
- Explore what works for you — the author stresses it is important to investigate what specifically helps you, using the activity lists below [#578].
- Why: "Каждый раз, когда вы намеренно выводите свою нервную систему из стрессовой реакции и возвращаете ее в состояние безопасности, вы увеличиваете вагусный тонус." (Every time you deliberately bring your nervous system out of a stress response and return it to a state of safety, you increase vagal tone.) [#578]

#### Down-regulating vs up-regulating activity lists
All from the attached PDF [#578].

Down-regulating (calming) core actions: breathing practices (slow, deep breathing), tapping, grounding, chanting mantras, or stimming to discharge excess energy. Named tools: progressive muscle relaxation; tapping; binaural beats and calming music; guided visualisation; breathing practices; yoga.

Up-regulating (energising) core actions: activities that mobilise the body for action — tapping, rocking, dancing, movement, pacing back and forth, creating pressure or friction (e.g. rubbing hands). Named tools: fast breathing techniques; listening to energetic music; lifting weights; doing a stimulating activity; brisk walking; exposing yourself to sunlight.

Contrasting qualities of experience (calming ↔ energising):
- familiar situations or activities ↔ new situations or activities
- slow pace ↔ fast pace
- slow, rhythmic, repetitive movements ↔ fast, rhythmic movements
- repetitive, calm music or sounds ↔ loud, fast, energetic music
- warm temperatures ↔ cold temperatures
- soft and few colours ↔ bright colours/variety
- weak stimuli (smells, tastes) ↔ strong or intense stimuli
- quiet setting ↔ loud setting
- dim lighting ↔ bright lighting
- repetition ↔ surprise
- stability ↔ irregularity/spontaneity
- deep pressure/weight ↔ movement

Calming (down-regulating) activities:
- rocking; rocking in a rocking chair
- wrapping up in a warm, heavy blanket
- taking a hot shower or bath
- focusing on calming scenes such as a sunset or a peaceful meadow
- using soft lighting to create a cosy atmosphere
- sipping a warm drink, e.g. soothing herbal tea
- listening to soft or slow music
- following slow, even rhythms
- enjoying background sounds or relaxing music
- using soothing or gentle scents (essential oils, lotions, candles)
- applying deep pressure through hugs or massage
- tapping with a bean bag
- humming, chanting mantras or singing
- practising yoga
- green therapy (time in nature)
- using soft materials or textures such as a cosy blanket or soft pillow

Energising (up-regulating) activities:
- holding ice cubes
- eating fruit ice
- sour or spicy sweets
- chewing a lemon slice
- frozen fruit smoothies
- dancing to fast music
- splashing the face with cold water
- sitting in a cool room
- looking at unexpected visual stimuli
- changing patterns of light and shadow
- invigorating nature sounds (birdsong)
- listening to fast music
- strong scents (essential oils, lotions, candles)
- light touch
- a brisk walk in fresh air
- touching rough or prickly textures
- taking a cold shower or bath
- crunching ice cubes
- rubbing menthol cream into the skin or using mint or eucalyptus essential oils
- standing in front of a fan
- doing a few star jumps
- running cold water over the wrists
- chewing gum/mints

#### Self-reflection exercise on "overreacting"
Purpose: counter shame when told you are "слишком бурно реагируете" (overreacting) or "чересчур чувствительны" (too sensitive). Premise: the autonomic nervous system works automatically, like sneezing — it fires without conscious control. If you recognise that your body easily goes into fight-or-flight (sympathetic mode), reflect on [#373]:
1. What thoughts and beliefs have you formed about these experiences?
2. Do these thoughts help you, or do they cause shame and increase stress?
3. How could you look at these situations anew to develop a kinder attitude toward yourself?
The exercise is taken from Megan Anna Neff's workbook "Нейроотличная нервная система" (The Neurodivergent Nervous System) [#373].

#### Bilateral stimulation via 8D audio
Question posed: is 8D audio "EMDR therapy, only in music format"? The author's answer: it is not real EMDR, but 8D music can have a similar calming effect, helping cope with stress and anxiety in the moment [#351].

- How it works. 8D audio uses equalisation, panning and effects to create the sensation of sound moving around the listener's head — an immersive experience as if you were in the centre of a concert hall with musicians moving around you. Headphones are required for the full effect. It works on the principle of bilateral stimulation, similar to EMDR, which may help "balance" the brain, giving relaxation and mental clarity [#351].
- What bilateral stimulation (BLS) is. A psychotherapeutic method based on alternating stimulation of the left and right hemispheres; it can be visual, auditory or tactile. BLS is a key component of EMDR therapy used for PTSD and other psychological trauma; research, per the post, shows BLS may help process traumatic memories by reducing their emotional intensity. Its mechanism is linked to the orienting reflex — the nervous system's natural reaction to new stimuli — which diverts attention from anxious thoughts and promotes relaxation [#351].
- Safety. BLS is considered safe for most people but may cause unexpected reactions in people with heightened sensitivity to sensory stimuli or certain neurological conditions [#351].
- Potential benefits: (1) better concentration and focus, especially for people with ADHD; (2) reduced anxiety and stress through the calming effect; (3) a sense of "clearing the brain" of intrusive thoughts. Important: these benefits are only subjective listener reports; there is not yet enough research proving this self-help tool effective [#351].
- Possible side effects: dizziness or nausea in some listeners; sensory overload, especially in autistic people [#351].
- Not recommended: listening for a long time; running an EMDR session on yourself using music or other variants of the bilateral stimulation used in EMDR [#351].
- Bottom line. 8D music does not replace professional therapy but can be a useful tool for managing stress; as with any new method, listen to your body and stop if discomfort appears [#351].

### Detailed insights

#### Dorsal vagal shutdown (hypoarousal)
Dorsal vagal shutdown, also known as hypoarousal, is a type of "freeze response" that happens when facing overwhelming stress or trauma. It is a natural, normal reaction that lets the body save energy and protect itself when we cannot fight or flee; however, getting stuck in it can badly worsen quality of life [#60].
Symptoms may include: fatigue; dissociation; numbness; foggy mind; immobility; depression; fainting [#60].
It is important to understand that the state signals deeper psychological problems such as chronic stress or trauma, rather than being a standalone disorder: it arises as a symptom of ongoing issues (sustained stress, unresolved trauma or other mental-health problems), pointing to a deeper need for intervention. Treatment targets these underlying problems through therapy, medication and lifestyle changes aimed at stress management and self-care [#60].

#### Why regulation matters for rest and burnout recovery
The ND nervous system can get stuck in fight/flight or freeze in response to everyday stressors, unable to switch automatically into recovery and rest when the "danger" has passed. As a result, even when we set time aside to rest, we may not get its full restorative effect — which hurts burnout recovery, because rest time is a valuable resource we pin our hopes on. Techniques that switch the nervous system into safety mode make rest more effective and widen the window of tolerance (see *Nervous-system state shifting*) [#578].

#### Author's case: somatic discharge of hyperarousal in therapy
Working on the nervous system was the first self-help accessible to the author before she received treatment for САТК (the abbreviation is not expanded in this post) and therapy for cPTSD (кПТСР), and it still helps her. Her psychotherapist is certified for cPTSD work, trained in nervous-system stabilisation, knows the window of tolerance, and takes into account the author's other diagnoses, since each adds its own nuances to how the nervous system works [#729].
- Technique 1 — free discharge movement. When the author's activation rose during a session, the therapist suggested standing up together and finding a body movement comfortable for her that would help "release" excess energy. The author swayed side to side, dangling her arms "как-будто у меня вместо рук рукава" (as if I had sleeves instead of arms). This released the energy that was flooding and overloading her [#729].
- Lesson on unmasking. Writing this, she realised this is how unmasking should happen: without the therapist's suggestion she would most likely have sat holding everything in, and a shutdown would have happened at home, as it had before [#729].
- Technique 2 — single-leg balance. Next, standing on one foot with the other knee bent and balancing (the therapist did it all alongside her). This switched the brain further, because it got busy with the important task "нам надо не упасть" (we must not fall). The author's opinion: such natural shifting of the brain's attention helps well in the moment and offloads the nervous system [#729].
- She files both as ways of moving the nervous system from hyperarousal to a regulated state [#729].

#### Author's experience with 8D audio
The author liked the first audio (The Tucker Zone — warning: it contains a knock on the door) because while listening she visualised everything happening, which felt like training in being present. As a person with severe dissociation she finds it a useful way to hook her attention. The sound is very realistic, so she advises not to set volume to maximum. The songs she likes, but they make her motion-sick because the movement is much faster, so she can listen to only one at a time. As a handy way to cope with stress she likes it a lot; she cannot say how effective it is, but it does switch her mind, as attention quickly latches onto the rhythm and the changing position of the sound [#351].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The 8D audio benefits are explicitly only subjective listener reports; there is not enough research proving effectiveness. Do not self-administer EMDR with music or other BLS; avoid long listening; stop at discomfort; autistic and sensory-sensitive people may get overload, dizziness or nausea [#351].
- Several research claims (cold exposure raising HRV, the "om" chanting study, ten minutes of laughter, meditation and brain inflammation) are stated in the translated presentation without naming the studies [#159].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the presentation lists "ИСР" alongside vagal tone on the meditation slide; it is likely a typo for ВСР (HRV), but the source does not say [#159].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: pages 23–26 of the presentation, the linked articles (#34, #35, #56, #60, #103) and all video posts (#445, #499, #500, #505, #527, #529) carry content not available in the export [#159, msg #34, #35, #56, #60, #103, #445, #499, #500, #505, #527, #529].

## Sensory processing, overload and sensory regulation

### What this covers

This topic collects the channel's material on sensory overload in adults with ADHD and autism: the definitions of sensory regulation, dysregulation and self-regulation; the channel's central thesis that sensory regulation is the foundation on which emotional, behavioural and executive-function regulation rest; practical sensory regulation strategies (a sensory safety plan, environment modification, mindfulness and self-care, sensory-friendly activities); a first-person account of overload being mistaken for panic attacks; a comparison of stimming in ADHD and autism; and a short note on visual snow. According to the source, sensory overload "может значительно оказывать влияние на нашу жизнь, доставляя дискомфорт и вызывая постоянные циклы выгорания" (can significantly affect our life, causing discomfort and triggering constant cycles of burnout), so addressing it can significantly improve quality of life and help break the endless burnout cycle [#41].

### Core ideas

- Sensory overload is common in both ADHD and autism, but the stated cause differs. In ADHD the cause is difficulty *processing* sensory information; in autism it is difficulty *regulating* sensory input (see *Sensory overload in ADHD vs autism*) [#171, #172].
- Sensory regulation is the foundation of self-regulation. When the sensory system is out of balance, emotion regulation, focus and attention, control of actions and behaviour, and executive functions all suffer (see *Sensory regulation as the foundation of self-regulation*) [#46, #174, #175].
- Sensory regulation should be prioritised. Traditional approaches that target emotions or behaviour directly are, in the channel's presentation, ineffective when sensory dysregulation is present (see *Prioritising sensory regulation*) [#182, #183].
- Sensory overload is stress, and it can masquerade as panic. The presentation states "Сенсорная перегрузка = стресс" (sensory overload = stress) [#184]; a translated first-person post describes years of believing one had panic attacks that were in fact overload (see *Overload mistaken for panic attacks*) [#638].
- Stimming is regulation and communication, not bad behaviour. In ADHD it relates more to attention and releasing energy; in autism to self-regulation and sensory balance; both deserve respect (see *ADHD vs autism stimming comparison*) [#733].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### What sensory regulation is and what it looks like
Definition (presentation card): "Сенсорная регуляция - это процесс обработки сенсорной информации и управления уровнем возбуждения" (Sensory regulation is the process of processing sensory information and managing the level of arousal). It influences our ability to self-regulate and to adapt to the environment, and it allows us to filter, interpret and adapt to sensory stimuli [#168].

The six markers of working sensory regulation ("Как выглядит сенсорная регуляция?" — *what does sensory regulation look like?*) [#169]:
1. Focused attention — attention can be held on what matters.
2. Optimal response to sensory stimuli — reactions are proportionate, neither excessive nor absent.
3. Smooth transitions between activities — switching from one activity to another is not disruptive.
4. Comfortable interaction with sensory experiences — sensory input is engaged with without distress.
5. Emotional balance — emotions stay in equilibrium.
6. Enjoyment of sensory experiences — sensory input can be a source of pleasure.
[#169]

#### What sensory dysregulation is and what it leads to
Definition: "Сенсорная дирегуляция [sic] - это состояние, при котором наша система обработки сенсорной информации не справляется с сенсорными стимулами" (Sensory dysregulation is a state in which our sensory processing system cannot cope with sensory stimuli). It can lead to six outcomes [#170]:
1. Overload (перегрузка) — too much input.
2. Underload (недогрузка) — too little input; the source thus frames dysregulation as two-directional, not only "too much".
3. Increased or decreased sensitivity to sensory stimuli — hyper- or hypo-sensitivity.
4. Difficulty concentrating.
5. Emotional problems.
6. Reduced functioning (снижение функциональности).
[#170]

The concluding card adds that sensory dysregulation can deeply affect emotional experience in two opposite ways — "усиливая чувствительность или приводя к отключению" (heightening sensitivity or leading to shutdown) [#185].

#### Sensory overload in ADHD vs autism
The presentation devotes one card to each condition, with the same structure: cause → manifestations → what is important.

ADHD [#171]:
- Statement: people with ADHD often experience sensory overload.
- Cause: difficulties *processing* sensory information.
- Manifestations: (1) emotional outbursts; (2) difficulty concentrating.
- What matters: (1) recognising the influence of sensory overload; (2) developing strategies to manage it.

Autism [#172]:
- Statement: sensory overload is also common among autistic people (the card's OCR reads "артимом" [sic], i.e. autism).
- Cause: difficulties *regulating* sensory input.
- Manifestations: (1) overwhelming reactions to sensory stimuli; (2) heightened sensitivity to sensory input.
- What matters: (1) understanding the impact of sensory overload; (2) developing strategies for navigating and coping with the sensory environment.

The 2024 article post adds that in today's world, with an endless flow of information, stimuli and irritants every day, people with ADHD may feel sensory overload especially acutely, and everyday sensory impressions can turn into unbearable load. The article (written by an author drawing on personal experience and professional knowledge in clinical psychology) covers sensory processing, common symptoms of overload of the senses, and ways to ease or prevent the discomfort of overload; its stated purpose is to help readers adapt to and understand their own sensory needs [#25].

#### Self-regulation and its three components
Definition: self-regulation is the ability to manage thoughts, emotions and behaviour. It helps cope with difficulties, make decisions, manage emotions and maintain balance [#173]. It includes:
1. Self-awareness (самосознание) — recognising and understanding our emotions, thoughts and physical sensations.
2. Self-control (самоконтроль) — the ability to manage impulses and regulate our behaviour even in difficult situations.
3. Self-monitoring (самонаблюдение) — tracking our own progress, reflecting on our actions and making adjustments as needed.
[#173]

#### Sensory regulation as the foundation of self-regulation
The channel's central model: sensory regulation is the base layer; emotional, behavioural and executive regulation sit on top of it. The original article post lists four areas affected when the sensory system is in imbalance [#46]:
1. Emotional regulation — sensory dysregulation makes it harder to control our emotions.
2. Focus and attention — our ability to concentrate suffers when the sensory system is in imbalance.
3. Management of actions and behaviour — sensory dysregulation prevents us from effectively controlling our actions and reactions.
4. Executive function problems — sensory dysregulation can overload the brain, and as a result executive function suffers.
The article states that sensory regulation "служит основой для различных других систем саморегуляции" (serves as the basis for various other self-regulation systems), including emotions, executive functions and behaviour, and that different sensory regulation strategies can improve life [#46].

The presentation spells the link out [#174, #175]:
- Sensory regulation is the foundation for self-regulation; it influences emotions, behaviour, attention and relationships; dysregulation interferes with self-regulation [#174].
- Impact of sensory overload: it makes managing emotions harder; weakens attention and executive functions; reduces control over actions; interferes with regulating relationships [#174].
- Improved sensory regulation supports self-regulation, creates a balanced sensory environment and helps cope with overload [#175].
- Conclusion: sensory regulation is the basis of self-regulation; strengthening sensory regulation improves self-regulation; developing both systems allows one to function successfully in a world of sensory stimuli [#175].

Layer 1 — Sensory regulation and emotion regulation [#176, #177]:
- Impact of overload on emotions: it disrupts emotional well-being; causes heightened emotional reactions; leads to irritation, anxiety and nervous breakdowns [#176].
- Emotion regulation is defined as the ability to understand, manage and respond to emotions, and as a set of skills for recognising and managing emotions [#176].
- Link: sensory regulation provides the basis for emotion regulation. "Хорошая сенсорная регуляция = более стабильное эмоциональное состояние" (Good sensory regulation = a more stable emotional state); problems with sensory regulation = difficulty managing emotions [#177].
- Conclusion: meeting sensory regulation needs = more successful emotion regulation [#177].

Layer 2 — Sensory regulation and behaviour regulation [#178, #179]:
- Sensory dysregulation leads to overload; disrupts attention, focus and emotions; overloads both the cognitive and the emotional system; and weakens three abilities: (a) concentrating on tasks, (b) filtering out non-essential stimuli, (c) taking part in tasks and activities [#178].
- Behaviour regulation is the ability to manage and modulate actions, reactions and behaviour. It includes: (1) self-control; (2) impulse management; (3) making considered decisions; (4) adapting to change and new environments; (5) maintaining socially acceptable behaviour [#178].
- Mechanism: problems with sensory processing (overload / high sensitivity) affect behaviour regulation because sensory difficulties trigger stress reactions, and stress makes it harder to modify actions and behaviour [#179].
- Conclusion: sensory regulation and behaviour regulation are interconnected; sensory dysregulation can negatively affect behaviour regulation [#179].

Layer 3 — Sensory regulation and executive functions [#180, #181]:
- Executive functions are cognitive processes for: planning; organisation; concentration; impulse regulation; achieving goals. They include working memory, flexible thinking, self-control and problem solving. They matter for goal-directed behaviour, decision making and task management [#180].
- Interrelation: sensory problems overload the brain and affect executive functions; sensory regulation supports executive functions and improves regulation of attention and concentration. Conclusion: the two are closely linked; supporting sensory regulation raises effectiveness and improves cognitive functions [#181].

#### Prioritising sensory regulation: building the foundation for effective regulation
Purpose: argues for the order of intervention — sensory first [#182, #183].
- Traditional approaches: focus on regulating emotions or behaviour; do not take sensory regulation into account; are ineffective when sensory dysregulation is present [#182].
- Sensory regulation: the foundation for effective self-regulation; it provides stability for regulating emotions and behaviour [#182].
- Three ways to remove sensory dysregulation: (1) creating a sensory-friendly environment; (2) providing sensory breaks; (3) using sensory-oriented interventions and therapy [#182].
- Result: a solid base for self-regulation is built, and the effectiveness of emotion and behaviour regulation increases [#183].
- Comprehensive approach: takes into account the interconnections between sensory, emotional and behavioural regulation and paves the way to successful self-regulation [#183].

The concluding card summarises the method: by prioritising sensory regulation — integrating and adjusting sensory input, managing levels of arousal, attention and emotional reactions — we create an environment that improves emotional resilience and regulation skills, contributing to overall well-being. "Признание важности сенсорной безопасности раскрывает силу саморегуляции" (Recognising the importance of sensory safety unlocks the power of self-regulation). Sensory regulation forms the basis for navigating the world with more comfort and well-being; whether you are a neurodivergent person or a caregiver, implementing sensory regulation strategies creates a supportive environment and strengthens self-regulation skills [#185].

#### Three sensory regulation strategies
Goal: support sensory regulation and improve well-being [#184].
1. Sensory safety plan (план сенсорной безопасности).
   - Identify the actions/experiences that give you regulated sensory input — the card's examples: calming sensory activities, music, sensory toys.
   - Draw up a plan for managing sensory triggers and sensory-saturated environments (i.e. decide in advance what you will do when you meet a trigger or must be in a high-stimulation place).
2. Environment modification.
   - Adjust the lighting and reduce noise.
   - Use visual supports and organisation systems.
3. Mindfulness and self-care (Сознательность и самоуход).
   - Practise mindfulness techniques: deep breathing, meditation, mindful (body) scanning.
   - Prioritise self-care: sensory breaks and releasing excess energy.
Reminders on the card: "Сенсорная перегрузка = стресс" (sensory overload = stress) and "Обучение расслаблению - важный инструмент" (learning to relax is an important tool) [#184].

#### Sensory-friendly activities
Definition: "активности, которые учитывают индивидуальные сенсорные потребности человека" (activities that take a person's individual sensory needs into account) [#54].
Benefits for neurodivergent people listed by the author [#54]:
1. Fewer stimulating impressions that lead to overload.
2. Creating a predictable and controllable environment.
3. Offering different sensory options to adjust the level of stimulation.
4. Developing self-awareness skills and understanding of one's sensory needs.
5. Fewer distractions and better concentration.
6. Higher motivation and engagement.
Effects on general well-being [#54]:
1. Lower stress and anxiety.
2. Higher levels of happiness and well-being.
3. Better quality of life.
How to use the author's list: it is only an example of possible activities; the important thing is to observe what you personally like and what suits you, use the example to compose your own list, and use that list at the moment you need it. The author closes: do not be ashamed of the features of your nervous system, since caring for it can significantly improve quality of life [#54].

#### Overload mistaken for panic attacks
A translated first-person post (source: FINCHOFAUTISMLIFE) titled "Это были не панические атаки. Это была перегрузка." (These were not panic attacks. It was overload.), subtitled "Мой аутичный мозг пытался мне что-то сказать" (My autistic brain was trying to tell me something). The author spent years believing they had panic attacks, but it was stimulation overload in an autistic adult [#638].

1. Symptoms that looked like panic: rapid heartbeat; tightness in the chest; the urge to run away; tears out of nowhere. "Naturally, I thought: this is anxiety." [#638]
2. The mismatch that gave it away: the episodes were not connected with anxiety or fear. They happened in specific conditions — noisy places; bright light; crowds; constant conversations; or having to do too many things at once [#638].
3. Turning point: learning about sensory overload made everything clear: "Я не паниковал(а), я был(а) перегружен(а)" (I wasn't panicking, I was overloaded) [#638].
4. How overload feels (distinguishing signs): the brain seems to switch off; noise and light become unbearable; sudden irritability or "shutdown"; a sudden feeling of cold or heat; an acute urge to run away or hide; sometimes even losing the ability to speak [#638].
5. Understanding: being autistic, their nervous system perceives the world differently, which means they can get overloaded "даже если "ничего не случилось"" (even if "nothing happened") [#638].
6. What they do now instead of blaming themselves: take breaks in silence; allow themselves to stim; avoid bright light and chaotic places; say "no" without guilt; plan rest time after socialising; use grounding techniques; use daytime naps [#638].
7. Message to the reader: if this is familiar, you are not being dramatic, not broken and not "too sensitive"; you may be autistic, sensory-sensitive or both. "Твоё тело просто просит покоя" (Your body is simply asking for rest/peace) [#638].

#### ADHD vs autism stimming comparison
Translated post (source: ashahaidelhi). Stimming (self-stimulatory behaviour) is often misunderstood, yet it is one of the most important ways children (and adults) with autism and ADHD regulate their inner world. The comparison runs along five dimensions [#733]:

1. When stimming appears
   - ADHD: often during boredom or low concentration; helps regulate attention and relieve tension; usually decreases with full engagement.
   - Autism: often during sensory overload or strong emotions; gives a sense of comfort and predictability; may intensify with stress, emotional arousal or in new places.
2. Purpose
   - ADHD: relieving restlessness; staying alert in boring situations; works as a way to "wake up" the brain ("разбудить" мозг).
   - Autism: calms the nervous system; helps cope with excess sensory information; provides self-soothing and emotional regulation.
3. Typical kinds
   - ADHD: nail biting; chewing pencils/clothes; tapping or drawing; bouncing or jiggling a leg.
   - Autism: rocking back and forth; hand flapping or spinning; lining objects up in a row; repeating words, phrases or sounds.
4. Impact on daily life
   - ADHD: perceived as "fidgeting" in class; can genuinely improve concentration when allowed; often wrongly perceived as a lack of discipline.
   - Autism: may look unusual to others; sometimes misread as "strange behaviour"; extremely important for managing emotions and sensory needs.
5. Adults' reactions
   - ADHD: adults often say "sit still" or "stop it"; support includes stress toys, movement and chewing aids.
   - Autism: sometimes considered "inappropriate"; support means safe conditions for stimming and spaces that take sensory needs into account.

Key thought: stimming in ADHD is more connected with attention and releasing energy, in autism with self-regulation and sensory balance; both kinds are natural and deserve respect. Instead of trying to stop stimming, offer safe options — stress toys, chewing accessories, sensory pauses or calm spaces; these small supports help children feel understood. "стимминг — это не «плохое поведение», а способ коммуникации" (stimming is not "bad behaviour" but a way of communicating) [#733].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The ADHD/autism cause distinction (processing vs regulating sensory input) is stated on slides without further explanation or evidence [#171, #172].
- The claim that traditional emotion/behaviour-focused approaches are ineffective under sensory dysregulation is the presentation's position; no studies are cited [#182].
- The panic-vs-overload account is a single person's translated experience, not a diagnostic criterion; it does not claim panic attacks never occur in autistic people [#638].
- The stimming comparison is a generalised translated infographic-style post; its categories (e.g. typical stims per condition) are tendencies ("часто", "обычно" — *often, usually*), not rules [#733].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the full texts of the three articles, the activities list and the video in #450 were not available [#25, #41, #46, #54, #450].
- Several slide transcriptions contain OCR typos ("дирегуляция", "артимом", "звоции"); the meanings (dysregulation, autism, emotions) are clear from context [#170, #172, #176].
