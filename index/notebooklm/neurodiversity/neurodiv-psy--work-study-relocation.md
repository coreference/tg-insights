## Neurodivergent people at work and university: job fit and accommodations

### What this covers

This topic gathers everything the channel taught about autistic and ADHD people in two settings: higher education and employment. It covers the research picture (lower graduation rates, very high unemployment), the concrete difficulties people meet (adaptation, social life, sensory overload, onboarding, hiring interviews), which kinds of work tend to fit, a needs pyramid derived from a study of autistic women at work, the role of values and special interests in motivation, and a practical toolkit of accommodations that a person can arrange without disclosing a diagnosis. According to the source, the core problem is not neurodivergence itself but a mismatch between the person and the environment — and that mismatch can be reduced [#200, #306, #371, #406, #413].

This part covers one forwarded post (#193) from the channel «Нейросеть» («о нейроотличности»): Лиза Носова's longread «Аутичные люди и работа» (Autistic people and work), with illustrations by Сашик, saved in full as a Telegraph article. It reviews research on autistic people's employment — statistics from several countries, why the job interview is the hardest stage, the difference between helping by changing the person and helping by changing the environment, which interview adjustments are reasonable, which workplace supports exist and how well they are studied — and ends with a caveated list of Russian organisations that help autistic people find paid work. According to the author, employment improves the lives of disabled people (independence for them and their families, economic contribution, higher quality of life), yet autistic people are rarely employed, so it matters what kind of help actually works [#193].

Relation to the existing topic file: the existing file (from the channel author's later posts #406 and #413) already says the hiring process tests social conformity rather than job skills, lists onboarding/sensory/stigma causes of job loss and gives a self-arranged accommodation toolkit for post-Soviet conditions. This longread extends it with the research evidence behind the interview problem, the person-vs-environment framing, the employer-side interview adjustments, workplace accommodations at the organisational level, the critique of masking-oriented training, and Russian supported-employment organisations. It contradicts in numbers one figure: #406 cites 22% of autistic people officially employed in the UK, while this article cites 29% for 2021 (see *Caveats*) [#193].

### Core ideas

- Neurodivergence does not by itself prevent work — mismatch does. Research cited by the channel author says most autistic adults with low support needs and most adults with ADHD adapt well and perform in very diverse career areas when they choose suitable work; what hinders them is the mismatch between environment and person, and accommodations measurably raise productivity and job satisfaction [#406].
- The labour market does not reflect this. Unemployment among autistic adults is 50–80% and ADHD adults are 2–3 times more likely to be jobless; the hiring process itself tests social conformity rather than job skills (see *Causes of unemployment and job loss*) [#406].
- At university, studying is the easier part; adaptation and social life are the hard parts. Autistic students graduate far less often (39% vs 59%), yet 48% are satisfied with their academic success; the main difficulties are adaptation (including a higher risk of abuse in dormitories) and social life (see *Autism and higher education*) [#201, #202, #203, #204, article #200].
- The main task at work is not to burn out. In the autistic women's study, avoiding (primarily neurodivergent) burnout is the base on which everything else rests; sensory fit and fit to the cognitive profile are must-haves [#371].
- Connection of work to personal interests and values is a "should", not a "nice to have". Long-term endurance on willpower alone does not work for autistic people and is "a guaranteed path" to burnout and depression [#371].
- In post-Soviet realities, official accommodations are almost impossible to request; ask for adjustments as personal preferences, without naming a diagnosis, and use self-arranged tools (see *Accommodations without disclosure*) [#413].
- Jobs reported as fitting share flexibility, autonomy, concreteness, impact, few people and variety — readers' jobs almost never involved strict organisational requirements or routine, unchanging processes [#306].

- Autistic people are rarely employed, though they can work long and successfully. When they leave a job it is because of social deficits, not because they cannot cope with the work duties [#193].
- There are two directions of help, and each can help or harm: work with the autistic person (e.g. teaching skills) or work with the environment (e.g. inclusion training for colleagues) [#193].
- The interview is the hardest stage. Autistic people are consistently rated worse than non-autistic people with otherwise equal qualifications, because interviews weigh social skills by neurotypical standards even when the job does not need them [#193].
- Preparing the person alone is not enough and is unfair: you cannot prepare for everything, and it puts a disproportionate load on autistic people, who must work much harder for the same result [#193].
- Advice that pushes masking is harmful, because masking damages mental health [#193].
- Combinations of strategies work best, especially in the long term; standard disability accommodations may not be enough for autism [#193].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Autism and higher education - difficulties and self-help accommodations
Purpose: a card series by the channel author, based on a talk she gave at a conference about her own experience of getting a university degree as an autistic person, describing what difficulties autistic students face and what to do about them. Title card: «АУТИЗМ И ВЫСШЕЕ ОБРАЗОВАНИЕ СЛОЖНОСТИ И АККОМОДАЦИИ» (Autism and higher education: difficulties and accommodations). The same text was also published as a Telegraph longread «Аутизм и высшее образование» (Autism and higher education), which follows the same structure — background data, adaptation, social features, academic features, how to help yourself — with a few differences in wording noted below [#200, img #200, article #200].

The author's own verdict: university was still better than school for her, even though she was dying of anxiety every exam session: «В моем опыте университет все еще был лучше, чем школа, хотя каждую сессию я умирала от тревоги» (In my experience university was still better than school, although every exam session I was dying of anxiety). She asks readers what their own experience of studying was like [#200].

Background data. Compared with non-autistic peers, autistic school pupils prepare less for university admission and less often enter full bachelor's programmes (the card specifies «полноценные четырехлетние бакалаврские программы» — *full four-year bachelor's programmes*; the article says just «полноценные бакалаврские программы» — *full bachelor's programmes*). During study they more often experience higher levels of loneliness, anxiety and depression. Graduation rates of autistic students are markedly lower (39%) than for peers in the general population (59%) or for students with disabilities (50%), per Petcu, Zhang & Li (2021). The comparison group for the 50% figure is worded differently: the card says students «с другими типами инвалидности» (with other types of disability), the article «со всеми типами инвалидности» (with all types of disability) [#201, article #200].

Difficulty area 1 — Adaptation. The article calls this, «конечно» (of course), one of the main trials at university: the adjustment period, the transformation «из школьника в студента» (from a school pupil into a student). Its five components [#202, article #200]:
1. A new environment and new people.
2. Different rules of studying than at school.
3. Moving away from home and losing one's support system.
4. Difficulties with household skills — the card gives cooking and cleaning as examples.
5. Dormitories — the card adds living together with strangers; the article adds that «аутичные люди чаще сталкиваются с абьюзом в общежитиях» (autistic people more often face abuse in dormitories).

Difficulty area 2 — Social features. «Зачастую это основная сфера сложностей» (Often this is the main area of difficulty). Its components [#203, article #200]:
1. Trouble making friends.
2. High social anxiety and emotional dysregulation.
3. Lack of knowledge about autism among the people around (Hillier et al., 2018).
4. Bullying (Connor, 2013).

Difficulty area 3 — Academic features. With studying itself things are much better: «С самой учебой все намного лучше» (With studying itself everything is much better). Recent research shows 48% of autistic students are satisfied with their academic success (Jackson et al., 2018). The difficulties that remain (Gurbuz et al., 2019) [#204, article #200]:
1. Speed of information processing.
2. Sensory overload during classes.
3. Time management.
4. Difficulty asking questions.
5. Oral presentations and oral answers.
6. Group work.
7. Motivation.

How to help yourself («Как помочь себе?») — ten self-help accommodations, identical in the cards and the article [#205, #206, article #200]:
1. Research as much information about the university as possible in advance and find the curriculum («учебный план»).
2. Find a psychologist, counsellor or coach who will support you, work with you on emotional regulation and help, for example, to compose a letter to a teacher.
3. Ask teachers for the slides of their classes, or photograph them during class.
4. Ask classmates for their lecture notes — «возможно, за ответную помощь или шоколадку :)» (*perhaps in exchange for help in return or a chocolate bar :)*).
5. Bring fidget toys, headphones and similar items.
6. Don't forget to take breaks, eat and drink.
7. Don't be shy about leaving the class if you feel unwell («если стало плохо»).
8. Try to negotiate written instead of oral examination in some subjects.
9. Look for support groups inside or outside the university.
10. Ask for help and advice about your difficulties — this can be done in Telegram groups or on Reddit.

New challenges (card 8): the final card, titled «НОВЫЕ ЧЕЛЛЕНДЖИ...» (New challenges...), shows a news headline (NY Post) next to a ChatGPT screen: a Purdue University professor was accused of being an AI bot because her e-mail lacked "human warmth", and answered «Я просто аутичные» (OCR of the Russian rendering; *I'm just autistic*). The lesson implied by the card is that in the age of chatbots autistic people's direct, low-affect written style can be mistaken for AI text — a new kind of misunderstanding [#207].

#### Five job areas reported as fitting ADHD/ASD
Origin: the author of the channel *как помыть посуду* (forwarded by this channel) asked readers what jobs they do and how well they fit their ADHD/ASD, then grouped the many answers by type of work. A side observation: there were almost no positions with strict requirements on how work is organised and almost none involving routine and an unchanging work process [#306].

1. IT: testing, working with text, analytics, programming. Main traits: clear requirements and understandable processes; a need for attention to detail; a minimum of distractions, especially in technical tasks; often the possibility to work independently or with minimal social interaction. Why it suits neurodivergent people: such positions allow entering hyperfocus and being more productive than usual; repeatability, structure and clarity of actions lower anxiety before new tasks; remote work [#306].
2. Creative and visual professions: artist, web designer, perfumer's assistant, stylist. Shared traits: visual or tactile perception; the chance to express oneself and be creative; often project or freelance work with a flexible schedule. Why it suits: creativity can help cope with emotional tension; a flexible schedule allows accounting for energy patterns, sensory overload and planning problems [#306].
3. Education and languages: tutor and teacher, translator. Shared traits: often one-to-one interaction; passing on knowledge about your special interest; the possibility to build the learning process your own way. Why it suits: teaching gives a sense of being useful to society and to a specific person. Minus: problems with self-organisation may arise, and teaching in a school can create problems because of the "group" nature of the work (overload, difficulties with interactions) [#306].
4. Working with living beings and manual work: veterinary medicine, gardening, taxidermy, jewellery making. Shared traits: working with one's hands, with "reality", not only mental activity; often physical activity; involvement of the senses. Why it suits: less abstraction, more concreteness; moving and working with one's hands in natural conditions reduces tension and anxiety; working with animals means no masking and pleasant interaction [#306].
5. Independent, flexible employment: entrepreneurship, sales. Shared traits: being your own boss or working in an informal structure; being able to influence the volume of tasks and when they are done; more freedom, fewer routine demands from above. Why it suits: neurodivergent people often find it hard to adapt to rigid structures and bureaucracy, whereas here they can build work processes completely around themselves [#306].

#### Six principles for choosing work with ADHD/ASD (job bingo chart)
From the common traits of readers' jobs, the *как помыть посуду* author compiled (and, she notes, supplemented herself) a list of traits of an "ADHD/ASD-friendly" job, drawn as a picture titled (in OCR-garbled form) *Main principles for choosing a job with ADHD/ASD*. She suggests using it as a bingo chart when looking for a new job — ticking off how many principles a vacancy meets [#306, img #306].

1. Flexible schedule and autonomy — so that you can work in moments of concentration and rest when overloaded without consequences for the work [#306].
2. Not detached from your plans — the work should be linked to your interests and plans; otherwise there will not be enough energy left for self-realisation and leisure, which leads to burnout [#306].
3. Minimum bureaucracy and abstraction — it is easier to work with concrete things, the senses, visuals or data than with abstract requirements [#306].
4. About impact — the work should produce a visible result and give a feeling of being needed by people and of the work's importance. Being an insignificant cog in the system will not work for long [#306].
5. Alone or one-to-one — fewer people means less anxiety, less masking, less overload. A small team is better than a large staff (the card reads «Маленький коллектив • большой штат», the symbol between the two presumably meaning "better than" — (!) UNCLEAR: OCR lost the comparison sign) [#306].
6. Creativity and variety — routine, monotonous work is tiring, while varied and creative tasks sustain interest in the work, pushing back the feeling of "rusting" («заржавления») [#306].

#### Pyramid of autistic people's needs in the work environment
Origin: preliminary results of a study by the author of the channel *Об аутичных женщинах, по которым не скажешь* (About autistic women you wouldn't guess are autistic), forwarded here. For some respondents many workplace needs are systematically unmet, which leads to overloads and burnouts. Others managed to set up or find a suitable environment and processes, thereby lowering the frequency of burnouts and staying longer at the same company. The author arranged the needs as a pyramid with layers from critical (must have) to desirable (nice have); she had not yet collected anything for "nice have", so the pyramid is truncated (the top is a question mark). She asks readers what they want but would give up without changing the job itself [#371, img #371].

The base, per the author: «основная задача на работе у нас - не выгореть» (our main task at work is not to burn out) — first neurodivergent burnout, then professional burnout, though in an autistic person's experience the two seem tightly intertwined [#371].

Layers, from bottom to top [#371]:

Layer 1 — Physical environment: fit to the sensory profile (MUST HAVE; level of physiological needs). Task: prevent or reduce the frequency of overloads and burnout. Needs [#372]:
1. The ability to control the space: lighting, sound, temperature, smells, layout.
2. Space and time for timely sensory deprivation.
An "office sensory deprivation kit" (the card notes it was generated with ChatGPT): earplugs; sunglasses with side shields; noise-cancelling headphones; a tube hoodie / hood; a sleep and pause mask; a weighted lap pillow; compression gloves; an ointment or barrier spray / odour neutraliser. A subscriber joked that the kit should also include a wallet with money to buy all this [#372].

Layer 2 — Organisation, processes, atmosphere: fit to the cognitive profile and to motivation features (productivity cycles) (MUST HAVE; level of physiological needs). Task: prevent/reduce overloads and burnout and raise the effectiveness of work. Needs [#373, #374]:
1. Ability to control the schedule: hybrid, remote work, extra days off, alternating days of activity and types of tasks (for example, a day of teamwork, then a day of quiet solo work from home), a flexible length of the working day depending on individual activity cycles [#373].
2. Ability to control the types of tasks and formats of work based on one's strengths, competences, inclinations and limitations [#373].
3. An inclusive atmosphere/culture: an accepting team informed about neurodivergence — the possibility of NOT masking all the time [#373].
4. Detailed onboarding of a new autistic employee; reducing uncertainty in tasks, feedback and planning [#373].
5. Finding ways of assigning tasks that do not provoke demand avoidance — the card offers tentative options with question marks: free choice from a list? finding a person as supervisor with whom there is a match in values and approaches, if it did not work out with the direct manager? [#374]
6. Ability to control task deadlines and avoid hard deadlines [#374].
7. Ability to control hyperfocus, including with external tools [#374].
8. Ability to control the style and frequency of communication — as a rule it should be structured, have a clear goal and meaning, and be time-limited [#374].

Layer 3 — Meanings: fit of the work or of individual tasks to the person's values and/or interests (SHOULD HAVE; level of psychology and identity). Task: maintaining motivation, overcoming barriers, increasing (preserving) productivity [#371, #375]. The author stresses that this is not "nice have" but "should": the work should relate to you personally. This noticeably lowers stress and the probability of burnout — provided the physiological-level needs are met. Achieving goals and getting results can itself be a special interest; a special interest is not necessarily a field of activity or your current hobby: «Это что-то, контактируя с чем, вы чувствуете прилив сил, вдохновение, радость или просто покой» (It is something in contact with which you feel a surge of energy, inspiration, joy or simply calm). This does not mean you must monetise your hobby; but if your work contains something that resonates with you personally and brings joy, life becomes much easier. Patience and moving purely on willpower do not work for autistic people in the long term — it is possible, but it is a guaranteed path to burnout and depression [#371].

Example from a study participant whose value is helping people (consciously adding meaning to everyday hard tasks): she dislikes phoning both strangers and acquaintances, but sometimes has to call colleagues in other cities to agree changes to ticket or hotel bookings. It becomes easier to contact them first when she realises she is helping them; she is not calling to chat about the weather but with a concrete question needing a concrete answer: «Вот это меня смиряет с» … «необходимостью идти на контакт» (This reconciles me with the need to make contact) [#375].

Layer 4 — NICE HAVE: empty, marked "?" — no data yet [#371].

#### Causes of unemployment and job loss in autistic and ADHD adults
From the channel author's post "(Не)трудоспособные: нейроотличия и работа" (*(Un)employable: neurodivergence and work*) [#406].

Weaker zones for autistic people:
1. Insufficient or poor-quality onboarding (help adapting) at a new workplace.
2. Not understanding the unwritten rules of social interaction.
3. Mismatched communication styles and misunderstanding from those around.
4. Uncomfortable sensory features of the environment.
5. Stigma around autism.

Additional difficulties for people with ADHD:
1. Difficulty planning and organising time, missing deadlines — especially when there are no clear instructions about deadlines and consequences.
2. Chronic lateness.
3. Impulsivity, which is easily read as unreliability.
4. Fast burnout from work processes not designed for their pace and way of concentrating.

One more, equally important cause — the hiring process itself: «сам процесс найма устроен так, что оценивает не рабочие навыки, а соответствие ожиданиям общества и компании» (the hiring process itself is designed to assess not work skills but conformity to the expectations of society and the company) — predictability, visible friendliness, the ability to talk about yourself quickly and smoothly. An autistic or ADHD person can have the needed skills and completely fail the interview, because the interview tests the wrong abilities [#406].

The remedy: accommodations — flexible schedule, the possibility of remote work, clear written instructions instead of vague oral ones, structured onboarding. When they appear, results change noticeably: both productivity and job satisfaction rise [#406].

#### Accommodations without disclosure - four-area toolkit
Context (channel author): for a neurodivergent person already on the labour market, «просить у работодателя официальные аккомодации в постсоветских реалиях почти нереально» (asking the employer for official accommodations in post-Soviet realities is almost unrealistic). Disclosing a diagnosis often equals the risk of not getting an offer or being refused under a plausible pretext. The most one can afford at an interview is to carefully ask about conditions ("is the schedule flexible", "how is feedback organised") and look at the conditions potential colleagues work in. If already employed, explain your neurodivergent traits as personal features or preferences without naming the diagnosis: «я продуктивнее работаю, когда слушаю музыку» (I work more productively when I listen to music), «мне иногда важно походить по кабинету туда-сюда и подумать» (sometimes it's important for me to walk back and forth around the office and think). The toolkit below does not depend on whether the company is ready to talk about neurodivergence; some items you do yourself, others you can propose as a way to "help you understand tasks better and cope better" [#413].

Area 1 — Job search [#413]:
1. Courses and mentoring to build the skills needed for job search and interviews; or work on these competences with a coach, career consultant or psychologist; if money is short, YouTube has many free videos on passing interviews. (The author's own upcoming webinar/course with Карина Аруштова is mentioned here.)
2. Ask acquaintances for advice, watch videos, read advice on writing CVs and cover letters. It is always good if a knowledgeable person looks over your CV — or, as a last resort, an AI chatbot if nobody is around (the author notes the AI conversation is complicated and does not go into it). The key is to understand what exactly the recruiter/employer wants to see.
3. Remember you are not obliged to match a vacancy 100%; autistic black-and-white thinking can get very much in the way here.
4. Don't be afraid to embellish your work experience: «Нормально написать себе 9 месяцев работы в компании вместо 6» (It's normal to write 9 months of work at a company instead of 6).
5. During the interview, if possible, assess how well the workplace fits you on various indicators: noise and light levels, the team, workload, number of interactions per day.

Area 2 — Sensory and emotional needs [#413]:
1. Noise reduction: noise-cancelling headphones and earplugs. Popular options: Silensys headphones and Loop earplugs; cheaper: ordinary construction ear defenders or earplugs sold everywhere.
2. Light: ideally the ability to control lighting; in reality, photochromic glasses (lenses that change colour depending on lighting).
3. Breaks in quiet places to avoid overstimulation — scout in advance where you can escape to: outside, a storeroom, the toilet or another unpopulated place.
4. Bring fidget toys for stimming; you can choose something as inconspicuous as possible.
5. Tools for mood monitoring and emotional regulation: mood-tracking apps (Finch — the author: «оч люблю!» (really love it!) — How We Feel, Daylio; the source itself ends the list with «и др.») and mindfulness/relaxation apps (Headspace, MindShift; again «и др.» in the source).

Area 3 — Social adaptations [#413]:
1. Ideally, informing employers and colleagues about neurodivergence, support and emotional regulation. In reality, disclosure sometimes leads to dismissal — but you can talk about your traits without the diagnosis, e.g. «Знаешь, я не очень хорошо понимаю сарказм, не мог бы ты говорить более прямо, что тебе не нравится?» (You know, I don't understand sarcasm very well, could you say more directly what you don't like?).
2. Acceptance and respect from colleagues — something to use as a criterion when choosing a team/company.
3. Socially oriented coaching or psychological counselling to acquire communication skills and navigate workplace relationships and etiquette.
4. Speech-to-text programs if dictating is easier than typing (many operating systems have this built in today).
5. Augmentative and alternative communication devices that help people with limited verbal skills communicate.
6. Friends/acquaintances (or, again as a last resort, AI chatbots) who can help decode what colleagues want from you when you can't figure it out yourself.

Area 4 — Workplace routine, tasks and related communication [#413]:
1. Apps for task management, planning, prioritisation and decision-making, such as Todoist, Google Calendar, Sectograph and many others.
2. A fixed or flexible work schedule, depending on your needs.
3. Ask for concrete feedback — you can ask for examples of what was great and what was not, and next steps.
4. If relations with colleagues and manager are normal, ask them to set clearer tasks and deadlines; if not, make the vague task as clear as possible yourself (see *Making a vague task concrete*).

#### Making a vague task concrete
When a manager gives a fuzzy instruction and you cannot ask them to clarify, break it into concrete actions yourself. The author's example (with a joke as the last step): «мне сказали быть организованнее, что я могу сделать? 1. завести трекер задач; 2. записать, что от меня ожидают в ближайшую неделю; 3. поплакать» (I was told to be more organised, what can I do? 1. set up a task tracker; 2. write down what is expected of me in the coming week; 3. have a cry) [#413].

#### Two directions of help - working with the person vs working with the environment
Purpose: the author's lens for evaluating any solution to autistic people's problems, including employment. States and charities in various countries try to help autistic people find work, and solutions can go in two directions [#193]:
1. «Чтобы сделать лучше, мы работаем с аутичным человеком» (To make things better, we work with the autistic person) — for example, by giving them the necessary skills.
2. «Чтобы сделать лучше, мы работаем с окружающей средой» (To make things better, we work with the environment) — for example, by running inclusion training for the person's colleagues.

The author's verdict: «Оба этих направления могут быть по-своему полезны, и оба могут принести вред» (Both directions can be useful in their own way, and both can do harm). The rest of the article applies this lens first to hiring, then to support on the job [#193].

#### Hiring stage, direction 1: preparing the person
Getting a job is a multi-step process that can overload at different stages (for example, when responding to a vacancy), but the interview is usually the hardest. Preparation of the person takes two forms [#193]:
1. Help with job search — e.g. discussing different options given the candidate's needs, preferences and strengths.
2. Courses or trainings on how to get a job, what will happen and how to behave at an interview.

Why it helps: prepared people, autistic ones included, really can perform better. If a person knows what awaits them, how hiring works and what questions may be asked, they are less anxious and more likely to meet the employer's expectations. Why it is not enough on its own: first, you cannot insure against everything; second, and more important, «этот подход возлагает непомерную ответственность на аутичных людей» (this approach places an exorbitant responsibility on autistic people), who have to work much harder than non-autistic people for the same results [#193].

#### Criteria for judging interview-preparation advice (NAS vs Autism Speaks)
The author contrasts two concrete materials, which together give the criteria for telling good preparation advice from bad [#193]:

"Not excellent, but not bad" example — the National Autistic Society free course on finding employment (free, registration required, aimed at UK residents). It contains:
- general rules of conduct (e.g. clean, ironed clothes);
- the recommendation to ask for special conditions (accommodations) if you need them;
- example answers to likely questions, which help you understand what the interviewer expects [#193].

A page from the course on clothing (translated in the post) advises [#193]: think in advance about what you will wear; clothes should fit the role — more formal for an office, more casual for, say, an animal centre — though for interviews most people dress more smartly to make a good impression. The usual choice is a dark suit, shirt and tie; other options are a dress, or smart trousers and a shirt/jacket, preferably in muted/neutral colours. Also: have a smart bag for your things (do not bring a plastic bag); make sure your shoes are clean; make sure your clothes are washed and ironed.

"How not to do it" — the Autism Speaks article on preparing for an interview. The author calls it bad for three reasons [#193]:
1. It places responsibility on autistic people where it could be placed on the employer.
2. It over-controls candidates without separating more important factors (reading example questions in advance) from less important ones (sitting down only after the interviewer does).
3. It puts heavy emphasis on autistic people masking as neurotypical — «и это плохо, потому что такая маскировка вредит психическому здоровью» (and this is bad, because such masking harms mental health).

So the implied criteria: share responsibility with the employer, prioritise what matters, include asking for accommodations, and do not train masking [#193].

#### Reasonable adjustments for a job interview (NAS course, 8 items)
An interactive task from the NAS course: if you stated on your application that you are autistic, you can ask for reasonable adjustments; the learner is asked which of these are reasonable for an interview. The answer: «You might be surprised to know that actually all of the examples shown can be examples of reasonable adjustments» (all the examples shown can be reasonable adjustments). The eight adjustments [#193]:
1. Providing additional information before the interview: a map of the building, photos of the room, details of the tasks.
2. Telling you if you are giving too much information.
3. Sharing the interview questions in advance.
4. Not assessing you negatively based on body language or eye contact.
5. Asking specific questions and questions based on past experience instead of vague or hypothetical ones.
6. Allowing you to bring a "translator" who can rephrase unclear questions or prompt for more information.
7. Allowing extra time to answer.
8. Providing work trials or portfolio-based interviews instead of face-to-face questions.

#### Hiring stage, direction 2: changing the environment
Factors on the environment side [#193]:
1. General autism awareness among HR staff. When people have at least some idea of a candidate's possible traits, their readiness to hire an autistic person rises. In UK and Australian studies, telling HR that a candidate was autistic shifted interview ratings slightly in a positive direction — but these were experiments, not real hiring. Autistic people from Canada reported mixed experience: sometimes they were refused after disclosing the diagnosis, sometimes the opposite.
2. Specific changes: adapting questions, assessment criteria and the environment. In one study, autistic and neurotypical people were interviewed with standard questions and rated; six months later the experiment was repeated with more concrete, structured questions. Autistic candidates were rated worse both times, but the gap shrank the second time. The author found no studies testing autistic candidates in different interview environments, but reducing sound and visual noise "sounds like a good idea" wherever people work, though its effect is hard to assess statistically.

#### Workplace support - person-and-environment support vs three environment-level changes
Working with the person and the environment together: extra support at the workplace, training and supervision on work matters and workplace behaviour. The author calls this one of the most common and fairly successful kinds of help. Training has been delivered in many formats — via people and technology: videos laying out every stage of the work process step by step, interactive materials, audio training, behavioural programmes. It also includes extra supervision in the first stages of the job and tutoring, where the tutor does not directly control the person but can be asked for help. The failure case: behaviourist trainings that target not only work processes but also background behaviour that does not interfere with work but seems strange to others — for example, programmes to reduce stimming at the workplace; «Сейчас, к счастью, таких всё меньше и меньше» (Now, fortunately, there are fewer and fewer of these) [#193].

Working with the environment — three kinds of change [#193]:
1. Physical modifications of the workplace — mostly creating a sensory-friendly, low-stimulus environment (low arousal environment): a separate quiet room, dimmed light, no background music, TV or radio, permission to wear headphones. Some studies also used visual prompts: organisers, lists, checklists, schedules. Their effectiveness is hard to measure in workplace studies, mainly because papers describe them poorly; still, both sensory-friendly environments and visual prompts often help autistic people in life and are widely used in many areas.
2. Training for all employees — programmes for the manager or the whole staff. E.g. the NAS course for all employees covers: what autism is in general; differences in information processing; possible triggers and causes of stress at work for autistic employees; accommodation options. Such programmes are few and their effect alone is hard to judge. Autistic people in interviews say colleagues' and management's tolerance, understanding of autism and an inclusive attitude strongly affect their work — it is just unclear whether trainings produce that.
3. Accommodations touching work rules and company policy — alternative or shortened working hours; changed instructions (as direct as possible, in another form or modality); periodic supervision; a structured break. Sometimes changes to the work process itself: the person works only in the back room with no contact with customers, or different requirements apply on "good" and "bad" days. These are common for many disabilities, but for autism they may be insufficient. «Лучше всего работает комбинация разных стратегий, особенно в долгосрочной перспективе» (A combination of different strategies works best, especially in the long term).

### Detailed insights

#### Stand-up comedy as a fit for an autistic person: the case of Татьянида
The needs-pyramid post includes an interview with the autistic stand-up comedian Татьянида (channel НЕЙРОТРОЕЧНИЦА!), asked how she came to stand-up, whether it contains her special interests and whether it matches her values [#371].
- Why she started: she took stand-up courses to lower the intensity of her anxiety about doing something wrong in communication with people (without understanding what) and embarrassing herself. For her it was like deciding to skydive: what could be scarier than going on a small bar stage where nobody cares about you, telling 10 jokes, and nobody laughing. The point, she says: «всем страшно, все шутят в тишину, одна и та же шутка заходит в одном зале и не заходит в другом» (everyone is scared, everyone jokes into silence, the same joke lands in one room and not in another) — i.e. failure is normalised and not personal [#376].
- Why it turned out to fit: «я шла за опытом позора на сцене, а получила Джек-пот» (I went for the experience of shame on stage and got the jackpot): humour and joke writing are a very clearly described and regulated thing, with room to play for someone with ASD. Her colleague keeps a spreadsheet measuring the level of laughter for each joke at each show. There are clear schemes and rules: how jokes are written, how many minutes a set takes, how to dress, in what order everything goes; even crowd work and improvisation have a set of conventions and rules [#377].
- Special interest link: she prefers writing jokes because it combines her interests in studying people's behaviour and reactions and in conveying information as a unit of content (video, poem, joke, reel). Fitting a thought into the form of a joke is "like Tetris or match-three" [#377].
- Outcome: she writes jokes "for the drawer" more often than she performs, but each performance is a small feat for her and she is terribly proud of herself — «А я мало от чего получаю яркие эмоции» (And I get vivid emotions from few things) [#377].
Lesson in context: a field that looks socially terrifying can fit an autistic person when it has explicit rules, measurable feedback and overlaps with special interests and values (exposure to social fear, studying human reactions) [#371, img #376, #377].

#### Reframing the question
The channel author's hopeful conclusion: the situation is gradually changing and can be influenced — more people learn that everyone and every brain is different and that neurodivergence exists at all, and work formats are changing too. The question «что со мной не так» (what is wrong with me) is gradually giving way to «что подходит и не подходит именно мне» (what does and doesn't suit me specifically) [#406].

#### Why statistics are missing in Russia
There are no employment statistics for autistic people in Russia, and even rough estimates are impossible — not least because it is hard to count autistic adults: officially the diagnosis is not given in adulthood, and only recently did the practice of changing it on reaching adulthood stop [#193].

#### Help with work in Russia: supported-employment organisations
The author lists organisations that help autistic people find work in Russia. Her key condition for such programmes: people must be paid for the work, «иначе это нельзя назвать работой» (otherwise it cannot be called work). Her caveat: treat the list critically — she could not check the quality of each organisation or its fit with the neurodiversity paradigm (in which very few Russian organisations work), and she does not like all of them; but in Russia people, especially autistic people with intellectual disability and their families, often have very limited choice, so it would be wrong to stay silent about existing options. ### Evidence and numbers

All figures as cited by the channel author in the employment post [#406]:
- Research shows most autistic adults with low support needs (the author notes official level divisions may not reflect real needs) and most adults with ADHD adapt well to the work environment across very diverse careers when they choose suitable work; successful employment significantly improves quality of life for both groups.
- 50 to 80% of autistic adults have no job; the figure varies greatly by country and counting methodology.
- In the UK only 22% of autistic people are officially employed — the lowest rate among all groups of disabled people — while more than 80% of non-disabled people work.
- Adults with ADHD have a 2–3 times higher risk of being unemployed than neurotypical people, and a 60% higher probability of losing an existing job.
- About 67% of people with ADHD are in full-time employment versus 87% of people without ADHD.
- With accommodations, both productivity and job satisfaction grow noticeably.

University figures are given in *Autism and higher education* (39% / 59% / 50% graduation; 48% academic satisfaction) [#201, #204].

- UK, 2021 statistics (Office of National Statistics): only 29% of autistic people employed, versus about 50% on average for all disabled people [#193].
- EU, 2019 (Autism-Europe presentation to the European Parliament): about 10% [#193].
- Australia, 2018 (Australian Bureau of Statistics): 38% — "slightly better, but also bad" [#193].
- Autistic candidates are consistently rated worse at interviews than non-autistic ones with otherwise equal qualifications (the author cites two studies) [#193].
- Disclosure experiments in the UK and Australia (Sasson & Morrison 2019; Flower, Dickens & Hedley 2021 are in the reference list): disclosure slightly improved ratings; Canadian autistic adults report mixed real-world outcomes [#193].
- Adapted-questions study (Maras et al. 2021 in the reference list): more concrete, structured questions narrowed but did not close the rating gap [#193].
- Workplace accommodations evidence draws on a scoping review by Khalifa et al. (2020) and employment-programme reviews by Hedley et al. (2017) and Scott et al. (2019), per the reference list [#193].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The workplace needs pyramid is explicitly based on preliminary results of a study of autistic women; the "nice have" layer is empty and the author asks readers for input [#371].
- The job areas and six principles come from an informal reader survey on another channel, and the author admits she supplemented the principles with her own ideas (shown as struck-through "дополнила от себя") [#306].
- Unemployment figures for autistic adults vary strongly by country and methodology (50–80%); the channel author also notes that official support-level divisions may not reflect real needs [#406].
- In the education area, teaching in a school is flagged as a possible problem (group work, overload) despite education being listed as a fitting field [#306].
- The advice to "embellish" work experience (9 months instead of 6) is the channel author's own pragmatic recommendation [#413].
- Wording differences between the education cards and the Telegraph article: "other types of disability" (card) vs "all types of disability" (article) as the 50% graduation comparison group; "four-year" bachelor's programmes appears only on the card; the dormitory-abuse remark appears only in the article [#201, img #202, article #200].

## Autism and emigration

### What this covers

This topic gathers two posts. The first is a three-part post by the channel author, an autistic woman who had moved out of her home country more than a year before. It covers why immigration is especially demanding for autistic people, which countries' immigration laws exclude autistic applicants, and what autistic immigrants themselves recommend [#390, #391, #392]. The second is a guest post by Fridrich, an AuDHD psychologist, giving a five-point checklist of "small" needs to check before choosing a country, plus a closing argument that one's own needs, not other people's norms, decide quality of life [#399, #400]. The author notes that much of the first post also applies to people who moved to another city or region, not only another country: "В чем-то пост будет актуален и для людей, которые переехали в другой город/регион!" (In some ways the post will also be relevant for people who moved to another city/region!) [#390].

### Core ideas

- Immigration is a test at the limit of capacity for autistic people. The author asks the reader to picture an airport at rush hour, an unfamiliar city, foreign speech from every side and no familiar landmarks. Finding your way in such a situation is hard for anyone, but for autistic people it "может быть настоящим испытанием на пределе возможностей" (can be a real test at the limit of one's capacity) [#390].
- The load is multi-layered and usually unsupported. Immigrants have to learn a new language and culture and also get past significant social, sensory and bureaucratic obstacles, "зачастую без какой-либо поддержки и понимания со стороны принимающего государства" (often without any support or understanding from the receiving state) [#390].
- Some countries' immigration laws are ableist. Canada, New Zealand and Australia have historically excluded autistic people as a "burden" on healthcare (see *Discriminatory immigration laws*) [#391].
- People cope, and autistic immigrants have practical recommendations on routine, money, sensory strategies, language, community and autistic organisations (see *Six recommendations*) [#392].
- Small, specific needs decide whether life in a new place is bearable. Fridrich argues that emigrant chats talk about work, study and language, but for autistic people it is the combination of "small things" (climate and microclimate, food, medicine, community, places to rest) that "может сделать жизнь невыносимой" (can make life unbearable) [#399].
- Your own needs are valid even if others find them silly, because you, not they, live with them every day (see *Choosing for yourself*) [#400].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Eight unique difficulties for autistic immigrants
Purpose: the author's list of what autistic people face most often when moving ("Вот с чем сталкиваются аутичные люди чаще всего при переезде" — *Here is what autistic people most often face when moving*) [#391].

1. Sensory overload at every step. Airports, train stations, new neighbourhoods and a huge number of unfamiliar sounds, smells and people. Without one's usual self-regulation tools and without a familiar space to hide in, this drains a person very quickly [#391].
2. Breakdown of familiar routines. The author calls routine "зачастую основа психологической устойчивости аутичного человека" (often the foundation of an autistic person's psychological stability). Moving "обнуляет все" (resets everything to zero): the daily schedule, usual routes, favourite places [#391].
3. Loss of the support system. Family, old friends, a doctor you trust, specialists who know you all stay behind. Building a new system in the new place takes time and energy, and there is often too little of both already [#391].
4. Unfamiliar social rules. Every culture has its own unwritten norms: how to greet people, how directly you can speak, whether to kiss when meeting, when silence is appropriate and when it isn't. What worked at home can be perceived completely differently in the new place [#391].
5. Language barrier. It is not only about grammar. There are idioms, sarcasm, hints and meaningful pauses. On top of that come differences in auditory processing, and the fact that autistic people "зачастую и на родном языке половину информации не успевают понять" (often fail to take in half the information even in their native language) [#391].
6. Bureaucracy. Filling in documents, visa interviews, phone calls to government services, waiting in queues. The author sums up the oral side as "Устное общение, кошмар, ужас, стресс" (Oral communication, nightmare, horror, stress) [#391].
7. Access to specialised help. Finding a psychotherapist or psychiatrist who works with autistic adults is hard even at home. In a new country you also have the language barrier, not knowing how the system works, and often long waiting lists or high prices [#391].
8. Double stigma. Being autistic already means meeting incomprehension, and so does being an immigrant. Together the two factors create extra vulnerability [#391].

#### Six recommendations from autistic immigrants
Purpose: what autistic immigrants themselves most often recommend ("Вот что чаще всего рекомендуют сами аутичные иммигранты") [#392].

1. Create a routine. Even small daily habits help lower anxiety. Bring familiar and favourite things such as plush toys and collectible items to help you feel more comfortable [#392].
2. Work out where your money will come from. A benefit (for refugees) or remote work is very important for getting through the first months in the new place more easily [#392].
3. Plan sensory strategies. Look for quiet places in advance. Carry your usual self-regulation items, for example sunglasses, sunscreen with a comfortable texture, or headphones [#392].
4. Learn the language gradually. Don't rush, because Google Translate can handle most tasks. If you have shutdowns, carry pictures or cards in the needed language. Learn at your own pace, and "не ожидайте, что вы быстро заговорите, если вы не особо много разговариваете и на родном языке" (don't expect to start speaking quickly if you don't talk much in your native language either) [#392].
5. Find your people. There are often immigrant communities where you are moving. Don't expect to make friends with locals straight away. Start with compatriots, with whom you at least share a language. Immigrant chats also contain a lot of useful information, with the caveat that "иногда бывают неприятные разборки" (sometimes there are unpleasant squabbles) [#392].
6. Write to autistic organisations and communities (if there are any). They help you find community, rights protection and resources [#392].

#### Fridrich's five things autistic people should check before emigrating
Purpose and context: guest author Fridrich reads emigrant chats from time to time and sees that they skip things that turned out to matter to him. People there usually write about work, study or language. He does not say these are unimportant, but argues that autistic people should pay attention to a set of things whose combination "может сделать жизнь невыносимой" (can make life unbearable). His examples come from emigrating from Ukraine to Austria (Vienna) in 2022 and from Austria to Spain in 2026. He has also moved within his country and to Latvia [#399].

1. Sensory needs (climate and microclimate). If you are sensitive to heat and/or cold, the climate can become real torture. But climate is not the whole story. In Vienna almost no homes have air conditioning, and he "буквально страдал каждое лето" (literally suffered every summer). In Spain it is much hotter, but there are air conditioners. His conclusion is that the microclimate decides as much as the climate, "вообще я бы сказал, что он решает куда больше" (actually I'd say it matters far more). Lesson: check indoor conditions, not only the weather [#399].
2. Food. If you are a selective eater like him, you may find there is nothing for you to eat. Money can solve this, as it can many needs, especially sensory ones, but not everyone has money. In Vienna he had "практически нечего есть" (practically nothing to eat), "как бы странно это не звучало" (however strange that may sound). Check the other country's food habits and whether Ukrainian products, or whatever products you are used to, are available [#399].
3. Medical care. As a trans man, access to hormone replacement therapy was very important to him, but the point is wider: many people take psychiatric medication or other drugs. He advises carrying a supply for six months, or even a year for medications you take permanently, because getting them in the new country may be difficult. On the other hand, in some countries (Austria, Germany) they may be covered by insurance, which can lower living costs a lot. In Austria even gender transition can be done through insurance. In some countries it may be easier, or harder, to get a diagnosis if you don't have one yet. He "brought" his ASD diagnosis with him and was assessed for ADHD (and prescribed medication) in Vienna [#399].
4. Community. It is important to have your own people, ideally before you leave: people who already know how things work and/or speak the language and can support you. He says this matters especially for queer people, because in emigrant chats a shared emigration experience "вообще не равен отсутствию цисгетеронормативности и рефлексии привилегий" (is not at all the same as an absence of cis-heteronormativity, or as reflecting on one's privilege) [#399].
5. Opportunities for rest. Nature is very important to him, and Vienna is far from green. He was going crazy from the lack of enough trees, shade and nooks: "это город – одно большое открытое пространство" (this city is one big open space). At first this may seem like nonsense, he says, but combined with other unmet sensory needs, having no way to recover and rest "делает из жизни один большой мелтдаун" (turns life into one big meltdown) [#399].

### Detailed insights

#### Discriminatory immigration laws
The author writes that immigration law in several countries has historically not accepted autistic people, treating them as a "обузой" (burden) on the healthcare system [#391]:
- Canada: autistic migrants have been refused citizenship or even deported on the assumption that they would put too much load on healthcare. Reforms announced in 2018 were supposed to make access easier for people with disabilities, but problems remain [#391].
- New Zealand: permanent resident status is effectively out of reach for autistic people who are judged to need "значительной поддержке" (significant support) [#391].
- Australia: strict "медицинские критерии" (medical criteria) apply. If an "чрезвычайно высокая" (extremely high) load on the social system is expected, no visa is granted, and autistic applicants often fall under these restrictions [#391].

These laws are harshly criticised as a form of "системного эйблизмa" (systemic ableism) [#391].

The workaround and the author's verdict. If you really want to go to one of these countries, there is a way out: don't mention ASD, and don't ask the state for any support until you have citizenship. The author's verdict: "Но это такой себе выход" (But that's a so-so way out) [#391].

#### The author's conclusion
Autistic immigrants carry a double load: adapting to a new culture while living with autistic traits. The author's view is that with support, changes in legislation and growing acceptance they can build their lives successfully and share their experience with other immigrants and autistic people. She ends with an affirmation: "этот мир принадлежит вам так же, как и нейротипичным людям, и вы имеете право на комфортную жизнь!" (this world belongs to you just as much as to neurotypical people, and you have the right to a comfortable life!) She also asks readers what else they would recommend to autistic people moving to another country or region [#392].

#### Choosing for yourself: your needs decide quality of life
Fridrich's overall message is that your needs ("именно ваши!" — *yours specifically!*) are important even if the people around you find them silly or not worth attention. Everyone around him loved Vienna and stared wide-eyed when he answered that what bothered him was too few trees. But, he points out, "они-то спросили и забыли, а мне (и вам) с этим условным отсутствием деревьев сталкиваться каждый день своей жизни" (*they asked and forgot, while I (and you) have to face this figurative lack of trees every day of our lives*) [#400].

The practical rule that follows: the fact that a place suits everyone around you, or that something like language doesn't matter to you at all, is "не повод этих всех слушать и не выбирать себя" (not a reason to listen to all of them and not choose yourself). Choose the place against your own list of needs, not against the general consensus [#400].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The legal situations in Canada, New Zealand and Australia are given as the author's summary, with no sources, dates of the current rules or further detail. Only the 2018 Canadian reform is dated, and the author says problems persist after it [#391].
- Hiding an ASD diagnosis to get through immigration is described as possible but explicitly not endorsed ("такой себе выход" — *a so-so way out*) [#391].
- Fridrich's checklist is drawn from his personal experience (Vienna vs Spain) and from his position as a trans, queer, AuDHD person. He notes that money can solve many of the food and sensory problems, but not everyone has it [#399].
- The two posts give slightly different advice on community. The channel author suggests starting with compatriots in immigrant chats [#392]. Fridrich warns that for queer people emigrant chats may be cis-heteronormative, and recommends having your people in place before you leave [#399]. These are different emphases, not a direct contradiction.
