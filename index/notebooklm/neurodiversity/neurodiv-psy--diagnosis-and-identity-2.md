## Self-diagnosis, impostor syndrome and accepting a neurodivergent identity

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel author's posts on how people come to understand themselves as neurodivergent (ADHD, autism and other neurodivergences): why many start with self-diagnosis, how to explore oneself responsibly, the common objections to self-diagnosis and the author's answers to them, the fear that autism and ADHD are just "fashionable diagnoses", impostor syndrome and internalized ableism, what to do after the discovery, and an honest personal account of what changed (and what did not) after diagnosis. The author writes from both sides: as a psychologist and as someone whose own path "started with self-diagnosis" and who says that without internet resources she "would never have received the help she needed" [#279]. She frames the topic as painful because colleagues (psychologists and psychiatrists) often treat self-diagnosis and "навешиванию на себя ярлыков" (putting labels on oneself) negatively [#279].

### Core ideas

- Self-diagnosis is an understandable, often necessary route, not a pathology. People turn to it because competent specialists are scarce, assessment is expensive and slow, and many were dismissed by specialists before; it brings self-understanding, community, accessibility and less stigma — but carries a real risk of error, which professional diagnosis reduces (see *Four alleged dangers* and *Four benefits*) [#279, #281, #286, #287].
- Visibility is not prevalence. A diagnosis becoming visible online creates a feeling of an "epidemic", but historically the problem was underdiagnosis; almost every diagnosis that became visible was first called a "trend" and later became ordinary clinical reality [#365, #366].
- Not having every trait does not rule out a diagnosis. Autism is heterogeneous; diagnosis requires notable social difficulties and restricted/repetitive/inflexible patterns, but no autistic person has all possible manifestations [#299].
- Doubt is normal and persistent. Impostor syndrome and internalized ableism can remain years after a formal diagnosis; learning more about autism and ADHD is what increases self-acceptance [#224, #327].
- Diagnosis reframes the past and stops self-blame, but does not remove all suffering. It allows one to reinterpret "laziness" as difficulty, set boundaries and build a manageable life, yet some things cannot be accommodated [#84, #328].
- Irritation with other autistic people is often internalized ableism — an openly autistic person mirrors what we learned to hide — though sometimes it is simply a clash of communication and sensory needs [#336].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Five steps after learning you are neurodivergent
Purpose: a card series (idea by @dt.perry, made by @ia_psy) for someone who has realized — through self-exploration, various resources and possibly professionals — that they have ADHD, autism and/or another neurodivergence, and asks "Что дальше?" (What next?) [#82, img #82]. The five steps:

1. Find a safe space ("Найдите безопасное пространство"). Communicating with other neurodivergent people is described as "то, что даёт нам силу и валидирует наш опыт" (what gives us strength and validates our experience). Find a community of people with the same neurodivergence where you can feel you belong and be yourself [#83].
2. Practice self-acceptance (Russian card title: Практикуйте принятие себя). Realizing you have particular diagnoses helps you re-examine all the experiences where you could not cope and failed — failures you believed were due to your own laziness and unwillingness. It is an opportunity to work on accepting yourself with all your traits and limitations [#84].
3. Educate yourself and your loved ones ("Займитесь образованием себя и близких"). Learn more about your neurodivergence from literature, blogs or a psychologist. Why: by learning how your brain works and getting to know other people's experiences, you will learn to feel less guilt and understand how to take better care of yourself. Also tell your close ones about your neurodivergence in a form they can understand — they too need to understand what is going on and how best to support you [#85].
4. Build new boundaries ("Выстраивайте новые границы"). With new knowledge of yourself and your traits you can set new boundaries to take better care of yourself. Example given: you now understand that going to a shopping mall with friends is too much sensory load, so you can propose an alternative that is more comfortable for you [#86].
5. Ask for and accept help (Russian card title: Просите и принимайте помощь). After long years of not understanding what is happening to you, of self-criticism and masking, it can be hard to seek help. Turn to people who can support you. Signature line: "Нейроотличия с нами на всю жизнь, но мы не должны в справляться с трудностями в одиночестве" [sic] (Neurodivergence is with us for life, but we don't have to cope with difficulties alone) [#87].

#### The path of realizing one's neurodivergence (stages)
An image (idea credited to @autisticpositivity) shows the author's own path as a sequence of self-descriptions, illustrating how doubt persists at every stage, even after a formal diagnosis [#224]:
1. "я странная, тревожная и ленивая" (I'm weird, anxious and lazy) — pre-awareness, traits read as character flaws.
2. "похоже на СДВГ, но, может, я ищу оправданий" (looks like ADHD, but maybe I'm looking for excuses) — first suspicion, immediately undercut by doubt.
3. "у меня СДВГ" (I have ADHD).
4. "у меня есть аутичные черты, но недостаточно для диагноза" (I have autistic traits, but not enough for a diagnosis).
5. "мне диагностировали РАС, но, наверное, я обманула врача" (I was diagnosed with ASD, but I probably deceived the doctor) — impostor feelings even after a professional diagnosis.
6. "я аутичный человек с СДВГ :)" (*I'm an autistic person with ADHD :)*) — acceptance.
[#224]

#### Why people do not "just go to a doctor" (five reasons)
The author's list of reasons people self-diagnose instead of seeking formal assessment [#281]:
1. There are still few specialists in the post-Soviet space who understand neurodivergence.
2. It is expensive and the wait is long ("Дорого и долго ждать").
3. Past invalidation ("обесценивание") by specialists.
4. "Синдром самозванца" — impostor syndrome (doubting one "deserves" to seek a diagnosis).
5. A feeling that there is no point in a formal diagnosis.
[#281]

#### How to explore your condition without a doctor (three rules)
For when "Вы не можете попасть к врачу, а разобраться хочется" (you can't get to a doctor, but you want to figure it out) [#282]:
1. Start with questions. Why do you want to know whether you have a diagnosis? How will you use the information to help yourself? What will happen if your assumption is not confirmed? [#282]
2. Don't rely on a single source of information. Take tests, study the diagnostic criteria for both adults and children, read books, talk with people who have lived neurodivergent experience ("носителями нейроотличного опыта") [#282].
3. Cross-check information in different sources and don't believe everything people say [#282].

#### Four alleged dangers of self-diagnosis and the author's replies
The card series "Опасности самодиагностики" (Dangers of self-diagnosis) lists common objections and responds to each [#283, #284, #285, #286]:

1. Fear that the self-diagnosed will spread stereotypes and lies about neurodivergence. Reply: anyone can and does spread wrong information — "Даже специалисты" (Even specialists). Examples she expects many have seen: posts about treating autism with anti-worm (antiparasitic) drugs and the "СДВГ огненного кольца" ("ring of fire" ADHD). Her recommendation: check the information you want to share and avoid generalizations when speaking about neurodivergence [#283].
2. Feeling that the self-diagnosed draw resources away and devalue the suffering of other people. Illustrated by a typical statement: "She says she's autistic though she talks to people normally. My son has real autism, it's really hard for him. So the concept of autism is getting blurred and people will stop understanding how bad it is for my son and me." Reply: every neurodivergence is a spectrum, so manifestations vary widely; "Существование людей с низким запросом в поддержке не отменяет существование людей с высоким запросом в поддержке" (The existence of people with low support needs does not cancel the existence of people with high support needs). And as for resources and state help, especially for adults, she notes there is hardly any to speak of yet [#284].
3. People label themselves, then restrict themselves in everything and make excuses. Illustrated by: "Invented ADHD for himself and now doesn't want to study at university, but actually he's just lazy!" Reply: in neurodivergent communities she mostly does not see attempts at self-restriction; rather, posts about understanding how your brain works, support and self-compassion — about how to help yourself cope with something, not about what you are now not allowed to do [#285].
4. The self-diagnosed person is actually wrong, will now be less ready to accept the correct diagnosis, and may even harm themselves. Reply: she concedes this one — a person can of course be mistaken. There is always a risk of attributing symptoms of another neurodivergence or disorder to autism or ADHD, and it is easy to miss co-occurring disorders when you focus on one condition. Our self-knowledge suffers from biases. A doctor (or even several independent doctors) can also err, "но она намного ниже!" (but the probability is much lower!). Firm rule: "Не принимайте препараты без назначения врача!" (Do not take medication without a doctor's prescription!) [#286].

The card "Плюсы самодиагностики" lists four items (only headings are given on the card) [#287]:
1. Understanding oneself and making life easier already now (without waiting for formal diagnosis).
2. Entering the community.
3. Self-diagnosis is accessible.
4. Reducing stigmatization.

#### Risks vs benefits of a diagnosis becoming popular
The author calls popularity "палка о двух концах" (a double-edged sword) [#365].

Possible risks:
1. Pathologizing traits common to all people.
2. Ignoring comorbid disorders — anxiety, depression, (c)PTSD — and refusing treatment for them.
3. Self-prescribing medication.
4. Building an identity around the label ("я такой, потому что я аутичный" — *I'm like this because I'm autistic*).
5. Spreading distorted information.
[#365]

What popularity gives:
1. Growth of awareness and reduced discrimination.
2. Diagnosis of those historically overlooked — girls, adults, people who mask heavily.
3. Communities and support.
4. Reduction of secondary risks: depression, addictions, burnout.
5. Language to describe one's own experience: not "я истеричка" (I'm hysterical), but "у меня мелтдауны, когда я перегружаюсь" (I have meltdowns when I'm overloaded).
[#365]

#### What helps with the fear of "just following a trend" (four steps)
The author says this thought is very common and very human — it comes to her too [#366]:
1. Accept that exploring yourself is normal. We constantly build hypotheses and test them. Even psychiatrists can be wrong. Criteria and diagnoses themselves change: not long ago people called themselves "аспи" (*Aspie*, after Asperger syndrome), and now in many countries it is no longer singled out as a separate disorder [#366].
2. Shift the focus from the label to what helps. Most often we need information about a diagnosis in order to understand how to help ourselves. If advice for autistic people works for you — even if you don't yet know whether you have the diagnosis — keep using it [#366].
3. Explore yourself from different angles. Read books, take tests, watch videos by specialists. Ask differential questions: are attention problems a prolonged depression or ADHD? Few friends — because of social anxiety or because of autism? [#366]
4. Seek professional diagnosis if you have the opportunity [#366].
Conclusion: "Пытаться понять, что с вами происходит, это круто и не стыдно (и не следование моде)" (Trying to understand what is happening to you is cool and not shameful — and not following fashion); "Вы имеете право искать ответы. И сомневаться по дороге нормально" (You have the right to look for answers. And doubting along the way is normal) [#366].

#### Life after diagnosis — what got easier and what did not
The author's personal account, a few years after her ADHD (autumn 2021) and autism (2022) diagnoses [#327, #328]. See *Detailed insights → The author's story* for background.

Areas that got easier:
1. Work. She has worked as a psychologist for 4 years without reaching serious burnout (unlike at her first job). Caveat she gives: she is privileged enough to work from home and choose her hours. ADHD planning skills help her organize her schedule, autism-related skills help regulate load. Autism does not disappear — she gets very tired from working with people — but now she understands what is happening and can rest in time [#327].
2. Rest. She learned to distinguish her emotions better and understand their causes. She knows she absolutely needs at least one full day at home per week with minimal communication. She rarely sticks with the same hobbies for long but stopped scolding herself: "хобби должны приносить удовольствие, а не становиться второй работой" (hobbies should bring pleasure, not become a second job) [#327].
3. Mental health. She was in therapy both before and after diagnosis; both were useful, but after diagnosis therapy became more effective. Through the joint efforts of several psychiatrists a working medication regimen was found that helps with anxiety and ADHD [#327].
4. Physical health. She tries not to avoid doctors; with paid specialists she states her autism directly, brings a fidget for stimming and asks for a pause if overloaded. For the first time in a long while she has exercised regularly for several months — motivated not by health but by seeing people she likes at the classes [#327].
5. Unmasking. She masks her autistic and ADHD traits less in safe situations, but still masks in some contexts — with people she barely knows or with state services (she does not want police or migration officers to notice her behaviour) [#327].
6. Relationships (friendships and romantic). She used to force herself to socialize when she didn't want to; now she considers whether she has the energy. She better understands with whom she is comfortable and who accepts her unmasked. Over the years many friends discovered they are also neurodivergent, which brought them closer. Her partner is also autistic; they met on a Discord server devoted to their special interest [#327].
7. Self-acceptance. She treats herself with more understanding and compassion: "если не гнобить себя 24/7 и позволять себе испытывать эмоции, то жить становится проще" (if you don't beat yourself up 24/7 and allow yourself to feel emotions, life gets easier). Sometimes understanding her limitations still upsets her: she used to think she could do anything if she tried hard enough; now she understands there are things she cannot achieve even if she "pulls herself together". And impostor syndrome still sometimes convinces her she is "самый нейротипичный человек на Земле" (the most neurotypical person on Earth) [#327].

Areas that did not get easier:
1. Meeting people. It got harder, not easier: she now recognizes emotions better and feels awkwardness and discomfort more sharply when strangers try to talk to her; and since she masks less, she may seem "strange" and "unfriendly" [#328].
2. Sensory experience. She notices much better what is happening with her senses, which turned out to be tiring: where she used to feel general discomfort and irritation, she now perceives the details, and sensations became sharper. On the other hand, they are easier to respond to and to find accommodations for [#328].
3. Household skills. She still has to talk herself into cooking or cleaning; it is a boring, tiring routine and did not get easier [#328].
4. Study. Her last university study was before diagnosis; later courses, including long ones, were not easier — she is constantly distracted and struggles to do assignments, and periodically convinces herself a master's degree is "не по силам" (beyond her), partly because of ADHD [#328].
Bottom line: knowing she is neurodivergent and getting diagnoses "не избавило меня от всех страданий" (did not rid me of all suffering); some things simply cannot be adapted to or accommodated. But it helped her stop blaming herself for being built differently; she understands herself better and is learning to build a life that is within her capacity [#328].

### Detailed insights

#### What self-diagnosis is
Self-diagnosis is defined as "процесс идентификации и диагностики нейроотличий, заболеваний и состояний у себя, без участия врача" (the process of identifying and diagnosing neurodivergences, illnesses and conditions in oneself without a doctor's involvement). The internet, books and medical reference books, friends and relatives can help in this process [#280]. The post introducing the series frames it with the question "Так ли она страшна?" (Is it really so scary?) and announces sections on why people don't go to a doctor, how to explore one's condition, and the pros and cons of self-diagnosis [#279, img #279].

#### Impostor syndrome and internalized ableism in the author's own words
The author received her diagnoses as an adult, and impostor syndrome and internal ableism still torment her. She lists the recurring thoughts [#224]:
- "Как так получилось, что до 23 лет никто не обращал внимания на мои особенности?" (How come nobody noticed my traits until I was 23?)
- "Мои симптомы недостаточно выраженные, чтобы говорить о себе как о нейроотличном человеке" (My symptoms aren't pronounced enough to call myself neurodivergent)
- "Я не могу быть аутичной/с СДВГ, у меня же есть работа!" (I can't be autistic/have ADHD, I have a job!)
- "Я притворяюсь нейроотличной, потому что хочу быть не как все, мне просто нравится вешать на себя ярлыки..." (I'm pretending to be neurodivergent because I want to be different, I just like putting labels on myself...)
Such thoughts still come often, but "чем больше я узнаю про аутизм и СДВГ, тем с большим принятием я отношусь к себе" (the more I learn about autism and ADHD, the more accepting I am of myself) — she is struck by how different people can be and how differently traits manifest. The lesson: knowledge about the diversity of presentations is the antidote to "not autistic enough" thinking [#224].

#### "Autistic traits I don't have" — why missing traits don't disprove a diagnosis
ASD is "неоднородная группа нарушений/особенностей развития" (a heterogeneous group of developmental disorders/differences), so manifestations vary greatly; hence the saying "нет двух одинаковых аутичных людей" (no two autistic people are the same) [#299]. The author often sees autistic people disbelieve their diagnosis because they don't see the entire possible set of symptoms in themselves — e.g., someone has almost all autistic manifestations but no need for routine, and because of this cannot accept the diagnosis and start receiving help [#299].

As a person with diagnosed ASD (+ADHD), she lists the autistic traits she does NOT observe in herself, to help others accept their condition [#299]:
1. No speech or intellectual developmental impairments (which occur in some autistic people). She has a rhotacism (cannot pronounce "r") but thinks it is unlikely related to autism. She was not developmentally delayed as a child and, according to her parents, never had skill regression.
2. She can mask and make eye contact when needed (though most often she doesn't want to).
3. She can take turns in dialogue and ask many questions about the other person. She no longer has a problem of talking nonstop about a topic she's interested in without noticing what the other person thinks (this did happen in adolescence). She can sustain a topic the other person chose even if uninteresting to her, or at least listen.
4. People don't complain that she is overly direct in a way perceived as rude, so she counts that difficulty as absent too.
5. She usually understands hints, though she prefers people to say clearly in words what they want.
6. No difficulty understanding proverbs, sayings, idioms and metaphors, especially in her native language.
7. She feels emotional empathy in response to someone's pain and discomfort — sometimes even too strongly, so she can't watch certain videos and films if she notices a character's discomfort.
8. Thanks to years of psychotherapy she distinguishes her emotions well, so she has no alexithymia.
9. She has a good imagination: as a child she acted out many stories with toy animals and dolls, wrote stories and drew imaginary scenes.
10. No (noticeable to her) behavioural rituals; she does not feel a need to do things the same way — she can take different routes to different shops and then cook different food.
11. She is fairly flexible about her schedule: gets up and goes to bed at different times, no fixed daily routine (she says it is more honest to say that having any schedule is hard for her because of ADHD). However, she can feel discomfort if she had planned rest and is asked to urgently do some tasks.
The key rule she restates: for a diagnosis, there must be notable difficulties in the social sphere and patterns of restricted, repetitive or inflexible behaviour or interests — but not every autistic person has all existing manifestations: "Наши мозги и истории развития слишком сильно отличаются, чтобы мы все вели себя одинаково" (Our brains and developmental histories differ too much for us all to behave the same) [#299].

#### The author's story: why nobody suspected until 23
When she learned about ADHD and autism, "многое встало на свои места" (a lot fell into place). Until 23 she had never seen a psychiatrist, and those around her had rather stereotyped ideas about neurodivergence, so it was hard to suspect it. At school she was labelled "тревожная", "чувствительная", "неаккуратная", "умная, но ленивая", "странная", "на своей волне" (anxious, sensitive, untidy, smart but lazy, weird, on her own wavelength) and talked like a "бандитка" (a gangster), but nobody thought that differences could be behind this. She was diagnosed with ADHD in autumn 2021 and autism a year later in 2022; she already suspected both, so the news was no shock. She read and watched a lot about neurodivergence to understand what to do with the new knowledge about her brain [#327]. Lesson: stereotyped ideas in one's environment hide neurodivergence behind character labels.

#### "Fashionable diagnoses": visibility versus prevalence
People often worry "аутизм и СДВГ - это просто модные диагнозы, они сейчас у всех" (autism and ADHD are just fashionable diagnoses, everyone has them now), with understandable concerns behind it: "people watch TikToks and invent problems", "there didn't used to be so many autistic people", "what if I invented all this and now excuse everything with neurodivergence?" Some psychiatric diagnoses increasingly appear in media, are actively discussed on social networks and gather whole communities, creating a feeling of an "epidemic". The central point: "рост видимости диагноза - это не то же самое, что рост его распространённости" (growth in a diagnosis's visibility is not the same as growth in its prevalence) [#365].

Historical diagnoses once called "fashionable", per the author [#365]:
1. 1990s–2000s: depression. Mass awareness campaigns appeared and doctors began actively prescribing antidepressants; it seemed "now everyone has depression". In reality people simply stopped hiding symptoms and began seeking help.
2. 2000s: ADHD. In the US and Europe there were theories that ADHD was a pharmaceutical-company conspiracy and that children "just need better upbringing". At that time diagnostic criteria were changing and for the first time included adults, as well as girls and women — hence the feeling that the diagnosis was "given to everyone".
3. Same period: bipolar disorder. After the bipolar spectrum was broadened and bipolar II began to be actively diagnosed, people spoke of a "bipolar epidemic". Later some cases were reassessed as ADHD, BPD or trauma disorders — which the author calls a natural process for a developing science.
4. With the growth of the internet: borderline personality disorder (BPD) and dissociative identity disorder (DID) went through a similar story at different times.
5. Now: the conversation is about ADHD and autism in adults.
The author's conclusion: historically the problem was underdiagnosis rather than "fashion"; largely thanks to social media, psychiatry has finally begun to see neurodivergent girls, adults and those who successfully masked for years [#365]. Almost every diagnosis that became noticeable was first declared a "trend", and then simply became part of normal clinical reality [#366].

#### Internalized ableism: why other autistic people can irritate us
Some autistic people notice that other autistic people irritate them, or experience irritation directed at them by other autistic people (often their own parents). The author's explanation is internalized ableism: when a person absorbs the idea that being different is "bad" or "wrong", they apply it both to themselves and to other people, even if they are autistic themselves. Then an autistic person who doesn't hide their traits becomes "зеркалом того, что мы в себе когда-то научились прятать" (a mirror of what we once learned to hide in ourselves). We may feel shame, anger, anxiety — not because something is wrong with that person, but because it hurts to look at what was once rejected in us [#336].

An alternative explanation: sometimes it is not about acceptance but about communication and sensory differences — people can overload each other even when they respect and accept differences [#336].

Reframe: "Раздражение не делает нас плохими" (Irritation doesn't make us bad). It is a signal that a fear of being "too autistic" still lives inside, which can be dealt with [#336]. The accompanying meme shows one person labelled "аутичный" (autistic) talking ("БЛА БЛА") and another labelled "на 1% более аутичный" (1% more autistic) — humorously illustrating the dynamic [#336].

### Evidence and numbers

- Research shows that more than half of social-media content about mental disorders (*52–84%*) may be unreliable, as cited by the author [#365].
- A 2025 study (Karasavva et al.) of content under the #ADHD hashtag found that after watching ADHD TikToks participants tended to overestimate the prevalence of its symptoms and perceived even low-quality videos as useful. The authors called this the "эффектом палки о двух концах" (double-edged sword effect): popularization reduces stigma and helps people recognize themselves, but can at the same time blur the boundary between a clinical condition and normal traits. The author's takeaway: rely on varied sources and treat them critically [#365].
- The author's own timeline: no psychiatrist until age 23; ADHD diagnosed autumn 2021, autism 2022; 4 years working as a psychologist without serious burnout; needs at least 1 full day at home per week [#327].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The author concedes that self-diagnosis can be wrong (symptoms of another condition misattributed to autism/ADHD, comorbidities missed, self-knowledge biased) and that doctor error is possible but much less likely; she does not resolve this into a rule beyond "seek professional diagnosis if you can" and "never take medication without a prescription" [#286, msg #366].
- Her account of life after diagnosis is explicitly personal and she flags her own privilege (remote work, choosing her hours) as a factor in avoiding burnout [#327].
