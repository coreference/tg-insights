## Autistic communication, empathy and friendship

### What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel taught about how autistic people communicate and relate: a long checklist of communication traits in autistic adults, concrete examples of literal interpretation, a cheat sheet on when to apologize, autism as a culture and identity, the tendency to personify objects, a research review of autistic friendship, an informal emotional-empathy survey run by the channel, and the double empathy problem with its large replication. It also covers two practical/representational items: an AI browser extension for subtext and small talk, and the autistic Barbie doll discussion. The source frames these as ways to counter myths (that autistic people do not need or cannot do friendship, that they "lack empathy", that their communication is universally impaired) and to help autistic readers understand and accept their own style. [#8, #128, #72, #264]

### Core ideas

- Autistic communication is highly heterogeneous: some speak, some do not; some talk a lot, some little; some express themselves vividly, others adapt hard to the interlocutor. Any single trait can occur in anyone — only the combination points to a diagnosis. [#8]
- Literal interpretation is common: autistic people answer the question that was literally asked, not the implied one, and are not joking when they do so. [#26]
- Over-apologizing is common and harmful to self-respect; apologies belong to real harm or violated personal principles, not to existing, having needs or being different. [#61]
- Besides autism as a diagnosis there is autism as a culture and identity, which some (not all) autistic people embrace, and belonging to a group helps people feel and validate their selves. [#64]
- Autistic adults personify objects far more often than neurotypicals; this both reduces loneliness and causes painful empathy for things — the opposite of the "no empathy" stereotype. [#72]
- Autistic people do want friendship; a systematic review shows they value close, understanding, non-judging friends (often other autistic people) yet frequently feel lonely, disoriented and exhausted by masking. [#128]
- In the channel's survey, having a diagnosis did not on average lower emotional empathy and sometimes raised it substantially. [#240]
- The core of the double empathy finding replicated (autistic-to-autistic information transfer is as accurate as neurotypical-to-neurotypical), but the predicted breakdown in mixed chains did not replicate in the larger, more diverse sample. [#264]

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Checklist of typical communication traits of autistic adults (21 items)
Purpose: the channel "tried to collect a list of typical traits in communication in adults with ASD" ("Попробовали собрать список типичных черт в общении у взрослых с РАС" — "We tried to put together a list of typical communication traits in adults with ASD"). It is a self-recognition list, not a diagnostic tool. The items, in the source's order and with the source's explanations: [#8]

1. Difficulty entering a conversation, ending a conversation, or understanding whose turn it is to talk. [#8]
2. Speaking in a monotone, even voice — or, conversely, with intonation that seems unnatural to others. [#8]
3. Speaking in memorized phrases picked up in various places. [#8]
4. Or repeating the interlocutor's phrases. [#8]
5. Small talk is hard — whereas infodumping is easy ("Small-talk - это трудно (зато инфодампинг - это легко!)" — "Small talk is hard (but infodumping is easy!)"). [#8]
6. Lying is difficult or unpleasant, because it requires theory of mind — looking at the situation from another person's role. [#8]
7. Directness is more comfortable. [#8]
8. Abstract concepts are hard to understand. [#8]
9. Things are taken literally; metaphors and proverbs can be baffling. The source's example of irritation: people who say "I'll be a minute" ("я на минуту") and then come back after five minutes. [#8]
10. Other people say they do not understand how you feel or what you are thinking. [#8]
11. Facial expressions and body language say little to you. [#8]
12. Either a lot of shyness and uncertainty about when it is okay to speak, or sharing a huge volume of information on topics that interest you without really noticing whether they interest others. [#8]
13. …Or, conversely, anxiously assessing from the interlocutor's reactions whether everything you just said was socially acceptable. [#8]
14. It is easier to talk "at people" than "with people" — in the format of a monologue rather than a dialogue ("Проще говорить “в людей”, чем “с людьми”" — "It's easier to talk 'at people' than 'with people'"). [#8]
15. Difficulty understanding and reading "social signals". [#8]
16. Maintaining eye contact while communicating is hard. [#8]
17. Having your own pet words and unique expressions. [#8]
18. Voice volume does not match the setting: people ask you to speak either more quietly or louder. [#8]
19. Gesturing a lot while talking — or not gesturing at all. [#8]
20. Communicating non-verbally — with cards, a speech synthesizer or something else. [#8]
21. Needing time to process what the interlocutor said and figure out what to answer. [#8]

How to read the list: the authors stress the diversity of traits and manifestations — "Разные аутисты общаются по-разному" ("Different autistic people communicate differently"), which is why the list is so heterogeneous and contains opposite poles (monotone vs. exaggerated intonation; many gestures vs. none; shyness vs. infodumping). Caveat from the source: these characteristics can be present in any combination in any person — with ASD, without ASD, with anything else; "лишь сочетание симптомов говорит о диагнозе" ("only the combination of symptoms indicates a diagnosis"). [#8]

#### Apology cheat sheet (when to apologize and when not)
Purpose: for people who tend to apologize excessively or find it hard to tell when an apology is due; the source calls it "короткая шпаргалка" ("a short cheat sheet"). [#61]

Apologize when:
1. Your actions hurt someone's feelings. [#61]
2. You made a mistake that harmed someone. [#61]
3. You did something that contradicts your own principles. [#61]

Do not apologize for:
1. Taking up space. [#61]
2. Consuming resources. [#61]
3. Existing. [#61]
4. Expressing yourself. [#61]
5. Asking about something. [#61]
6. Acting proactively. [#61]
7. Being different from others. [#61]
8. Choosing in favour of your own wants and needs. [#61]
9. Disappointing someone. [#61]
10. Expressing an alternative point of view. [#61]
11. Saying no. [#61]
12. Saying yes. [#61]
13. Feeling emotions. [#61]
14. Having needs. [#61]
15. Having desires. [#61]
16. Something you have already apologized for once. [#61]

Why: apologizing simply for breathing is not just unnecessary — it affects your sense of self-respect and prevents you from feeling that you really have the right to take up space, feel emotions and be authentic ("Извинения просто за то, что вы дышите, не просто не нужны: они влияют на ваше чувство самоуважения" — "Apologies simply for breathing are not just unnecessary: they affect your sense of self-respect"). [#61]

#### Noticing-your-apologies exercise
What to do: spend a couple of days noticing how often you apologize and in which contexts it happens. Why: the author suggests you may discover something curious ("Вдруг найдётся что-нибудь любопытное!" — "Maybe you'll find something interesting!"). No further steps are given. [#61]

#### Nine themes of autistic friendship experience (systematic review)
Source: a review by a team of Australian researchers of all existing studies on how autistic people understand and experience friendship, retold by Lena Foer. Design: 568 articles found by keywords; 22 studies met criteria (they studied how autistic people understand friendship and what their experience in it looks like); most from Europe, USA, Japan and Australia; participants were children, adolescents and adults with ASD, 252 people in total. [#128]

How autistic participants define friends: those who will care for and protect them in hard times; who understand, respect and treat them without judgment; who are similar to them — also in some way "not like everyone else" or, more specifically, also living with ASD. Many noted that with other autistic people they can behave more authentically and mask less. [#128]

The beliefs participants mentioned most often (each a theme of the review):
1. "They make my life happier" ("Они делают мою жизнь счастливее"). Friends help, share joys, give a sense of belonging and social security. Friends also help navigate complex social contexts; by modelling how friends do it, autistic people develop their own social skills. [#128]
2. "I don't know if I have friends" ("Не знаю, есть ли у меня друзья"). Mainly children and women said they poorly understand what friends are at all and whether others like them. Children often find it hard to make the first step and wait for peers' initiative; women more often feel separate even while being part of a social group. Lena adds an aside that here she found something in common with the gender attributed to her. [#128]
3. "A small group of friends". Both children and adults with ASD say they have only a few friends; some are content with this, others would like more. [#128]
4. "Loneliness and the need for close people". Despite a strong drive for closeness, many autistic people feel loneliness, isolation and exclusion from society. [#128]
5. "Social disorientation". Problems understanding body language and social hints and difficulty sustaining conversation lead to anxiety and uncertainty in social situations; therefore people with ASD often prefer online communication (more time to answer, less non-verbal signalling). Some attend social-skills trainings. [#128]
6. "Looking normal is hard work" ("Выглядеть нормальным - тяжелая работа"). Masking autistic traits and trying to meet social expectations leads to burnout and distress. [#128]
7. "They will get tired of me" ("Они от меня устанут"). Autistic people often worry that friends will lose interest and leave; this anxiety is fed by difficulties building trusting relationships and by fear of evaluation and rejection. [#128]
8. "Many friendships that ended". Difficulties understanding the social side of ASD can cause conflicts or drifting apart. Adolescent girls with ASD turned out to be more committed to an "all or nothing" position in conflicts: either they took all responsibility for every misunderstanding and rushed to apologize in any unclear situation, or they saw the other person as the source of all troubles with whom there was nothing more to talk about. [#128]
9. "All this exhausts me" ("Меня все это утомляет"). Adult participants more often reported anxiety and fatigue in social contexts, which led to avoiding communication. The need to follow social norms, sudden sensory irritants and atypical reactions to them contributed to overload and fatigue — and with that, to a reduced desire to engage in friendship. [#128]

Lesson drawn by the post: the review counters both myths it opens with — that autistic people do not need friends at all, and that they cannot be friends even if they want to. [#128]

#### Diffusion chain (broken telephone) method and the Registered Report format
- Diffusion chain method: adults play "broken telephone" — one person is told a story, retells it to the next, who retells it to the next, and researchers count how many details survive to the end. Chains can be homogeneous (all autistic, all neurotypical) or mixed, which lets researchers compare information transfer within and across neurotypes. [#264]
- Registered Report: the journal accepts the paper for publication before data collection according to one plan; hypotheses and design are fixed in advance, and the paper is published in any case, even if nothing "good" is found. Why it matters: it closes the loophole of "we got something other than expected, let's rewrite the hypothesis to fit the result". [#264]

### Detailed insights

#### Literalness in everyday life (Lena Foer's examples)
Lena Foer gives two personal dialogues illustrating that autistic people often take everything very literally: [#26]
- Her husband comes out of the bathroom: "Oh, you've dressed to go out." She: "Yes, I was just waiting for you to come out of the bathroom so I could use it." He: "Ah, okay. Where are you going?" She: "To the bathroom." [#26]
- A friend at a restaurant asks: "Have you tried this soup?" She: "Yes." The friend orders the same soup, tastes it: "This is awful!" She: "Yes, I also think the soup is disgusting." Friend: "Why didn't you tell me? I asked you on purpose!" She: "Ты спросила, пробовала ли я его. Я пробовала. Ты не спрашивала, понравился ли он мне." ("You asked whether I had tried it. I had. You didn't ask whether I liked it.") [#26]
Lesson: the answers are accurate responses to the literal question, not jokes — "И я не пыталась быть забавной ни в одной из этих историй" ("And I wasn't trying to be funny in either of these stories"). The implied question (where are you going out to; would you recommend it) is what gets missed. [#26]

#### Autism as culture and identity
Besides autism as a diagnosis — with diagnostic criteria, symptoms and the like — there is autism as culture and identity. Not all people with ASD relate to it: some simply live with the diagnosis, solve the problems it brings into life and otherwise do not think about it much. Others perceive autism as part of their identity; these people more often join autistic communities. Such communities have favourite topics, typical jokes and memes, shared values, their own pet words, role models, a commonality in how they see and think about themselves, and their own lore. [#64]

Most of this is connected one way or another with symptoms; but culture is precisely what is built on top of our life circumstances as a way of thinking about them ("культура - это как раз то, что надстраивается на наши обстоятельства жизни, как способ думать про них" — "culture is exactly what is built on top of our life circumstances as a way of thinking about them"). [#64]

Evidence status: research on ASD as a culture is vanishingly scarce (the post links one PMC article as an example), but there is more general research on how group belonging helps people better feel their "self", validate their traits and perceive themselves more positively. [#64]

#### Object personification
Definition: transferring human characteristics onto objects or abstract concepts. The post's examples: a pillow may be very offended if you don't take it to bed; the number 4 is always a little angry; the last banana on the shelf suffers from loneliness. The post title imitates this: "Этот постик очень расстроится, если вы его не прочитаете!" ("This little post will be very upset if you don't read it!"), which is roughly the title of the article it discusses (a UCL paper on personification in autism). [#72]

- Link to autism: apparently yes — adult autistic people personify objects much more often than neurotypicals. [#72]
- Good or bad: on one hand, an animated world reduces loneliness and gives an opportunity to model social situations. On the other, empathy toward objects is often first of all painful empathy. The meme in the post illustrates this: a caption "AUTISTIC PEOPLE DON'T HAVE EMPATHY!!!1!!1!11!" next to "Me, too afraid to throw the juice box out because it'd make the box sad". [#72, img #72]
- Mechanism: at least in some cases it works like synesthesia — when the brain confuses signals from different senses. [#72]
- What to do with it: live — building good relationships, including with the inanimate world ("Жить - строя классные отношения в т.ч. с неодушевленным миром!" — "Live — building great relationships, including with the inanimate world!"). [#72]

#### Mentalizing and narrative coherence (2023 review, retold by Lena Foer)
Origin: the channel author found a long, "очень интересную" ("very interesting") 2023 review article (ScienceDirect) about how our ability to understand what people — and we ourselves — think and feel is connected with our ability to tell stories, and retold it on Telegraph "в переводе на нормальный язык" ("translated into normal language") under the title «Ментализация и нарративная связность у взрослых с аутизмом» ("Mentalizing and narrative coherence in adults with autism"). The post calls it "наконец и что-то содержательное" ("finally something substantial"). The content of that retelling follows. [#214, article #214]

Theory of mind and the Sally–Anne task. When autism is discussed, theory of mind is often mentioned: the ability to understand that another person's knowledge, beliefs and desires may differ from our own. Many people with ASD have difficulty solving theory-of-mind tasks. The most standard one is the Sally and Anne task: while Anne was out of the room, Sally moved Anne's toy from the shelf into a chest. When Anne comes back, where will she look for the toy? Children with theory-of-mind problems answer that she will look in the chest — i.e. they do not account for the fact that Anne did not see the move and still believes the toy is on the shelf. [#214]

Mentalizing as the broader capacity. Theory of mind is one component of mentalizing (ментализация) — the general ability to understand mental states, including one's own thoughts and feelings (self-directed mentalizing, "направленная на себя") and the states of other people (other-directed, "направленная на других"). [#214]

What adults with autism find easy vs. hard. Research cited in the article shows that autistic adults often solve "Sally and Anne"-type situations easily but have difficulty with advanced mentalizing tasks such as understanding sarcasm, metaphors and social norms. Hardest of all for autistic people is intuitive, automatic mentalizing; the author illustrates this with the question "знакомо ли вам чувство, как будто всем вокруг выдали руководство по взаимодействиям, кроме вас?" ("do you know the feeling that everyone around was given a manual for interactions except you?"). When there is time to think, autistic people do better thanks to compensatory strategies: where others have intuitive knowledge, autistic people memorize social norms and cope through memory and logic. Lesson: the deficit is mainly in speed/automaticity, not in the capacity to reason about minds when given time. [#214]

Narrative coherence — definition and structure. Narrative coherence (нарративная связность) is the ability to create structured, consistent stories — with a beginning, an ending, and development of characters and their emotions. Narratives have:
- a macrostructure — the overall organization of the story; [#214]
- a microstructure — the linguistic elements; [#214]
- and three dimensions: contextual (describing the events themselves), chronological (temporal sequence) and thematic (personal evaluations, emotions, motives). [#214]

Typical narrative difficulties in autistic adults (five). Adults with autism often find narrative coherence hard. Usually this means:
1. Difficulty determining what is the point and what are insignificant details. [#214]
2. Too much or too little information for the context. [#214]
3. Difficulty planning and organizing the story in one's head in advance and sequentially. [#214]
4. Less use of connecting words (e.g. "поэтому" — "therefore", "и" — "and"). [#214]
5. Difficulty recalling specific autobiographical details and imagining oneself in the future. [#214]

How these problems affect the three dimensions.
1. Contextual coherence: because of weak autobiographical memory and difficulty taking another person's perspective, autistic people find it hard to judge how much context the listener needs. The author's framing of the dilemma: should you retell your whole day, or jump straight in ("с места в карьер") to the main point? [#214]
2. Chronological coherence: difficulty arranging events in chronological order and establishing cause-and-effect links; autistic people rarely recall specific time markers. The author jokes that such a story can look like roughly any Charlie Kaufman film ("стойте, значит ли это...?" — "wait, does that mean...?"). [#214]
3. Thematic coherence: autistic people have difficulty expressing their feelings and making sense of experience in a social context, and rarely include talk about mental states in their narratives. They prefer structured stories with an emphasis on sensory details instead of emotional aspects. [#214]

The link between mentalizing and narrative coherence. The author finds this link especially interesting. Almost any story we tell is filled with some "mental" meaning that requires understanding mental states. Research shows that people with better-developed mentalizing skills produce more coherent stories — and, in the other direction, the better a person understands and retells social narratives, the better they understand other people's states and their own. The relationship runs both ways. [#214]

Similarity matters (link to double empathy). An "interesting fact" from the article: autistic people understand and empathize with other autistic people better, which points to the importance of perceived similarity between oneself and others for effective mentalizing. The same holds for stories: it is easier for an autistic person to retell the narrative of another autistic person. (Compare the double empathy findings in the next section.) [#214]

Neurobiology: the cerebellum. At the neurobiological level these processes also appear to be linked, and the cerebellum plays a large role. Traditionally it is associated primarily with motor functions, but it is now considered an important node in the brain networks responsible for both mentalizing and narrative coherence. The cerebellum probably helps organize sequences of events and predict mental states, which is critical for both functions. The source hedges this ("судя по всему" — "apparently", "Вероятно" — "probably"). [#214]

#### The channel's emotional empathy survey (EES bot)
Set-up: the author built a bot to test emotional empathy using the EES scale and promised results if more than 80 responses came in; after a bit over two weeks 509 people had taken it. [#240]

Sampling disclaimer (the author's own): the link was posted in two channels — one for neurodivergent people, one for people with diagnoses related to strong emotions (e.g. BPD) — and on the author's Facebook. So the sample is very specific and unlikely to reflect the "average population". [#240]

Overall results:
- Mean score 44.14, noticeably higher than in the original EES study (33). Explanation given: the original study had equal numbers of men and women, while this sample was heavily skewed toward women, so the result is close to the female mean. [#240]
- Range very wide: from −120 to 122. The distribution had a "dip" in the centre and was shifted toward the poles. Possible explanation offered: 133 participants reported being neurodivergent, and the author states it is known that in neurodivergent people empathy is more often either high or low rather than "average". [#240]

Gender differences:
- Women (394 people): 47.95 (original study: 44). [#240]
- Men (88 people): 27.68 (original: 23). [#240]
- Other / not stated (27 people): 42.11. [#240]
Because women dominate, the overall mean is nearly the women's mean; overall the results match the scale authors' data, and bot respondents were only slightly more empathic than respondents of the 1970s study. [#240]

Diagnoses and empathy (mean scores):
- No diagnoses (176 people): 43.56 — practically the same as women in the original paper. [#240]
- Neurodivergence: 36.77. [#240]
- Affective disorders (depression, bipolar disorder): 59.77. [#240]
- Personality disorders: 27.20. [#240]
- Anxiety disorders: 36.30. [#240]
- Stress disorders (PTSD, complex PTSD and other stress disorders — the source's "и др."): 62 — flagged by the author with "(!)". [#240]

Conclusion by the author: having a diagnosis on average did not lower scores and sometimes substantially increased emotional empathy. The results confirm the trends described by the scale's authors while adding accents for specific communities; the specifics of emotional empathy in neurodivergence and in affective, stress and personality disorders are called "very interesting". [#240]

#### The double empathy problem: the 2020 study and its replication
Background: the double empathy problem became a very popular topic after a 2020 study: autistic people understand other autistic people no worse than neurotypicals understand neurotypicals, and problems arise only in mixed chains. The news spread "like wildfire" ("как лесной пожар"), and it has now been repeated on a larger sample. [#264]

2020 study: Catherine Crompton (University of Edinburgh) and colleagues had adults play broken telephone using diffusion chains (see *Diffusion chain method*). 72 participants; three chain types — autistic, neurotypical and mixed. Result: autistic chains transmitted information as accurately as neurotypical ones; in mixed chains details were lost noticeably faster, and people in them felt less comfortable with each other. This became the widely cited confirmation of the double empathy problem. [#264]

Replication: the same team with colleagues from Nottingham, Glasgow and Dallas re-tested the result as a Registered Report. The sample was more than four times larger: 311 people, three sites in two countries, chains of six people, two stories. The same hypothesis was registered. [#264]

Results of the replication:
1. Autistic chains again transmitted information no worse than neurotypical ones — this part was confirmed. [#264]
2. Mixed chains this time did not lag behind either: the predicted breakdown at the junction did not occur, on neither of the stories. [#264]
3. Sense of rapport was more nuanced. Each participant rated it twice — as listener and as storyteller. Neurotypical chains rated rapport highest: as storytellers they beat both mixed and autistic chains; as listeners, only mixed ones. Autistic chains never beat mixed ones. So the expected "it's easier with your own" was confirmed only by half — and that half is about neurotypicals. [#264]
4. Bonus, also replicated from the earlier work: when participants were told who in the chain was autistic, the sense of rapport rose for everyone. [#264]

Why results diverged (the authors' explanation; they say they did not expect it): sample diversity. In 2020 participants were recruited in one city and were similar to each other; now they varied widely in age, gender and IQ, and included both diagnosed and self-identified people. When people in a chain are separated by many things at once, the effect of matching neurotype can get lost among the rest. Authors' conclusion: the success of a conversation is determined not only by whether the person in front of you is autistic, but also by whether you match on everything else. [#264]

What follows: the most important part held — autistic people transmit information to each other exactly as accurately as neurotypicals do to each other. This directly contradicts diagnostic criteria, where social communication impairments are written as a universal trait, and the authors state this explicitly. The mixed-pair breakdown part now looks less robust. [#264]

#### Autistic Barbie: representation discussion
Mattel released a series of Barbies including one with autism, one with Down syndrome, a visually impaired one and one with diabetes. Katya Klimochkina, who says she had no firm position on the idea of an autistic doll, read the Reddit thread on the autistic Barbie and summarized the points: [#255]
1. Most commenters agree a doll is not expected to show autism "correctly" or "accurately". Looking for a specific support level in it seems odd and too "adult" — children don't think or play in such categories. Katya highlights the point that levels are about diagnosis, not personality, and considers the absence of a label a big plus. [#255]
2. Many write that the doll's strength is its flexibility: accessories can be removed, used differently or not used at all. This is read as respect for how varied autism is and as not "cramming everything in" and labelling again. [#255]
3. The AAC device provoked the most emotion. Katya suggests (hedging that she may be wrong) that among Russian speakers access to alternative communication tools is still quite limited, so it might not provoke such a reaction there. What it comes down to positively is a sense of visibility — recognition that people may not always speak, may not speak orally, or may not speak the same way every day. [#255]
4. Many were initially sceptical and then stopped themselves: it is a children's toy, not a symbol of autistic identity. Parents' comments share stories of children recognizing themselves in the doll and ceasing to be embarrassed about their headphones or stims — which Katya sees as evidence it worked for those it was made for. [#255]
5. Criticism exists — about the doll's quality, the clothes, and fear that autism will be reduced to a set of accessories. But even critical comments rarely say "this is a terrible idea"; people care how it was made and with whom, and the autistic community's participation in developing the doll is perceptible. [#255]
Katya's verdict: the autistic Barbie "has the right to exist on the shelf" not because it "explains autism" but because it does not even try; if even one child (or adult) feels a little less lonely with it, it has done its job. [#255]

### Tools and resources mentioned

- Autentik AI Text Helper (Chrome extension, AI-based). Two functions: a Text Clarifier that explains hidden meanings, subtext, nuances and implied feelings in written communication (e.g. a metaphor or set expression whose hidden meaning is unclear); and a Reply Helper that helps formulate a clear, polite answer for polite small-talk moments, starting a dialogue and closing it — especially useful for questions like "how are you?". The creators say it is designed specifically for the autistic community and that the goal is not to change your authentic voice or make you imitate others but to lend a hand in routine message exchanges. ### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The communication trait list is not diagnostic: any trait can occur in anyone; only the combination matters. [#8]
- Empathy survey: self-selected, very specific sample (neurodivergent channel, strong-emotion-diagnosis channel, the author's Facebook), heavily female; the author warns it likely does not reflect the general population. The claim that neurodivergent empathy is "more often high or low rather than average" is stated as known, without a cited source. [#240]
- (!) CONTRADICTION (within the empathy post): the post says diagnoses on average did not lower scores, yet the listed means for neurodivergence (36.77), anxiety disorders (36.30) and personality disorders (27.20) are below the no-diagnosis group (43.56); only affective (59.77) and stress disorders (62) are higher. [#240]
- Double empathy: the original 2020 mixed-chain breakdown did not replicate; the authors attribute this to sample diversity and say they did not expect it. The channel hopes for further surprising findings. [#264]
- Object personification mechanism is synesthesia-like "at least in some cases" — hedged. The link to autism is stated as "по всей видимости" ("apparently"). [#72]
- Research on autism as a culture is vanishingly scarce; the self-image benefits come from general group-belonging research. [#64]
- Mentalizing/narrative claims are the author's plain-language retelling of a single 2023 review; the cerebellum role is hedged as "apparently"/"probably". [#214]
- The AI helper had not yet been tested by the author at posting time. [#52]
- On the Barbie AAC point, Katya hedges that she may be wrong about limited AAC access among Russian speakers. [#255]
