## Masking, camouflaging and unmasking

### What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel Нейропряность (Neurospicy) published on autistic masking and camouflaging: the research definition (Hull's three-part model), why camouflaging is costly in energy and mental health and leads to autistic burnout, how masking hides autism so well that it is missed by others, by diagnostic questionnaires and by the "gifted child" label, a full self-assessment workbook for spotting one's own masking strategies, and two reflections on unmasking that warn against simply "tearing off the mask" and describe it instead as slow, safe, values-based emergence of a self. According to the source, masking is for autistic people "не просто способ вписаться, это выживание в преимущественно нейротипичном обществе" (not just a way to fit in, it is survival in a predominantly neurotypical society), and its price is autistic burnout [#24].

### Core ideas

- Masking is survival, and it has a price. In a world that expects conformity, masking autistic traits and imitating neurotypical behaviour is an everyday survival practice for autistic people, and the cost is autistic burnout. The channel's own introductory article set out to explain the essence of masking, its consequences, and strategies that reduce the *need* to mask, making communication and finding connection easier "в мире, где быть "своим" часто кажется роскошью" (in a world where being "one of us" often feels like a luxury) [#24, img #24].
- Camouflaging is the umbrella; masking is one part of it. Camouflaging = compensation + masking + assimilation (Hull et al.); in everyday speech "masking" is used as a synonym, but clinically masking is a subset (see *Social camouflaging model*) [#156].
- Camouflaging costs energy and harms mental health. It requires time, effort and energy, is linked to burnout, depression and anxiety, and raises the risk of autistic burnout starting or worsening [#156, #696].
- Good masking produces disbelief and lack of support. People who camouflage well are often not believed when they disclose autism, so they receive poor support, and exhaustion and burnout become frequent [#156, #403, #404, #519].
- Masking keeps people safe — so unmasking requires safety. Unmasking without a sense of self, values, or a safe environment can lead to social punishment and new internalised messages of being "bad" (see *Unmasking as excavation*) [#73, #518].
- Unmasking is emergence, not return. For people who masked from early childhood, a fully formed self may never have had a chance to develop; unmasking lets it appear for the first time, non-linearly and in the body (see *Unmasking as emergence*) [#660].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Social camouflaging model (Hull et al., 2018)
Purpose: a research model, translated by the channel from an autistic burnout guide, describing how some autistic people use a set of actions and strategies to navigate the neurotypical social world better, fit in, build connections, prevent rejection and social ostracism, or gain acceptance from others (Hull et al., 2017; 2018). The three forms:

1. Компенсация (Compensation) — strategies used to actively compensate for difficulties in social situations. Examples given: copying body language and facial expressions; learning social cues from films and books; using pre-prepared social scripts in different contexts [#156].
2. Маскирование (Masking) — strategies used to hide autistic traits or create a neurotypical image. Examples: adjusting face and body to look confident and/or relaxed; forcing eye contact [#156].
3. Ассимиляция (Assimilation) — strategies used to try to fit into social situations. Examples: playing a role; avoiding or forcing oneself into interaction with others; feigned interest; exaggerating expressions and enthusiasm for other people's sake [#156].

Terminology note from the source: "В разговорной речи маскирование часто используется как синоним камуфлирования. Однако с клинической точки зрения маскирование считается подмножеством камуфлирования" (In everyday speech masking is often used as a synonym for camouflaging. Clinically, however, masking is considered a subset of camouflaging) (Hull et al., 2018) [#156].

Why it costs so much: autistic people have to set aside time to rehearse social interactions, and acting and suppressing one's true behaviour require more effort/energy than authentic behaviour. This harms overall mental health (Alaghband-Rad et al., 2023; Cook et al., 2021), further increasing the risk of autistic burnout appearing or worsening [#156].

#### Camouflaging self-assessment workbook — Part A (Lindsey Mackereth)
Purpose: for people who suspect they mask their neurodivergent traits but do not know *how*. The checklist and practical guide, created by Lindsey Mackereth and translated by the channel ("Исследование камуфлирования [+ Другие нейрокомплексные социальные различия]" — *Exploring camouflaging [+ other neurocomplex social differences]*), aims to deepen awareness of masking and help take next steps in integrating that new information. It contains screening items, assessment questions and data on common neurodivergent social traits and common signs of masking, plus bonus application questions to work through alone or with a psychotherapist, coach, partner or friend; the post suggests printing it and taking it with you (for example to a session) [#496].

How to use: Part A lists common camouflaging/masking strategies found in autistic people. They can also occur in other neurocomplex people, so the instructions encourage everyone to look through it even if they do not identify as autistic. Put a ✔ next to every item that resonates to identify areas of possible social deviation from neurotypical norms. Each section ends with an "Other:" line for your own items [#496].

Compensation strategies / signs [#496]:
1. Creating scripts for social situations — relying on pre-prepared scripts or rehearsing conversations in advance to make interactions go smoothly.
2. Repeating phrases exactly as others said them — imitating and using the exact words and phrases other people use.
3. Using behaviour learned from watching other people's interactions — often observing and adopting others' behaviour to fit in socially.
4. Practising facial expressions and body language — regularly rehearsing and refining expressions, gestures and body language to match allistic (non-autistic) people.
5. Researching social skills to improve one's own — actively seeking resources and information on social skills to improve interactions and seem more allistic.
6. Learning and using social skills seen on TV and in films — taking inspiration from social skills shown in media and building them into one's own interactions.
7. Deliberately copying someone's behaviour, body language or expressions during interactions — consciously imitating and mirroring others to blend in.
8. Watching others to understand social skills — spending considerable time observing and studying how others navigate social situations in order to learn and imitate their skills.

Masking strategies / signs [#496]:
1. Constant preoccupation with how others perceive you and a drive to meet their expectations.
2. Carefully adjusting body language to seem interested and engaged in conversations.
3. Heightened awareness of the impression you make on others, leading to shyness/self-consciousness.
4. Constant monitoring and adapting of body language and facial expressions to fit social norms.
5. Excessive focus on managing your own facial expressions and body language in social situations.
6. Feeling you must maintain eye contact even when it is uncomfortable or overwhelming.
7. Constant vigilance in tracking and adjusting body language to project relaxed behaviour.

Assimilation strategies / signs [#496]:
1. Conversations feel unnatural and do not flow smoothly.
2. You often catch yourself pretending to be "normal" in social situations.
3. You rely on the support or presence of others to navigate social interactions.
4. You believe you cannot fully be yourself around others.
5. You feel the need to put on a mask or façade to navigate social situations.
6. You try to avoid or minimise interactions with others in social settings.
7. Social situations feel like performances rather than opportunities for genuine connection.
8. Interacting with others requires conscious effort and feels forced.

Part A exercise — 5 reflection questions [#496]:
1. Choose one trait from the camouflaging sections that matches your current social strategy.
2. What messages did you receive over your life that reinforced this behaviour? From whom? When?
3. How has this behaviour helped you socially in the past?
4. What has been the personal cost of this behaviour?
5. Have there been times you felt you did not need this behaviour in a social setting? Where? When? Who were you with?

The sequence moves from naming a strategy, to its origin (who taught it), to acknowledging its protective function, to its cost, and finally to locating situations where it was unnecessary — i.e. evidence of safe contexts [#496].

#### Neurodivergent social differences checklist — Part B (Lindsey Mackereth)
Purpose: Part B lists common social differences of neurocomplex people (the source lists autistic people, people with ADHD, gifted people "и т.д.") found in autistic people. Again tick ✔ every item that resonates, to identify areas of possible social deviation from neurotypical norms. Part B draws on the adult ADHD diagnostic interview DIVA and the autism interview guidelines MIGDAS-2 (see *Tools*). The "more common neurodivergent social traits/differences" [#496]:

1. You prefer one-to-one conversations.
2. You have a small circle of friends.
3. You are an independent thinker and do not always agree with others.
4. You tend to have difficulties with authority.
5. You are less comfortable in unstructured social situations (e.g. free mingling) than structured ones (e.g. doing an activity together).
6. You need depth and complexity in conversations and friendships.
7. You are easily distracted in conversation when other conversations are going on in the same space.
8. You are easily distracted by your own thoughts while talking with others.
9. You are easily distracted by external stimuli while talking with others.
10. You find it hard to speak at a socially acceptable volume; you often talk too loudly or too quietly.
11. You prefer to talk during activities meant to be enjoyed quietly (e.g. watching films).
12. You often need a lot of words to say something.
13. You often do not give others a chance to get a word in.
14. You find it hard to start and/or end a conversation.
15. You often finish other people's sentences.
16. You have an opinion on everything and voice it immediately.
17. You quickly tire of relationships and friendships.
18. You often take on other people's tasks without being asked.
19. You often get irritated when the pace of conversation feels too slow.
20. You often get irritated when you perceive communication as inefficient.
21. You have less motivation/need for social interaction than other people you know.
22. You prefer not to be touched by other people.
23. You get upset when people around you do not care about larger systemic problems.
24. You need more time alone than most people you have met.
25. You need a little more time to think before answering in conversation.
26. You prefer to talk about your special interests rather than other topics (this item appears twice in the translated list).
27. You get irritated when others interrupt your routine tasks or activities.
28. You enjoy only certain kinds of humour, often not those popular with the majority.
29. You find it hard to keep a conversation going on abstract/small-talk topics ("поддерживать течение разговора на отвлеченные темы").
Plus an "Other:" line [#496].

Part B exercise — 4 reflection questions (answer as you see fit; "Доверьтесь своим ощущениям" — *trust your feelings*) [#496]:
1. Which social difference you ticked in Part B has, in your view, caused the most social or communicative discomfort/conflict in important areas of your life?
2. How do you currently handle this difference when it shows up in front of other people?
3. How do you currently handle it internally when it arises — what do you tell yourself?
4. How could you change your inner dialogue in such situations to show even more respect for the particularities of your own thinking, while still respecting the particularities of the thinking of the people around you?

#### Unmasking as excavation, not disclosure (Настя's reframe)
Purpose: a first-person account by Настя of why her first unmasking failed and how values-based integration training shifted her approach "от Раскрытия к Раскопкам" (from Disclosure/Uncovering to Excavation) [#73, img #73].

1. The failed model — unmasking as tearing off a mask. She takes words very literally, so she first imagined unmasking as removing a mask lying over the "real me": if she could just peel off the surface layer, a whole unmasked autistic person would magically appear underneath. In practice she thought this meant letting herself stim or look odd or awkward (which she often did anyway despite her efforts), or completely ignoring social rituals and conventions [#73].
2. The mistake — focusing on outward behaviour. She treated unmasking as "обратному тренингу социальных навыков" (reverse social-skills training): getting rid of "fake" behaviour without asking how she actually felt about it [#73].
3. Why it was dangerous. She still did not know who she was behind the mask, so removing her neurotypical "costume" was useless and dangerous. "Наши маски, какими бы разрушительными они ни были, формируются для нашей защиты" (Our masks, however destructive they are, are formed for our protection). She ended up socially punished, and without any sense of self or understanding of her values that punishment was devastating. With no armour left, she created new social messages that she was "bad" or "wrong" to replace the ones she had been trying to get rid of [#73].
4. The shift — finding values in one's stories. She was hesitant to return to unmasking at all after it "ended very badly"; the only reason she joined Values-Based Integration training was that she had read about the process of finding values in one's own stories, which resonated enough to overcome her fears. The direction implied by the title is excavation: digging for who one is and what one values before changing outward behaviour [#73].

#### Unmasking as emergence of an authentic self (Lindsey Mackereth)
Purpose: a therapist's description, translated by Настя, of what unmasking looks like over time and why the metaphor itself can mislead [#660].

1. The question and the grief. In therapy the question often arises: «Кто я, если я не играю роль?» (Who am I if I am not playing a role?). It rarely has quick answers. It is usually followed by a period of quiet mourning — for the years spent adapting, for effort that went unnoticed, and for the self that was never given a chance to develop [#660].
2. Something forms in the silence. Preferences start to reappear. Boundaries become clearer. Autonomy stops feeling like a risk and is experienced as a right. A client may notice that silence nourishes more than conversation, or that a simple "no" without explanation brings unexpected relief. Stimming returns. The joy of solitude is rediscovered. Pleasure in choosing for oneself [#660].
3. Why small changes matter. These changes are subtle but very significant: each act of self-respect signals safety to the nervous system, and each moment of authenticity strengthens new neural connections — neuroplasticity working "не ради выживания, а ради целостности" (not for survival but for wholeness) [#660].
4. The metaphor's limit. "Unmasking" implies a fully formed self was simply hidden and waiting to be found; but for many autistic adults, especially those who masked from an early age, that self never had a chance to fully develop. Hence: "Анмаскинг — это не возвращение к зарытой истине. Это позволение истине впервые проявиться." (Unmasking is not a return to a buried truth. It is allowing the truth to appear for the first time.) [#660].
5. What the process is like. The emergence is rarely linear; it is embodied, repetitive and often uncomfortable. It requires painstaking work of retraining a nervous system that has long been on guard, and it requires trust — not only in relationships but in one's own instincts, needs and perception [#660].

### Detailed insights

#### What masking feels like from the inside
Lindsey Mackereth: "Masking isn't just "acting normal" or "playing along." It's an emotional marathon demanding hyper-awareness of one's behavior and others' reactions, constant editing of natural impulses, and a relentless internal commentary at full speed." The three components are: hyper-awareness of one's own behaviour and of others' reactions; constant suppression/editing of natural impulses; and a non-stop internal monologue running at full speed. The quote comes from her Substack piece titled "Leadership Coaching's Dirty Secret: Burning Out Highly Masked Autistic Adults" [#696, img #696].

#### Masked traits are invisible to others but not to the person (Lou, @neurodivergent_lou)
- Autistic people can mask their traits so well that others do not notice them, even when those traits significantly affect the person's life; this is why the inner experience of autism must be understood, not only its outward appearance [#403].
- Lou's own example: in some situations she masks so thoroughly that her "постоянные трудности с социальным общением и взаимодействием" (*persistent difficulties with social communication and interaction* — the diagnostic wording) are invisible to others, while inside she is carefully controlling her communication and imitating other people. This leads to exhaustion and harms her mental health and her sense of self [#404].
- Difficulty with change and routine can be missed because it doesn't match the textbook description. For a long time Lou thought she had no such difficulties, because she did not recognise herself in how the criterion "ограниченные и повторяющиеся модели поведения, деятельности или интересов" (restricted and repetitive patterns of behaviour, activities or interests) was described. Her actual difficulties were: losing the ability to do anything if the time of a meeting or plan changes; constant vague anxiety about the future; difficulties when the usual route has to change [#405].
- Other ways difficulty with change can show up: inability to switch between tasks (e.g. extreme difficulty getting out of bed); when packaging or a food recipe changes, "we" may never eat that food again; difficulty buying or using new things; feeling overwhelmed when the arrangement of objects in a room changes; difficulty finishing or starting a new series or book [#406].
- Screening questionnaires can miss autism. Some autism-scale questions seem random — e.g. preferring the library to a party is treated as a possible indicator of autism — but those asking do not consider the hidden reasons for such a choice. Autistic people can enjoy parties and be very social. Questions based only on stereotypes can confuse and prevent autism from being noticed [#407].

#### The cost of passing: "high-functioning" and misread signs
- Erin Ekins (@QueerlyAutistic): autistic people whom society considers "high-functioning" tend to have a hugely disproportionate level of mental illness and trauma, to the point of it effectively limiting and threatening their lives. "То, что мы МОЖЕМ притворяться, не значит, что это нас не убивает." (The fact that we CAN pretend does not mean it isn't killing us.) The channel tagged it #Жиза (so relatable) [#519, img #519].
- Rebecca Murter's card reframes misattributed experiences: it was not clinical depression — it was burnout; not panic attacks — meltdowns; not always anxiety — sensory overload. "Это был аутизм" (It was autism) [#525].
- Pete Wharmby's card (forwarded from Нейропанк): "MASKING KEEPS AUTISTIC PEOPLE SAFE... ....DON'T TELL US WE CAN UNMASK IF YOU HAVEN'T MADE IT SAFE." The lesson: masking is a safety strategy, so the responsibility for making unmasking possible lies with creating safe conditions, not with telling autistic people to stop [#518, img #518].

#### The "gifted child" label as a mask for neurodivergence (MtfoxX3 thread)
- Claim: if you were a "gifted child" at school and were never diagnosed with anxiety, depression, ADHD or autism, you should probably get checked [#520].
- The author's impression is that "в 9 случаях из 10" (in 9 cases out of 10) the "gifted child" label appears because the child: (1) fears failure and puts too much pressure on themselves; (2) prefers studying to socialising; (3) has excessively high expectations of themselves; (4) hyperfocuses on studies [#521]; (5) neglects self-care if they have "not earned" it; (6) bases self-esteem on how well they do at school; (7) often befriends teachers/adults rather than peers, because a destructive need to be "adult" robs them of childhood — "and many other unhealthy patterns" [#522].
- Mechanism: in academic settings it is much easier to call such children "gifted" than to investigate the underlying reasons for their behaviour. The result is often that the correct diagnosis and treatment come only after finishing school, when the brain has little time left to develop [#523].
- Caveat by the author: not all "gifted children" will have any of this; the point is that the "gifted child" label leads many people not to get the diagnosis and support they need [#524].

#### Masked autistic people are disbelieved
Livingston et al. (2019) report that autistic people who camouflage their traits are often not believed when they disclose their autism and are therefore poorly supported because of their neurotypical presentation; this lack of support meant exhaustion and burnout were frequent [#156].

## What autism is: the neurodiversity paradigm

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's foundational material on what autism (and, more broadly, neurodivergence) *is*: a full translated definition of autism by Nick Walker written outside the pathology paradigm, a glossary of the four core neurodiversity terms, five scientific models for thinking about autism and ADHD as spectra, the double empathy problem as a counter-theory to "mind-blindness", and a children's book that flips the usual perspective by describing a neurotypical boy through an autistic child's eyes. The channel frames this as important because how autism is defined decides where responsibility for social failures is placed, who gets support, and whether difference is treated as a deficit or as a variant [#65, #75, #76, #397].

### Core ideas

- Autism is a genetically based neurological variant, not a disorder by scientific fact. Per Nick Walker, calling autism a disorder is a value judgement; the neurodiversity model treats it as part of natural human biodiversity (see *Nick Walker's definition of autism*) [#76].
- Social difficulties are a by-product, not the core. Walker argues that autistic social struggles stem from intense, chaotic sensory and cognitive experience that eats attention and energy, plus social rejection — not from inherent "social and communication deficits" [#76].
- Communication breakdown is two-way. The double empathy problem (Milton, 2012) says NT–autistic misunderstanding is mutual; autistic people communicate and empathise well with other autistic people (see *Double empathy problem*) [#75].
- Autism and ADHD are spectra of many traits with no sharp line between norm and pathology. Several models compete to describe that spectrum; the practical consequence is to focus support on real needs rather than on whether diagnostic criteria are met (see *Five models of the neurodiversity spectrum*) [#396, #397].
- Perspective-taking reveals that "typical" is also strange from the outside. The channel author values imagining oneself fully in another's place, which makes any choice look understandable and enables constructive response instead of condemnation (see *Perspective reversal*) [#65, #66].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Four requirements for a good introductory text on autism
Nick Walker opens his definition by stating why he wrote it: the world needed a good basic introductory text "What is autism?" that would meet four criteria [#76]:
1. Consistent with current scientific data — «соответствовал современным научным данным» (consistent with current scientific data). The definition must reflect what research currently shows rather than outdated models [#76].
2. Not based on the pathology paradigm — «не основывался на парадигме патологии» (not based on the pathology paradigm). It must not frame autism as a disease or defect to be fixed [#76].
3. Short, simple and accessible — so that anyone can read and understand it [#76].
4. Formal enough for professional and academic use — so it can be cited and used by specialists, not only by the community [#76].

#### Nick Walker's definition of autism (non-pathology paradigm)
Purpose: a concise, citable, scientifically grounded description of autism that does not use deficit language. The channel posted it as a translation of Walker's text from Neuroqueer Heresies. Its components, in the order of the text [#76]:

1. What autism is. «Аутизм - это генетически обусловленный неврологический вариант человека» (Autism is a genetically based human neurological variant). The complex set of interrelated characteristics that distinguish autistic from non-autistic neurology is not yet fully understood, but current data indicate that the main difference is an especially high level of synaptic connectivity and responsiveness of the autistic brain [#76].
2. What this does to experience. High connectivity tends to make the autistic person's subjective experience more intense and chaotic than that of non-autistic people, on both the sensorimotor and the cognitive level: the autistic mind tends to register more information, and the impact of each bit of information tends to be stronger and less predictable [#76].
3. Autism is developmental. It begins in the womb and has a pervasive influence on development at many levels throughout life. It produces distinctive, atypical ways of thinking, moving, interacting, and sensory and cognitive processing. A common analogy is that autistic people have a different neurological «операционная система» (operating system) from non-autistic people [#76].
4. Prevalence and the rise in diagnoses. Current estimates put one to two percent of the world's population as autistic. Although the number of people diagnosed has risen steadily over recent decades, the evidence indicates the increase reflects greater public and professional awareness rather than an actual increase in the prevalence of autism [#76].
5. Heterogeneity and disability by context. Despite shared neurological features, autistic people differ greatly from one another; some show exceptional cognitive talents. But in a society built around the sensory, cognitive, developmental and social needs of non-autistic people, autistic people are almost always disabled («ограничены в возможностях», *limited in their capabilities*) to some degree — sometimes very obviously, sometimes more subtly. In this framing, the limitation arises from the fit between the person and a society designed for others [#76].
6. Why social interaction is hard (the mechanism). Social interaction is one context in which autistic people tend to struggle consistently. The causal chain Walker gives: (a) an autistic child's sensory experience is more intense and chaotic than a non-autistic child's; (b) the ongoing task of navigating and integrating that experience takes more of the child's attention and energy; (c) so there is less attention and energy left to focus on the subtleties of social interaction; (d) difficulty meeting non-autistic social expectations often leads to social rejection; (e) rejection further compounds social difficulties and impedes social development [#76].
7. The misreading this produces. Because of that chain, autism is often misconstrued as essentially a set of "social and communication deficits" by people who do not realise that the social challenges are «лишь побочными продуктами интенсивного и хаотичного характера аутистического сенсорного и когнитивного опыта» (merely by-products of the intense and chaotic nature of autistic sensory and cognitive experience) [#76].
8. Disorder vs. neurodiversity. Autism is still widely regarded as a "disorder", but in recent years this view has been challenged by proponents of the neurodiversity model, which holds that autism and other neurocognitive variants are simply part of the natural spectrum of human biodiversity, like variations in ethnicity or sexual orientation — which were also pathologised in the past. Walker's conclusion: «описание аутизма как расстройства представляет собой ценностное суждение, а не научный факт» (describing autism as a disorder represents a value judgement, not a scientific fact) [#76].

#### Core neurodiversity vocabulary
The channel's glossary post ("Let's sort out the terms") defines four terms, giving the Russian and English forms [#616]:
1. Нейротип (Neurotype) — the way a person's brain works, often matching the person's diagnosis or its absence. Both "autistic" and "neurotypical" are neurotypes; i.e. neurotypical is itself a neurotype, not the absence of one [#616].
2. Нейроразнообразие (Neurodiversity) — describes a *group* of people with different neurotypes. The word "neurodiverse" can be used for a group that includes autistic people, neurotypical people, people with ADHD and people with РЛ; the author presents these as examples of an open-ended list. So neurodiversity is a property of a group, not of an individual [#616].
3. Нейроотличный (Neurodivergent) — a person whose neurology or brain structure differs substantially from the norm. The term is usually used for people with developmental and learning disorders [#616].
4. Нейротипичный (Neurotypical) — a term that arose in the autistic community, referring to a person with typical neurology, someone without developmental or learning disorders. The abbreviation НТ (NT) is used in the channel's other posts [#616, #66].

#### Five models of the neurodiversity spectrum
Source: a post by Yana ("Dzhumka") shared by the channel, "Neurodiversity: spectrum, trait overlaps and diagnostic problems". Context: the modern world is gradually moving away from treating neurodivergences — especially autism and ADHD — as strictly separate diagnoses and increasingly sees them as spectra: complex, multidimensional structures reflecting the diversity of human behaviour and cognition. This highlights the richness of variation but also the difficulty of diagnosing and supporting people at the intersection of normal and pathological traits [#397].

What a "spectrum" means. It can be understood more "strictly" — including only possible combinations of traits from the approved diagnostic criteria — or more "loosely", allowing that every person on the planet has some neurodivergent traits to some degree, i.e. is «немного аутичен» (a little autistic). How exactly to define the spectrum so as to effectively help the neurodivergent people who need it is the main subject of debate among current researchers (psychiatrists and geneticists) [#397].

What is already certain, per Yana: autism and ADHD should be viewed as variations of many traits, in particular attention problems, sensory sensitivity, impulsivity and difficulties with social interaction. These traits may be barely noticeable or so pronounced that they start to limit daily life. Scientists and neurodiversity advocates stress that there is no clear boundary between norm and pathology, which shifts diagnosis and support toward individual needs [#397].

The five models proposed so far:
1. Simple Spectrum (Модель простого спектра). Autism and ADHD form one continuous continuum. *All* people have traits associated with these conditions, but only in some do they reach a clinically significant level. Analogy: like blood pressure or cholesterol — small deviations can be normal, large ones may require intervention [#397].
2. Far End (Модель крайнего выражения). Autism and ADHD are the extreme points of a shared continuum. A person may be "a little autistic", but only those at the very "edge" of the spectrum receive a diagnosis. The model focuses attention on the most pronounced cases [#397].
3. Network Continua (Модель сети континуумов). Traits associated with autism and ADHD are treated as interconnected nodes. Example: sensory processing problems can amplify attention difficulties, and impulsivity can worsen mood-regulation problems. Each node sits on its own continuum — in one person one trait is stronger, in another person a different one [#397].
4. Complex Spectrum (Модель сложного спектра). Autism and ADHD are a multidimensional space where traits intersect not only with each other but also with features of other conditions, such as anxiety disorders. This helps account for the complexity of trait interactions and the uniqueness of each person [#397].
5. Emergent Far End (Модель "порога возникновения", "threshold of emergence"). Autism or ADHD manifests only when a certain threshold concentration of traits is reached. Example: if difficulties in sensory processing, attention and social adaptation intensify to a critical mass, a clinical picture forms [#397].

Four themes the post raises, as listed by the channel when sharing it; the channel describes Yana's post as the result of reading a huge number of studies and articles and singling out the most relevant points [#396]:
1. From categories to a continuum. The move from strict diagnostic categories to understanding neurodivergence as a spectrum or continuum, which allows a wider range of presentations and individual features to be taken into account rather than forcing people into or out of a box [#396].
2. Overlapping traits. The complexity and multidimensionality of neurodiversity: different traits can overlap and reinforce each other, creating a unique combination in each person [#396].
3. Norm vs. pathology. The problem of drawing the line between "norm" and "pathology" in the context of neurodiversity — a line that directly affects who gets diagnosed and how help is provided [#396].
4. Support without a diagnosis. The need to support people whose features make life difficult even if they do not meet all the criteria for an official diagnosis, focusing on the person's real needs and not only on diagnostic criteria [#396].

#### Double empathy problem (Damian Milton) versus mind-blindness theory (Simon Baron-Cohen)
This is Part 1 (introduction) of the channel author's own article series, which she says she compiled over a month from many sources with her own translation of the theory [#75]. Guiding questions: is it only autistic people who struggle to understand neurotypicals, and can empathy across the spectrum boundary be a *mutual* problem?

1. The mind-blindness theory. In the mid-1980s the prominent researcher Simon Baron-Cohen claimed that autistic people have «умственной слепотой» (mind-blindness) — a fundamentally impaired theory of mind. Theory of mind is defined as the ability to represent other people's beliefs, emotions, desires and intentions, and to understand and predict their behaviour [#75].
2. Its reach. The concept is still taught in many psychology textbooks and is supported by more than 75% of the top scholarly articles on theory of mind and autism in Google Scholar. It also guides work in sociology, philosophy, economics, anthropology and robotics. Through this lens Baron-Cohen and followers, until recent years, explained autistic "impairments" in empathy, verbal and non-verbal communication and communication in general — even though the very concept of autism, its components and diagnostic criteria changed enormously over those 40 years [#75].
3. Autistic pushback. Since the 1990s autistic activists such as Jim Sinclair argued that their ways of communicating do not mesh easily with neurotypical ones [#75].
4. Milton's theory (2012). Autistic researcher Damian Milton published the essay "On the ontological status of autism: the 'double empathy problem'", proposing that communication breakdowns between NT and autistic people are a two-sided problem — a difficulty of *mutual* understanding («взаимо_понимания») [#75].
5. What later research showed. This became a turning point. Subsequent work showed that most autistic people fit into groups without problems, communicate effectively, show social reciprocity, and empathise sensitively and accurately — with other autistic people. Understanding and empathising was difficult only between members of different groups (on and off the spectrum) [#75].
6. Implications. These findings shake the usual foundation and remove the *sole* responsibility for unsuccessful social contact from autistic people. They also echo the neurodiversity principle that autistic people generally have a different approach to information at every stage — gathering, processing, storing and exchanging it [#75].
7. Insider grounding. Milton stresses that the theory corresponds to «аутистической феноменологии, полученной из инсайдерских источников» (autistic phenomenology obtained from insider sources) — it reflects the personal experience and perception of autistic people [#75].
8. Status of the evidence. Scientific support is growing but not yet stable, and as of 2024 the leading classifications, ICD and DSM, still follow the earlier reference points in their criteria. M. Lerner (Stony Brook University) is quoted: «Не все исследователи настроены на это новое направление» (Not all researchers are on board with this new direction) and the double empathy problem is, empirically, a younger theory [#75].

#### Perspective reversal: "Why Johnny Doesn't Flap: NT is OK!"
Purpose: a perspective-taking exercise. The channel author says she likes the idea of relativity — how much one's view can change if one imagines oneself as precisely as possible in another person's place, with their experience, ideas, values and thinking algorithms. She believes this approach makes any choice or action of that person appear understandable and justified, and thus lets one do something constructive with it instead of blindly condemning it or considering it "bad", "unpredictably dangerous" or "alien" («чужаком») [#65].

The tool for this is the 2015 children's book by Clay and Gail Morton (illustrated by Alex Merry), whose title card reads "Why Johnny Doesn't Flap: NT is OK!". The author notes that some sketches may be dated but most still seem valuable, and she shared a Russian translation she found online [#65, img #65].

The book is narrated by an autistic child describing his neurotypical friend Johnny. Mum explains that Johnny is NT — he doesn't have autism, so his brain works differently — and that this is fine. Each vignette describes ordinary neurotypical behaviour as an autistic person might perceive it, ending with the refrain "and that's okay" [#66]:
1. Punctuality. Johnny is due at 4:00 but sometimes arrives at 3:58 or 4:03. The narrator gave him a watch for his birthday, yet "he still has this problem"; despite his "terrible punctuality" they have fun [#66].
2. Order of activities. Sometimes Johnny wants checkers then cards, sometimes cards then checkers — «Он не умеет сохранять одинаковый порядок каждый раз» (He can't keep the same order every time) [#66].
3. Eye contact. Johnny looks you straight in the eyes when talking, which can be very uncomfortable — but he doesn't do it to spite you, he's just like that [#66].
4. Scripting. Johnny watches the same cartoons but never repeats the intro word for word («никогда не повторят [sic] вступление дословно»); the narrator isn't sure Johnny even memorised it — "he pays attention to strange things" [#66].
5. Expressing joy. When something wonderful happens, Johnny doesn't flap his hands or jump; he just stretches the corners of his mouth and opens his eyes a little wider. "Maybe he doesn't know how to show emotions" [#66].
6. Special interests. Johnny has no topic he knows everything about (World War II, dinosaurs, trams); he seems uninterested in detailed information the narrator shares, and is unlikely to become an expert in anything — but he's a good guy [#66].
7. Uneven knowledge. Johnny functions well at school and follows rules, but asked simple facts — which company made the lift, the computer or the cameras — he stares blankly. He understands some things well and struggles with others; "but that's true of everyone" [#66].
8. Emotional reactions. Johnny never has a meltdown when something terrible happens, like a fire alarm or a cancelled art lesson. He is afraid of what others will think; the narrator thinks he locks his feelings inside, "but that's just how he copes" [#66].
9. Literal language. Johnny "has communication problems": he says the maths test was «проще пареной репы» (*simpler than a steamed turnip* — an idiom for "very easy") when he means it was easy, and says «Учитель метал гром и молнии» (The teacher was throwing thunder and lightning). The narrator tries to explain that turnips have nothing to do with a maths test, but Johnny doesn't seem to understand that one should say what one means; eventually the narrator decodes his strange phrases [#66].
10. Constant socialising. At recess Johnny always wants to play with other kids and never withdraws into his own world; the narrator wonders whether he ever has time to sit and think about his favourite advert or metro announcement — "maybe he's too fixated on socialising" [#66].
11. Conclusion. Being friends with an NT child is interesting; he has many quirks that can be annoying until you get used to them. Mum says everyone's brain is built differently and these differences are not bad. «Я думаю, что быть НТ – это нормально» (I think being NT is okay) [#66].

The lesson: every trait that clinical language describes as an autistic deficit (lack of eye contact, literal speech, rigid routines, intense interests, flapping) has a mirror image; from an autistic baseline, neurotypical norms (approximate timing, idioms, constant socialising, muted joy, suppressing distress for others' opinion) look equally odd — differences, not defects [#65, #66].

## Autistic identity, community and disability pride

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's material on autistic identity and self-acceptance: a first-person critique of the phrase "don't let autism define you", a newcomer's guide to the norms of the autistic community (self-diagnosis, functioning labels, the term "Asperger's", identity-first language, unmasking, diversity of autistic experience, "you are not broken"), the meaning of Disability Pride Month and its flag, and a "hexagon" of six core autistic needs. According to the sources, it matters because autistic people are often made to feel "not good enough" or "worse than others" in a neurotypical world, masking demonstrably harms mental health, and acceptance and pride are framed as a form of resistance and even a life-saving process [#338, #560, #562].

### Core ideas

- Autism is inseparable from the person. The forwarded authors (neurodivergent_lou, autisticvintage) say autism shapes how they perceive and experience the whole world; without it they would not be themselves. Hence the rejection of "don't let autism define you" and of "person with autism" (see *Why "don't let autism define you" is problematic* and norm 5 below) [#336, #559].
- Pride and acceptance are acts of resistance, not denial of real difficulties: autism brings both joys and real limitations, and being affected by it does not make anyone a failure or a bad person [#338, #339].
- Each autistic person's self-understanding is legitimate, including those who do feel a separation between "person and autism" or who are not yet at the point of pride [#340, #561].
- The community has explicit norms that a newcomer should know (7 norms, see framework) [#555].
- Disability is a natural part of human diversity; Disability Pride Month and its flag express this [#71].
- Autistic wellbeing rests on six needs (the 6C hexagon): sensory comfort, own pace, community, special interest, freedom, meaning [#772].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Things to know if you are new to the autistic community (7 norms)
A forwarded carousel by autisticvintage titled «Вещи, которые стоит знать, если вы новичок в аутичном сообществе» ("Things worth knowing if you are new to the autistic community") [#555]. It sets out seven community norms, each with its reasoning:

1. We accept self-diagnosis («Мы принимаем самодиагностику»). Reasoning: according to the author, the diagnostic criteria for autism were based on studying only white cisgender boys, so the way people are tested for autism reflects how autism feels for one demographic group only. Many professionals still hold a very stereotyped picture of what autism looks like, so anyone who is not a white cisgender boy — including women, non-binary people, people of colour, LGBT+ people — can find it very hard to get diagnosed. There are also financial barriers. Because of all these barriers, the community welcomes self-diagnosed people «с распростертыми объятиями» ("with open arms") [#556].
2. We do not use functioning labels («Мы не используем функциональные ярлыки»). The terms "high-functioning" and "low-functioning", and also autism levels 1, 2 and 3, are described as «просто нейротипичные конструкции, используемые для оправдания поддержки или отказа в поддержке аутичным людям» ("simply neurotypical constructs used to justify granting or denying support to autistic people"). Reasoning: autism is a spectrum, there is no "low" or "high"; one person can struggle in one area and excel in another; and needs often change daily depending on many external factors that affect the capacity to cope with the world. The whole idea is called mistaken because "high-functioning" does not mean superiority or that the person needs no help, and "low-functioning" does not mean the person is incapable or worse than others [#557].
3. The term "Asperger's" ("Aspie") is largely rejected in the community. The author gives "some of many weighty reasons": (a) Hans Asperger, after whom the syndrome was named, was a Nazi; (b) it is no longer a diagnosis under DSM-5 — what used to be classified as Asperger syndrome is now simply part of autism spectrum disorder (ASD, РАС); (c) it is itself a functioning label (see norm 2) [#558].
4. Most of the community prefers "Autistic" over "Person with autism". Despite what specialists teach, most autistic people prefer to identify as autistic rather than as "people with autism". If you are autistic and prefer "person with autism" for yourself, you have every right to use it and ask others to use it for you; when speaking about others, the default is "autistic", because that is what the majority prefers. For most, "person with autism" can be offensive, because «Наш аутизм является неотъемлемой частью того, кто мы есть, без него мы бы не были собой» ("Our autism is an inseparable part of who we are; without it we would not be ourselves") [#559]. 5. Learning to unmask is a hard but necessary process. Definitions: masking is hiding autistic traits to appear neurotypical; unmasking is learning to accept your autistic traits rather than suppress them. Why it matters: it is a process of self-acceptance, and moreover, the author states, it has been proven that masking raises stress levels, harms mental health, leads to autistic burnout and even raises suicide risk — so learning to unmask «может быть буквально спасающим жизнь процессом» ("can be a literally life-saving process") [#560].
6. There is no right or wrong way to be autistic. The author is proud to be autistic and comfortable with her diagnosis, but says it is fine if you do not feel that yet. Social media shows many people who have radically accepted their autism; she hopes every autistic person will one day feel the same, and it is okay if you are not at that point of your journey yet. Every autistic person's path differs, and so does their attitude to it: how they stim, how they cope with difficulties or achieve success, what housing suits them, whether they can work or attend school — all of this varies from person to person, and no autistic experience is more valid or more correct than another [#561].
7. You are not broken. Some people will try to convince you that being autistic means being broken, damaged or somehow worse than others; the author calls this "incredibly far from the truth". Being autistic means nothing was ever wrong with you: the reason you were different was never a malfunction or defect. «Ваш мозг не сломан, он просто устроен по-другому» ("Your brain is not broken, it is just built differently"); there is nothing to "fix", because your brain works as it was meant to [#562].

#### Hexagon of autistic needs (6C)
Created by autisticResearch (forwarded from the channel «Об аутичных женщинах, по которым не скажешь», "About autistic women you wouldn't guess are autistic"), offered «Вместо мемов» ("instead of memes") as a «гексагон аутичных потребностей» ("hexagon of autistic needs") [#772]. The cover card places 6C аутичного человека (the 6 C's of an autistic person — all six needs begin with the Russian letter С) in the centre, with six sides: Сенсорный комфорт (sensory comfort), Свой темп (own pace), Сообщество (community), Специнтерес (special interest), Свобода (freedom), Смысл (meaning) [#772]. Each need is unpacked on its own card:

1. Сенсорный комфорт — Sensory comfort [#773]:
   - understanding and accepting your own (sensory) needs;
   - being able to control your space and stimuli — light, sound, smell, texture;
   - the right to take pauses.
2. Свой темп — Own pace [#774]:
   - extra time to process and respond;
   - a minimum of sudden switches;
   - allowing yourself to be slower than others.
3. Сообщество — Community [#775]:
   - a few people who are "your own" («своих») and places where you are accepted as you are;
   - the opportunity to take part in shared activities;
   - people you can turn to for help without shame or judgement.
4. Специнтерес — Special interest [#776]:
   - deep immersion in a favourite activity as a source of energy and competence;
   - dedicated windows in the schedule for it;
   - the opportunity to discuss it with someone interested and share findings and results.
5. Свобода — Freedom [#777]:
   - to be yourself without a mask: loud or quiet, slow or fast, sensitive and inattentive — «любым» ("any way at all");
   - to choose the format of communication, relationships, employment, environment, workload and tasks;
   - to stim when you want;
   - to ignore, refuse, say "no";
   - to contemplate — simply to be, without evaluations, results, achievements;
   - time alone: for rest, reflection, fantasies, inventing new ideas.
6. Смысл — Meaning [#778]:
   - doing what aligns with your values;
   - accepting your own clear goals instead of «так принято» ("that's how it's done");
   - looking for meaning if you want to, even if people say you are "overcomplicating everything" («всё усложняешь»).

#### Disability pride flag symbolism
From the channel author's (Настя) post on Disability Pride Month [#71]. Each coloured stripe has its own meaning:
1. Red — physical disability.
2. Gold — neurodivergence.
3. White — invisible disabilities and conditions that have not yet been diagnosed.
4. Blue — emotional and psychiatric disability, including mental illness, anxiety and depression.
5. Green — sensory disability, including deafness, blindness, lack of smell, lack of taste, auditory processing disorder and all other sensory impairments.

The faded black background symbolises mourning and anger for victims of ableist violence and abuse. The diagonal band cuts across the walls and barriers that separate disabled people from "normative" society, and also represents light and creativity breaking through the darkness [#71].

#### Goals of Disability Pride Month
Disability Pride Month is an annual event, usually marked in July, aimed at [#71]:
1. raising awareness of the rights and problems of disabled people;
2. celebrating the contribution of disabled people to society;
3. promoting the idea that disability is a natural part of human diversity;
4. fighting stigma and discrimination;
5. encouraging inclusivity and accessibility in society.

The month is meant to help disabled people be proud of themselves and their identity and to foster wider acceptance and understanding of disability. Disability pride, per the post, consists in accepting and respecting each person's uniqueness and perceiving it as a natural part of human diversity [#71].

### Detailed insights

#### Why "don't let autism define you" is problematic (neurodivergent_lou)
A forwarded carousel by neurodivergent_lou, introduced with: «Они говорят: «Не позволяй аутизму определять тебя», не понимая, что вся моя жизнь проходит через призму аутизма...» ("They say 'Don't let autism define you', not understanding that my whole life passes through the prism of autism...") [#334, img #334]. The author's argument, step by step:

- The phrase presupposes that autism is bad. It is said with the best intentions, but it seems to come from the idea that autism is something inherently bad or shameful. For the author, autism is more complex: in daily life it really does impose limitations, yet being autistic is something she celebrates («чествую») — «Без аутизма я бы не была собой» ("Without autism I wouldn't be myself") [#335].
- Autism shapes all perception. Her autism affects how she perceives and experiences the world; it defines her existence in it; she sees and interacts with the world through the prism of autism. It gives her incredible passions, the joy of stimming and intensity of emotions, but also brings sensory overload and communication difficulties. Sometimes this makes existing very hard, but she cannot separate any of it from who she is at her core [#336].
- Double standard with other identities. When she talks about other aspects of herself — being a friend, a sister — nobody tells her those things don't define her. She asks whether this is because those aspects are considered socially acceptable, and whether it is linked to society often associating autism with deficits [#337].
- The phrase pressures toward masking. Unintentionally, it sounds as if she should not use autism as an excuse (as others see it); it makes her feel she must suppress the traits that outwardly show her autism and not accept her autistic identity. «Но я устала подавлять и игнорировать эти аспекты себя» ("But I'm tired of suppressing and ignoring these aspects of myself") [#338].
- Pride as resistance. For her, accepting and being proud of her autism is «акт сопротивления в нейротипичном мире» ("an act of resistance in a neurotypical world"), where autistic people often feel "not good enough" or "worse than the rest" [#338].
- Being affected is not failure. The phrase also reads to her as a call not to let autism affect her ability to communicate, work or take care of herself. But autism really does affect all of these, and that does not make her a "failure" or a "bad person" [#339].
- Caveat — other views are valid. Many existing narratives assume a separation between "the person and their autism", and if an autistic person feels it that way, that is completely normal. Every autistic person will have their own perception of what it means to be autistic, and that perception can change over time [#340].
