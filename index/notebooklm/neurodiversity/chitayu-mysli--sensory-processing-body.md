## Building and using your sensory profile

### What this covers

How the channel author (an autistic psychologist writing about autism and ADHD, #РАС) explains the sensory profile: its three response types (hyper-, hypo-, mixed), the sensory systems it covers (including the less obvious interoception and temperature), a method for mapping your own profile, and concrete, system-by-system adaptations for interoception, vision, touch and smell. It also covers sensory seeking, mixed sensitivity, the extra costs of living with these features ("autism tax") and a reframing of so-called "VSD" symptoms. According to the author, the sensory profile is one of the B criteria for diagnosing ASD and strongly affects wellbeing and quality of life; knowing your own parameters lets you regulate them (e.g. in summer heat) instead of just suffering [#475, #198].

### Core ideas

- A sensory profile is an individual map of hyper-, hypo- and mixed reactions across all perception systems, not only light, smells and sounds. See *Sensory profile components* and *The seven sensory systems* [#475, #480].
- Sensory features are near-universal in ASD: the author cites research that over 90% of people with ASD have sensory particularities [#475, #673].
- You can and should map your profile yourself — observe for a week, use questionnaires, rate each system in a table, look for patterns, then optimize environment, planning and strategies (see *Four-step method*) [#480].
- Hyposensitivity is as important as hypersensitivity. It underlies sensory seeking, self-regulation and safety (not noticing pain, hunger, cold, injuries) and must be actively "topped up", not ignored [#669, #562].
- Mixed sensitivity is the most common profile, and it varies by system, within a modality and by state (overload → hyper, exhaustion → hypo); adaptation must therefore be individual, "by needs, not by diagnosis" [#673, #565].
- Sensory features cost extra effort and money — the "autism tax" [#507].
- Many bodily "VSD" symptoms in autistic people are really manifestations of anxiety, overload, interoception problems or shutdown/meltdown, requiring regulation work rather than "VSD treatment" [#702].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Sensory profile components (hyper / hypo / mixed)
The author defines the sensory profile as «индивидуальная схема восприятия сенсорных стимулов» (an individual scheme of perceiving sensory stimuli) made of three aspects [#475]:
1. Hypersensitivity (гиперчувствительность) — an acute reaction to stimuli that seem insignificant to most people, for example loud sounds or bright light [#475].
2. Hyposensitivity (гипочувствительность) — an insufficient reaction, because of which the person needs more stimulation to notice a stimulus [#475].
3. Mixed reactions (смешанные реакции) — a combination of hypo- and hypersensitivity depending on the type of stimulus (expanded in *Mixed sensitivity* below) [#475].

Sensory sensitivity shows up as heightened or lowered reaction to sounds, light, smells, textures and other stimuli; researchers study it to understand how it shapes behaviour, perception and quality of life in ASD [#475].

#### The seven sensory systems of the profile
"Roughly speaking", the author says, the sensory profile consists of our perception systems [#480]:
1. Visual system — light, colours, visual movement.
2. Auditory system — loudness of sounds, tonality.
3. Touch (осязание) — textures, temperature, touch.
4. Smell and taste — odours and taste sensations.
5. Proprioception — the sense of your body in space.
6. Vestibular system — balance, the sense of movement.
7. Interoception — sensations of your own body: hunger, thirst, fatigue. [#480]

Two refinements from other posts: temperature sensitivity is, strictly speaking, one of the sensory systems in its own right; it can be lowered or heightened, and it can concern heat, cold or both [#198]. And touch usually includes pain and pressure, since all those receptors sit in the skin — but a person can have different sensitivity levels for touch, pain and pressure separately [#560].

#### Four-step method to compile your sensory profile
Purpose: to understand your own features system by system; the author proposed reading the principles, then running a "week" of different sensory systems with observations shared in comments, and recommends making yourself a table and studying each system separately [#480].
1. Observe yourself for a week. Notice which stimuli cause discomfort and which, on the contrary, bring satisfaction. Example questions: Which sounds are pleasant to you and which irritate you? Do you like soft or hard textures in clothing? How do you react to bright light or dim lighting? Write the observations in a diary so that patterns stand out [#480].
2. Use ready-made questionnaires. Use special questionnaires for assessing the sensory profile, e.g. the *Sensory Profile Questionnaire* (W. Dunn), and questionnaires adapted for children and adults with ASD [#480].
3. Rate each sensory area. Create a table with categories (vision, hearing, touch and the other systems listed above — the source writes «и т.д.») and mark how strongly each stimulus evokes positive or negative emotions in you [#480].
4. Look for patterns. Once the table is done, note which stimuli most often cause you inconvenience. These can be zones of hypersensitivity (loud sounds, strong smells) or zones of hyposensitivity (you barely react to certain stimuli, for example cold) [#480].

#### Three ways to use sensory-profile data
What to do with the results [#480]:
1. Optimize the environment — create a comfortable space, e.g. use dimmed lighting or noise-cancelling headphones.
2. Plan activities — take sensory preferences into account when planning the day, e.g. avoid crowded places if you are hypersensitive to noise.
3. Sensory strategies — develop self-regulation methods such as breathing exercises, sensory toys or rest breaks. [#480]

#### Three effective support strategies (from the research overview)
Because understanding a person's sensory profile is important for individualized therapy and support, the author lists effective strategies [#475]:
1. Sensory diets (сенсорные диеты) — selecting specific sensory activities to improve self-regulation.
2. Providing sensory safety — creating a comfortable environment, e.g. noise-cancelling headphones.
3. Sensory integration therapy — working with an occupational therapist (эрготерапевт) to improve processing of sensory stimuli. [#475]

#### Interoception — signs and adaptations
The author chose to start the self-study week with interoception as "the most difficult and unclear" system, "because it affects the receptivity of everything else". Definition: «Это понимание сигналов своего тела, эмоций и ощущений» (It is understanding the signals of your body, emotions and sensations) [#482].

Signs of interoceptive HYPERsensitivity [#482]:
1. You feel hunger and fatigue too vividly.
2. Any bodily discomfort is "hell" and is felt very sharply right away.

Signs of interoceptive HYPOsensitivity [#482]:
1. You poorly understand what you feel and what your body feels.
2. Fatigue, hunger and the urge to use the toilet seem to appear "suddenly" — when you can no longer hold out, or when you already feel marked discomfort and/or pain.
3. You need more time and/or information to understand your sensations.

Author's own example: she has interoceptive hyposensitivity — she can go a long time without eating until she starts to feel slightly nauseous or her stomach hurts, and does not realize she is tired until she gets a headache [#482].

What to do with HYPERsensitivity [#493]:
1. Use extra communication and sometimes warn people in advance that it is hard for you to tolerate hunger, cold and similar states.
2. Create visual prompts for yourself reminding you that if something feels very uncomfortable, you are not dying — it is just your body's heightened sensitivity.
3. Where possible, reduce the level of discomfort: always carry snacks, find out where the toilets are along your route.
4. Plan frequent breaks to recover.
5. Learn self-regulation skills. [#493]

What to do with HYPOsensitivity [#493]:
1. Also plan breaks — forced ones, "even if you don't want to, even if you aren't tired".
2. Schedule meals by timetable and alarm clock, even small portions and even if you are not hungry.
3. Use mindfulness skills to increase contact with and understanding of your sensations "at least a little".
4. Think through ways to avoid dehydration. [#493]

#### Vision — signs and adaptations
The author notes that people most often complain about bright light, especially white, but visual features have many more manifestations [#499].

Signs of visual HYPERsensitivity [#499]:
1. Light irritates — especially white, especially bright, especially in the evening.
2. It is much easier to focus on details.
3. Lots of different patterns, textures, details and colours around you irritate.
4. Bright light can cause up to a feeling of pain in the eye.
5. Rapid fatigue in the bright time of day and/or in summer.

Signs of visual HYPOsensitivity [#499]:
1. Distance to objects is not read very well.
2. It is hard to search for objects; you don't notice them.
3. Difficulty describing differences between pictures and objects.
4. Low lighting irritates.
5. Rapid fatigue in the dark.

What to do with visual HYPOsensitivity [#503]:
1. Put all objects — especially those you need — in visible places; deliberately design a storage system.
2. Look at how you light your space, especially the workplace: use several light sources, possibly differing in intensity and colour temperature.
3. Again: a storage system — less clutter makes it easier to find things.
4. Use brighter colours around you; "don't be afraid to stimulate yourself with this".
5. You need large bright windows, plus extra walks outside — "никаких темных плотных штор!" (no dark heavy curtains!). [#503]

What to do with visual HYPERsensitivity [#503]:
1. Reduce lighting brightness, especially white and/or fluorescent light.
2. Minimalism — both in interior (the author calls a beige interior "top") and in the number of things lying in open space.
3. Fewer patterns, different textures and colours around you.
4. Instead of overhead lighting — soft, warm, low lighting from several sources, so you can combine them for different needs.
5. Blackout curtains are the best choice.
6. Sunglasses: buy several pairs with different tint levels for different situations. «Ходить в метро в очках - нормально!» (Wearing glasses in the metro is normal!). Stash them in all your bags if you tend to forget them. People who wear prescription glasses can order photochromic ("chameleon") lenses. [#503]

#### Touch — signs and adaptations
Touch usually includes pain and pressure (all skin receptors), but sensitivity to each can differ in one person [#560].

Signs of tactile HYPOsensitivity [#560]:
1. High pain threshold.
2. Preference for tight, close-fitting clothing.
3. Dense plaids, heavy blankets, several blankets have a good effect.
4. Preference for lots of strong hugs and contact with close people in general.
5. Liking to touch things and to contact different textures.
6. Liking massage and contact interactions.

Signs of tactile HYPERsensitivity [#560]:
1. All tactile sensations irritate (combing hair, long hair, hairstyles).
2. Only certain textures and fabrics can be worn.
3. Clothing tags irritate.
4. Hard to wear anything that presses, e.g. tights.
5. Water can be irritating.
6. Contact with many textures is tiring.
7. Low pain threshold.
8. Everything heavy is tiring.
9. Wanting to avoid unnecessary contact with other people.

The author describes herself as apparently mixed in touch: high pain threshold and love of everything heavy (hypo), but tags and various textures are "just hell" (hyper) [#560].

What to do — the author first reminds that this must be looked at individually, especially with mixed manifestations [#565].

With tactile HYPOsensitivity [#565]:
1. Load yourself with different textures, especially ones you like; assemble a little box of different pleasant toys.
2. Find activities/hobbies where you touch something pleasant.
3. Buy a weighted blanket, a weighted scarf ("yes, these exist too"), or simply wrap yourself tighter in a plaid.
4. Ask partners, close people and friends to make tactile contact with you more often and to hug you tightly.
5. Go for massages and to activities where you feel your body; you can train with wrist/ankle weights.
6. Choose denser, close-fitting clothing.

With tactile HYPERsensitivity [#565]:
1. No wool, synthetics or prickly materials.
2. No tags or pronounced seams.
3. If hair and hairstyles irritate, cut your hair shorter, to the length at which it stops irritating.
4. Look for suitable seamless underwear.
5. Look for suitable bed linen.
6. Try to have less contact with textures you find unpleasant.
7. Choose light oversized clothing.
8. Pick a light, pleasant blanket and pillow.
9. Stock up on clothes you are definitely comfortable in, in several copies.
10. Audit everything you touch that might make you avoid things or get tired. [#565]

The author's own must-haves (a mixed profile): for hypersensitivity — giving up towels in favour of a pleasant, wide waffle bathrobe, only disposable cotton face wipes, specific bed linen, no tags; her curly hair causes the most trouble and she has not yet found an ideal solution. For hyposensitivity — she adores clay, has stocked up on hand putties («жвачками для рук», *hand gums*), looks for soft-touch items, touches everything, sleeps under a heavy blanket or two, and carries her "legendary" travel plaid for flights. She stopped wearing skinny jeans entirely; she is sometimes drawn to body-hugging dresses but never wears them for long during the day [#565].

#### Five ways to protect yourself from smells
For olfactory hypersensitivity in ASD, prompted by a "life hack" video the author came across (she sets aside its deodorant part) [#302]:
1. Vaseline under the nose — the most accessible and simple method. Buy vaseline and apply it under the nose before going out or whenever something stinks; it protects from odours. If you have a favourite scent you are willing to smell all the time, do as in the video: melt the vaseline and drip in essential oil or perfume.
2. A mask — a mask also removes part of the smells.
3. Scent beads on the mask — as an add-on, you can buy scented capsules on AliExpress (like those in cigarettes) and crush one on the mask before putting it on.
4. Pathologists' ointment — "elusive, but said to be great"; applied under the nose the same way. (!) UNCLEAR: the source does not name the product.
5. Little jars of perfume / aromatic oils you like, to carry with you. [#302]

Rule: for "super-protection" — first apply something under the nose, then put a mask on top [#302].

#### Sensory seeking — examples and five reasons it matters
Definition: «Сенсорный сик (от англ. sensory seeking) — это активный поиск определённых ощущений» (Sensory seeking is the active search for particular sensations). A person with ASD may literally crave sounds, touch, movement, smells in order to feel alive, engaged, collected. «Это не каприз и не привычка — это нейрофизиологическая потребность нервной системы» (It is not a whim or a habit — it is a neurophysiological need of the nervous system). The author notes that hypersensitivity ("too loud", "bright light hurts", "can't stand touch") is what people usually hear about, while hyposensitivity and seeking stay in the shadow — wrongly [#669].

Examples of sensory seeking [#669]:
1. Rocking, spinning, jumping.
2. Seeking tactile sensations: stroking, fidgeting with, feeling things.
3. Listening to loud music, repetitive sounds.
4. Chewing inedible things, pressure on the jaw.
5. Strong hugs or hitting the body.
6. All the examples already discussed under lowered sensitivity (see the hypo lists above).

Why hyposensitivity and seeking matter [#669]:
1. The basis of self-regulation. Without the needed stimuli a person may be in a "sensory pit" (сенсорная яма): sluggish, irritable, unable to start an activity. Seeking helps them switch on and pull themselves together.
2. It affects behaviour. Much "incomprehensible behaviour" is an attempt to get needed sensations; ignoring it means fighting symptoms rather than the cause.
3. A safety issue. With hyposensitivity a person may not feel pain, hunger or cold and may not notice injuries.
4. Key to adapting the environment. Understanding what a person seeks lets you organize sensory breaks, use sensory stimuli as motivation, and create a suitable space for rest and engagement.
5. Improves quality of life. Seeking can and should be taken into account in education, therapy and daily life; this improves wellbeing, reduces anxiety, helps focus and "be yourself". [#669]

Takeaway: «Гипочувствительность — не «ничего страшного». Она так же значима, как и гиперчувствительность.» (Hyposensitivity is not "nothing to worry about". It is just as significant as hypersensitivity.) and sensory seeking is "not an oddity but an important path to balance and comfort" [#669].

#### Mixed sensitivity — three forms, why it matters, how to support yourself
Many people think a person with ASD is either hypersensitive or hyposensitive, but in practice the picture is more complex and for most autistic people it is mixed [#673].

Three forms of mixed sensitivity in one person [#673]:
1. Across different systems — e.g. hypersensitivity to sounds and hyposensitivity to pain.
2. Within the same modality — e.g. panic at loud sounds yet not noticing quiet speech or rustling.
3. Depending on context and state — in overload a person may become more hypersensitive, and when exhausted, on the contrary, drop into the "sensory pit".

Hearing example: a reaction of horror or pain to a vacuum cleaner, siren or slammed door (hyper), and at the same time not noticing being called from across the room, or a habit of always playing loud music (hypo). «*Это не противоречие* — это отражение нестабильной нейросенсорной обработки, когда мозг то перегружается, то «проваливается».» (This is not a contradiction — it reflects unstable neurosensory processing, when the brain now overloads, now "falls through".) [#673]

Why it matters [#673]:
1. You cannot draw conclusions from a single reaction: if a person doesn't react to your voice, it doesn't mean they don't suffer from noise on public transport.
2. Adaptation must be flexible and personalized, especially in schools, clinics and offices.
3. It explains behavioural "paradoxes" — when the same stimulus at different times provokes opposite reactions.

How to support yourself [#673]:
1. Keep a sensory diary — note reactions and changes.
2. Make sensory adaptations according to needs, not according to the diagnosis.
3. Learn self-regulation skills (breathing, grounding, sensory breaks).
4. Use sensory profiling that accounts for both hyper- and hypo-fragments.

Closing line: «Смешанная чувствительность — это не "ошибка системы", а её сложность.» (Mixed sensitivity is not a "system error" but its complexity.) The deeper we understand this, the fewer "oddities" and the more compassion — especially toward oneself [#673].

#### Four sources of "VSD"-like autonomic symptoms
Answering a reader's question, the author states that VSD (vegetative-vascular dystonia) is an outdated, unscientific diagnosis not used in international classifications. Autistic people do not have a separate "VSD" condition, but may have autonomic symptoms linked to [#702]:
1. Sensory overload.
2. Anxiety and panic attacks.
3. Interoception difficulties (reading body signals).
4. Dysregulation of the autonomic nervous system (especially heightened sympathetic activity).

What gets called "VSD" — tachycardia, tremor, dizziness, a feeling of not getting enough air — is in fact often a bodily manifestation of anxiety, overload or shutdown/meltdown within autism. This calls not for "treating VSD" but for work on regulation, the sensory environment and psycho-emotional support [#702].

### Detailed insights

#### What research says about the sensory profile (author's overview)
The author's research summary, in four points [#475]:
1. Neurophysiological mechanisms. Sensory disturbances in children with ASD may be one of the main problems hindering their social adaptation and integration; studies show deviations in the filtering and processing of sensory information in children with ASD.
2. Impact on daily life. Sensory features often lead to difficulties in social interaction, learning and adapting to the environment. Examples: hypersensitivity to sounds can cause fear of public places; hyposensitivity to pain — increased risk of injury. Understanding the profile is important for individualized therapy and support.
3. Age-related changes. The sensory profile can change with age. In children with ASD sensory features are more often more pronounced than in adults, but in some people they persist for life. Early identification and correction can improve adaptation and quality of life.
4. Gender differences. Studies show sensory features may differ in men and women with ASD; women more often show hyposensitivity and compensatory strategies in daily life, which underlines the need to account for gender in therapy approaches. [#475]

The author frames sensory features in ASD as «не просто симптомы, а важная часть индивидуальности человека» (not just symptoms but an important part of the person's individuality); deeper study of the profile helps understand the inner world of autistic people and build a more inclusive environment [#475].

#### Temperature sensitivity and heat
Everyone knows heatstroke rules (hats, drink more water), but bodies of neurodivergent people can react differently to heat, because temperature sensitivity is itself a sensory system that can be lowered or raised, and can concern heat, cold or both. The author tolerates both heat and cold badly, heat worse: after a day outdoors in the shade at 38 °C on holiday she felt "as if I had been unloading train wagons" rather than doing nothing at the sea, and spent the next day in her room sleeping until evening. Conclusion: it is very important to know your sensory profile parameters not only for light, smells and sounds but also your reaction to temperature, so you can regulate it, especially in summer [#198].

#### Weighted blankets and tactile "topping up"
With lowered tactile sensitivity the sense needs extra loading (донагружать): tighter clothing, heavy blankets, wrapping tightly in a plaid. The author adds a mechanism: increased tactile stimulation can activate our safety system, so these methods help lower the sense of threat and relax. Pressure-touch is read by the brain as hugs, releasing oxytocin and other hormones that help the body understand that it is safe [#562].

Her experience as a case lesson [#562]:
- She always sleeps under a blanket, even in summer (a sheet alone feels cold and makes relaxing impossible), often under two blankets just for more weight.
- Mistake: too heavy. Years ago she and her husband bought a double weighted blanket of 13 kg. She liked sleeping under it, but it was objectively very heavy — sometimes it felt like it was "flattening" her — and very hard to move on and off the bed without her husband's help. It ended up unused in a basket by the bed.
- Second attempt: 6 kg. Because anxiety and the need for tactile loading remained, she tried a 6 kg single weighted blanket — "a much more pleasant weight" under which she really relaxed, slept more deeply, and had fewer nightmares lately. She valued its smooth Tencel fabric, which does not irritate the skin; the combination of pleasant fabric and weight gives a hug effect that adds to relaxation.
- Construction claim (from a sponsored post): the brand says its blanket has 11 layers versus 5–7 in cheap analogues, which distributes the weighting filler as evenly as possible so it does not roll into a corner, so the blanket presses evenly and gently on the whole body. This is advertiser material, not independent evidence.
- Who it's for, per the author: people with hyposensitivity to touch/weight, high anxiety, stress and trouble relaxing; she "highly recommends" the practice of sleeping under a weighted blanket.



#### The "autism tax" (налог на РАС)
By analogy with the term "ADHD tax", the author argues ASD also involves extra costs in both energy and money. Her example: she needs sport, but training alone is unrealistic (ADHD), group classes are very hard — many people, fast fatigue and overload, arms and legs get mixed up (she repeatedly does an exercise wrong because she can't work out "arm one way, leg the other"), little benefit, plus the stress of being touched by a near-stranger. So she can only train individually, with a person vetted over time. Other items in the "autism tax": buying only certain clothes, searching for suitable earplugs, and suitable food. (Context: she was training despite a week of migraine and overwork because "behavioural activation won't do itself".) [#507]

### Evidence and numbers

- Over 90% of people with ASD have certain sensory features (research cited by the author; "up to 90%" in the later post) [#475, #673].
- About 45–65% of autistic adults describe having hypo- and hypersensitivity simultaneously (Robertson & Simmons, 2015; Tavassoli et al., 2014, as cited by the author). Mixed sensitivity most often appears in the auditory, tactile and vestibular modalities; during overload the hyper part dominates, during exhaustion the hypo part [#673].
- Weighted blanket weights from the author's experience: 13 kg double — too heavy; 6 kg — comfortable [#562].

## Stimming, special interests and repetitive behaviours

### What this covers

Three posts by the channel author on autistic repetitive behaviour and focus: what stimming is and what it is for (answering a subscriber question about whether masturbation can be a stim), how to decide whether hair pulling or skin picking is OCD or autistic stimming by looking at the behaviour's function, and what distinguishes a special interest from an ordinary hobby and from a hyperfixation. According to the author, the distinctions matter practically: stimming is a regulation tool that is costly to suppress, and for neurodivergent people a special interest is "не просто хобби, а ресурс" (not just a hobby but a resource) [#624, #626, #704].

### Core ideas

- Stimming is defined by repetition, not by form. Any repeated movement or action can be a stim — muttering sounds, rocking, fiddling with objects, even masturbating. See *Three functions of stimming* [#624].
- Using stimming is usually cheaper than suppressing it. Stimming can be controlled, but only at a high energy cost, so if it breaks no social norms and helps with stress, the author holds it is easier to use than to suppress [#624].
- Hair pulling and skin picking are classified by function. The same visible behaviour can be OCD or autistic stimming; what decides it is what the behaviour does for the person — see *Function test* [#626].
- A special interest is deep, sustained and regulating, distinct from a hobby (less emotionally charged, part of everyday routine) and from a hyperfixation (a flash, intense but unstable) [#704].
- A special interest is a resource for neurodivergent people — for recovery, meaning, connection, career and stability [#704].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Three functions of stimming
Purpose: to understand why a person stims and so whether the stim is serving them. The author defines stimming as "повторяющееся поведение, которое выражается в определенных движениях или действиях" (repetitive behaviour expressed in particular movements or actions) and concludes "поведение может быть любым" (the behaviour can be anything). Her examples: making sounds under one's breath ("буркать под нос какие-то звуки"), rocking, fiddling/sorting through something ("перебирать что-то"), and even masturbating — which answers the subscriber's question "Может ли быть стиммингом мастурбация?" (Can masturbation be stimming?) in the affirmative [#624].

Stimming has several functions:
1. Relaxation and lowering discomfort — "расслабление и снижение уровня дискомфорта (особенно при стрессе и напряжении)" (relaxation and reducing the level of discomfort, especially under stress and tension) [#624].
2. Help with concentration — "помощь с концентрацией внимания" (help with focusing attention) [#624].
3. Getting pleasure — "получение удовольствия" (obtaining pleasure) [#624].

How it feels and what to do with it: from the inside stimming is felt "как навязчивая потребность что-то сделать" (as an obsessive/insistent need to do something). It can be controlled, but this takes "очень много энергии" (a great deal of energy). Hence the author's rule: if a stim does not violate social norms (her example caveat: "не будем мастурбировать на публике" — we won't masturbate in public) and helps cope with stress, it is easier to use it than to try to suppress it [#624].

#### Function test - OCD vs stimming for hair pulling and skin picking
Purpose: answering the question "Трихотилломанию можно рассматривать как форму стимминга при РАС ?" (Can trichotillomania be considered a form of stimming in ASD?). The author says behaviour involving pulling out hair, picking at skin and similar must be investigated carefully, because it can be either a form of OCD or stimming in ASD. The method is to establish the function of the behaviour ("выясняем функцию поведения"):

1. Anxiety-only, cyclical pattern → most likely OCD. If the behaviour happens at the peak of anxiety ("на высоте тревоги"), lowers it in the short term, and then repeats cyclically time after time, it is most likely OCD. The author hedges that it can still look like stimming, so the assessment is long and laborious ("долго и муторно обследуем" — we examine at length and tediously) [#626].
2. Broader functions → most likely stimming. If the behaviour has not only the function of relieving anxiety but also relaxation, getting pleasure and improving concentration (i.e. the functions listed in *Three functions of stimming*), it is most likely stimming [#626].

Caveat on the classification itself: with excoriation (skin-picking) disorder and trichotillomania "всё очень пока не очевидно" (everything is still very unclear); some researchers and scientists propose classing them as addictions, since many people experience pleasant sensations and pleasure from the behaviour [#626].

#### Six markers of a special interest
Definition: a special interest is "не просто интерес" (not just an interest) but "интенсивный, глубокий и устойчивый фокус, который занимает большое место в жизни человека" (an intense, deep and sustained focus that takes up a large place in a person's life). It need not be lifelong, but within its period of life it is something that truly absorbs you ("прямо поглощает вас"). It can be about anything, from broad to narrow; the author's examples: dynasties of China, a specific music album, types of tea, disease classification systems, a particular character [#704].

How to tell it is specifically a special interest:
1. Calm and belonging, not just joy — the topic brings not merely happiness but a sense of peace, "«я на своём месте»" ("I am in my place") [#704].
2. Depth and system — the interest is often deeper, and organised more systematically, than other people's; one wants not just to know but to really understand/work it out ("хочется не просто знать, а разбираться") [#704].
3. Need to share and organise — a need arises to share, to talk about it, to put the knowledge in order [#704].
4. Intertwined with identity — it can become part of who the person is [#704].
5. Regulation — it often works as a way of regulating oneself: it calms, centres, stabilises [#704].
6. No practical goal needed — it does not always have a "practical" purpose; the interest exists for its own sake [#704].

#### Special interest vs hobby vs hyperfixation
- Ordinary hobby: more often built into everyday life and does not always carry a strong emotional charge. A special interest is "чуть ближе к жизни" (a bit closer to life): it is not just "liked" but woven into thinking, speech and lifestyle [#704].
- Hyperfixation: often a flash — sudden, powerful, with sharpened attention, but unstable. A special interest is long-lasting and rhythmic, sometimes with breaks but with a return to it; it is about depth more than peak intensity. The author notes that often, especially in people with ASD/ADHD, a hyperfixation arises on the basis of a special interest, lasts some time and then gets replaced [#704].

#### Five ways a special interest works as a resource
Why distinguish it at all: because for neurodivergent people a special interest is not just a hobby but a resource. It can:
1. help a person recover/restore their energy ("помогать восстанавливаться") [#704];
2. give a sense of meaning [#704];
3. be a way of communicating with others [#704];
4. turn into a profession or a mission [#704];
5. simply be "«тем, что держит»" ("the thing that holds you together / keeps you going") [#704].

The post was prompted by subscribers sharing their special interests the previous day, which raised the question of how hobby and hyperfixation differ from a special interest [#704].

## Physical comorbidities: sleep, pain, migraine and eating

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel author's posts and answers to readers on the bodily problems that often come with autism (РАС) and ADHD (СДВГ): pain felt in dreams and nightmares (and its link to PTSD), migraine, chronic muscle pain (sensory overload, hypermobility, fibromyalgia), connective tissue dysplasia, sleep problems in autistic adults, daytime sleepiness, ADHD sweet cravings and overeating, and sensory eating difficulties, including the author's own trial of medical nutrition drinks. The author presents these problems as common among neurodivergent people and often unnoticed by them ("могут о ней не догадываться" — they may not suspect it), so recognising them and getting to the right doctor matters [#543, #185, #472].

Much of the material is the author's own experience (nightmares with pain, migraine attacks, torn ligaments, being unable to eat during burnout) combined with her reading of research; she is open that research on several of these questions is thin or inconclusive [#8, #315].

### Core ideas

- Pain in nightmares is linked to PTSD. Nightmares with physical pain are most common in PTSD; normally pain is dulled in sleep; dream pain comes from the dream's content, not a real stimulus [#8].
- Migraine is much more common in autistic adults (42% vs 10%), especially autistic women, and autistic people, nonverbal ones in particular, struggle to recognise and describe its symptoms. Migraine is a chronic condition that needs a neurologist [#185].
- Chronic muscle pain is common among neurodivergent people, but nobody knows the exact cause. The author sorts it into three variants and advises tracking muscle pain as a sign that you are tired and need to recover [#315].
- Connective tissue dysplasia may be linked to autism and mental disorders. A reader's research summary and a 2022 study frame ASD partly as a connective-tissue problem [#543].
- Autistic sleep problems have three sources: a brain that won't rest at night, late melatonin, and sensory discomfort in bed. Usual advice doesn't work; regular hours, personal trial-and-error and a doctor do [#472].
- Daytime sleepiness in ASD/ADHD comes from high fatigability and from raised rates of sleep disorders [#541].
- ADHD sweet cravings have two different causes (the brain's need for glucose vs impulsive dopamine-driven eating), and each needs a different response [#607].
- Eating difficulties in ASD/ADHD can be sensory overload, not an eating disorder; when tired or overloaded the author can only tolerate soft, dense, mild-tasting food. Self-knowledge of one's sensory profile is lifelong [#838].
- Medical nutrition drinks can carry you through bad days when nothing else goes down [#839, #862].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Four findings on pain in dreams
The author woke up feeling physical pain after being killed in a nightmare, and searched for why we feel physical pain in sleep. She says she has been "замучали кошмары, особенно с физическими ощущениями" (worn out by nightmares, especially ones with physical sensations) and that not much research has been done on this [#8]. What she found:
1. Pain nightmares are most frequent in PTSD. She calls this the most obvious finding: nightmares with physical pain occur most often in PTSD [#8].
2. Normally pain is dulled during sleep. This dulling gives relief to people with chronic pain [#8].
3. Dream pain has a different source from "real" pain. Its source is the content of the dream, not a stimulus from real life. She adds that pain is mostly processed by the brain, not at the site of the injury, which makes dream pain logical [#8].
4. What happens in the brain. Nightmares are associated with loss of grey-matter volume in anterior limbic/paralimbic structures that are sensitive to PTSD, and with functional changes in areas involved in controlling REM sleep. In her reading this confirms a link between PTSD and disturbed sleep [#8].
Her conclusion, half-joking: "похоже придется и дальше просыпаться от того, что тебя убили во сне" (it seems I'll have to keep waking up from being killed in my sleep), meaning the findings explain the phenomenon but offer no fix [#8].

#### Six signs of migraine in autistic people
Purpose: people with ASD, especially nonverbal people, often find it hard to understand and put migraine symptoms into words, so others (and they themselves) can watch for these behavioural signs [#185]:
1. Changes in eating habits [#185].
2. Nausea or vomiting [#185].
3. A sudden need to be in a dark place (photophobia, светобоязнь) [#185].
4. A sudden need to be in a quiet place (phonophobia, фонофобия) [#185].
5. Repeated touching of or hitting the head [#185].
6. Eye movements or problems that point to an aura: tunnel vision, blind spots or coloured spots [#185].
Next step: migraine is a chronic condition that needs treatment by a neurologist. The author urges readers bothered by headaches not to be afraid to go to a цефалголог (headache specialist) and get checked for migraine [#185].

#### Three variants of chronic muscle pain associated with neurodivergence
Background: after asking her readers, the author was surprised how many neurodivergent people have chronic muscle pain. Her research gave two results: there are many such people, and nobody yet knows exactly what it is linked to [#315]. The variants:
1. Pain from sensory overload. Her proposed mechanism, marked by herself with a question mark: you get very tired from sensory load, brain and body become markedly fatigued, and pain receptors are activated. She says this isn't fully understood and she will keep looking [#315].
2. Hypermobility syndrome. Hypermobility itself puts a large load on the muscles, which causes pain. Here she cites Asztély et al.: 77% of neurodivergent women had chronic pain, and fibromyalgia was described in almost a quarter of people with ASD [#315].
3. Fibromyalgia. Not everyone has it (she gives "всего 2-3%", only 2–3%). The criteria are fairly strict: pain must last at least 3 months in a row. She also found a lot of data on people who complain of similar symptoms that do not fully meet fibromyalgia criteria, and it is unclear where to classify them [#315].
Practical takeaway: whatever the cause, track your muscle pain as a symptom that you are tired and urgently need to recover [#315].

#### Three problems of autistic sleep and three things to do
From the post "Сон, аутизм и ода ночным совам" (Sleep, autism and an ode to night owls). The author compares sleep for an autistic adult to a trip to IKEA: you go in for one thing and come out three hours later, tired, but without a bed. Her point is that the problem is not choosing the wrong pillow [#472].

The problems:
1. "Ночь — это моя зона комфорта" (Night is my comfort zone). Many autistic adults complain that at night the brain doesn't rest but throws a party: new ideas, things to think about, an awkward situation from 7 years ago. You lie there counting the moments when you could have said something smarter instead of counting sheep. She says advice like "drink warm milk" or "read boring books" doesn't work here [#472].
2. "Свет, камера, мелатонин!" (Lights, camera, melatonin!). In many autistic people the brain "doesn't like" melatonin. The sleep hormone arrives late, so autistic people only start yawning when others are already asleep. Melatonin tablets are unreliable for some ("то сработает, то нет", sometimes it works, sometimes not), but she says recent meta-analyses show the problem really is melatonin [#472].
3. Sensory hell. For many autistic people "Which pillow do you like?" sounds like a provocation: the pillow is too hard, the blanket too hot, the bedding rustles too much. Night becomes a test drive of fabrics, temperatures and positions, and the sound of a fridge in the next room becomes the main villain of the night [#472].

What to do:
1. A regular schedule helps, but… Regular sleep times do help. The caveat is that life gets in the way: you plan to go to bed at 11 and end up watching videos about odd octopus facts until 3 a.m. [#472].
2. Keep trying until you find your own remedy. Some are helped by white noise (the author personally loves it), some by a warm shower before bed, some by reading the tax code. The key is to find what specifically "switches you off" [#472].
3. See a doctor, sometimes a sleep specialist (сомнолог) directly. The problem might have a simpler solution: for example, polysomnography can reveal sleep apnea, and that may turn out to be the cause [#472].
Closing advice: if insomnia seriously troubles you, go to a doctor to find out what it is actually linked to, and try melatonin [#472].

#### Causes of daytime sleepiness in ASD/ADHD
A reader's question: a support group found that many members have attacks of daytime sleepiness; is ASD to blame, and is there a link between ASD/ADHD and hypersomnia? The author's answer lists possible causes [#541]:
1. High fatigability. Many people with ASD/ADHD tire very easily, so they may badly want to sleep during the day (and not only then; her favourite: "встал и уже устал", got up and already tired) [#541].
2. Sleep disorders. Neurodivergence is often accompanied by sleep disturbances, so there is a raised likelihood of narcolepsy, or of other sleep disorders (apnea, insomnia, restless legs syndrome) that lower the quality of night sleep, so the person is sleepier in the day [#541].

#### Two-cause method for ADHD sweet cravings and overeating
A reader asks what a person with ADHD can do to stop overeating and snacking, since the brain sometimes demands sweets and fighting it is very hard. The author jokes "nothing", then gives a method: work out the cause of the overeating in each specific case, then respond to that cause [#607].
1. Cause 1: the brain needs glucose. A strong sweet craving may mean you are under heavy mental load right now and the brain is asking for glucose. Her reasoning: neurons are almost the only cells in the body that can't build up a glucose reserve, so they only have glucose from the blood; without glucose there is no energy and the cells suffer and die. Response: eat, and eat more than just sweets, so the brain gets enough nutrition [#607].
2. Cause 2: impulsive overeating. People with ADHD are prone to impulsive overeating because (a) food gives fast dopamine reinforcement, and (b) food is always a clear, pleasant activity when you are bored or procrastinating. Response: the STOP skill (навык СТОП) helps a lot to stop and switch to other activities, along with ADHD skills for coping with procrastination [#607].
(!) INCOMPLETE: the steps of the STOP skill and the specific ADHD anti-procrastination skills are not explained in this message [#607].

#### Sensory-overload eating difficulty (self-observed pattern and two conclusions)
The author's own case. When she was first considering that she might be autistic, Maria asked whether she had any difficulties with food, and she could only think of lifelong inability to eat mushrooms (because of the texture) [#838].

In fact she had nearly always had trouble accepting food, and years of therapy didn't fix it. Two things were going on: (a) the classic pattern of poorly sensing hunger and, ADHD-style, forgetting to eat; and (b) something she couldn't explain: simply being unable to eat. She stresses she never had an eating disorder (РПП) and likes a variety of foods, just not the food she herself put in the fridge yesterday or that morning. She could walk hungry around the fridge and be unable to make herself eat [#838].

The vicious circle she describes: you are hungry and don't eat → you start to feel nauseous → eating becomes even harder → you are even hungrier [#838].

The insight: as soon as she is tired or overloaded, she can't tolerate any food texture except something moderately soft and dense with a mild taste; for her that is soft curd snacks (творожки) and bananas. She identifies this as sensory overload plus tactile-gustatory hypersensitivity, and says it turned out to be "банально просто" (banally simple) [#838].

Her two conclusions:
1. You may think you already understand your sensory profile, but because of masking or reduced interoception you may still be missing some overload moments. Exploring yourself is truly lifelong [#838].
2. Food problems are very common in ASD and ADHD, but can be so unobvious that people are even diagnosed with and treated for an eating disorder. The topic needs to be explored very gently if there are difficulties [#838].
Open end: her next plan is to work out what else she can eat, since you can't live on bananas for long [#838].

### Detailed insights

#### Migraine and autism: prevalence
According to the latest research and statistics the author cites, migraine is much more common in autistic adults (42%) than in the general population (10%). As in the general population, women are three times more likely than men to have migraine, so autistic women are especially prone to it. She wrote the post during a severe migraine attack that "paralysed" her for almost the whole day [#185].

#### Connective tissue dysplasia and neurodivergence
A reader wrote that about ten years ago she and her daughter were diagnosed with undifferentiated connective tissue dysplasia (ДСТ), which at its most basic is a disorder of collagen synthesis. She remembered reading that people with ДСТ are more prone to mental disorders than the population average, and she noticed people with both ASD and ДСТ in the chat, although people usually don't know they have the diagnosis. Her search found [#543]:
- Two Russian-language papers, both in paediatrics. In one, children from a psychoneurological clinic (ПНД) were counted by disorder to see what percentage also had confirmed ДСТ; the total came to 51% of all of them [#543].
- The other Russian paper studied how ДСТ and ASD are specifically linked [#543].
- One English paper that tried to diagnose ASD from anomalies in lung structure [#543].
- All the papers recommended that paediatricians who suspect or confirm ДСТ also refer the child to neurologists and psychiatrists. The papers are relatively recent, from the 2020s [#543].

The author calls this a problem neurodivergent people often face without suspecting it, and links it to her chronic muscle pain post (see *Three variants of chronic muscle pain*). She says she has suspected ДСТ in herself since medical school: all her life she has torn ligaments, and in 7th grade one cervical vertebra slipped off the second when she just reached for a towel. She also mentions a 2022 study whose authors suggest looking at ASD as partly a connective-tissue disorder, with consequences for brain function [#543].

#### Nutrition mixes for people who can barely eat
During a flare-up of autistic burnout the author almost stopped eating altogether, and readers wrote to her about low body weight from problems with food and water. In despair she tried an option Maria (whom she calls an excellent specialist in eating problems) had recommended [#839].

What "неешки" (non-eaters) can use:
- Protein. The obvious first option, but she personally can't stand (1) the aftertaste of protein and (2) a smoothie-like texture, which is also why kefir doesn't work for her. She jokes that bananas with animal protein would solve her problem [#839].
- Medical nutrition mixes (liquid and dry), used for people with eating disorders, cancer and food problems in general. Most often these are Nutrilak products, but there are others. She ordered ones with a cooling ("с холодком") flavour, which appealed to her because they are made for people whose taste perception is impaired [#839].

Result of the test: she liked Nutridrink overall, especially the version for people with heightened or distorted taste perception. For days when nothing goes down and the body feels bad, she calls it a really good option, and keeps a small stock in the fridge for bad days. The photo shows the product: Nutricia Nutridrink Compact Protein, in a cool red-fruit/berry flavour (packaging text in Scandinavian languages, e.g. "Cool red frugtsmag") [#862, img #862].
