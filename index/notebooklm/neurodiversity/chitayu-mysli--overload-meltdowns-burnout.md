## Meltdowns, shutdowns and acute overload

### What this covers

This topic gathers the author's posts on the acute end of neurodivergent overload: what a meltdown and a shutdown are, how they look in adults (often unlike the "classic" child picture), why acute stress lasts longer in autistic, ADHD and traumatised people, and the "empty head" state. It also contains her practical prevention kits — for going to a new place, for emotional overload from films/books, and for airports and flights. According to the author, recognising these states matters because what relatives call "hysterics", laziness or weakness is often an overload reaction with neurophysiological causes [#84, #688, #689].

### Core ideas

- Overload resolves either as a meltdown or a shutdown. When a person with ASD gets overloaded (sensory, emotional, by the number of people; the source leaves this list open-ended), it can pour out as a breakdown (meltdown) or a stupor (shutdown) [#281].
- Adults, especially women with high intelligence, look different. Meltdowns and shutdowns in adults — especially level-1 support and broader phenotype — can differ greatly from how they "classically" look in children [#84, #281].
- Neurodivergent nervous systems digest acute stress more slowly and need different recovery tempos; this is not weakness or manipulation [#688].
- An empty head is a signal, not a defect — "Это не надо чинить, это надо заметить" (This doesn't need fixing, it needs noticing) [#689].
- Unpredictability is a core trigger, which is why previewing places, knowing spoilers and preparing scenarios help [#221, #337].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### What a meltdown is and why it happens
1. Definition. A meltdown is basically a "срыв" (breakdown) in autism spectrum disorder [#84].
2. Mechanism (author's explanation). In ASD, brain networks are, in her words, much smaller and less well differentiated than in other people, so arousal is often more active and chaotic. Added to this are heightened sensitivity to certain sensory stimuli and fast fatigue from emotions, other people, communication and similar. All this causes a strong overload of the nervous system and leads to the breakdown [#84].
3. How it looks. The "classic" image shown of autism — children who hit themselves or others, scream and cannot stop — are indeed meltdown manifestations, but meltdowns can be far less obvious, "Особенно у взрослых, особенно с сохранным и высоким интеллектом, особенно у женщин" (Especially in adults, especially with preserved and high intelligence, especially in women) [#84].
4. How to detect it in practice. The author says one often has to ask directly "а у вас бывают истерики"? (do you have tantrums/hysterics?). She hates the term but everyone understands it in everyday terms. What relatives call hysterics may be a meltdown: outbursts of irritability, aggression, crying, self-harm, throwing things. Her conclusion: "человек не "истеричка", а просто у него перегрузки" (the person isn't a "hysteric", they simply have overloads) [#84].

#### Non-obvious signs of a shutdown
Purpose: recognise a shutdown in adults when it doesn't look like the textbook description [#281].
- Textbook definition: freezing, or an unbearable desire/attempt to leave the current uncomfortable situation. The author says life is a bit more complex [#281].
- Frozen-phone analogy: imagine your phone has hung — it either opens the app you need very slowly, or opens some things and not others, or hangs on one screen entirely. The same happens to a person with ASD: in reality it looks and feels like loss of part of one's functions [#281].
- Concrete signs: (1) you want to say something and physically cannot; (2) someone speaks to you, you hear them but don't understand what is said, or don't understand the text in front of your eyes; (3) a persistent desire to turn around and leave, up to pronounced physical discomfort [#281].
- Author's personal trigger: it happens to her every time she stands before a person behind a counter or "glass", especially at control points or borders — and regardless of the language spoken; "на русском я буду тупить так же" (in Russian I'll be just as slow) [#281].

#### Preparing to go to a new place (hypercontrol kit)
Problem: people with ASD often happily go to a place they like and know well, but a new place can cause a real stupor, up to being unable to leave the house at all. The author's counterintuitive remedy is "гиперконтроль" (hypercontrol) [#221].
1. Preview the place on maps. Look at how it looks on Google Maps, view pictures or street photos; with Yandex or Google Maps you can view practically the whole route as panorama. This can lower anxiety and tension from meeting the new [#221].
2. Read/watch reviews of the place online — especially good if it is a video review [#221].
3. Carry a touchable safety object — something you associate with safety: a keychain, fidget, toy, anything; use it when you feel discomfort [#221].
4. Pre-play alternative scenarios. If you fear things will go off-plan, disappoint you and lead to a shutdown/meltdown, think through different variants of events and prepare "a little" for each; this usually reduces the frustration from the mismatch with reality [#221].

#### Acute stress in neurodivergent people
What acute stress is (author): not just "worried and let go" but an inner siren that suddenly switches on and drowns out everything — body, attention, ability to think, sometimes literally. It is a physiological reaction to a threat (or what the brain judged a threat): the sympathetic nervous system starts, hormones are released, then "бей, беги, замри" (fight, flee, freeze) — or go silent, repent, or blame; it differs between people [#688].

What changes with neurodivergence (4 items):
1. Autism: the ability for fast sensory adaptation is often impaired → the brain stays overloaded longer [#688].
2. ADHD: the self-regulation system can be "дырявой" (leaky) → harder to bring oneself back to balance [#688].
3. Chronic traumatisation (including complex PTSD): acute stress almost always activates the past rather than the present [#688].
4. Hypersensitivity to stimuli: the depth and duration of the reaction increase [#688].

How it can look (5 items): loss of speech for 10 minutes; trembling, tears, a feeling of "no air"; feeling that you have "fallen apart"; obsessive rumination over the situation; inability to return to tasks even the next day [#688].

Framing: it is not weakness and not "overacting" or manipulating — it is pronounced stress that a neurodivergent organism cannot digest as fast as a neurotypical one [#688].

What can help (5 items):
1. Remove stimuli — light, noise, speech, people [#688].
2. Anchor yourself in the body [#688].
3. Feel something simple and real [#688].
4. If you have strength, name it aloud: "я сейчас в реакции стресса" (I'm in a stress reaction right now) [#688].
5. If not, simply let yourself lie down and recover, without demands or explanations [#688].

The author's stance: neurodivergent people don't have to "cope faster"; they truly live load differently and truly need different recovery tempos [#688].

#### Four causes of an empty head and what helps
The state: sometimes the author and many neurodivergent people have literally nothing in their heads — no thoughts, ideas, directions; "Как будто внутренний экран погас" (As if the inner screen went dark). No access to resources, creativity, focus — even to desires and needs. She says it is much more common in ASD and ADHD than assumed and has neurophysiological causes; it is not "bad mood", weak character or laziness [#689].

What may be behind it:
1. Sensory and emotional overload — after difficult events, communication, trips, decisions the brain goes into a protective "energy-saving mode" [#689].
2. Task switching in ADHD — a context change can leave a "hang" where the old task is done and the new one isn't loaded; "Это не прокрастинация — это реальный «глюк переключения»" (This isn't procrastination — it's a real "switching glitch") [#689].
3. Neuro-vegetative rollback — after long tension, the self-regulation system can't immediately switch back on, like after an adrenaline wave [#689].
4. Autistic burnout or shutdown — after holding on "in a mask" for a long time, trying to be understandable, social, organised — and suddenly you can't do anything, even think [#689].

Key rule: notice it, don't fix it. Emptiness is sometimes the brain's way of saying: "я исчерпал ресурс. Мне нужен покой, а не новая цель." (I've exhausted my resource. I need rest, not a new goal.) [#689]

What can help (4 items):
1. Simple sensory stimuli — warmth, a scent, a texture, a favourite drink [#689].
2. Safe silence [#689].
3. Breathing — without trying to "catch your breath", just in the background [#689].
4. A notice to yourself: "это временно. это не про меня как личность. это просто пауза" (this is temporary. it's not about me as a person. it's just a pause) [#689].

The author adds that the neurodivergent brain often works in spurts, non-linearly, and "emptiness" is part of the process even if hard to accept [#689].

#### Airport and flight meltdown kit (7 items)
Context: the author had a meltdown in Phuket airport — not her first airport meltdown but the first this strong — crying while standing in the check-in queue. She notes it is easier to know it's a meltdown than to think you're just a strange adult woman crying in a queue, but it still feels very vulnerable because you can't leave the queue. She shares that she has to apply the skills herself [#801].
1. Headphones — she never enters an airport without them; ideally in direct reach, e.g. hanging on the neck to put on instantly [#801].
2. Comfortable clothing, ideally with a big hood to pull over yourself as far as possible and narrow the visual field [#801].
3. Something for stimming to hold, also in direct reach — great if it can hang right on the backpack. This time she took nothing and regretted it [#801].
4. Eye mask / dark glasses — can help a lot, especially the mask once inside the plane [#801].
5. Something to distract yourself — e.g. writing a post (as she did), a book, a film [#801].
6. Food — if you know a food that calms you, take it in advance or find it at the airport; she asked her husband to buy her hot chocolate [#801].
7. Maximum isolation on boarding — combine everything above: hood on head, mask on eyes, headphones on top. She carries a super-soft, warm travel blanket everywhere and wraps herself in it first thing; "Таскать его бесит, но он помогает всегда!" (Lugging it around is infuriating, but it always helps!) [#801].

#### Extra travel tips for unfamiliar places (4 items)
1. Look at the airport/station plan in advance [#801].
2. Delegate tracking the process and choosing queues to a companion, if you have one [#801].
3. Pack a bag/cosmetic pouch with all the kit items above straight away [#801].
4. Half-joking: don't fly through Phuket airport — seriously, in her experience airports in China were more organised and less crowded, and lacked the many, in her words, "мало вменяемых соотечественников, которые лезут везде без очереди" (barely sane compatriots who cut in line everywhere) [#801].

### Detailed insights

#### Emotional overload from films, series and books
The author realised (after discussing it with her husband) where emotional overload from films/series/books comes from: they often contain unpredictable plot twists that you don't know even if you guess, and the reaction is to unpredictability — like a "sharp change of plans". That is why on a 2nd, 3rd or 4th viewing/reading the overload is gone, and also when you know the main plot spoilers; she sometimes uses this (not deliberately) when she has roughly understood what will happen in a book and then reads it calmly with pleasure. Her advice: if you worry and have ASD, "используйте маленькие спойлеры" (use small spoilers) [#337].

## Autistic and ADHD burnout, fatigue and limited energy

### What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel teaches about chronic fatigue and burnout in autistic (РАС/ASD) and ADHD (СДВГ) people: the mechanisms that make them tire faster, the definition and course of autistic burnout and how to tell it from depression, a forwarded model of the ADHD work-burnout cycle with supporting studies, the author's candid account of living close to burnout herself, a concrete ChatGPT setup she used to offload executive functions while burnt out, and her advice to count energy instead of tasks. The source frames this as important because constant exhaustion is "the most frequent complaint" of neurodivergent people and is often misdiagnosed, sometimes leading to inappropriate psychiatric treatment [#88, #155].

### Core ideas

- Neurodivergent people have a smaller "battery" and a larger nervous-system load, so tiring more than others is normal, not a failing; they are people with special needs who tire faster and need more rest (see *Why neurodivergent people tire faster*) [#80, #88].
- Fatigue is not laziness or poor organisation: resources are objectively smaller, so the question is not "how to get everything done" but "what drains me most" [#805].
- Autistic burnout is a distinct state, not depression: it follows months of overload, involves temporary skill loss (especially communication), takes months to recover from, and medication helps little — so prevention matters (see *Autistic burnout*) [#155, #617].
- ADHD burnout at work follows a repeating cycle driven by dopamine-seeking novelty, praise, then depletion, shame and procrastination; executive-function deficits are the key mediator (see *The ADHD work-burnout cycle*) [#765].
- There is no magic way to be neurodivergent, super-productive by neurotypical standards and feel well — the author herself has not solved this and openly frames it as an unresolved dilemma between suffering from non-fulfilment and suffering from constant overload [#420, #770, #793].
- Plan by energy, not by to-do count; one big finished task beats five small unfinished ones [#805].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Why neurodivergent people tire faster
Purpose: explain the chronic exhaustion that neurodivergent people describe, so it is not misattributed. The author says constant, exhausting tiredness is the most common complaint you hear from neurodivergent people, and it leads to "a mass of interesting diagnoses" — ВСД, asthenia, vitamin deficiency, even depression — "whatever imagination allows" [#88].

1. ADHD: constant switching between distraction and concentration. With ADHD the brain keeps going "distracted – concentrates – distracted – concentrates". What costs other people a small amount of energy and actions is "huge work" for a person with ADHD — "literally". The author's image: you are not simply walking from point A to point B, but on the way you visit 30 more points far off the direct route, and not even in order [#88].
2. Autism: processing a flood of high-intensity sensory information. With ASD the brain simultaneously processes a mass of information that is highly intense for it: every touch of clothing on the skin, light reflecting off snow, the rustle of a bag, body and ambient temperature. If on top of that you have to communicate with people, the load on the brain multiplies instantly [#88].
3. Result: tired without visibly doing anything. From the outside you seem not to be doing much, yet you get exhausted "как собака сутулая" (like a hunched dog — a jokey idiom for being dead tired). The accompanying image says "Я ОЧЕНЬ УСТАЛ ПОСТОЯННО УСТАВАТЬ ОТ СВОЕЙ УСТАЛОСТИ" (I am very tired of constantly being tired of my tiredness) [#88, img #88].
4. Conclusion: treat it as a special need. It is important to remember that neurodivergent people are people with special needs: they tire faster, need more rest, and also need their non-obvious needs to be met [#88].

The author repeats the same point in #805: neurodivergent people spend a great deal of energy on sensory signals and task switching, which is why even cleaning is "a whole big task" [#805]. In #80 she phrases it as: the neurodivergent "battery" is often much smaller than other people's, and the load on the nervous system much greater, so tiring more than others is normal [#80].

#### Autistic burnout — definition, course and differential from depression
Purpose: recognise autistic burnout and avoid confusing it with depression.

1. Status of the term. There is not yet an official rubric for it in the classifications; the term was singled out fairly recently, but there is already research and an understanding that this state differs strongly from other rubrics and cannot be assigned to them [#155]. In April 2025 the author added that over the last 5 years about 40 studies on it have appeared — she checked PubMed "literally yesterday" [#617].
2. Definition and trigger. It is a state that develops after prolonged — usually several months of — overstrain and overload in an autistic person [#155].
3. Symptoms. Not just apathy and fatigue: the person may suddenly and temporarily lose skills, especially communication skills. Example: any communication — even the idea of it — causes strong aversion, crying, despair, up to losing the physical ability to speak [#155].
4. Key difference from depression. It is often mistaken for a depressive episode. The main differentiating feature is the absence of a persistently lowered mood for most of the day over more than 14 days [#617].
5. Why the confusion matters. The author has seen people hospitalised in psychiatric hospitals during such a state, where "all the schemes in the world" were tried and did not work, because pills cannot help much in this case [#155].
6. Recovery and prevention. Getting out of autistic burnout is not easy and takes time — again, usually several months — so it is very important to work on preventing it [#155].

Related: a reader asked about "ВСД" under extreme stress or sensory load in autistic people (she had a year of accumulated stress plus a series of sad events). The author answered that if this means "внутренний сенсорный дисбаланс" (internal sensory imbalance), then (1) it is not a separate condition but an exacerbation of the person's ASD sensory profile against the background of stress, and (2) most likely the person has autistic burnout and must deal with that, not only with the sensory side. In general this means removing load as much as possible, especially social load, and minimising sensory load [#701].

#### The ADHD work-burnout cycle (5 stages)
Source: a post forwarded from the channel NeuroDive | РАС и СДВГ. Purpose: explain why people with ADHD frequently burn out and change jobs, and whether this is an ADHD feature rather than a general "zoomer" trend — the post answers that it is indeed a tendency seen in people with ADHD [#765].

1. Pleasant novelty period = a sharp dopamine release. The person dives very actively into the new job, gets to know the team, is inspired by the tasks and often takes on far more load than needed and than they can handle (e.g. answering work chats at night). Why: at the least because they more easily catch hyperfixation on something new and interesting [#765].
2. Reinforcement of the behaviour. The person starts being praised for initiative, responsibility and the other "-ities" the company values so much. This gives new portions of dopamine [#765].
3. Motivation drop, errors and anxiety. As soon as the energy runs out (quite quickly in this mode), lateness, forgetting and careless mistakes begin, and anxiety rises sharply. For a while the person holds on through shame: they were just praised, now they are letting people down, and they "should be coping!" [#765].
4. Procrastination. It keeps growing; staying late in the evenings and working weekends become more frequent; less and less time is left for rest. Dopamine is already at zero, and forcing oneself to work and concentrate gets ever harder [#765].
5. Burnout. At some point everything starts breaking — both the tasks and the person — and shame and anxiety rise sharply. After a while this leads to burnout and disappointment in oneself / the job / the profession [#765].

After the cycle: then comes recovery — though more often just "eating/drinking the problem away" to hide it deeper, hoping it passes on its own — and the vicious circle closes. Many people with ADHD go through more than one such cycle. The end result: a constant feeling that one must be better, faster, stronger so as not to let others down, with the background "favourite records" of "high anxiety and shame" and "perfectionism is my middle name", which feed a hyperfocus on approval, a desire for quick success and unstable self-esteem [#765].

Takeaways stated by the post:
- Constantly maintaining the image of a good employee and overworking demand super-efforts from executive function, which in many people with ADHD is weakened to begin with [#765].
- Sensory sensitivity — especially if high — can "eat up" remaining resources, forcing a person to compensate by any means even when the body "can't pull it" anymore [#765].
- Burnout in people with ADHD requires constant prevention; one must pay very close attention to the quantity and quality of rest, which many people with ADHD find hard as it is [#765].

#### ChatGPT project setup for offloading executive function during burnout (5 steps)
Context: in May 2025 the author was hit by autistic burnout plus a pile of health problems; the number of things she *could* do fell to a minimum while the number she *had* to do did not decrease, which she found very hard to bear. In the first week she switched off all personal chats for a week and cut all contacts except work ones; from the next week she brought everything back, but replying to people and entering communication, especially non-work, remained hard. In this situation she "discovered the potential of AI" — half a year earlier a friend had laughed at her for not using AI, and she did not understand why she would need it [#661].

What she started using it for [#661]:
- tracking completion of tasks against a to-do list, and messaging it whenever she remembers something on the go;
- asking it to track her load level and assess it from two sides: work efficiency and time management, and her personal state;
- keeping track of food, because executive functions are at zero, so understanding what to eat and remembering it is a problem.

Setup — done in several steps inside a separate project, so the shared information is processed together [#662]:
1. Load the medical context. Feed it all current lab tests, describe your state, diagnoses (the source closes this list with "и т.д.") [#662].
2. Describe the current load. Describe all current workload, what tasks you have and what needs to be done [#662].
3. Evaluation chat with a strict two-role prompt. In a separate chat enter the prompt: "я буду загружать тебе и описывать дела в течение дня, которые я делала, а ты должен максимально честно и строго оценивать с 2х позиций: управления, рабочей эффективности и тайм-менеджмента, и моего врача/психотерапевта на основе текущего самочувствия. После этого давать рекомендации по изменению нагрузки и планирования." (I will upload and describe the tasks I did during the day, and you must evaluate them as honestly and strictly as possible from 2 positions: management, work efficiency and time management, and my doctor/psychotherapist based on my current well-being. After that, give recommendations on changing load and planning.) [#662].
4. Task-capture chat. In another separate chat in the same project, give a simple prompt: you will send tasks as you go — ones to be done or already done — and it should systematise them and each time reply with an update in the form of a checklist [#662].
5. End-of-day handover. At the end of the day copy that checklist into the well-being evaluation chat, adding how you feel and how the day went [#662].

Verdict and notes: after two weeks of testing the author said she likes the experiment very much and "quite recommends" this use of the chat for people with similar difficulties. She deliberately tested GPT with tricky questions and restrictions so that it would not over-praise her, and it "seems to be holding up well". It also turned out it can "scold"; her friend joked that she had driven even GPT to that. Incidentally, her GPT speaks of itself in the feminine gender [#661, #662].

#### Three things to check first with ASD + ADHD + high fatigue
A reader with autism + ADHD and high fatigue asked what to look at first and how to help herself, since she literally cannot get things done — it is "either cleaning or work". The author answered that, first, look not at "how to get everything done" but at what exactly drains you most, because resources are objectively smaller and this is not a question of willpower [#805]. Points to note:
1. Fatigue is not laziness or bad organisation. Neurodivergent people spend a lot of energy on sensory signals, task switching (the source adds "и т.д.") — so cleaning is a whole big task [#805].
2. Sensory load often eats more energy than the tasks themselves [#805].
3. Multitasking with ADHD is an illusion. We can do only one thing at a time; the faster we switch back and forth, the more tired we get. Better one large task a day than five small ones left unfinished [#805].

Bottom line: "считаем количество своей энергии, а не дел, которые вы собирались сделать" (count the amount of your energy, not the tasks you planned to do) [#805].

### Detailed insights

#### The author's own experience of overload and near-burnout
- Year-end ADHD crunch (Dec 2023). "In the best ADHD traditions" she worked through the pre-New-Year rush: writing lectures until 3 a.m., consulting 12 hours a day, signing a mountain of documents and running job interviews — then lying face-down in the pillow while trying to clean the whole flat for New Year. Her lesson: be gentler to yourself and to each other, and remember that neurodivergent people's battery is smaller, so rest when you can [#80].
- "Balancing on the knife-edge of burnout" (Dec 2024). Asked by a commenter how she manages not to burn out, being so active and positive, she answered that for years she has been constantly balancing on the edge of burnout and cannot get into a more or less normal state. Concrete signs [#420]:
  - Her hours of individual appointments keep decreasing because she "simply can't cope".
  - In spring, seeing more than 100 new people in 2 months while opening a clinic nearly finished her off, and she wound that work down.
  - She had to hire an assistant to book appointments and answer booking questions, because she cannot handle that much even written communication.
  - When not working, she lies down; her therapist keeps working with her to reduce load and learn to rest.
  - Even sleep, always her stable support, fails her: either nightmares from working with PTSD, or her ADHD brain, overloaded, does not let her fall into deep sleep and keeps thinking.
  - Conclusion: there is no magic way to be a neurodivergent person, super-productive by the standards of neurotypical society, and feel good — so rest more and criticise yourself less.
- Autistic burnout (May 2025) — described in the ChatGPT section above: cutting personal chats for a week, then continued difficulty with non-work communication [#661].

#### Autistic people with active social lives and careers: energy on credit
Patients ask her how autistic people who communicate actively and build careers survive — how can they be so active? Her answer: the universal reply is that everyone is different, and some also have ADHD. But the result is always the same — severe fatigue, up to being unable to reply to messages. You can borrow strength from your body for communication and career, but it is always strength "on credit", and the loan later has to be repaid. She admits she has no answer for her readers or herself about what to do, especially if you want a career; her therapist expects her to solve it somehow, but "radical acceptance" has not happened yet. This came after she lectured on ADHD and burnout [#770].

#### Ableism toward oneself and the unresolved dilemma
For a very long time, in her personal therapy, she and her therapist have been trying to figure out how to normalise her load so she does not feel constantly tired; every week she also sees many clients with the same complaint. At every appointment she says "вам нужно подогнать по возможности условия среды под ваши особенности" (you need to adjust environmental conditions to your traits as far as possible), sincerely, knowing it helps — the problem is the "as far as possible": every neurodivergent person (like every neurotypical) has different possibilities, values, environment, profession [#793].

The open questions she poses: how to live this life fully without constantly feeling run over by a tractor; how to realise ambitions; how to earn a living often enough; how to have and raise children, especially without help. Her current view is that any choice produces suffering — either from non-fulfilment or from constant overexhaustion. This is how her therapist came to call it ableism toward herself and her neurodivergence. She firmly knows that to feel better you must accept your traits, but accepting yourself is one thing and the neurotypical world — often not kind even to neurotypicals — is another. She invites readers to share how they actually cope with living fully without dying of chronic fatigue [#793].

### Evidence and numbers

Cited in the forwarded NeuroDive post [#765]:
- One study found that executive-function deficits in employees with ADHD mediate the link between ADHD and occupational burnout — i.e. constant load on planning, self-control and organisation is the key factor of exhaustion.
- A review of adults with ADHD notes higher stress levels and a greater number of sick days; the average "excess" loss of working time is about 8.4 days a year compared with controls.
- The study "Sensory Over‑Responsivity as an Added Dimension in ADHD" found that 43% of women and 22% of men with ADHD have sensory hyper- or hyposensitive reactions — an additional "weight" on load.
- The recent article "Altered somatosensory processing in adult ADHD" showed adults with ADHD have increased tactile sensitivity and sensory overload, correlating with ADHD symptom severity.

From the author: about 40 studies on autistic burnout in the last 5 years (her PubMed check, April 2025); autistic burnout usually develops over several months and recovery usually takes several months; the depression criterion she contrasts it with is persistently lowered mood most of the day for more than 14 days [#617, #155].

## How neurodivergent people rest and recover

### What this covers

This topic gathers the author's teaching on rest for neurodivergent people (ADHD, autism, also depression and executive dysfunction): what counts as rest, the three kinds of rest and how to combine them, how to learn the hardest one (doing nothing), why everyday rest has to be planned and done "through I-don't-want-to", how to recover after social overload and holidays, how to return to work, the concept of resilience as flexibility and recovery, and the author's annual year-review ritual with a full workbook. The author argues that rest is "unfairly little talked about" and that people with neurodivergence are likely not able to rest well by default, so rest must be learned like a skill [#168, #174].

### Core ideas

- Neurodivergent people genuinely struggle to rest, and procrastination is not rest. Rest is defined by its goal — restoring strength — not by the absence of work [#168].
- Rest comes in three kinds that must be combined according to one's needs; the "resource" kind most people call rest is itself tiring and only works on top of the two basic kinds (see *Three kinds of rest*) [#168].
- Doing nothing is a skill learned in tiny increments and at first brings anxiety, guilt and shame (see *Graduated practice of doing nothing*) [#174].
- Everyday rest has to be planned, especially with ADHD, depression and executive dysfunction; planning clarifies values and does not kill spontaneity (see *Seasonal rest lists*) [#272].
- After intense socializing, recovery may require silence and solitude [#463].
- Return to work gradually after any long break and plan rest and self-reinforcement from day one [#470].
- Resilience means bending and recovering, not enduring; the Russian "устойчивость" carries a heroic "stand to the end" meaning that loses vulnerability, temporary setbacks and the need for rest [#817].
- A structured yearly review done with a close person helps "tame" ADHD and focus on what matters, including rest and recovery from burnout [#813, #814, #815].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Three kinds of rest
Purpose: to stop treating "not working" (or procrastination) as rest and to deliberately combine different kinds of rest according to current needs. The author frames this "conditionally" ("условно мы можем выделить") [#168].

1. State of stillness, or doing nothing (Состояние покоя или ничегонеделание). Lying still without sleeping. The author calls it "the hardest exercise if you are not asleep". How: learn from pets — watch how your cat lies and sluggishly looks out the window or simply dozes, and imitate that [#168]. How to train it is described in *Graduated practice of doing nothing* below.
2. Process activity (Процессуальная деятельность). Activity where "the samurai has no goal, only the path" ("у самурая нет цели, есть только путь"). Examples given: knitting, assembling a diamond mosaic, re-reading books you already know, walking a familiar route on "autopilot". The principle: motor activity works while the brain works minimally. Why: according to the author it helps switch on the brain areas responsible for rest and "unloading" the brain [#168].
3. Resource activity (Ресурсная активность). The things people often consider rest: going to potter about at the dacha, going hiking, visiting an exhibition, reading a difficult book, watching a new educational film. It gives moral strength, but the author stresses it is "rest after which you need more rest" ("это отдых, после которого нужен ещё отдых") and cannot be "natural". Order rule: first you need the previous two kinds; resource activity comes on top of them [#168].

The post ends: this is how we learn to relax, slow down ("тормозиться") and rest [#168].

#### Graduated practice of doing nothing
Written as a follow-up after many readers wrote about how hard "doing nothing" is [#174].
1. Expect discomfort. Learning it is really hard; at the start it can cause a lot of anxiety, guilt and even shame, because of the belief that one must be useful and effective non-stop [#174].
2. Start tiny, not with an hour. Do not try to begin with an hour or another long stretch. The author's comparison: that is like sitting down to meditate with Buddhist monks for a full day without ever having tried meditation; she adds that some people actually get psychoses going off to retreats ("это не шутка" — "this is not a joke"). Start with 30 seconds, 1 minute, 2 minutes, increasing gradually [#174].
3. Handle distraction gently. Do not scold yourself for getting distracted; simply and softly bring yourself back to the activity [#174].
4. Watch your pets rest. Observe how they rest — this is both a good mindfulness practice and a way to learn the skill [#174].
The attached meme shows the familiar paradox: a caption reads "ничего не сделал" (did nothing) all day, followed by "я так усталь" (*I'm so tired*, with a deliberate typo) [#174].

#### Seasonal rest and resource-activity lists
Purpose: to make sure rest and resource activity actually happen, since for everyone — and "especially" people with ADHD, depression and executive dysfunction — rest and resource activity have to be planned and done through "I don't want to" ("через "не хочу"") [#272].
- How: the author writes lists by season — a plan for summer, for autumn, for the winter holidays, continuing through the year [#272].
- Realistic expectation: she admits that far from everything gets done, but the lists give a feeling that life is not passing by and that she manages at least something that fills her life besides work [#272].
- Example: she caught "the last carriage of summer" with a friend at a beautiful picnic, and now has a warm memory that the summer contained such a picnic — even though that morning she did not want to go anywhere and felt "сдалось мне это всё" ("who needs all this"). Lesson: sometimes (or often) this is work for the long term [#272].
- Objection — "then it is no longer rest / where is the spontaneity?": the author calls this a trap. Making plans and a rough idea of what you want in a given period helps you understand yourself, your values and needs; *how* to carry them out is a separate question. The picnic itself happened spontaneously: "let's go on a picnic tomorrow, since you're on vacation, I have a daytime break between work, and summer isn't over yet" — which she calls maximal spontaneity that she and her friend (who is also autistic) keep training [#272].
- Conclusion: plan, plan and plan again everything you can [#272].

#### Gradual return to work after a long break
For anyone sad about having to start working after the holidays [#470]:
1. Ease in. The author says scientists advise returning to work after any long break gradually, not throwing yourself straight into a 10-hour workday where possible. Start with planning and prioritization — she stresses that this is also an important part of WORK [#470].
2. Schedule rest and rewards right away. Immediately plan rest/resource activities and how you will reinforce yourself; she reminds that self-reinforcement is "ОЧЕНЬ ОЧЕНЬ ВАЖНО" ("VERY VERY IMPORTANT") [#470].
3. Expect possible post-vacation blues. Coming out of a vacation can trigger an almost depressive state: lowered mood, wanting to cry, wanting to quit. It is usually called "post-vacation syndrome or blues". Response: give yourself time to adapt and plan daily and weekly rest — the author says this helps [#470].

#### Year-review ritual and workbook
Purpose (author's own): to "rein in" her ADHD a little and help focus on what is really needed, including rest and recovery, since her burnout "is not going to leave by itself" [#813].

How the author does it: for the second year in a row she and a close friend do the year review together at year's end, following the workbook "by the book", writing it "long and tediously". It always takes no less than 3 hours, but helps see the past year more objectively rather than only emotionally. She laughed reading what she wrote to herself a year earlier [#813]. The workbook is "nothing amazing" but has many questions, which they like. What she values most is that someone does it with her: alone she could not sit for 3 hours, and a person who knows her well helps recall achievements and difficulties and adds an outside view. She strongly recommends doing it with someone close [#814]. The workbook itself also suggests doing it with friends over tasty drinks, sharing memories and building plans, as a way to strengthen friendship and create new warm memories [#815].

Her resulting focus for the new year: besides work tasks, (1) structuring life, including planning, because that skill dropped sharply during the chaotic previous year; (2) driving — she wants to really try driving, although in the moment it causes strong stress. Her closing thesis: without thoughtful goal-setting it is very hard to get where you want [#813].

Workbook contents (2024/2025, by @juu.be) — stated purpose: to effectively sum up the outgoing year and plan the next one, a tool for pleasant reflection and analysis [#815].

- Task 1 — Events and achievements. Recall and write down no fewer than 10 main events or achievements of the year in each life sphere, including successes that seem insignificant. Spheres: work; family/relationships; friends/social life; rest/impressions; self-development and spirituality [#815].
- Task 2 — Wheel of balance (Колесо баланса). Rate different life spheres on a 1–10 scale: draw a circle, divide it into sectors matching the spheres, rate each. The analysis reveals imbalance and helps plan next year's actions toward more harmony. Example template sectors: hobbies, health, self-realization, family, finances, love, career, friendship [#815].
- Task 3 — Letter to the future (Письмо в будущее). Described as a creative tool of self-reflection and motivation, "a dialogue with your future version". Steps: (1) start with the date of writing and the date you plan to read it; (2) describe your current state — goals, feelings, worries, what you are proud of; be frank with yourself; (3) ask yourself several important questions whose answers you want to know in a year; (4) share your predictions about what will happen in the next year — realistic, but not afraid to dream; (5) give yourself a few pieces of advice you believe will help reach your goals and cope with difficulties. Afterwards put the letter in a safe place where you won't lose it — an envelope, a desk drawer or a special place for important things [#815].
- Task 4 — Questions of the year (Вопросы года), meant to analyze the year more deeply and draw valuable conclusions [#815]:
  - How would you describe the year in one sentence?
  - What did you do for the first time?
  - Which achievement of the year are you proudest of?
  - What was the brightest event of the year?
  - What advice would you give yourself a year ago?
  - Which of your actions would you like to repeat in the new year?
  - How has your attitude toward yourself changed?
  - What new did you learn about yourself?
  - What is your main achievement in study or work?
  - Which set goals did you achieve?
  - What do you need to get rid of or let go?
  - What new appeared in your life?
  - Which life spheres need developing?
  - What valuable lesson did you take from the most difficult situation?
  - What can you praise yourself for?
  - 10 warm memories?
  - Best experiment of the year?
  - Which goals should be carried over to next year?
  - What is the most valuable gift you received?
  - What would you do differently, and why?
  - Which goals lost relevance this year?
  - What new skills and abilities did you acquire?
  - Which decision or words would you repeat again?
  - How have your views on life changed?
  - Which events do you remember with a smile?
  - Whom do you need to thank?
  - Which book or film had the greatest impact on you?
  - What is your main victory and your main failure?
  - What success factors can you identify?
  - What three best decisions did you make this year?
- The workbook closes by wishing the answers serve as a compass toward happiness in the coming year and encouraging the reader not to forget goals and dreams [#815].

### Detailed insights

#### Social recovery after holidays
After a "crazy" December 31st, the author spent 2 days lying in the dark without communication, "degrading with my phone"; it was (and still is) hard even to go through the congratulation messages. Her reminder: if you were socially active at the end of December, you may simply need to sit quietly at home, possibly even alone. She returns to social life slowly while unsure it is time yet, and for holiday outings she chose quieter Zvenigorod and advised against going into central Moscow at that time. She closes by wishing readers to rest and do resource activity [#463]. Similarly, after the next New Year break she says she was incredibly happy to sit at home "like an owl" ("как сыч") all those days and was sorry it ended [#813].

#### Resilience as flexibility, not endurance
- Definition: resilience (резильентность), literally, is the ability of a system (a person, psyche, community) to adapt to stress, loss or adverse conditions, recover from them and keep functioning without irreversible damage. The author notes this is not new to psychology but is important for building one's life, especially with neurodivergence [#817].
- Translation problem: it is usually translated as «устойчивость» (stability), «стрессоустойчивость» (stress tolerance), «психологическая устойчивость» (psychological stability), but none of these conveys the original meaning. The author attributes this to a difference in mentality: in English, resilience is about flexibility, about "bouncing back" — recovering, changing under the influence of the environment, not just enduring. In the Russian cultural code, "устойчивость" means holding out no matter what, not breaking, not showing weakness, "standing to the end" like a bogatyr [#817].
- What gets lost: flexibility and recovery. Originally resilience includes vulnerability, the possibility of a temporary setback and the need for rest — rather than dying on the battlefield like a hero. Hence: the "stable person" in our idea is the one who doesn't fall and doesn't give up; the "resilient" one is the one who can fall and then recover [#817].
- In her skills-training group, participants tried to translate the term into one word; her favourite joke option was "терпила" (roughly "a pushover / one who just puts up with everything"), which she acknowledges is not what it means — yet it captures the Russian endurance connotation [#817].
- Her invitation: adopt the concept and the term to learn to take better care of yourself and allow yourself a step back, instead of being a "steadfast tin soldier" all the time [#817].

#### A rest experience for hyper-controllers: the art retreat
- Context: Moscow's pace "hit her on the head" so that she was glued to her chair all week; out of desperation she signed up with an ADHD buddy-coach to do something about her schedule, also seeing it as useful client experience from the other side of ADHD help [#557].
- She describes the art retreat as the most pleasant rest experience in a long time: a feeling of being at a children's but comfortable camp. There is a schedule, you are fed well, taken for walks every day, and have classes in drawing, vocals, acting and similar. Everyone around behaves like teenagers and chats about trivia like "what colour do you like" rather than how many patients you saw or what business you opened. She notes she still dislikes the word "retreat", but there was no esotericism [#557].
- Acting class gave her several insights, some of which she wants to take into her own work. Her verdict: a super experience for hyper-controllers — people for whom it is hard to relax, feel their body and do strange, illogical things. Rated 10 out of 10 [#557].
