## Research on autism and ADHD: brain, genetics, diagnosis trends and reading

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's retellings of scientific studies on autism (РАС) and ADHD (СДВГ): the role of the amygdala and hippocampus in autism, a sequencing study on networks of autism-related genes, a large Swedish study comparing diagnosis rates with symptom levels, a Cambridge study in Nature splitting autism into early- and late-diagnosed trajectories, and a Reddit study of how online talk about ADHD and autism has converged. It also contains two reading guides: a review of a clinical textbook on autism in adults and a list of recent books by autistic authors. The authors present these as material that helps understand where autistic and ADHD traits may come from, why diagnoses are growing, and how to read online self-recognition critically [#60, #78, #203, #245, #257, #282, #285].

### Core ideas

- The amygdala matters in autism not only for fear but for social interaction, emotion regulation and anxiety; findings on its size and activation are contradictory, and the author treats them as candidate "explainers" of social difficulties rather than settled facts (see *Amygdala in autism*) [#60].
- Autism is not tied to one mutation; it arises from disruptions in a complex network of genes where disturbing one gene changes many others, which explains how genetically different people end up with similar autistic traits (see *Gene regulatory network model*) [#203].
- Diagnoses of ADHD and autism have risen sharply over two decades while symptom levels in the population stayed stable, so talk of an "epidemic" is unfounded (see *Five hypotheses*) [#245].
- Early-diagnosed (before age 6) and late-diagnosed autism may be biologically different conditions with different genetic profiles and developmental paths that converge on the same diagnosis (see *Two-trajectory model*) [#257].
- Online discourse about ADHD and autism has grown more similar, and recognizing oneself in other people's stories can be useful, but it is not diagnosis and does not prove the conditions themselves have become more similar [#282].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Amygdala in autism — the competing explanations
Purpose: to understand why the amygdala (амигдала, a.k.a. миндалевидное тело) is studied in autism and what the evidence does and does not say. Author: климочкина катя [#60].

1. What the amygdala is. A brain structure involved in "обнаружении угроз и обработке эмоций" (threat detection and emotion processing). The author calls it the brain's «датчик дыма» (smoke detector) that reacts and signals "act". It is usually associated with fear and anger, but in the context of autism it was found to matter for social interaction. Because emotions are necessary for social interaction, the amygdala becomes even more relevant to autism [#60].
2. Evidence from monkeys. Studies on monkeys showed that changes in amygdala structure and function are linked to social difficulties resembling autism. If the most dominant monkey in a social group gets amygdala damage, its position in the hierarchy soon drops sharply [#60].
3. Evidence from humans with amygdala damage. People with a damaged amygdala show autism-like social behaviour — for example avoiding eye contact and having difficulty reading facial expressions — but do not meet the other diagnostic criteria for ASD. Lesson: amygdala damage reproduces part of the social picture but not autism as a whole [#60].
4. Specificity problem. Altered amygdala structure or function has been linked to almost all mental disorders, from anxiety and bipolar disorder to schizophrenia, which makes it hard to say how this region could uniquely explain autistic traits. The author adds «И все же.» (And yet.) [#60].
5. Size findings — contradictory. Since the 2000s, autism research has shown fewer neurons in the amygdala of autistic people. Some studies found the structure unusually small; others suggest the amygdala is often enlarged in children with ASD. "Ясности нет" (there is no clarity) [#60].
6. Growth trajectory — the reconciling observation. Normally the amygdala keeps growing into adulthood longer than other brain areas. In autistic people, by contrast, it grows faster than in non-autistic people in early childhood, up to about age 12, and then narrows and may even shrink. This could reconcile the "enlarged in children" and "small" findings [#60].
7. Reduced-function hypothesis. From these contradictory size data came the suggestion that "сниженная функция амигдалы препятствует способности аутичных людей обращать внимание на социальную информацию и интерпретировать ее" (reduced amygdala function hinders autistic people's ability to attend to and interpret social information) [#60].
8. Over-activation counter-finding. When assessing facial expressions, autistic people show less amygdala activation. However, when they attend to the area around the eyes, the amygdala lights up more strongly on MRI. So perhaps difficulties interpreting emotions result from *excessive* amygdala activation. The author notes (struck through) that at this point she had more questions than before [#60].
9. Hippocampus and connectivity. The hippocampus (the author jokes: "hippo + campus", a hippo in a camp) is needed to mark emotionally important events in memory; this lets the brain coordinate the fight-or-flight response («бей или беги») in dangerous situations, and together with the prefrontal cortex it helps modulate and control emotional reactions. Weak connectivity between the amygdala and other brain areas, especially the prefrontal cortex, may contribute to social difficulties in autism [#60].
10. Anxiety. The amygdala "shines" (is active) when a person is anxious. In an autistic person the amygdala does not shine (reduced activity) in relation to social difficulties — so the brain does not interpret social interactions as anxiety-provoking situations [#60].

Author's conclusion: the amygdala's involvement in autism goes beyond fear processing and covers social behaviour, emotion regulation and anxiety; she finds it exciting that research connects weak amygdala–prefrontal connectivity or the speed of amygdala development to social difficulties as *объясняторы* ("explainers") [#60].

#### Gene regulatory network model of autism (3 findings)
Source: a fresh preprint on bioRxiv (2025), retold via an article in The Transmitter, summarized by климочкина катя. Purpose: to understand how genes influence the development of autism [#203].

1. Network effects. Disrupting the work of one autism-associated gene can cause "изменения во многих других генах" (changes in many other genes) important for brain growth and development. Hence autism is not linked to one specific mutation; it arises from "сбоев в сложной сети генов" (failures in a complex network of genes), where a problem in one place can affect many other processes — the author compares it to Jenga. This explains "почему люди с разными генетическими особенностями могут иметь похожие черты аутизма" (why people with different genetic features can have similar autistic traits) [#203].
2. Master regulator genes CHD8, REST and ZFX. Researchers singled out these as especially important. They are "authoritarian" and control many other genes, so studying them specifically may help understand autism. There is a supposition that in the future working with these regulatory genes may help develop more targeted strategies to improve cognitive and emotional functions [#203].
3. Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNA). RNA molecules that do not code proteins but regulate other genes' activity, control cellular processes such as growth and development, and help maintain chromosome structure. Their influence on autism-related genes had not been examined in such detail before. Understanding them may open new ways of understanding, especially where mutations reduce the activity of important genes, and may explain why some autistic people are more prone to "эмоциональному истощению или переутомлению" (emotional exhaustion or overfatigue) [#203].

Takeaway: instead of studying only individual genes, it matters how they interact. The study helps explain why autism is so genetically diverse yet leads to common traits and difficulties; and it already found genes somehow related to cognitive functions or emotional exhaustion, which the author calls hugely important [#203].

#### Five hypotheses for why diagnoses rise while symptoms do not
Context: the Swedish study (see *Evidence and numbers*) found stable symptom levels but sharply rising diagnoses. The post lists factors that "most likely" contribute [#245]:

1. Broadened understanding of diagnostic criteria — more people now fall under the definitions of ASD and ADHD than before.
2. Growing awareness — parents, teachers and doctors recognize signs better, so children are referred for assessment and diagnosed more often.
3. Diagnostic substitution (диагностическая субституция) — people who used to get other diagnoses (for example developmental delay or, say, BPD/ПРЛ) now receive an ASD diagnosis.
4. Lowered diagnostic threshold — modern ASD and ADHD diagnoses are made on fewer symptoms than before (cited as proof: Arvidsson et al., 2018 for ASD; Kazda et al., 2023 for ADHD).
5. Changed perception of problems — parents today more often see ASD and ADHD symptoms as complicating their child's life, even at the same symptom severity.

The authors' global conclusion: claims of an autism or ADHD «эпидемии» (epidemic) are unfounded. The channel adds that the next exciting question is how to manage diagnosis and treatment when one diagnosis includes people with a huge range of problems and impairments [#245].

#### Two-trajectory model of autism (early vs late diagnosis)
Source: Cambridge researchers in Nature, analysis of 45,000+ autistic people; the channel calls it "the best scientific paper on ASD for 2025 (in my opinion)". Main finding: people diagnosed early (before 6) and late have different genetic profiles and developmental trajectories — "Это не вопрос своевременности диагностики — это биологически разные состояния" (This is not a question of timely diagnosis — these are biologically different conditions) [#257].

Metaphor: one sea (the autism diagnosis) fed by two rivers flowing from different sources (genetics), through different landscapes (different age patterns), which finally merge (because symptoms are shared, and diagnosis is made by symptoms) [#257].

Early diagnosis trajectory:
1. Social/communication difficulties visible from early childhood.
2. Frequent speech and motor delays.
3. Moderate genetic link with ADHD / mental disorders.
4. "Classic" presentation — symptoms look the way psychiatrists expect them to [#257].

Late diagnosis trajectory:
1. Early childhood often looks neurotypical at first glance.
2. Difficulties show up vividly in adolescence.
3. Very high genetic correlation with ADHD (0.5–0.7), depression, PTSD.
4. High comorbidity rates.
5. Speech usually normal [#257].

Striking point: the genetic profile of late autism is closer to ADHD/depression/PTSD than to early autism. Longitudinal data: four cohorts (UK, Australia) followed children from birth through adolescence, observing behavioural and emotional development, and confirmed two trajectories — (1) early: difficulties from infancy; (2) late: increasing difficulties in adolescence [#257].

Practical significance: for late-diagnosed people, the absence of early signs may reflect a different developmental pattern, not «маскировку» (masking). The genetic correlation with depression/PTSD explains how often these diagnoses co-occur and helps diagnosis [#257].

### Detailed insights

#### How online talk about ADHD and autism converged (Reddit study)
Researchers did not survey anyone; they analysed 478,915 Reddit posts from 2012–2022 [#282].
- Mutual mentions: in r/ADHD, mentions of autism rose sharply in 2021 and overtook mentions of bipolar disorder and OCD. In r/autism, ADHD also gradually came up more often. From 2020 the number of users participating in both communities grew noticeably. So it is not only that people learned more about ASD or ADHD — they increasingly consider them together [#282].
- Shift from "what's wrong" to "how to live": after 2020 both communities discussed work and career, relationship difficulties and somewhat diagnosis more often. r/autism gained threads on job search and maintaining friendships; r/ADHD on career difficulties and productivity apps. People increasingly ask not just «что со мной не так» (what is wrong with me) but «как мне вообще с этим жить» (how do I live with this at all) — why it is hard to keep a job, maintain relationships or get adequate diagnosis as an adult [#282].
- Value of others' experience (the "swap"): a person with ADHD reads how an autistic person gets tired after socializing and recognizes the feeling; an autistic person reads about difficulties organizing daily life and thinks "me too". This can broaden self-understanding and suggest which questions to discuss with a specialist [#282].
- Limit of self-recognition: recognizing yourself in someone else's experience is not the same as having the same diagnosis or mechanism. "Диагностика по тикотоку не проканала, поэтому по реддиту тоже не вариант" (Diagnosis via TikTok didn't work, so via Reddit isn't an option either) — yet there is some benefit. Example: a person may avoid noisy events because of sensory overload, fear of negative evaluation, or general exhaustion — the behaviour looks the same from outside, the causes differ. Conversely, two people with the same diagnosis can have completely different difficulties [#282].
- What the study does and doesn't show: in online discussions, ADHD and autism become increasingly similar by the context in which they are discussed — but that is similarity of public representations, not evidence that the conditions themselves became more similar. Researchers analysed Reddit, not clinical data, so the work shows changes in public discourse and does not prove the prevalence of ADHD+autism co-occurrence increased [#282].
- Author's conclusion: social networks and Reddit are not just distributors of information; they take part in shaping the language people use to describe their own experience — which the author finds no less interesting a research subject than the diagnostic categories themselves [#282].

#### Book review: Autism Spectrum Disorder in Adults (Barahona Corrêa & van der Gaag, eds.)
Reviewer: Lena Foer; cover reads "Autism Spectrum Disorders in Adults", Bernardo Barahona Corrêa, Rutger-Jan van der Gaag, Editors [#78, img #78].
- What it is: not popular science. A collection of reviews on autism in adults, with chapters by specialists from different countries [#78].
- Contents: a huge section on the neurobiology of autism linking what happens in the brain to specific ASD manifestations (reviewer: "офигенно!", awesome); a section on ASD in girls and women and how the diagnosis gets confused with practically anything "typically female"; a section on autism and ageing, which the reviewer has not met anywhere else; a large part on diagnosis in adulthood and how to distinguish adult ASD from disorders it is commonly confused with. All illustrated with clinical cases and a surprisingly warm, non-stigmatizing view of the disorder [#78].

Reader-fit guide ("should you read it?"):
1. Psychologist or psychiatrist wanting to work with ASD — definitely yes; the reviewer has not found a more useful book for herself.
2. You have a special interest in ASD — yes, you'll like it.
3. You want to learn about specific subtle aspects of the diagnosis or one of the problem areas described — yes.
4. Different psychiatrists give you five different diagnoses including autism (and you're willing to dig in) — it may be very helpful.
5. You want general, easily written information about the diagnosis — probably look for something else [#78].

### Evidence and numbers

Swedish study (University of Gothenburg and Karolinska Institutet), published in ScienceDirect [#245]:
- Background figures quoted: in the late 1970s autism prevalence was estimated at 0.2%; by the early 2020s it reached 2.8% in the USA, and in Sweden 5.8% among adolescent boys and 4.0% among girls.
- Growth is most visible in adolescence and adulthood: mean age at ASD diagnosis rose from 9.6 years in 1998 to 14.5 years in 2018.
- ADHD: in the USA diagnosis prevalence among children rose from 6.1% in 1997–98 to 14.2% in 2021–22; in Sweden in 2022, 10.5% of adolescent boys and 6.0% of girls had an ADHD diagnosis.
- Method: data from CATSS (Child and Adolescent Twin Study in Sweden), where parents answered questions about ASD and ADHD symptoms in their children by age 18. Sample: 9,870 people born 1993–2001. ASD symptoms: 12 questions matching DSM-IV criteria. ADHD symptoms: 17 items of the ABCL/18-59 questionnaire measuring attention and executive function problems (but not hyperactivity).
- Results: over the nine-year period, no increase in mean ASD symptom level in the whole sample or separately for boys or girls — consistent with an earlier study of roughly the same group showing stable autism symptoms in nine-year-olds over a decade despite rising clinical diagnoses. ADHD: stable in boys, but a statistically significant though small increase in girls; the share of girls reaching the 90th percentile of ADHD symptoms rose on average 5.3% per year, while boys' stayed unchanged.
- Channel's reading of the girls' ADHD rise: hard to draw firm conclusions, so it stays uncertain; the author hopefully speculates that culture may now let girls show themselves a bit more and not internalize everything.

Cambridge / Nature study: 45,000+ autistic people; genetic correlation of late-diagnosed autism with ADHD 0.5–0.7; four longitudinal cohorts (UK, Australia); the post also says researchers in four different countries tracked children from birth through adolescence (see *Two-trajectory model*) [#257].

Reddit study: 478,915 posts, 2012–2022 (see *How online talk… converged*) [#282].

Books by autistic authors (compiled from the catalogue The Autism Books by Autistic Authors) [#285]:
- In Russian, recent: "Нейроотличные" (Neurodivergent) by Peter Ampe and Emily Rammant (Alpina, 2025). Both describe themselves as neurodivergent: Ampe has pronounced ASD and ADHD traits, Rammant ADHD and OCD. About strengths and finding a work environment that fits you.
- In Russian, older translations: Temple Grandin, Donna Williams, Iris Johansson, Naoki Higashida — still worth reading, but classics, not new releases.
- Megan Anna Neff, "The Autistic Burnout Workbook" (2025) — by the Neff of Neurodivergent Insights, whom the channel constantly cites; a workbook for assembling a personal plan for recovering from burnout.
- Sonny Jane Wise, "The Neurodivergent Friendly Workbook of DBT Skills" (new edition February 2026) — DBT skills adapted for neurodivergent people by an autistic author with ADHD; five sections, one entirely on sensory needs, which classic DBT lacks.
- Wenn Lawson, "Autism and Being Monotropic" (2025) — one of the authors of monotropism theory explains it for practitioners.
- Damian Milton (ed.), "The Double Empathy Reader" (2025) — collection on double empathy: theory, lived experience, and what to do about it in practice.
- Devon Price, "Unmasking for Life" (2025) — sequel to "Unmasking Autism", on how to live on once the mask is off.
- Rachel Moseley, Julie Gamble-Turner, "Autistic Menopause" (January 2026) — a topic on which there is almost nothing elsewhere.
- Robert Chapman, Sue Fletcher-Watson, "Neurodiversity: A Very Short Introduction" (March 2026) — short introduction in the Oxford series, for a compact overview of the field.
- Catherine Asta, "Rediscovered" (2025) — for women with late-discovered autism, on rebuilding yourself after an adult diagnosis.
- Sarah Hendrickx, "The Complete Guide to AuDHD" (November 2026) — autism and ADHD together across the lifespan.

Where to search yourself: the project The Autism Books by Autistic Authors is a catalogue including only books by autistic authors, sorted by year of publication; you can dig there by your need — burnout, work, parenting, late diagnosis. A noticeable share of these books is published by Jessica Kingsley Publishers, and almost all have e-book versions, which helps if paper books can't be delivered to you [#285].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- (!) CONTRADICTION: amygdala size findings conflict (fewer neurons / unusually small vs often enlarged in children with ASD); the post offers the early-overgrowth-then-shrinkage trajectory as a partial reconciliation, and activation findings point both to reduced function and to over-activation (eye region). The author explicitly says there is no clarity [#60].
- The amygdala is implicated in nearly all mental disorders, so it cannot uniquely explain autism [#60].
- The gene-network study is a preprint; therapeutic use of regulatory genes is only "a supposition" for the future, and the lncRNA–exhaustion link is phrased as "may explain" [#203].
- The girls' ADHD symptom rise in the Swedish data is small and hard to interpret; the channel's cultural explanation is a hopeful speculation. ADHD was measured without hyperactivity items [#245].
- (!) UNCLEAR: #257 says researchers "in four different countries" tracked children, but the longitudinal section lists four cohorts from only the UK and Australia [#257].
- The Reddit study reflects public discourse, not clinical data; it does not show that ADHD–autism co-occurrence increased [#282].
- The "best paper of 2025" verdict and the book verdicts are the channel authors' personal opinions [#78, #257, #285].
