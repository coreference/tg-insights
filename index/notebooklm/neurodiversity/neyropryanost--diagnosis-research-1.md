## Recognizing autism in adults: criteria, hidden traits and late diagnosis

### What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel taught about recognizing autism in adults: the DSM-5 / DSM-5-TR criteria rewritten in plain, neurodiversity-affirming language; a 50-item checklist of lesser-known adult traits mapped to those criteria; the broader autism phenotype; pathological demand avoidance; the reasons autism is often recognized only late in life (masking, life demands outgrowing capacity, literal reading of criteria and screening questions, recognition via one's child, misattributing traits to anxiety or depression); how to tell a meltdown from a panic attack; and whether a formal diagnosis matters. According to the source it matters because many autistic adults do not see themselves in the deficit-focused, stereotyped descriptions of autism, are misdiagnosed or never diagnosed, and keep pushing themselves without accommodations until they reach meltdowns and burnout [#138, #401, #438, #313].

Part 2 of this topic is a single seven-card series by neurodivergent_lou ("Neurodivergent_lou"), translated by the channel Нейр∞тлично and forwarded to Нейропряность on 2024-11-22: "Аутичные черты, которые, как я считала, у меня отсутствуют (и как они на самом деле проявлялись)" (Autistic traits I believed I didn't have — and how they actually showed up) [#321, img #321]. For each of seven traits the card gives (a) "Почему я предполагала, что у меня нет этой черты" (why I assumed I didn't have this trait) — the misconception that made her rule it out — and (b) "Как проявлялась эта черта?" (how did the trait show up?) — the concrete everyday manifestations [#322–#328]. It matters for late recognition because each misconception is a reason an autistic adult may answer "no" to themselves (or a screener) about a trait they actually have.

Relation to the existing topic file: this series extends three threads already present — Lou's point that autistic people read criteria literally and in black-and-white terms and so assume they lack traits they have (#401–#402; this card series is a worked, first-person demonstration of exactly that), the humorous "I can't be autistic, I don't have sensory issues" list (#444; card 2 here gives a sober version), and the meltdown-vs-panic-attack comparison (#483–#490; card 7 restates in brief that she mistook meltdowns for panic attacks). New material not in the existing file: the misconceptions about social difficulties (comparing oneself to an autistic relative), routine (seen as stubbornness and selfishness), stimming (equated only with hand-flapping), special interests (stereotyped as trains/numbers and "knowing everything"), and literal understanding (understanding idioms but not indirect requests). No contradictions with the existing file were found.

### Core ideas

- The criteria describe difficulties relative to a non-autistic world. In plain language, category A means it is hard for you to communicate the way non-autistic society expects, in more than one setting; Lindsey Mackereth reads all the deficit-worded DSM criteria as potential difficulties for a good quality of life in a neurotypical world (see *DSM-5 criteria category A explained* and *Full DSM-5-TR criteria*) [#140, #480].
- Adult autism often looks different from the textbook. Many traits are subtle, masked or compensated (scripts, intense eye contact, studying human behaviour), so a checklist of lesser-known traits is offered to make them visible (see *50 lesser-known traits*) [#475].
- Late recognition has identifiable causes. Masking, demands that only later exceed capacity, limited and neurotypical-authored information, parents who do not believe it, long waits and poorly informed specialists, and recognizing oneself only after one's child is diagnosed (see *Six reasons*) [#435].
- Literal thinking can hide autism from the autistic person and the screener. Autistic people may read criteria and questions literally and in black-and-white terms, concluding they lack a trait they actually have [#401, #402, #805].
- If you think you may be autistic, adapt your life as if you are. Lou argues the worst outcome of doing so is merely having helpful strategies [#310, #314].
- PDA is an anxiety-driven need for autonomy, not wilfulness. Adults with PDA may be unable to do even what they want once it feels like a demand; support starts with replacing irritation by understanding, then trust, permission, declarative language and letting them lead (see *Pathological demand avoidance in adults*) [#29].
- A diagnosis is not required for self-help but has real benefits — validation, ruling out other conditions, screening for physical comorbidities, earlier adaptation and (for ADHD) medication [#575].

- Stereotyped or literal pictures of a trait make people rule it out. In every one of the seven cases Lou ruled herself out by comparing herself to a narrow image (hand-flapping, trains, idioms, "everyone feels it like this") while the trait was present in a different form (see *Seven autistic traits*) [#321].
- Traits get reattributed to character or other conditions. Routine needs were read as stubbornness and selfishness, sensory overload as "coping badly", meltdowns as panic attacks [#323, #324, #328].
- Familial baseline hides traits. If an (autistic) relative behaves the same way, one's own social difficulties seem normal [#322].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### DSM-5 criteria category A explained in plain language
Source: a card series by neurodivergent_lou, translated by Нейр∞тлично and forwarded by Настя, titled *Explaining autism diagnostic criteria — the full criteria in easy-to-understand, neurodiversity-supporting language*. The posted part (Part 1) covers category A only [#138, #139].

Category A as a whole — deficits in social communication and social interaction across multiple contexts. Explained in two halves: "deficits in social communication and interaction" means it will be hard for you to communicate the way non-autistic society expects; "across multiple contexts" means you have difficulties in more than one place — at home, at school, at work, or elsewhere [#140].

Category A has three sub-criteria [#139]:

1. Sub-criterion 1 — deficit in social-emotional reciprocity. Plain meaning: difficulty with the contradictory nature of neurotypical communication. Ways it can show up [#141]:
   - infodumping about special interests in conversations;
   - frank, direct answers to questions;
   - masking social difficulties by studying and following socially acceptable scripts;
   - interrupting other people in conversations;
   - not initiating conversations with others;
   - not answering questions in conversations;
   - not keeping up small talk, for example about the weather or traffic.
2. Sub-criterion 2 — deficit in nonverbal communicative behaviour (e.g. gestures) used in social interaction. Plain meaning: difficulty understanding and using nonverbal communication — gestures, facial expressions and eye contact — according to non-autistic norms. Ways it can show up [#142]:
   - no eye contact, or very intense eye contact;
   - masking by tracking and imitating how neurotypical people make eye contact or use facial expressions;
   - trouble interpreting other people's gestures;
   - facial expressions that do not fit the situation, for example laughing in a sad or tense situation.
3. Sub-criterion 3 — deficit in developing, maintaining and understanding relationships. Plain meaning: difficulty starting, maintaining and understanding relationships. Ways it can show up [#143]:
   - difficulty understanding how to make friends, even when you genuinely want friendships;
   - difficulty maintaining friendships once they have started;
   - feeling on the sidelines of social groups;
   - not understanding the point of friendship.

#### Full DSM-5-TR criteria for autism spectrum disorder
Reproduced (in Russian translation) as part of Lindsey Mackereth's checklist. Her note: although the DSM criteria are written from a deficit point of view, she regards them as potential difficulties for a high quality of life in a neurotypical world. A footnote adds that the full DSM-5-TR criteria are on pages 6–7 of the manual [#480, #479].

- A. Persistent impairments in social communication and social interaction across multiple contexts, shown by ALL of the following, currently or by history [#480]:
  - A1. Impairments of social-emotional reciprocity — ranging from an atypical social approach and failure of normal back-and-forth conversation, to reduced sharing of interests, emotions or affect, to failure to initiate or respond to social interactions.
  - A2. Impairments in nonverbal communicative behaviour used for social interaction — ranging from poorly integrated verbal and nonverbal communication, to abnormalities in eye contact and body language or impairments in understanding and using gestures, to a total lack of facial expression and nonverbal communication.
  - A3. Impairments in developing, maintaining and understanding relationships — ranging from difficulty adjusting behaviour to suit various social contexts, to difficulty in sharing imaginative play or making friends, to absence of interest in peers.
- B. Restricted, repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests or activities, shown by AT LEAST TWO of the following, currently or by history [#480, #481]:
  - B1. Stereotyped or repetitive motor movements, use of objects or speech (e.g. simple motor stereotypies, lining up toys or flipping objects, echolalia, idiosyncratic phrases).
  - B2. Insistence on sameness, inflexible adherence to routines or ritualized patterns of verbal or nonverbal behaviour (e.g. extreme distress at small changes, difficulty with transitions, rigid thinking patterns, greeting rituals, needing to take the same route or eat the same food every day).
  - B3. Highly restricted, fixated interests that are abnormal in intensity or focus (e.g. strong attachment to or preoccupation with unusual objects, excessively circumscribed or perseverative interests).
  - B4. Hyper- or hyporeactivity to sensory input or unusual interest in sensory aspects of the environment (e.g. apparent indifference to pain/temperature, adverse response to specific sounds or textures, excessive smelling or touching of objects, visual fascination with lights or movement).
- Additional criteria [#481]:
  - C. Symptoms must be present in the early developmental period — but may not become fully manifest until social demands exceed limited capacities, or may be masked by learned strategies later in life. (This clause is the formal basis for the "demands exceed capacity" and "masking" reasons for late recognition below.)
  - D. Symptoms cause clinically significant impairment in social, occupational or other important areas of current functioning.
  - E. The disturbances are not better explained by intellectual disability (intellectual developmental disorder) or global developmental delay. Intellectual disability and ASD frequently co-occur; to make comorbid diagnoses of both, social communication should be below that expected for the general developmental level.

#### Six reasons a person may not be identified as autistic until later in life
Source: neurodivergent_lou, translated by Нейр∞тлично ("Причины, по которым человека могут не идентифицировать как аутиста до более позднего возраста") [#435].

1. Masking of autistic traits. Autistic people sometimes unconsciously learn to hide their traits and imitate neurotypical behaviour to cope with a world that can be hostile to autistic people — e.g. suppressing stims or looking people in the eye even though it is uncomfortable. With age we may realize we have been hiding our traits, or lose the ability to mask because of exhaustion from long pretending; this can lead us or the people around us to realize we are autistic [#436].
2. Autistic traits may not be obvious until life's demands exceed our capacities. In childhood you may have coped somehow and others did not particularly notice your difficulties; when life's demands begin to exceed your capacities it can become obvious you are autistic. For example, in adolescence and adulthood social interactions become more complex and harder to understand or maintain, and pressure to be independent may increase, making autistic traits more visible [#437].
3. Awareness and understanding of autism are still limited — especially in marginalized communities. Part of the information about autism is written from the perspective of neurotypical people rather than autistic people themselves, so autistic people may not see themselves and their experiences in what is presented and do not realize they are autistic until later [#438].
4. Our parents may not believe we are autistic. Because of limited information and understanding, parents may not realize or even believe their child is autistic; this can stop a person recognizing their autism and be a barrier to formal diagnosis in childhood. Information about autism changes fast; there is still work to do, but there is more data now than the previous generation had [#439].
5. Problems with diagnosis. Some people realize as children that they are autistic but cannot get official confirmation until adulthood because of long waits and difficult access; Lou has heard of people waiting for years (the translator notes that some countries have waiting lists for assessment). Even at the top of the waiting list it can be hard to get a diagnosis because of outdated stereotypes or specialists' lack of knowledge about how autism presents [#440].
6. Not realizing you are autistic until your children are diagnosed. Many autistic people only realize it when their children are diagnosed; they then notice shared traits with their children. Autism is considered largely genetic, so if your child is autistic there is a high chance you are too [#441].

#### Meltdown vs panic attack comparison
Context: neurodivergent_lou explains that, before realizing she was autistic, she attributed her autistic traits to other things — she thought her communication differences were just social anxiety, executive-function difficulties were laziness, autistic burnout was depression, and for a long time she believed she had panic attacks when they were in fact autistic meltdowns [#483]. Autistic people can certainly have panic attacks; but because panic attacks are talked about more and better understood in today's society, undiagnosed (or not-yet-self-aware) people may not realize they are having a meltdown rather than a panic attack [#484]. The distinction matters because the responses differ: in a panic attack it can sometimes help to stay in contact with the trigger so the body learns to perceive it as safe and to handle stress, whereas in a meltdown the best solution may be the opposite — reducing exposure to triggers [#485].

Similarities between meltdowns and panic attacks [#486]:
- strong emotions;
- physical sensations;
- impact on daily life;
- loss of control.

Features that may be more specific to autistic meltdowns [#487, #488]:
- may have more triggers — for example sensory problems, a change of daily routine;
- more awareness of the triggers that provoke meltdowns;
- may have more early warning signs that a meltdown is happening — for example covering the ears, losing the ability to hide autistic traits;
- usually last longer and need more recovery time; while recovering from a meltdown we may lose the ability to do everyday tasks;
- avoiding triggers and reducing stress can prevent a meltdown;
- sensory sensations may intensify during a meltdown;
- outward expression can include crying, inability to communicate, lashing out, harmful stimming;
- a feeling of being overwhelmed predominates.

Features that may be more specific to panic attacks [#489, #490]:
- may have one specific trigger or no trigger at all;
- there may be no sense of stress building up; panic attacks can come on more suddenly;
- may last a shorter time with a shorter recovery — which by no means lessens how agonizing they can be; usually no loss of ability to do everyday tasks during recovery;
- may include physical sensations such as feeling short of breath, a racing heart, sweaty palms;
- avoiding anxiety triggers can make panic attacks worse over time;
- feelings of anxiety and panic predominate.

#### Five benefits of a formal diagnosis and ten things that help without one
Source: the channel author's post "Важна ли официальная диагностика РАС/СДВГ?" (Does an official ASD/ADHD diagnosis matter?), written through the lens of personal experience; the author stresses she is not a specialist, shares what she learns from professionals, and recommends doing your own research or consulting a specialist before changing your life. Neurodivergence is not cured, and some specialists consider a diagnosis unnecessary; the author agrees that no diagnosis is needed to take one's traits into account and help oneself, but sees several advantages of having one [#575]:

1. Validation and acceptance — for many, an important step toward understanding oneself.
2. Ruling out other conditions — making sure it is not something else (e.g. schizophrenia, bipolar disorder or C-PTSD), since some conditions need specific treatment such as therapy or medication; also ruling out physical conditions (e.g. PANDAS/PANS) that can look similar to autism.
3. Physical comorbidities — neurodivergence is often linked with physical conditions (hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, mast cell activation syndrome, postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome). According to the author, scientists think patients found to have autoimmune problems or the listed comorbid conditions should also be checked for neurodivergence, and vice versa; she speculates such protocols may be introduced in future and notes some doctors already watch for these patterns. Understanding one's physical features can greatly help in adjusting lifestyle and improving quality of life. She says the list is not exhaustive, only examples.
4. Early adaptation — earlier identification can help a person adapt their life so as not to harm themselves, not fall into burnout, not worsen chronic illnesses, and be less traumatized by life.
5. Medication support — specifically for ADHD there is medication support, which may also be a good solution.

What can really help — none of it requires a diagnosis [#575]:
1. Understanding triggers and overloads, and which tools protect us from triggers.
2. Mapping the nervous system ("картография нервной системы") to understand better when we are hyper- or hypo-activated and how to help ourselves at those moments, including meltdowns and shutdowns.
3. Pacing and balancing activity — "у нас очень ограниченные ресурсы и важно правильно их распределять" (our resources are very limited and it is important to distribute them correctly).
4. Exploring one's stimming needs.
5. Unmasking — exploring your own authenticity, not imitating other autistic people in "how to be autistic properly".
6. Whether we have a special interest and how it can help us self-regulate (a special interest is something we are very interested in, can get enormous pleasure from spending time on, and also think about at other times).
7. Understanding hyperfixations and hyperfocus, and how they can help or harm us.
8. Studying and training interoception (body signals) and understanding emotions, if there is alexithymia.
9. Accommodations for oneself so as not to fall into neurodivergent burnout, or to be able to recover.
10. Understanding our physical features, needs and limitations, if any.

Taking one's traits into account as an integral part of oneself, understanding needs and self-care can help both to avoid burnout and to get out of it.

#### Pathological demand avoidance (PDA) in adults — what it is, signs, and how to support
Source: the channel's post and the linked Telegraph longread "Патологическое избегание требований у взрослых – как помочь им выбраться из тупика" (Pathological demand avoidance in adults — how to help them get unstuck), translated and adapted by Настя specially for Нейропряность from an article on the Thrive Autism Coaching site ("Pathological demand avoidance in adults: how to help them get unstuck") [#29, article #29]. The post frames PDA (Russian abbreviation ПИТ) as a little-studied but extremely important phenomenon for understanding autism in adults: not an officially recognized diagnosis, it describes the situation in which people with ASD face enormous difficulties responding to everyday requests and expectations; the article covers PDA basics, its manifestations and strategies that can help autistic adults overcome these challenges and take part in everyday life [#29].

What PDA is [#29]:
- Although it is not recognized as a diagnosis in DSM-5, PDA is a set of personality traits and behavioural features that fit many people on the autism spectrum. It affects how they respond to everyday demands and expectations — both those they place on themselves and those others place on them.
- Many people in the autistic community prefer the name "Устойчивое стремление к автономии" (persistent drive for autonomy), which the article calls a more useful description because it goes to the heart of the matter. Autonomy is one of the most powerful human motivators; most people function better with a moderate degree of autonomy, but when this need becomes extremely pronounced it can seriously impair a person's ability to function.
- From the outside it can look as if they tend to refuse to cooperate with other people's requests, even when complying would be in their own best interest. Internally they may feel completely unable to do even what they themselves want to do, if those things feel like "demands".
- Definition: PDA is characterized by excessive avoidance of everyday demands and expectations — school assignments, work duties, daily life, social situations. It is "чрезвычайная, обусловленная тревогой потребность индивида контролировать свою жизнь" (an extreme, anxiety-driven need of the individual to control their life).
- Because DSM-5 has no diagnosis for this cluster of symptoms, at some point such people may have been given a diagnosis the translation renders as "синдрома оппозиционно-диссоциативного расстройства (ODD)" (literally "oppositional-dissociative disorder syndrome", abbreviated ODD; (!) UNCLEAR: the Russian name does not match the abbreviation as usually expanded, likely a translation slip) — which the article calls a dead-end diagnosis that unfortunately does not provide useful treatment or intervention.
- A recognizable conversational pattern: if many of your conversations with the person look like "Ты: Хочешь..." / "Они: Нет!" (You: Do you want… / They: No!), even when the request seems innocent to you or in their best interest, you may be dealing with PDA.
- Avoidance behaviours people with PDA may use: withdrawing from activities, avoiding social situations, distracting the person making the demand, inventing excuses to refuse, or even lying if they are desperate enough to escape the demand.
- PDA can even deprive people of what they want for themselves: they may genuinely want to do something, but because it feels like a demand to them, they simply cannot do it.

What counts as a "demand" — people with PDA may perceive a wide range of everyday situations as demands that others would not consider demands. Unexpected examples the article lists [#29]:
1. Simple requests for information or cooperation, e.g. "Ты собираешься принять душ сегодня?" (Are you going to take a shower today?) or "Можешь передать мне эту ручку?" (Can you pass me that pen?).
2. Following routines set by others, or being asked to change one's own routine in some way.
3. Social expectations — keeping up small talk, taking part in group activities, keeping in touch with people they consider friends.
4. Study or work tasks — completing assignments, following instructions, meeting deadlines.
5. Personal hygiene rituals — showering, grooming, getting dressed.
The article stresses that people with PDA can perceive and experience these seemingly mundane situations as overwhelming and anxiety-provoking, which leads to avoidance and attempts to resist or evade the perceived demands [#29].

Signs of PDA in adults — besides chronic irritation and shame, six common ways PDA can affect people [#29]:
1. Anxiety and stress. Extremely high levels of anxiety and stress in response to everyday demands and expectations, leading to avoidance and attempts to resist or evade demands, which keeps them stuck. (!) UNCLEAR: the translated sentence ends garbled — "что удерживает их в чувстве “в жизни." — the intended phrase is probably "stuck in life", but the text is broken.
2. Difficulty cooperating. Difficulty cooperating with requests and instructions, even simple ones or necessary tasks, because of their avoidance of perceived demands.
3. Social difficulties. Interestingly, autistic adults with PDA may seem more socially savvy and communicatively skilled than other autistic people and may use this to their advantage. However, these visible social abilities often mask difficulties in processing and understanding social communication and situations, which can lead to trouble fitting social norms and maintaining relationships, affecting their ability to form and keep social connections.
4. Emotion-regulation difficulties. Difficulty controlling emotions, which can lead to emotional outbursts, meltdowns ("кризисам") or shutdowns as a coping mechanism in response to perceived demands.
5. Rigidity and resistance to change. Resistance when their environment changes or when they need to move to another activity, life stage, job or other change. The article distinguishes causes: a person struggling with what is usually called autistic inertia is quite comfortable staying in the current state, whereas people with PDA really do want to change — they just cannot overcome the demand that the change requires.
6. Masking and camouflaging. Under the influence of shame, people with PDA may mask or camouflage to hide their PDA, making efforts to imitate neurotypical behaviour to cope with social expectations. This can be utterly exhausting and lead to burnout over time, which makes struggling with demands even harder — "Это зловещий круг" (It is a vicious circle).
The impact of PDA varies greatly from person to person, and each person with PDA has unique strengths and weaknesses; understanding and supporting the individual's unique needs and perspective can be decisive in providing appropriate support and accommodation [#29].

Strategies for supporting an adult with autism and PDA — the article's support method, in order [#29]:

0. Foundation: change your own view first. It is crucial to understand and internalize that nobody chooses to be like this: behind the observed behaviour lie biological, physiological and sometimes genetic causes. Believing that confusing behaviour is not deliberate completely changes your approach to the person. If you have any doubts, research PDA further until you feel able to withhold judgment about the behaviour you observe. Key line: "Ваше собственное чувство раздражения должно быть заменено пониманием и принятием, прежде чем вы сможете эффективно предоставлять поддержку." (Your own feeling of irritation must be replaced by understanding and acceptance before you can provide support effectively.)
1. Build the relationship. People with PDA experience so much shame that trust and mutual respect are key if you want to help. Most feel constantly misunderstood, so trust in people who hold power over them or try to influence them — parents, teachers, therapists, bosses — is not given until it is earned. Whether you have known the person for decades or just met, meet them where they are; you may need to dig a little to understand what is going on, because the shame of having this profile and the urge to hide it are very strong. How: "Устанавливайте контакт, проявляя искренний интерес к тому, кто они, что им нравится и не нравится, их страстям и тому, чего они хотят от жизни" (Make contact by showing genuine interest in who they are, their likes and dislikes, their passions and what they want from life) — to build or improve the relationship.
2. Get permission to help first. If you have a good relationship, it is fine to offer help creating a plan toward a specific goal — but in every case ask for and get permission to help with the plan before starting to solve problems. If they refuse, calmly respect the decision; you may ask again later, since the offer may be more fitting at another moment.
3. Learn to use declarative language. Why: people with PDA experience life as if everyone around demands something of them, without regard for how they perceive the demand; help feels done *to* them rather than *for* them, so they naturally resist as soon as the conversation becomes demand-oriented. Typical reactions: simply saying "no" and refusing to continue; starting to shut down and becoming unreachable; using sophisticated humour or excuses to dodge the demand; and, depending on emotional stability, difficulty managing emotions with a rapid switch from complete calm to explosive behaviour. How: the key to engaging them in productive conversations is using declarative rather than imperative language, at times when they are relatively calm, stable and open to talking. Declarative language helps them feel competent and understood rather than influenced. It naturally creates learning opportunities in seeing the big picture, reading nonverbal communication, problem solving, perspective-taking and self-advocacy — everything that helps them stop shutting down and move forward. Result: with a good relationship and successful use of this approach, the person feels secure enough to receive the help they need instead of feeling ashamed of their difficulties. (!) INCOMPLETE: the article does not give examples of declarative vs imperative phrasing; it refers readers to Linda K. Murphy's book (see *Tools and resources mentioned*).
4. Put them in the driver's seat ("Пусть они будут у руля"). After mastering declarative language, put the person in charge of their own life. Whether teenager or adult, this way of relating to the world has been with them since the start of life, and they have spent their whole life feeling things were done "to them" instead of "for them". Imposing your goals on a person with PDA is never a winning strategy; ideally, work on helping them reach their own goals, and at minimum jointly find where your goals overlap and start there. Even if they want to do something, if it feels like a demand it can become a barrier; so the first thing to do, if their inner dialogue is about how they "have to" do something, is to help them switch to recognizing that they *want* to do it — remind them how the demand relates to their overall goal to help make that shift.
5. Support executive-function difficulties. Executive-function problems are common in people with autism and PDA; they may literally need help identifying priorities, making a plan toward their goals, determining the next step, starting that step, staying with it and keeping momentum. But "helping" without first getting permission will feel more like an attack than help. Method: ask what their goal is, then ask what their plan is to achieve it — always let them go first. If they have no plan, or you know their plan is impossible, ask permission to help. If they agree, offer options for how you could help and let them choose. Useful questions, once you have made clear you want to support *their* goals (not your goals for them):
   - "Каков ваш план для...?" (What is your plan for…?)
   - "Какой следующий шаг?" (What is the next step?)
   - "Когда вы планируете это сделать?" (When do you plan to do it?)
   - "Как вы будете помнить о том, чтобы это сделать?" (How will you remember to do it?)
   - "Хотели бы вы, чтобы я вам помог напомнить?" (Would you like me to help remind you?)
   - "Есть ли что-то полезное, что я могу сделать, если вы забудете?" (Is there anything useful I can do if you forget?)
6. Be patient and flexible. Everyone has bad days, but a bad day for a person with PDA can mean literally nothing productive. Worry-free breaks when anxiety rises can help; access to a safe, demand-free space to retreat to can make a huge difference. If a problem-solving session or conversation becomes too heavy, encourage a break to relieve anxiety and return to the conversation later. If their plan seems ineffective, discuss it: confirm the goal is still their priority; then, instead of solving it for them, let them propose alternative ideas for reaching the same goal; only after that, ask permission to suggest the alternatives you have in mind. To keep the trust you have earned, step back when the person feels overwhelmed or paralysed by anxiety, then offer gentle, blame-free accountability once the anxiety recedes. If it seems they are never in a state to talk, it is time to seek professional help.

Conclusion of the article: PDA is a relatively little-known profile describing a subset of people with autism spectrum disorder, and it can significantly affect daily life. If you suspect you or someone you know has PDA, there is much you can do to help get unstuck: the first step is deep understanding of their difficulties and changing your own view of their persistence (the translation says "упорство", stubbornness/persistence); then build a trusting relationship, put them in the driver's seat of reaching their goals, and give patient, flexible support. Those with PDA do not choose to behave this way; with support and understanding they can be helped to live a full and meaningful life [#29]. The author's most important realization: her life will differ from neurotypical people's lives, she accepts this and will no longer demand that she "jump higher than her own head", because autistic people have specific physical and mental health risks; she will not wreck herself to look "normal" in someone's eyes. "Ставки слишком велики и мне кажется каждый человек имеет право знать об этом и принимать осознанный выбор" (The stakes are too high, and I think every person has the right to know about this and make an informed choice) [#575].

#### Seven autistic traits I thought I didn't have and how they actually showed up (neurodivergent_lou)
Purpose: a self-reflection template — for each trait, name the misconception that hid it, then list how it actually appears. Source: neurodivergent_lou, card series translated by Нейр∞тлично ("Перевод поста") [#321, img #321].

1. Social difficulties ("Социальные трудности").
   - *Why she assumed she lacked it:* she reasoned she could not be autistic because she socializes and communicates the same way as a family member of hers — "который, как я теперь думаю, аутичный" (who, as I now think, is autistic). The lesson: comparing yourself to a relative who is themselves (undiagnosed) autistic makes your differences look typical [#322].
   - *How it actually showed up* [#322]:
     - needing to prepare scripts in her head before interacting;
     - a feeling of never fitting in, like an alien on a foreign planet;
     - complete exhaustion from socializing, even after an event she enjoyed;
     - inability to figure out social hierarchy;
     - for a while having a special interest in human behaviour and body language as a way to "компенсировать" (compensate for) not understanding social cues.
2. Sensory issues ("Сенсорные проблемы").
   - *Why she assumed she lacked it:* she assumed everyone else perceives sensory stimuli with the same intensity she does, and that she simply "плохо с этим справляюсь" (copes with it badly). The lesson: without a comparison point, one's own heightened sensory experience is read as a personal failure to cope rather than a difference [#323].
   - *How it actually showed up* [#323]:
     - tearing all the labels off clothes;
     - difficulty with certain foods because of their texture — the very thought of eating a marshmallow makes her want to scream;
     - becoming suddenly irritated by many overlapping sounds;
     - smelling other people's perfume from far away;
     - craving certain sensory sensations, for example fizzy drinks, weighted blankets or loud music (i.e. sensory seeking, not only avoidance).
3. Need for routine ("Потребность в рутине").
   - *Why she assumed she lacked it:* she thought her need for routine and predictability was just her "упрямость и эгоизм" (stubbornness and selfishness) — a character judgement rather than a trait [#324].
   - *How it actually showed up* [#324]:
     - needing to take the same route every time;
     - difficulty with changing seasons, e.g. still wearing a warm coat in summer;
     - eating the same food over and over until suddenly hating it and never eating it again;
     - hating it when the supermarket rearranges where products are;
     - hating it when product packaging changes.
4. Stimming ("Стимминг").
   - *Why she assumed she lacked it:* she assumed that since she did not flap her hands, she did not stim; she had not realized stimming can be varied [#325].
   - *How it actually showed up* [#325]:
     - repeating phrases over and over;
     - twirling hair around her fingers;
     - walking on tiptoe;
     - watching moving objects;
     - cracking joints — the card ends this list with "и т.д." (an open-ended "and the like"), so the list is explicitly non-exhaustive.
5. Special interests ("Специальные интересы").
   - *Why she assumed she lacked it:* growing up she saw autistic special interests portrayed mostly as trains or numbers (she adds that both are absolutely valid); she also believed that for something to count as a special interest she had to know "absolutely everything" about it [#326].
   - *How it actually showed up* [#326]:
     - having interests considered "мейнстримными" (mainstream);
     - being so absorbed in interests that she forgets to eat and drink;
     - a feeling of body and mind "зажигаются" (lighting up) when thinking about the special interest;
     - collecting screenshots related to her interests.
6. Literal understanding ("Понимание вещей буквально").
   - *Why she assumed she lacked it:* she thought she did not take phrases literally because she knew that when people say "льет как из ведра" (it's pouring as if from a bucket — an idiom for heavy rain) they do not mean water pours from the sky out of buckets. The lesson: understanding set idioms does not rule out literal reading of indirect speech [#327].
   - *How it actually showed up* [#327]:
     - when people said "возьми стул" (take a chair — meaning "have a seat"), she thought they meant pick the chair up, not sit on it;
     - when people said the dishwasher needs fixing, she thought it was just a statement of fact (not an implied request to fix it);
     - when someone said "увидимся позже" (see you later), she thought they meant they would actually see her later that day, not just saying goodbye.
7. Meltdowns ("Мелтдауны").
   - *Why she assumed she lacked it:* she thought her autistic meltdowns were panic attacks; as an undiagnosed autistic person she did not realize that what she experienced was in fact an autistic meltdown (this echoes and confirms the fuller meltdown-vs-panic-attack comparison in the existing topic file) [#328].
   - *How it actually showed up* [#328]:
     - a feeling that sensations are turned up to the maximum — "на 11 из 10" (11 out of 10);
     - feeling in control all day but exploding as soon as she gets home (a delayed release after holding it together);
     - these states can last longer and need more recovery time;
     - outward expression can include crying, inability to communicate, aggression — again ending open-ended with "и т. д.", so the list is non-exhaustive.

### Detailed insights

#### "If you think you might be autistic, adapt your life as if you are"
neurodivergent_lou's main advice to people wondering whether they are autistic — which she notes oddly always seems controversial — is: if you think you may be autistic, help yourself as if you are [#310, #311]. Her reasoning:
- Non-autistic people generally do not spend months and months wondering whether they are autistic; people know their own brains best. So if you think you are autistic, use accommodations as if you are [#312].
- Autistic people often feel they must simply survive without any adaptations — if they are physically able, they should "just cope". Maybe that has been so until now, but being physically able does not mean you should. Pushing yourself just because you physically can may lead to meltdowns, exhaustion, inability to do what you want to do [#313].
- What is the worst that can happen if someone considers themselves autistic and implements strategies to support their needs, and it later turns out they are not autistic? The only result is that this person will have strategies that help them adapt and meet their needs [#314].

#### Literal reading of diagnostic criteria and screening questions
neurodivergent_lou points out that it often seems overlooked that autistic people may take diagnostic criteria, questionnaires and assessment questions literally. Because of how the questions and criteria are worded, autistic people sometimes do not understand how their experience fits the framework, so they may not realize they are autistic; this problem especially affects multiply marginalized people [#400, #401]. Part of the autistic experience can be literal perception of information, which probably extends to autism criteria and questionnaires. Autistic people may think in black-and-white categories, making it hard to see the "grey zone" in a question or statement, so they are more likely to assume they do not have an autistic trait when in fact they do [#402].

The channel author gives a concrete screening example from her own experience (an excerpt from a list she is writing, "What to consider when working with a neurodivergent patient (a neurodivergent patient's view)"). If the specialist doing the autism screening cannot dig into a question or ask for details to get a full ("true") answer, this can happen: the specialist asks whether the person has problems with socks, and the person answers "no" because literally they have no problem. But on clarification it may turn out there is no problem because they do not wear socks, or because they found particular socks of a particular brand, bought 20 pairs since they are the perfect height, do not squeeze and have no annoying seam or embroidered patterns — and now they have no problems with socks. She adds that this is just one example; literal perception is not limited to it, and an autistic person's true answer to the socks question may also simply be "I don't feel discomfort wearing any socks" [#805]. The lesson for screeners: follow up on "no" answers by asking what the person means and how they manage.

A related humorous first-person piece (canenotabeltofunction on tumblr, translated by Anne Green Moon) shows the same blind spot for sensory traits: "Я не могу быть аутичным, у меня нет сенсорных особенностей" (I can't be autistic, I don't have sensory issues), followed by "Также я:" (Also me:) and a list of sensory reactions [#444]:
- the person who recommended I try frozen grapes is my enemy;
- gagging when trying to eat freeze-dried raspberries;
- "can't sleep, I'm wearing a T-shirt";
- this yogurt has fruit pieces in it, so I will let it rot at the back of the fridge and starve before I eat it;
- if someone touches me right now I will become a safety threat;
- jumping off furniture;
- something is rustling somewhere in the next room, and if I don't find and destroy the source I will never sleep again;
- this person's car headlights were too bright for 0.5 seconds, so now I'll have a migraine for the next 4 hours;
- my hands are wet, MY HANDS ARE WET, MAKE IT STOP;
- what do you mean the clothes are dry? They are still damp, how can you not feel it?;
- two pebbles rubbed together and made a sound that made me think I'd broken a tooth by clenching my jaw too hard;
- if I'm made to wear jeans 0.3 seconds after crossing the threshold of my home, I'll start crying;
- frosted glass = evil;
- yes, I'll "waste" money buying peeled garlic because peeling garlic makes my hands sticky and I hate that;
- washing my hands every 5 minutes while cooking because I can't stand having anything on my hands.

The channel's caveat on this piece: your experience and opinion may differ; sensory features are a criterion from section B of the DSM, not every autistic person has them, and sensory features on their own are not a 100% indicator of autism [#444].

#### Misattributing autistic traits to other conditions
Before realizing she was autistic, neurodivergent_lou attributed her traits to other causes: communication differences → "just social anxiety"; executive-function difficulties → laziness; autistic burnout → depression; meltdowns → panic attacks [#483]. This matches checklist item 17 (a history of mental-health diagnoses that never felt quite right and/or ineffective medication attempts) [#475].

#### The broader autism phenotype (BAP)
A post forwarded from NO STIGMA (citing a Frontiers in Psychiatry article) explains the broader autism phenotype ("Расширенный фенотип аутизма", РФА) [#354]:
- Definition. It describes the subclinical range of autistic traits — traits not pronounced enough for an ASD diagnosis. A phenotype is a set of observable characteristics. People with BAP have autistic traits that do not significantly affect their quality of life. BAP is not an official diagnosis but a description of a set of traits, which show up differently in each person. People with BAP have social, nonverbal, verbal, behavioural, sensory and relational signs of autism, but not enough for an ASD diagnosis; they most often look like neurotypicals (non-autistic people) and need little or no support. [#354]
- The concept is debated. Some autistic self-advocates and experts consider it in conflict with the neurodiversity model; others consider it consistent with it. [#354]
- Relation to diagnostic criteria. An ASD diagnosis requires specific criteria in social interaction, verbal and nonverbal expression, relationships, behaviour and sensory processing; people with BAP may have autistic traits in several or even all of these categories and still need almost no support. [#354]
- Traits BAP may include [#354]:
  - difficulties with verbal and nonverbal communication [#354];
  - delayed speech development in childhood [#354];
  - a tendency toward rigidity, e.g. a need for routine [#354];
  - aloofness/detachment [#354];
  - excessive or insufficient sensory sensitivity [#354];
  - fixation on specific interests [#354].
- How BAP differs from autism. Unlike a formal autism diagnosis, which requires specific criteria, the range of BAP presentations is much wider. Both BAP traits and autistic traits appear within the first year of life. [#354]
- The masking problem. It is currently impossible to reliably distinguish BAP from so-called "high-functioning" autism, which feeds skepticism about BAP's legitimacy as a condition separate from ASD. This may be related to masking — a coping strategy autistic people use to survive in an ableist world. Masking, literally hiding or suppressing autistic traits, can make a clinically autistic person look like someone with BAP, leading to a missed autism diagnosis. [#354]
- Can someone with BAP later be diagnosed autistic? Signs of autism can appear in the first years of life; an autistic person may have BAP-level traits that do not reach ASD level early on. Later, when difficulties at school or work become more serious, a person with BAP may be diagnosed with autism. Chronic stress can lead to autistic burnout, which worsens skills and the ability to mask. But many people with BAP signs will never be diagnosed with ASD. [#354]
- Heritability. Research has found a very significant genetic/hereditary component of BAP. Studies show that [#354]:
  - relatives of people with ASD are more likely than average to show autistic or BAP traits [#354];
  - parents with BAP traits more often have several autistic children than parents without such traits [#354];
  - families with two or more autistic children more often have relatives with BAP than families with only one autistic child [#354].

#### Autistic psychiatrists recognizing autism in themselves and others
The channel author summarizes a qualitative study published 30 Oct 2024 by Cambridge University Press (BJPsych Open), "Autistic psychiatrists' experiences of recognising themselves and others as autistic" [#394]:
- Many autistic psychiatrists do not immediately realize their neurodivergence; it often happens gradually, through work with patients or observing their own children.
- Psychiatrists unaware of their own autism may underestimate or miss autistic signs in patients, because they may perceive some autistic traits as "normal" based on their own experience.
- The limited, stereotyped picture of autism learned in training can stop psychiatrists applying it to themselves and, consequently, to patients with less obvious presentations.
- Recognizing their own autism often leads to a "revolution" in their understanding and diagnosis of autism, letting them see a wider spectrum of presentations and notice nuances they used to miss.
- All participants reported being able to quickly recognize autistic patients and easily build good rapport with them after recognizing their own autistic identity; difficulties recognizing autism had occurred before that, especially when patients showed autistic traits and experiences similar to their own.
- Conclusion drawn by the author: unawareness of one's own autism among specialists can lead to under- or misdiagnosis of autism in patients, especially with less obvious presentations, while awareness helps them recognize it faster.
- Many autistic psychiatrists do not disclose their diagnosis because of stigma even within the profession, which the author reads as a need for a more supportive, inclusive atmosphere in medicine; the study also points to a need for better autism education in medical schools, including a broader picture of how varied its presentations are.
A participant quoted in the post: "Если мы говорим, когда видим пациента, "ну, я тоже так делаю", даже если мы говорим это про себя... мы говорим это, потому что думаем, что мы не аутисты... на самом деле мы даже не знаем так ли это, и многие из нас оказались аутистами... так как же мы можем точно диагностировать пациентов, если не распознаем себя?" (If, when we see a patient, we say "well, I do that too", even if we say it to ourselves... we say it because we think we are not autistic... in fact we don't even know whether that's so, and many of us turned out to be autistic... so how can we accurately diagnose patients if we don't recognize ourselves?) [#394].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The plain-language criteria series is only Part 1 (category A); category B explanations were not part of this topic [#138].
- The diagnosis-benefits post is explicitly personal experience by a non-specialist; the claim that "scientists believe" patients with autoimmune problems or hEDS/MCAS/POTS should be checked for neurodivergence (and vice versa) is relayed without a cited study [#575].
- Sensory features are a section B criterion that not every autistic person has, and they are not by themselves a 100% indicator of autism [#444].
- BAP is a contested concept (seen by some as conflicting with the neurodiversity model) and cannot currently be reliably distinguished from so-called "high-functioning" autism; many people with BAP signs will never be diagnosed with ASD [#354].
- PDA is not an officially recognized diagnosis (not in DSM-5); the community-preferred alternative name is "persistent drive for autonomy". The article is a translated coaching article, not research; its claim of biological, physiological and sometimes genetic causes behind PDA behaviour is stated without cited studies [#29, article #29].
- (!) UNCLEAR: two passages of the PDA translation are garbled — the ending of sign 1 ("что удерживает их в чувстве “в жизни.") and the Russian name given for ODD [#29].
- Meltdown/panic distinctions are framed as features that "may be more specific" to one or the other, not hard rules; autistic people can also have genuine panic attacks [#484, #487, #489].
- (!) UNCLEAR: in the checklist, item 2 is coded B2, A1-masking with a trailing 2, where the trailing "2" appears to be the footnote marker for the masking note rather than a criterion [#479].
