## Interoception, alexithymia and naming emotions

### What this covers

This topic gathers everything the channel Нейропряность taught about interoception — the sense of what is happening inside the body — and its consequences for autistic and ADHD people: trouble noticing hunger, thirst, heat, pain and breathing (homeostasis), trouble with self-regulation and emotion regulation, alexithymia (difficulty recognizing and naming emotions), empathy and relationships, emotional dysregulation, and the misreading of bodily arousal as emotions. It also collects the practical tools the channel offered: interoception exercises (mindful check-ins, body scan, interoception on the go, a heart-rate exercise), visual aids for naming feelings (feelings wheel, list of feelings, intensity indicator) and the idea of describing emotions in one's own non-standard terms [#20, #37, #38, #47, #49, #186, #284, #379, #799].

According to the source, this matters because many neurodivergent people only feel an emotion once it is already intense, cannot catch overload in time and so slide into meltdown or shutdown, neglect basic body needs, and are misjudged as cold or unempathetic, while understanding emotions is "key" to working through most traumas and difficulties [#47, #49, #284, #799].

### Core ideas

- Interoception is an internal GPS. It tells us we are hungry, cold, thirsty and also underpins emotional awareness; it has a physical and an emotional component (see *Two components of interoception*) [#20, #47, #186].
- Autistic and ADHD people often have interoceptive difficulties, which show up as forgetting to eat or drink, not noticing one is holding one's breath, or overheating; these break homeostasis and self-regulation (see *Six consequences of poor interoceptive awareness*) [#37, #186].
- Alexithymia is closely linked to interoception. Bodily sensations are tightly bound up with emotions, so improving interoception helps with recognizing emotions; Nastya recommends mastering interoceptive awareness first as the base for emotional awareness [#47, #49].
- Emotions are often felt only at high intensity (at least 8/10), so the stage of mild irritation is missed and one jumps straight to anger [#49].
- Regulate the nervous system first. Since overload is hard to notice, an early step in managing alexithymia is nervous-system regulation, including sensory regulation, to create safety and leave fight/flight/freeze [#47].
- Alexithymia is not absence of emotion or empathy; it is a feature of emotional awareness and of brain–body connection that becomes easier with understanding and support [#799].
- Visual aids and own-language descriptions help name feelings: the feelings wheel, list of feelings and intensity indicator; and allowing people to describe emotions as textures, colours, body sensations or energy rather than forcing "standard" terms [#49, #284, #379, #380, #381, #799].
- Interoception is how the body keeps homeostasis through non-automatic action: signals like hunger, thirst or cold call us to act, and "identify the need + respond" is the basis of self-regulation; when the first link fails, neurodivergent people risk dehydration, burns, sleep problems, UTIs and missed pain (see *Nine ways interoceptive difficulties disrupt homeostasis*) [#37].
- Arousal can be misattributed to the wrong cause (e.g. post-run heartbeat read as attraction), which is especially relevant to people who already find emotions hard to identify; emotion = arousal + the story we tell about it (bridge and adrenaline studies), and for neurodivergent people sensory overload is a common unrecognized source of irritability [#38].

- Naming an emotion goes from general to specific. Both handouts group feeling words under the same six core feelings: anger (Злость / Гнев), strength (Сила), sadness (Грусть), happiness (Счастье), fear (Страх) and calm (Спокойствие). You first pick the broad family and then a more precise word inside it (see *Feelings wheel*) [#383, #384].
- The wheel is a starting point, not a full catalogue. Its caption says it "не охватывает все возможные чувства" ("does not cover all possible feelings"). It is still an excellent starting point for identifying and describing your emotions [#384].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Homeostasis–interoception–self-regulation model (article "Autism, ADHD and homeostasis")
Source: the article "Аутизм, СДВГ и гомеостаз" ("Autism, ADHD and homeostasis: what you should know") by Megan Anna Neff, translated by Nastya for the channel [#37].

Entry self-check. The post and article open with three questions: do you forget to eat or drink water because you don't feel hunger or thirst; have you ever realised you are not breathing and need to take a breath; have you overheated because you didn't notice you were too hot? If so, "Возможно, у вас есть трудности с интероцепцией" ("Perhaps you have difficulties with interoception"). The article sets out to connect autism, ADHD and homeostasis through interoception, defining the two terms first [#37].

1. Homeostasis — definition and example. "Гомеостаз - это способность организма поддерживать постоянную внутреннюю среду." ("Homeostasis is the body's ability to maintain a constant internal environment.") Example: an adult's resting core body temperature is widely known to be 36.1–36.6 °C. If it rises just one degree above that range we have a fever; if it drops just one degree below, we become hypothermic. The body must stay within a specific range to perform the functions needed for survival, and it keeps core temperature there by maintaining homeostasis [#37].

2. What the body keeps in balance. Besides temperature, the organs regulate (the article's list, which ends "and much more"): (1) body temperature; (2) water and moisture levels; (3) CO2 and O2 levels; (4) blood sugar; (5) blood pressure; (6) mineral and nutrient levels; (7) hormone levels; (8) circadian rhythms [#37].

3. Why balance in both directions matters. Too-low blood sugar means cells do not get enough energy to function, but too-high blood sugar can damage cells. Too-low blood pressure does not let blood circulate to all parts of the body, but too-high pressure can rupture vessels. Maintaining equilibrium is therefore essential for survival [#37].

4. Automatic vs non-automatic mechanisms — where interoception comes in. The body has automatic mechanisms for homeostasis — sweating when hot, releasing insulin after a heavy meal. But it also depends on *non-automatic* processes: it sends signals such as hunger or thirst to indicate it needs food or water. Responding to these signals and making sure the body gets what it needs is how interoceptive perception connects to homeostasis. The article's framing question: if the body maintains so many balanced systems, how does it know when balance is disturbed? Answer: through interoception [#37].

5. Interoception — one of eight sensory systems. "Интероцепция - одна из восьми основных чувственных систем." ("Interoception is one of the eight main sensory systems.") Everyone knows the five senses (sight, hearing, touch, taste, smell), but there are three hidden ones many people overlook: balance (the ability to sense gravity), proprioception (knowing where our body is in space) and interoception (the ability to track internal states such as hunger and thirst). Interoception senses internal states of being: hunger, thirst, temperature, discomfort, pain, the need to use the toilet, breathing rate, sleepiness, wakefulness and even emotional states such as stress, fear and sadness. The body does this through many kinds of sensory cells called chemoreceptors distributed throughout the organism, so interoception is critical for the body to understand what it must do to stay in homeostatic balance [#37].

6. Link to autism and ADHD. Neurodivergence often brings sensory-processing difficulties; sensitivity to light, sound and touch in autistic and ADHD people is well known — but interoception is one of the three overlooked systems. Neurodivergent people can have difficulty processing input from all senses, including interoception, so autistic and ADHD people may struggle to tell whether they are hungry, thirsty, tired, hot, cold, uncomfortable or in pain, whether they are breathing properly, when they need the toilet, what emotions they are feeling, and even whether their leg has "fallen asleep" or that they might be hurting themselves [#37].

7. Self-regulation = identify + respond. When it is hard to understand what the body needs, we cannot act to support it. The ability to (1) identify what the body needs and (2) respond to those needs is, per the article, the basis of self-regulation. So difficulties with interoception and homeostasis mean difficulties self-regulating both body and emotions — one reason why interoception and building interoceptive awareness are such important components of support for many autistic and ADHD children and adults. Interoceptive signals "call us to act": thirst moves a person to drink, keeping the ideal hydration/electrolyte balance; hunger moves them to eat, keeping blood sugar balanced; cold moves them to put on a coat, keeping them in the ideal temperature range [#37].

8. Self-regulation without interoception. "Без осознания интероцепции это похоже на поход по горам без карты и компаса." ("Without interoceptive awareness it is like hiking in the mountains without a map and compass.") Without it we hardly know what the body needs here and now, which makes it hard to care for the body in ways that help us self-regulate and calm down. Interoceptive difficulty makes it harder to regulate emotions, attention and behaviour and can cause problems with eating, toileting and other physical aspects. "Понимание сигналов тела является основой гомеостаза, саморегуляции и самозащиты." ("Understanding body signals is the foundation of homeostasis, self-regulation and self-protection.") When interoceptive awareness is poor, the true source of discomfort cannot be pinpointed, so we cannot act to meet unmet needs and regulate our bodies [#37].

9. Summary of the article. Interoception — awareness of internal sensations like hunger, thirst and temperature — is key to responding to the body's signals and keeping balance and homeostasis; noticing these sensations and acting on them is the basis of self-regulation. With interoceptive difficulties it is harder to give the body what it needs; knowing what interoception is makes it easy to see how autism and ADHD can affect homeostasis and self-regulation [#37].

#### Nine ways interoceptive difficulties disrupt homeostasis
From the same article, introduced as "an incomplete list" ("неполный список") of how interoceptive problems can affect homeostasis. People with interoception problems may have difficulty [#37]:
1. Knowing whether they are hungry — so they may eat too much, too little, or not enough of what the body needs, which can disturb blood sugar and micronutrient levels.
2. Knowing whether they are thirsty — usually leading to dehydration, but it can also lead to fluid overload.
3. Sensing temperature, both surface and internal — raising the risk of hypothermia and hyperthermia, and making burns more likely.
4. Knowing when and how fast to breathe — which the article suggests may be behind the high rate of apnoea in autistic people.
5. Regulating blood pressure according to physical activity — the article suggests neurodivergence may play a role in conditions such as postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS).
6. Regulating stress and anxiety — leading to greater susceptibility to anxiety disorders, burnout, PTSD and other mental illnesses.
7. Knowing when they are tired and need to go to bed — making neurodivergent people more prone to sleep disorders.
8. Sensing internal pain or discomfort — so neurodivergent people may not realise when they need medical help or, conversely, think they need it when they don't.
9. Knowing when they need the toilet — which can lead to urinary tract infections and constipation.

#### Interoception → action → result chain (two diagrams)
Two translated diagrams in the homeostasis article, titled "Интероцепция, Гомеостаз и Саморегуляция" ("Interoception, homeostasis and self-regulation"), with the caption: interoception provides information that helps us act in ways that create stability. Each runs through the same five stages: feel a sensation → body state or emotion → called to action → action → result [#37].
- Diagram 1 (hunger): sensation — rumbling stomach; body state — hunger; called to action; action — eat; result — the unpleasant sensations go away and the body returns to balance. Explanation on the card: the body is out of balance (it needs food); it transmits this via an interoceptive signal, which mobilizes the person to meet the body's need so it can return to equilibrium (homeostasis) [#37].
- Diagram 2 (anxiety): sensation — trembling, tightness in the chest, fast heartbeat; emotion — anxiety; called to action; action — stimming, breathing exercises, using sensory calming tools; result — reduced anxiety. Explanation: here interoceptive signals help the person recognize their anxiety, which gives them the opportunity to act to bring the body back to balance [#37].
The article presents the two diagrams as examples of how interoceptive awareness is the key to homeostasis and self-regulation: in the first, perceiving hunger moves the person to behaviour that regulates the body's needs; in the second, interoceptive signals prompt the person to notice their anxiety [#37].

#### Two components of interoception
Source: the channel's presentation "Интероцепция и как ее улучшить" ("Interoception and how to improve it"), based on Megan Anna Neff's article "Interoception" (Neurodivergentinsights.com), shared after an online meeting on interoception [#186].
1. Definition. Interoception is the unique ability to feel what is happening inside our body; it is an internal GPS that helps us orient in physical and emotional space [#186].
2. Why it matters. It not only informs us about the body's current state but also lets us respond actively and caringly to its needs, which is essential for physical and emotional wellbeing [#186].
3. Two components. *Physical* interoception is feeling changes in the body's organs and systems; *emotional* interoception is the ability to be aware of and manage one's emotions [#186].
4. Link to self-care. Developing interoception skills makes it easier to perceive and respond to the body's needs, which promotes better self-care and "harmony with oneself" [#186].
5. Autistic specifics. For autistic people interoception can be especially hard; this causes problems recognizing and reacting to body signals, leading to stress, anxiety and difficulties with emotional regulation — so it is particularly important to develop this ability [#186].

#### Six consequences of poor interoceptive awareness
The presentation says autistic people face interoceptive difficulties more often than the general population, and that some traits usually attributed to autism itself are in fact the result of interoceptive difficulties: difficulty processing emotions (linked to increased anxiety); alexithymia (difficulty identifying and describing emotions); and empathy and perspective-taking (difficulty understanding another's viewpoint when we struggle to understand our own bodily experience). Interoceptive difficulties also make it hard to identify pain, hunger and thirst, which disrupts homeostasis, self-regulation and physical wellbeing and can leave an unclear sense of self and others, affecting health, relationships and emotion regulation [#186]. It then lists six consequences:

1. Difficulty maintaining homeostasis. Homeostasis is a system's striving to remain in balance; its hallmark is feedback loops, like a thermostat regulating room temperature, and the body runs thousands of them. Examples: glucose homeostasis regulated by insulin; thermoregulation through sweating and evaporation when overheated. The body responds automatically (signalling hunger or thirst), but interoceptive *awareness* — listening to those signals and acting on them — is also needed. Interoceptive signals mobilize us to act: thirst makes us drink (hydration and electrolyte balance), hunger makes us eat (blood sugar), cold makes us put on clothes (body temperature). Interoceptive difficulties make it harder to give the body what it needs. The fuller treatment of this point is the article in *Homeostasis–interoception–self-regulation model* above [#186].
2. Self-regulation. Problems with homeostasis are directly tied to problems with self-regulation, described as "поход в горы без карты и компаса" ("a hike in the mountains without a map and compass"). Interoceptive awareness is the virtual map telling us what the body needs right now; without it, it is hard to recognize signals and therefore to care for and soothe the body. Interoceptive difficulties directly affect regulation of emotions, attention and behaviour, with knock-on problems in eating habits and physical wellbeing. Body signals are a foundation of self-care and self-protection; when awareness is poor, the true source of discomfort becomes unclear, so one cannot respond effectively [#186].
3. Emotion regulation. Defined as an important subset of self-regulation: the ability to be aware of one's emotions and intervene to calm the body; any activity that reduces the intensity of an emotion counts as emotion regulation. Its effectiveness depends on perceiving one's emotional states, and interoception supplies awareness of the physical signals that *precede* emotional reactions. Regulation skills work better when one can recognize early signs of rising intensity. Because interoceptive difficulty breaks the link between physical signals and emotions, the presentation concludes that resolving interoception problems is the first step in building effective emotion-regulation skills [#186].
4. Health and wellbeing. Health depends on responding adequately to body signals and judging their meaning. People with limited interoception may face: (a) *hydration* — not noticing thirst leads to drinking too little, causing headaches and digestive problems; (b) *temperature regulation* — misreading body signals leads to wrong responses to temperature change, raising the risk of frostbite or heat stroke; (c) *nutrition* — trouble sensing hunger leads to irregular meals and an uneven diet; (d) *pain and injury awareness* — weakened pain signals slow the reaction to physical problems and raise the risk of complications. Managing health therefore needs not only knowledge about the body but the ability to respond to its needs [#186].
5. Sense of self. Interoceptive awareness builds a coherent sense of one's own personality and the awareness that we are not just a mind but a body. Kelly Mahler is quoted: "Интероцепция придает нам ощущение того, что 'это я; это мое тело;" ("Interoception gives us the sense that 'this is me; this is my body; this is how I feel'"). Research cited in the presentation says the insula activates during self-reflection, such as looking at oneself in a mirror or at photographs, and damage to it can affect a person's ability to perceive themselves as an individual [#186].
6. Relationships (and empathy). Poor interoceptive awareness affects understanding oneself and therefore understanding others; emotional awareness arising from interoception is the foundation of empathy. Studies of insula damage link it with reduced perspective-taking and adaptation to social norms. On empathy: when we struggle to recognize our own emotions and body states we have less personal experience to draw on to understand others; the use of similar neural connections for empathy towards oneself and others is sometimes called the shared brain network of empathy. On perspective-taking: difficulty understanding and distinguishing one's own emotions leads to significant difficulty understanding others' emotions (Cook, Brewer, Shah and Bird, 2013), which can cause serious social misunderstandings [#186].

#### Four ways to improve interoception
The presentation stresses that interoception can be improved with practice and support; it is a process requiring regular practice and patience [#186].
1. Practise interoceptive-awareness activities — body scanning, deep breathing, progressive muscle relaxation, awareness of feelings and mindful check-ins. Regular practice helps you perceive body signals and understand and manage emotions better [#186].
2. Create conditions and adaptations for your needs — adapt your environment so it supports bodily needs and takes interoceptive difficulties into account. The concrete adaptation offered: timers as reminders to eat, drink/hydrate and take sensory breaks [#186].
3. Seek neurodivergence-affirming support — professionals or others who understand neurodivergent people can offer recommendations and strategies [#186].
4. Be patient and self-compassionate — improvement takes time; everyone's interoceptive experience is unique, and it is fine to go your own way at your own pace [#186].
General notes on the activities: research shows interoceptive awareness can be developed with practice; improvement can help emotion regulation and physical and emotional health; try the exercises one at a time, mastering them gradually. The three activities are *Mindful check-ins*, *Body scan* and *Interoception on the go* [#186].

#### Mindful check-ins
Goal: improve interoception through conscious attention to physical sensations. Characteristics: can be brief (10 seconds) or long; done without judgement or evaluation; aimed at identifying and observing sensations. Key point: do them regularly throughout the day to develop awareness of bodily and emotional experiences [#186].
- Variant a — with an external stimulus (a drink): (1) hold a hot or cold drink, focusing on the sensations; (2) let go and notice the changes; (3) repeat [#186].
- Variant b — with physical activity: (1) do an exercise (stretching, squats and similar); (2) note changes in the body (temperature, breathing); (3) stop moving and observe the sensations; (4) repeat the exercise [#186].

#### Body scan
Goal: improve interoceptive awareness through sequential observation of sensations in the body [#186].
1. Find a comfortable, quiet place.
2. Start at the feet and move gradually up to the head.
3. Focus on each body part, noting sensations without judging.
4. Use deep breathing to concentrate.
5. When distracted, gently return attention to the body.
Key points: use specific *descriptive* words instead of evaluative ones; avoid general terms and detail the sensation — examples of descriptions: soft, tense, hot, buzzing ("мягкий, напряженный, горячий, жужжащий"); practise regularly. Recommendations: be patient and gentle with yourself; start with a partial scan and move gradually to a full one. The exercise develops finer perception of bodily sensations and a better body–mind connection [#186].

#### Interoception on the go
For people short on time: quick check-ins during the day that build the "muscles" of awareness of body sensations. It has two steps, and the first must be mastered before moving to the second [#186].
1. Step one — quick interoceptive check, once or twice a day for a few moments. Two ways: (1) set 2 timers to go off during the day as "moments of interoceptive check"; or (2) attach the check to an activity you do several times a day, preferably one that creates sensations — washing hands, washing dishes, showering, after eating. During the check simply notice the sensations you have during that activity [#186].
2. Step two — interoceptive connectors. Once more experienced, start linking those sensations to specific body states and emotions. Over time you begin to map which sensations correspond to hunger, irritability, satisfaction and so on [#186].

#### Mindful nervous-system stabilization exercise (Kelly Mahler)
Taken, per the presentation, from Kelly Mahler's book "Восьмая сенсорная система" ("The eighth sensory system"). It increases body awareness and teaches lowering nervous-system activity (calming the nervous system). It splits into mini-exercises that grow harder, and gives informal feedback on the body's state without complex equipment. Goals: (1) increase awareness of heart rate; (2) practise strategies for lowering nervous-system activity. Equipment: any device that records pulse — smart watch, Fitbit or finger oximeter — or measuring with your finger and a watch [#186].
1. Estimate, then measure. Do 30–50 seconds of activity that raises the pulse (jumping, push-ups, running in place). First pay attention to your pulse and try to estimate your heart rate; then measure it and see how close you were. Track this over time to follow improvement in interoceptive awareness [#186].
2. Lower it in 60 seconds. After doing step one several times, add step two: measure your pulse (finger oximeter or smartphone) and see how low you can bring it within 60 seconds. Experiment with different things — slowing your breathing, focusing on something calming and so on — and keep recording results to follow progress over time [#186].
Caveat from the presentation: for autistic people, strategies to lower nervous-system activity do not always work as well, so it may take time and many experiments to find the strategy that suits you [#186].

#### Feelings wheel (Gloria Willcox, 1982)
A circular chart translated by the channel; the card says it helps describe emotions more precisely, does not cover all possible feelings, but is an excellent starting point for identifying and describing emotions. It is offered as a visual helper for when it is hard to explain one's emotions or feelings, with a printable PDF version; the original article on the wheel is the "Колесо чувств: Как распознать свои чувства" ("Feelings wheel: how to recognize your feelings") post [#379, #49] [#379]. Structure: six core feelings in the centre, each with an inner ring of six more specific feelings and an outer ring of six even more specific ones (reading from the image; inner and outer words are given in ring order) [#379]:
- Гнев (anger) — inner: враждебность (hostility), боль (hurt), свирепость (ferocity), напряжение (tension), придирчивость (fault-finding), надоедливость (annoyingness); outer: эгоистичность (selfishness), ревнивость (jealousy), ярость (rage), раздражение (irritation), скептичность (scepticism), фрустрация (frustration).
- Страх (fear) — inner: слабость (weakness), неуверенность (insecurity), растерянность (bewilderment), угроза (threat), тревожность (anxiety), отвергнутость (rejection); outer: бесполезность (uselessness), неполноценность (inadequacy), озадаченность (puzzlement), нервность (nervousness), беспокойство (worry), исключение (exclusion).
- Счастье (happiness) — inner: оптимизм (optimism), взволнованность (excitement), игривость (playfulness), удовольствие (pleasure), принятие (acceptance), интерес (interest); outer: надежда (hope), энергичность (energy), возбуждение (arousal/excitement), радость (joy), значимость (significance), любознательность (curiosity).
- Сила (strength/power) — inner: гордость (pride), сила (strength), смелость (courage), признание (recognition), верность (loyalty), влиятельность (influence); outer: уверенность (confidence), успешность (success), креативность (creativity), уважение (respect), преданность (devotion), сфокусированность (focus).
- Спокойствие (calm) — inner: связь (connection), доверие (trust), благодарность (gratitude), надежность (reliability), вдумчивость (thoughtfulness), легкость (lightness); outer: причастность (belonging), чувствительность (sensitivity), любовь (love), безмятежность (serenity), расслабление (relaxation), безопасность (safety).
- Грусть (sadness) — inner: раскаяние (remorse), уязвимость (vulnerability), одиночество (loneliness), депрессия (depression), посредственность (mediocrity), смущение (embarrassment); outer: вина (guilt), ранимость (sensitivity to hurt), изолированность (isolation), несчастье (unhappiness), скука (boredom), стыд (shame).
How to use (from #49): when asked "how do you feel?" and you are confused, start from the centre and move outwards to a more precise word; the article also contains exercises for improving emotional awareness, and the author recommends starting with interoceptive awareness as the base [#49]. #### Describing emotions in one's own terms
From a post forwarded from the channel Кораблик Леськи (Leska). Purpose: for people who cannot express their emotions in "standard" language [#284].

Why naming emotions is hard for most people — five reasons listed by the author:
1. There is a huge number of differing and sometimes mutually exclusive definitions of every emotion.
2. There are no high-precision "reference samples" to check against — the illustrations and examples used in psychology often feel fake and exaggerated.
3. The intensity and expression of emotional reactions varies from individual to individual.
4. Many literary works romanticize actual mental disorders that seriously reduce quality of life and call them a "healthy emotional norm".
5. The same literature, and the media, ascribe to emotions qualities they do not actually have in practice.
Hence fitting felt emotions into "accepted terms" is a task of the "Божество" level (Deity-level) — like explaining a subject we don't understand well ourselves in a language we haven't properly learned and which changes from region to region [#284].

The approach: the author's own inquiries among people who struggle greatly to understand their emotions in "standard" language showed that much of the problem can be solved by the opposite of standardization — allowing oneself and others to describe emotions through any convenient images, i.e. "позволяя изъясняться на привычном, понятном человеку языке" ("letting a person speak in a language that is familiar and clear to them"). Seven modalities people use [#284]:
1. Texture — an emotion felt as liquid, dense, solid, ridged/embossed, gaseous.
2. Colour — not necessarily synaesthesia; it can be a "mental" colour, not a literal spot in the eye.
3. Body sensations — "warmth in the chest", "cold in the upper part of the head", "itching around the solar plexus".
4. Facial expression — some identify their emotions precisely through their own facial expressions, which arise somewhat involuntarily.
5. Chemical reactions — for example a bubbling, toxic acid, or an "outflow of warmth".
6. Movement of energy — some body part (or the whole body) feels like moving electricity, tension.
7. Images/metaphors — "a little cloud in the chest", "a river flowing through the whole body".
Claimed benefit: in the long run this approach improves understanding of oneself, one's needs and reactions, and also helps establish contact with other people [#284]. The autismservicesgroup text translated in #799 echoes this: not everyone experiences feelings through words — some feel them in the body, through energy, in patterns and cycles, or in silence, and all these ways are normal and legitimate [#799].

#### Twelve ways alexithymia can show up
A card series by neurodivergent_lou, forwarded from Нейр∞тлично. Definition card: alexithymia is difficulty recognizing, observing and describing one's sensations and emotions; non-autistic people can have it too, but it is more common in autistic people — about 50% of autistic people are thought to experience it (Milosavijevic et al., 2016). 2. Imagining how another person might feel in a given situation, because we have no idea what we actually feel [#606].
3. No emotional reactions to situations where others expect them — for example opening presents or a bereavement [#607].
4. Feeling tension build up in the body but not understanding which emotion it is [#607].
5. Experiencing emotions as physical pain [#608].
6. Having physical sensations — racing heart, butterflies in the stomach — but no emotion [#608].
7. Only being able to feel emotions when they are really strong; being struck by a sudden overwhelming wave of negativity [#609].
8. Talking about difficult things easily, because the related emotions are not felt [#609].
9. A constant sense of anxiety or tension from not knowing what exactly we feel [#610].
10. Experiencing alexithymia is likened to "смотреть телевизор в черно-белом цвете" ("watching TV in black and white"), unable to make out the colours — the specific emotions [#610].
11. Finding it easier to describe decisions in terms of logic rather than emotion; often answering questions with "I don't know" because we really don't know what we feel [#611].

#### Seven ways emotional dysregulation can look in autistic people
A second neurodivergent_lou card series forwarded from Нейр∞тлично, "Как может выглядеть эмоциональная дисрегуляция у аутичных людей?" ("What can emotional dysregulation look like in autistic people?") [#640]:
1. Trouble falling asleep because of the day's events. Strong emotions (anxiety, sadness, embarrassment) can prevent relaxing before sleep; we go over and over what we felt about a situation or how we think the day went, which hinders regulating the body and falling asleep [#641].
2. Trouble making decisions because of strong emotions. Example: in a restaurant, choosing from the menu can make us very anxious, which affects the ability to decide and can be overwhelming; executive-function difficulties and difficulty with change can also contribute [#642].
3. Trouble identifying specific emotions. Autistic people sometimes have alexithymia — difficulty noticing, recognizing and describing emotions; people with it may find it hard to distinguish emotional experiences from physical sensations. An autistic person may know they feel something very strong but not be able to say what, which causes confusion and overload [#643].
4. Physical symptoms from difficulty regulating emotions. Persistent physical symptoms such as gastrointestinal problems, general unexplained pain or exhaustion; and persistent signs of the body being in "fight or flight" — racing heart, dizziness, rapid breathing [#644].
5. Delayed emotional response. The author describes something significant happening while she feels complete numbness; the emotions may come only "спустя недели, месяцы или даже годы" ("weeks, months or even years later"). Conversely, something relatively minor may happen and she does not realise how much it affected her until weeks later her emotions suddenly spiral out of control [#645].
6. Internalizing emotional experiences as shame and guilt. Because we sometimes feel emotions very acutely, we may face judgement from others or end up in far-from-ideal situations; we then start internalizing — hiding inner tension and suffering — which sometimes leads to lower self-esteem and inner guilt and shame [#646].
7. Numbness. The opposite of intense emotions: total emotional numbness, where emotions are hard to feel at all and seem flat and monotonous; this is not easy to cope with [#647].

#### Alexithymia — what it is, what it is not, what helps
Text by autismservicesgroup, "Аутизм и алекситимия" ("Autism and alexithymia"), subtitle "when feelings are real but hard to name", translated by Nastya [#799].
- Definition: difficulty recognizing, understanding and describing one's emotions. "Это не отсутствие эмоций, а особенность эмоциональной осознанности." ("It is not an absence of emotions but a feature of emotional awareness.") [#799]
- How it may show up — it can be hard to: find words for feelings; understand what exactly you feel right now; distinguish similar emotions; explain your emotions to other people [#799].
- Why it is common in autism — autistic people often experience emotions differently because of: sensory-processing differences; interoception differences (sensing body signals from inside); a particular cognitive style; a habit of masking their emotions [#799].
- What it is not: lack of empathy; emotional coldness; indifference; a character flaw [#799].

#### Emotions and the emotion matrix (arousal × valence)

What emotions are. The article defines emotions as complex phenomena that interweave psychological states with physical sensations, spanning the joy of happiness, the depths of sadness, the intensity of anger, the acceleration of fear, the spark of surprise and the recoil of disgust. They are "much more than fleeting feelings": powerful forces that shape decisions, guide behaviour, influence interactions with others and play a critical role in overall health and wellbeing. The interdisciplinary study of emotions (psychology, neuroscience) tries to take their mechanisms apart [#38].

The author's autistic stance. #### Misattribution of arousal — the full article
Definition. Misattribution of arousal happens when we misidentify the cause of our emotional arousal; it illuminates the interplay between mind and body in forming emotions. "История, которую мы приписываем нашему возбуждению, значительно влияет на наш конечный эмоциональный опыт" ("The story we attribute to our arousal significantly influences our final emotional experience"). The theory explores how people can misread physiological arousal (a state of physiological alertness and activity) as caused by something else, such as sexual attraction, which often leads to distorted emotional experiences [#38].

Example — Lisa Feldman Barrett. In her book "Как создаются эмоции: Тайная жизнь мозга" ("How Emotions Are Made: The Secret Life of the Brain"), Barrett describes a first date on which she had a stomach flu: she did not realise how ill she was until the end of the date and attributed the butterflies in her stomach to attraction. The article calls this a great example of arousal and sensation being mistaken for attraction [#38].

Historical background. The study of misattribution dates to the 1970s, a significant period for research on human emotion. In a foundational 1974 study, Donald Dutton and Arthur Aron examined whether attraction becomes more likely in anxiety-provoking (arousing) situations. The phenomenon is therefore also called the "Эффект подвесного моста" ("suspension bridge effect"): environments that provoke high physiological arousal can lead to misinterpretation of emotions [#38].

#### Suspension bridge study (Dutton and Aron, 1974)
The article notes that the study reflects certain heteronormative biases of its 1970s design but still gave useful insight into emotional psychology [#38]. Procedure, step by step:
1. Two bridges. Male participants were asked to cross one of two bridges: a stable, modern bridge close to the ground, or a high, 450-foot (about 137 m), unstable suspension bridge that naturally provoked fear [#38].
2. Story task. After crossing, each man met a woman who asked him to write a story about a non-sexual, ambiguous picture; the stories were then analysed for sexual content [#38].
3. Contact offer. The woman then gave the men her contact details, supposedly for follow-up questions about her project [#38].
Results. Men who crossed the stable low bridge showed less interest in contacting the woman and wrote stories with less erotic material. Those who crossed the high unstable bridge, experiencing more arousal from its instability, more often expressed interest in the woman and often decided to call her later. Their adrenaline-raised arousal was misattributed to the woman's attractiveness — visible both in their greater willingness to initiate contact and in their more erotic stories. In essence they attributed the arousal from the bridge (faster heartbeat, an adrenaline "rush") to her attractiveness [#38].
Lessons the article draws. Our minds interpret physical sensations in fascinating ways; misattribution obscured the true source of the men's arousal, showing the power of context over emotional experience — the narratives we build around physical states can deeply shape emotional reactions and perceptions. The study highlights the link between physiological arousal, the narrative formed while experiencing it, and our emotional responses; external stimuli (crossing a shaky bridge) can strongly shape how we interpret internal emotions. Emotional reactions are therefore not entirely dependent on internal states but are also shaped by the environment, and the body's reactions can intertwine with and even transform emotional experience, leading us to interpret situations in unexpected ways [#38].

#### Consequences and the role in emotion regulation
Consequences. Understanding misattribution is described as vital because it strongly affects how we interpret emotions, with deep consequences for relationships and decision-making. It helps us see that emotional reactions are sometimes more about our immediate physical reactions to the environment than about the people or events in it. It also underlines the importance of self-awareness in regulating emotions — recognizing the true sources of feelings and responding more appropriately — which can lead to healthier relationships and more conscious choices [#38].

Role in regulation. "Распознавание истинного источника нашего возбуждения является ключом к более здоровому регулированию эмоций и более подлинным взаимодействиям." ("Recognizing the true source of our arousal is the key to healthier emotion regulation and more authentic interactions.") It helps distinguish emotions caused by external factors from those arising within us [#38].

The author's own example (sensory irritability). Neff writes that when she is sensorily overloaded she often feels heightened irritability. Before she understood this as sensory overload, she wrongly attributed the irritation to the people or situations around her. Now, with a better understanding of sensory regulation, she can identify the real cause of her discomfort more accurately: for example, the irritation she feels at the sound of her spouse crunching cereal comes from her sensory sensitivity — not from having suddenly developed a dislike of him (though before her "sensory enlightenment" she might have thought so). The lesson she draws: the theory is especially relevant to neurodivergent people, who often face many sensory triggers that cause unpleasant arousal; applying it lets us examine our emotional reactions more critically, which fosters emotional growth and improves our ability to communicate in both personal and professional life [#38].

#### Adrenaline study (Schachter and Singer, 1962) and the two-factor theory
The article places misattribution within a wider body of attribution theories — research on how people interpret and assign causes to their emotions and actions, which helps explain why people think, feel and behave as they do — and presents a second study that sheds light on misattribution [#38]. Procedure:
1. Injection. Participants were injected with epinephrine (adrenaline), producing physical arousal symptoms: rapid heartbeat, trembling, fast breathing. They did not know the true nature of the injection and believed it was a test of a vision drug [#38].
2. Informed vs uninformed groups. One group was told about possible side effects; the others were not [#38].
3. Confederate. Each participant was then placed in a room with a "confederate" (an actor playing a role in the experiment) who behaved either euphorically or irritably [#38].
Result. Participants' interpretation of their physical symptoms depended largely on the confederate's behaviour: those with the euphoric confederate perceived their arousal as happiness, those with the irritable one felt anger. Those who knew to expect side effects were less influenced by the confederate's emotions [#38].
Two critical conclusions the article draws:
1. Emotional contagion. Participants' emotional reactions to adrenaline shifted with the behaviour of the person in the room, and the unwarned were especially susceptible. When people have no explanation for their emotions, they tend to label feelings from their immediate context and are more open to the emotions of those around them [#38].
2. Beliefs shape the interpretation of emotions. Our beliefs or labels about bodily sensations shape emotional experience; the narrative the mind builds around physical sensations plays a significant role in how we ultimately interpret and live those emotions [#38].
Like the bridge study, this underlines the complexity of emotional processing and the importance of context and cognition [#38].

#### Misattribution of arousal FAQ (eight questions)
The article closes with a question-and-answer section offered as its summary [#38]:
1. What is the theory? When people experience physiological arousal they may misattribute its source, leading to wrong ideas about their emotions and behaviour.
2. What are its consequences? Significant: it can affect how people perceive their emotions and influence their decision-making and behaviour.
3. How are arousal and attraction connected? This link is a key element of the concept: people may confuse sensations of arousal with romantic or sexual attraction and change their emotional experience and behaviour accordingly.
4. How does this research add to our understanding of emotion? It shows the complexity of emotional experience and how people assign their arousal to specific emotional states, highlighting the role of cognitive processes in forming emotional reactions.
5. What is the two-factor theory of emotion? Proposed by Schachter and Singer: emotional experience depends both on physiological arousal and on cognitive interpretation; it is closely linked to misattribution because it stresses the role of interpretation.
6. Why is it relevant to love and relationships? People may misread arousal as signs of romantic attraction, which has consequences for how romantic connections are formed and evaluated.
7. How does it relate to cognitive dissonance? Both involve a mismatch between physiological arousal and cognitive interpretation; people may experience dissonance when they misattribute arousal and then try to reconcile the conflicting emotional and cognitive experiences.
8. What are the key findings on arousal and attraction? Arousal can influence perceived attractiveness and emotional experience in complex ways, giving insight into the interplay of physiological arousal and emotional interpretation.
Conclusion. Studies like the bridge experiment remind us of the complex nature of emotions: "Эмоции - это не только то, что мы чувствуем внутри; это также о том, как мы интерпретируем эти чувства в отношении к нашему окружению." ("Emotions are not only what we feel inside; they are also about how we interpret those feelings in relation to our surroundings.") Understanding this interplay helps us navigate our emotional world with more awareness and authenticity — a path of continuous learning [#38].

The fuller content of these ideas is in *Two components of interoception* and *Six consequences* above.

#### The autism–interoception paradox and the insula
The presentation describes a paradoxical picture: research shows that autistic people, despite *lower accuracy* of interoceptive data on objective tests (for example heartbeat-detection tests), often report *higher* interoceptive sensitivity and awareness of bodily sensations. The interpretation offered: autistic people may experience amplified signals while their ability to objectively measure and interpret those signals is less precise [#186].

The insula is called the "centre of interoception in the brain"; it is responsible for perceiving pain, emotions and awareness of body states. Research cited says people with high interoceptive awareness usually have a more developed insula, while those with weakened awareness show reduced grey matter and activity there. In autistic people the insula's response varies: some show hypoconnectivity (fewer connections) and reduced grey-matter volume, others hyperconnectivity, associated with heightened perception of interoceptive signals. The presentation cautions that autism manifests in diverse ways and brain-imaging results can be contradictory [#186].

#### Alexithymia and interoception: the link and the first step
Nastya's post describes alexithymia as a state in which identifying and describing feelings is hard: people may feel numb or experience many emotions at once, which makes understanding them difficult. Research, she writes, shows alexithymia is closely linked to interoception — "интероцепция — это наш внутренний GPS" ("interoception is our internal GPS") that tells us we are hungry, cold, thirsty. Since internal body sensations are closely tied to emotions, methods for improving interoception can help with being aware of and understanding emotions [#47].

Her own opinion: part of the difficulty of this state is that a person may struggle to prevent overload in time (because body sensations are impaired), and the overload can then lead to a meltdown or shutdown. Therefore one of the first pieces of advice for managing alexithymia is regulating the nervous system, including through sensory regulation. This is needed to create a condition of safety, which in turn helps the person switch from the "fight, flight, freeze" reaction to attending to sensations inside themselves [#47].

#### Why emotions are noticed late
In the feelings-wheel post Nastya writes that if you are confused when asked "how are you feeling?" you are not alone: many neurodivergent people struggle to identify emotions because "мы не чувствуем эмоцию до тех пор, пока ее интенсивность не станет хотя бы 8/10" ("we don't feel an emotion until its intensity reaches at least 8/10"). This is inconvenient because we can miss the moment we are slightly irritated and the next moment already feel anger and the urge to hit something. Identifying emotions is a process that requires patience and practice, and not everyone learns it right away; the author therefore recommends starting by mastering interoceptive awareness as an excellent base for emotional awareness. The article covers visual tools for identifying emotions and exercises for emotional awareness, with a "summary" column under each section and downloadable PDF tools [#49].

### Practical playbook

- Set timers to remind you to eat, drink and take sensory breaks; set two timers a day as interoceptive check moments [#186].
- Hook a 10-second check-in onto recurring sensory activities: handwashing, washing dishes, showering, after meals [#186].
- When scanning or checking in, use concrete descriptive words (soft, tense, hot, buzzing) instead of "good/bad" [#186].
- Before trying to name an emotion, calm the nervous system (including sensory regulation) so you move out of fight/flight/freeze [#47].
- To name a feeling: start at the wheel's centre (sadness, anger, fear, happiness, strength, calm), move outward to a more precise word, then rate its intensity 1–10 on the feelings indicator [#49, #379, #381].
- If standard words don't fit, describe the emotion by texture, colour, body sensation, facial expression, "chemistry", energy movement or an image [#284].
- When supporting someone with alexithymia: give time for the feeling to ripen, use visual aids and body maps, ask gently instead of demanding an immediate answer [#799].
- After physical exertion or stress, check whether arousal is being attributed to the wrong cause (e.g. a person rather than the run you just did) [#38].
- When irritated by a person, ask whether the real source is sensory overload (Neff's cereal-crunching example) before attributing it to them [#38].
- Place a feeling on the emotion matrix by asking two questions: how much energy (high/low arousal) and how pleasant (valence) [#38].

### Evidence and numbers

- About 50% of autistic people are thought to experience alexithymia (Milosavijevic et al., 2016, cited on the neurodivergent_lou card) [#605].
- Cook, Brewer, Shah and Bird (2013), cited in the presentation: difficulty understanding and distinguishing one's own emotions leads to significant difficulty understanding others' emotions [#186].
- Research cited in the presentation (no authors given): autistic people show lower accuracy on heartbeat-detection tests but report higher interoceptive awareness; higher interoceptive awareness goes with a more developed insula; the insula activates during self-reflection (mirror, photos) [#186].
- Normal resting adult core temperature 36.1–36.6 °C; one degree above = fever, one below = hypothermia (article #37's illustration of homeostasis) [#37].
- Dutton and Aron (1974): men crossing a 450-ft (~137 m) unstable suspension bridge wrote more erotic stories and more often contacted the woman they met than men on a low stable bridge [#38].
- Schachter and Singer (1962): adrenaline-injected participants read their arousal as happiness or anger depending on a confederate's behaviour; those told about side effects were less influenced [#38].
- Thresholds used in the methods: check-ins of 10 seconds or longer; 30–50 seconds of activity to raise the pulse; 60 seconds to lower it; 2 timers a day [#186].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The link between alexithymia and meltdown/shutdown via missed overload is explicitly Nastya's own opinion ("по моему мнению") [#47].
- The 8/10 threshold is presented as a general statement about "us" neurodivergent people, not a measured figure [#49].
- The presentation itself warns that brain-imaging findings on the insula in autism can be contradictory, and that calming strategies do not always work as well for autistic people [#186].
- The "own terms" approach rests on the forwarded author's personal inquiries ("Личные изыскания") among people with difficulties naming emotions, not on cited research [#284].
- The misattribution-of-arousal post's four reasons are general claims without cited studies [#38].
- The article itself flags that the 1974 bridge study reflects heteronormative biases of its design (only male participants, a female interviewer) [#38].
- Several health links in the homeostasis article are hedged as possibilities: breathing difficulty "может быть причиной" (may be a cause) of apnoea in autistic people, and neurodivergence "может играть роль" (may play a role) in POTS; the list of nine is explicitly "неполный" (incomplete) [#37].
