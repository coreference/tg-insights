## Autistic and ADHD communication styles, AAC and helper tools

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's material on how neurodivergent people communicate. It covers eight communication preferences autistic people may have, the idea of infodumping, ADHD speech features and how ADHD symptoms strain conversations and long-term relationships (with an 8-point list of what helps), what alternative and augmentative communication (AAC) is, its three technology levels and the pros and cons of five formats, and two tools from the Autentik project: one decodes idioms and drafts replies, the other assesses how toxic a message is [#46, #51, #74, #126, #127, #89, #90, #91, #199, #249]. The channel framed the autism post as an awareness piece for World Autism Awareness Day. The ADHD material is framed around the fact that people with ADHD often meet rejection and relationship problems because of their symptoms. The AAC material ends with a reminder that any difficulty with verbal speech is a valid reason to use accommodations [#51, #126, #94].

### Core ideas

- Autistic communication is different, not rude. Behaviours that look odd from outside, such as no eye contact, skipping small talk, replying days later or repeating your words, have reasons behind them: comfort, easier processing, less stress. They are not meant as dislike or mockery (details in *Autistic communication preferences*) [#51].
- Many autistic people prefer slower, more explicit channels that give them more control. Direct words over hints, text over speech, monologues in turns over rapid exchange, asynchronous messages, pauses in dialogue [#47, #48, #49, #50, #51].
- ADHD affects communication directly through inattention, hyperactivity and impulsivity. It also affects speech itself (volume, pauses, fillers) and the stability that close relationships need. 50 to 60% of children with ADHD have trouble with peer relationships [#126].
- ADHD communication problems can be worked on. Self-observation, questions, deliberate pauses, strategies against interrupting, fidget toys, notes, sharing feelings and deciding which relationships are worth the investment [#127].
- AAC is a legitimate accommodation for anyone who has difficulty with verbal speech. It ranges from high-tech apps and devices to cards, notebooks and sign languages, and each option has trade-offs [#90, #91, #94].
- Text tools can make implicit meaning explicit. The Autentik plugin explains idioms and suggests neutral replies. The Toximeter bot says how most people would perceive a message's tone, which helps when you cannot tell if someone is aggressive, or why someone thinks you were rude [#199, #249].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Autistic communication preferences (8 items)
Purpose: a post made for World Autism Awareness Day about communication features and preferences that autistic people *may* have ("которые могут быть у аутичных людей" — *which autistic people may have*). The idea is credited to thomashenleyuk. The same text appears on a set of image cards; the cover card lists all eight items [#51, #46]. The channel hedges throughout with "some autistic people", "sometimes" and "often". These are possible preferences, not universal rules [#51].

1. Preference for direct communication. In direct communication a person tells the other person directly what they want to say, for example in a message or in speech. Indirect communication includes body language, facial expressions and changes in tone of voice, and these "may be very hard to understand with autism" [#47, #51]. The card adds that when an autistic person wants to share feelings, they will often say directly how they feel and why, instead of hinting [#47].
2. Minimal eye contact. Eye contact can cause discomfort and can even distract autistic people from the conversation. The post stresses: "Отсутствие контакта глаз не означает, что человеку неприятно на вас смотреть, так просто комфортнее общаться!" (The absence of eye contact does not mean the person dislikes looking at you; it is simply more comfortable to communicate that way!) [#51, #47].
3. Avoiding small talk. Autistic people often do not see the point of small talk, "легкой болтовни ни о чем, просто чтобы избежать молчания и неловкости" (light chatter about nothing, just to avoid silence and awkwardness). They prefer to get straight to the point [#48, #51].
4. AAC and communicating in text. Some autistic people find it hard to keep communicating orally all the time, so alternative and augmentative communication (АКД, AAC) can help a lot. One example is a smartphone app that turns text into spoken speech. Autistic people may also move a conversation from speech to text in messengers when talking becomes hard. The card phrases this as the autistic person asking *you* to move the discussion from speech to text when they feel talking is getting hard [#51, #48].
5. Monologue exchange. Sometimes autistic people prefer to speak in monologues, taking turns. This lets them explain something better and lowers the stress of having to react immediately to each of the other person's remarks [#49, #51].
6. Asynchronous communication. Some autistic people may prefer asynchronous communication: you send a message without expecting an immediate reply. Example: an autistic person who gets a message from a friend may reply not right away but a couple of days later. Why: this gives time to think the answer through and put one's thoughts into words. Fast real-time exchange gives no time to think and can be very stressful [#51, #49].
7. More time to process information. Autistic people may process information more slowly, so it is sometimes important to slow down and add pauses to a dialogue with an autistic person [#51, #50].
8. Repeating after others (echolalia). Sometimes, instead of answering a question or remark, autistic people simply repeat the other person's words. The post's example: "Пойдешь со мной в магазин?" — "В магазин" ("Will you come to the shop with me?" — "To the shop"). This often happens involuntarily (echolalia) and is not meant to offend the other person or mock how they speak. Sometimes certain words and phrases just feel nice on the tongue ("просто прикольно ощущаются на языке"), and a conversation between two autistic people can look like both of them repeating the same phrase, word or sound together [#51, #50].

#### Negative manifestations of ADHD in conversation (5 items)
Context: people with ADHD often face communication difficulties, rejection by others and interpersonal problems because of ADHD symptoms. Inattention, hyperactivity and impulsivity directly affect how they talk with others [#126, #121]. The channel lists these "особенности диалога" (features of dialogue) [#126, #122]:

1. Excessive talkativeness. People with ADHD may talk too much, especially about things that interest them a lot. The post links this to infodumping ("привет, инфодампинг" — *hello, infodumping*) [#126, #122].
2. Impatience and interrupting. Very often a brilliant thought pops into the head of a person with ADHD mid-conversation, and they blurt it out without waiting for the other person to finish [#126, #122].
3. Distractibility. People with ADHD are often distracted by things around them and by their own thoughts, and they may tune out of the conversation [#126, #122].
4. Fidgeting, squirming, spinning on a chair, pacing the room. Others may read this as boredom and unwillingness to be in the conversation. In fact it is what helps people with ADHD concentrate on the conversation. The card puts the misreading as "impatience and unwillingness to be in the conversation" [#126, #122].
5. Oversharing. Impulsivity can make people with ADHD blurt things out before thinking them over. Others may see this as unnecessary information. The card adds that people with ADHD often thoughtlessly share overly personal information [#126, #122].

#### Negative manifestations of ADHD in close relationships (5 items)
ADHD can also play a negative role in building long-term friendships, romantic relationships and other relationships. People with ADHD may notice these manifestations [#126, #123]:

1. Forgetfulness. People want to feel important and to know that their friends are ready to share their successes and failures. When a friend keeps saying "Я этого не помню" (I don't remember that) or forgets about meetings, it looks as if they do not care about you [#126, #123].
2. Feeling overwhelmed. People with ADHD often struggle with everyday tasks and may feel overloaded by everything they have to do. When a lot is going on, listening carefully and remembering meetings can be very hard [#126, #123].
3. Inconsistency. Close relationships often need stable, regular contact, and people with ADHD can struggle with this as with any other routine. Example: one day you act as if you urgently need to see your friend, then you don't write to them for several months. The friend may feel you only use them when you have nothing else to do [#126, #123].
4. Boredom. Sometimes sitting at home with the phone, computer and TV all running at once is much more stimulating than time with loved ones, where you have to sit still and pay attention [#126, #123].
5. Comorbid neurodivergence and mental-health conditions. People with ADHD often also have other mental-health features and neurodivergences that affect how their communication skills develop. The examples given are depression, anxiety, autism and personality disorders [#126, #123].

#### What can help with ADHD and communication (8 items)
The second part of the ADHD post. The image cards carry six items and the text version adds two (interrupting less; defining the value of the relationship) [#127, #124, #125]:

1. Awareness (осознанность). The first step to better communication is to recognise which of your actions harm the interaction. Observe yourself and ask: "Вы активно слушаете или только ждете, когда сможете вставить свои пять копеек?" (Are you actively listening, or just waiting until you can put in your two cents?) [#127, #124].
2. Ask questions to stay engaged in the conversation. Example questions from the post: "Так, правильно ли я поняла, что у тебя проблемы с Сашей, и она не хочет работать над вашими отношениями?" (So, do I understand correctly that you are having problems with Sasha and she doesn't want to work on your relationship?) and "А ты мог бы рассказать подробнее, что случилось?" (Could you tell me in more detail what happened?) [#127, #124].
3. Pauses. If you tend to talk fast and a lot, you can deliberately pause every few sentences so the other person has a chance to react [#127, #124].
4. Try to interrupt less. When you feel the urge to interrupt coming, take a sip of water, jot down a note so you don't forget the thought, hold your breath for a second, or think "Расслабься, подожди" (Relax, wait). If you have interrupted someone, apologise and invite them to continue [#127].
5. Fidget toys. To get less distracted and stop getting stuck in your phone, bring fidget toys to touch, squeeze or roll during the conversation. That way the brain gets extra stimulation and gets less bored [#127, #125].
6. Take notes when you know you will forget something. Write down your friend's girlfriend's name, add your next meeting to the calendar, and make a list of the names of all the important people. Look at that list now and then to remember whom you need to contact [#127, #125].
7. Share your worries and feelings with loved ones. You do not have to tell loved ones about your ADHD. Still, talking about your state can help you grow closer and trust each other more, and it helps them understand you better. Tell them about your communication difficulties and how much you value the friendship and the time you spend together, and that you look forward to meeting again [#127, #125].
8. Decide what the relationship is worth. Close, trusting relationships don't just happen; they take care, time and effort. Ask yourself whether talking to this person matters enough to you to invest in it. "Выбор за вами." (The choice is yours.) [#127].

#### Three categories of AAC by level of technology
Definition first (from the forwarded Nerdivergent post): AAC is "форму адаптации общения под нужды людей с нарушениями речи" (a form of adapting communication to the needs of people with speech impairments) [#89]. The term refers to ways of communicating for people with speech impairments that are not verbal ("словами через рот" — *with words through the mouth*). *Alternative* communication replaces verbal communication in the short or long term. *Augmentative* communication is added on top of verbal communication. Many people use non-verbal communication every day, such as gestures. AAC, by contrast, is a way of communicating for people who for one reason or another cannot use verbal communication. Examples of who may need it: non-verbal autistic people, people with cerebral palsy (ДЦП), people in crisis [#90]. The post is tagged for cerebral palsy and autism [#89].

AAC splits into 3 categories by how technological the format is [#91]:
1. High-tech. Examples: apps you download onto a tablet, or separate gadgets that voice text aloud [#91].
2. Low-tech. Examples: sets of picture cards, or a notebook and pen [#91].
3. No-tech. Example: sign languages and partial sign languages [#91].

Each option has advantages and drawbacks, which the post lists format by format (next framework) [#91].

#### Pros and cons of five AAC formats
Transcribed from the image cards [#92, #93, #94].

1. App on a device (приложение на устройство) [#92]
- Pro: convenient if you already have the device.
- Pro: no need to carry extra devices.
- Pro: a large vocabulary, and you can add your own words.
- Con: the operating system does not always fit.
- Con: few apps are available in Russian.
- Con: other apps on the device can be stimulating and distracting.

2. Dedicated gadget (отдельный гаджет) [#92]
- Pro: intuitive devices that suit a wide range of people.
- Pro: created and tested by specialists in speech disorders.
- Con: you have to carry a separate device.
- Con: often expensive.
- Con: a limited set of options (words, voices), which sometimes cannot be changed.

3. Cards (карточки) [#93]
- Pro: easy to find or make.
- Pro: visual, and work both for the person speaking and for the person being spoken to.
- Pro: a cheap option.
- Con: a limited set, and it is hard to add others.
- Con: easy to lose if not fastened together.
- Con: words cannot be voiced.

4. Writing in a notebook or in phone notes (письмо в блокноте или в заметках в телефоне) [#93]
- Pro: simple to use.
- Pro: you can write the way you speak.
- Pro: easy to carry.
- Pro: cheap and/or already at hand.
- Con: risk of unclear spelling and/or handwriting, or letters that are too small.
- Con: words cannot be voiced (without additional apps).

5. Sign languages (жестовые языки) [#94]
- Pro: no other aids are needed.
- Pro: cheap or free.
- Pro: can be used at any moment.
- Con: you have to learn a new language.
- Con: the people around you also have to know the sign language.
- Con: not everyone can use their hands fully.

Conclusion of the card set ("В общем…"): any difficulty with verbal speech is valid and is reason enough to use accommodations, including AAC. Even if you have no such impairment yourself, it is always useful to keep in mind that your friends, acquaintances, clients and other people (the card says "и не только" — and not only them) may need AAC [#94].

#### Using the Autentik Text Helper browser plugin (3 steps)
A Google Chrome extension from the Autentik project that "расшифровывает значение текстов" (deciphers the meaning of texts) [#199]. How it works:
1. Install the plugin and turn it on [#199].
2. Pin it. After installing, open a new tab and pin the plugin to the menu bar: click the "Extensions" icon, then the "Pin" button. This keeps it from getting lost so it can start working [#199].
3. Select text, click the plugin icon and choose one of two options: "прояснить" (clarify) or "составить ответ" (compose a reply) [#199].
   - *Clarify* explains the meaning of the text in more detail. Example: you select the idiom "не сыпь мне соль на рану" (don't pour salt on my wound), and the plugin explains that it means "don't make my pain or problem worse", comparing it with how painful salt on a physical wound would be [#199].
   - *Compose a reply* writes a neutral, appropriate response to the selected sentence. Example: the author selected "как дела?" (how are you?) and got the reply "Всё хорошо, спасибо за проявленный интерес. А как у вас дела?" (All good, thanks for your interest. And how are you?) [#199].

### Detailed insights

#### Infodumping
Infodumping is a process in which a person, most often autistic or with ADHD, talks a lot and in detail about something that interests them. It usually takes the form of a monologue, as if dumping a pile of information onto the other person ("как бы сваливает на другого человека кучу информации"), often without realising whether the listener is ready to listen [#74]. The ADHD post ties excessive talkativeness about interests directly to infodumping [#126].

#### ADHD speech features
Speech features are found more often in people with ADHD than in neurotypicals. They concern pronunciation, fluency, tone and other aspects. People with ADHD may find it hard to control the volume of their voice, pause more often and use fillers like "эмм", "аа…" (umm, ahh) while talking. This sometimes causes irritation and misunderstanding in others [#126].

### Evidence and numbers

- From 50 to 60% of children with ADHD have difficulties in relationships with peers. The channel gives no study or source for this figure [#126, #121].

### Tools and resources mentioned

- Text-to-speech apps on smartphones. Named as an example of AAC that helps autistic people who find constant oral interaction difficult [#51].
- Autentik polite-replies Telegram bot. An earlier Autentik tool. The forwarding channel (Аутичные переводы) says its post about this bot was the second most popular on that channel. The plugin does the same job as this bot [#199].
- Autentik Text Helper (Chrome extension). Explains texts and idioms, and drafts neutral, appropriate replies (usage in *Using the Autentik Text Helper browser plugin*). The author's verdict: as a plugin it is more useful than the bot, because it works on other sites, in comments, on social networks and on forums. Limitation: for now it tends to give positive, affirmative replies; the developers plan to add settings for tone and context. It works in four languages: Russian, English, Spanish and French. If you read a text in another language, it works as a translator and writes its explanation in the language you set in the plugin. The forwarding author, a translator, says she "очень оценила" (really appreciated) it. She often meets idioms that mislead her because she takes them literally and does not recognise them as idioms, and now she can ask the extension instead of doing lengthy research [#199].
- Токсиметр (Toximeter), @autentik_toximeter_bot. An Autentik Telegram bot that the channel author says will be "супер полезен нейроотличным людям" (super useful for neurodivergent people). It helps detect aggression, devaluation and discrimination in texts and messages, and explains in detail how most people would judge the words in a text. When to use it: if you cannot tell whether the other person is being aggressive toward you, or, the other way round, if someone complains that you were rude and you don't understand why [#249]. Two worked examples:
  - The phrase "А что если ты используешь диагнозы, как оправдание своей лени?" (What if you use diagnoses as an excuse for your laziness?), which neurodivergent people often hear. The bot flags two characteristics: accusation of laziness, and devaluation of diagnoses/health problems. Verdict: "Определенно токсично" (Definitely toxic). Its assessment: the message accuses the person of using diagnoses as an excuse for laziness, which is a crude devaluation of real health problems. The wording carries judgement, is aggressive and is potentially insulting to people with any diagnoses. It shows disrespect and non-acceptance, ignoring that diagnoses can seriously affect a person's life and abilities [#249].
  - The question "Могу я тебе помочь с чем-то?" (Can I help you with something?). Result: "Определенно не токсично" (Definitely not toxic). Its assessment: a polite offer of help with no sign of aggression, insults or manipulation, a positive message that is unlikely to affect the recipient negatively, phrased correctly and kindly [#249].
  - The author asks readers whether they use other bots or services to understand people and phrase messages better [#249].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The autistic preferences are hedged in the source ("могут быть", "некоторые", "иногда", "зачастую"). They describe what *may* apply, not traits of every autistic person [#51].
- Small differences between the image cards and the text of the autism post: the card on text communication says the autistic person may *ask you* to move to text, while the text says they may move the dialogue to messengers themselves. The direct-communication card adds the sentence about stating feelings directly instead of hinting, which the text lacks [#48, #47, #51].
- (!) CONTRADICTION (minor): the ADHD card says fidgeting may be read as *impatience* and unwillingness to be in the conversation, while the text says *boredom* and unwillingness [#122, #126].
- Terminology varies: the channel writes АКД and "дополненная" (augmented) communication [#51], while the forwarded Nerdivergent cards use АДК and "дополнительная" (additional) communication, and one Nerdivergent card again says АКД [#90, #94]. All refer to AAC.
- (!) UNCLEAR: no source is given for the 50–60% statistic on peer difficulties in children with ADHD [#126].
- The Autentik plugin currently leans toward positive, affirmative replies, so its drafted replies may not fit every context until tone and context settings are added [#199].
