## How adult ADHD and autism are diagnosed

### What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel author, psychiatrist Anastasia Afanasyeva, teaches about how ADHD (СДВГ) and autism spectrum disorder (РАС) are diagnosed in adults (and briefly in children): what the diagnosis actually consists of (behavioural observation, detailed anamnesis, sometimes longer observation, tests as a bonus), why lab tests and imaging (EEG, MRI, genetic panels) cannot detect them, which ICD codes make an adult diagnosis official in Russia, why Russian state psychiatry often fails adults, how to pick a psychiatrist and talk to them, how AuDHD and masking hide criteria, and what diagnosis abroad is like. In her view, diagnosis matters because without it one cannot understand what is happening, build a competent treatment plan, or avoid ineffective or harmful therapy — and avoiding "labels" perpetuates stigma [#123, #783].

### Core ideas

- Adult ADHD and autism are officially diagnosable in Russia. ICD-10 contains both in the rubric of disorders that begin in childhood and persist throughout life; ICD-11 removes any age binding for autism. See *ICD codes* below [#124, #869].
- Diagnosis is clinical, not instrumental. It rests on observation, detailed anamnesis, sometimes longer observation and tests as a bonus; EEG only reliably shows epilepsy and sleep phases, MRI only structural lesions, and genetic panels give nothing usable [#598, #605, #606, #612].
- Not every psychiatrist is competent. Many are uninformed about adult ASD/ADHD (especially support level 1, masking women, broader phenotype); choose a specialist who works with neurodivergence [#603, #697, #746].
- Russian state psychiatry has structural reasons for missing adult autism — historical inertia, lack of training, bureaucratic barriers and difficulty with "mild" forms [#869, #59].
- Diagnosis is not "labelling" but understanding; neurodivergence should be checked early, because therapy that works for neurotypicals can be ineffective or worsening for neurodivergent people [#123, #783].
- Comorbidity complicates the picture. ASD and ADHD often co-occur and the combination (РАСДВГ) can mask individual criteria; diagnosis must be slow and detailed [#365, #612].
- Not everyone who suspects ASD has it, and neurodivergent people's complaints are not always caused by their neurodivergence — the diagnostic process checks this [#545].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Four components of diagnosing ADHD/ASD by international standards
Purpose: what a diagnosis must be based on. Afanasyeva states that "По всем международным стандартам, исследованиям и всем этим занудным вещам" (by all international standards, research and all these boring things) the following are needed [#605]:
1. Observation of behaviour during the meeting — what the clinician sees in the session itself [#605].
2. Detailed collection of anamnesis — a thorough life history [#605].
3. Sometimes observation over a longer period, especially if there are also comorbidities that need to be separated out [#605].
4. Test results "as a bonus" — supplementary, not decisive [#605].
She gives this in reply to someone advised to do a brain MRI: there are no analyses that indicate ADHD; MRI is "по сути 3D картинка мозга" (essentially a 3D picture of the brain) and shows nothing except cysts, tumours, cortical thinning and similar, so "на МРТ не тратьте деньги, лучше потратьте на что-то более приятное" (don't spend money on an MRI, better spend it on something more pleasant) [#605].

#### Three data sources for ASD diagnosis (child or adult)
Answering how she diagnoses ASD in adults (a reader had seen English-language discussions about multi-day assessments, parent interviews and "explain to an alien how to brush your teeth"), Afanasyeva says that whether the patient is a child or an adult, they rely on three sources of data [#612]:
1. Observation during the conversation — how the person communicates, how facial expressions show, how far facial expression matches what the person is saying [#612].
2. Detailed anamnesis — sometimes one meeting is enough; sometimes she sends the person away to observe their state and gather information before continuing [#612].
3. Tests as a bonus — not always needed, but they can give good ground for discussion, especially when questions remain after the first meeting [#612].
Because ASD rarely occurs alone, when other disorders are suspected and the client requests it, she diagnoses "всего остального человека" (the whole rest of the person) too. At live psychiatric appointments she tries to quickly ask about all possible symptoms in the first meeting, though this is not always possible [#612].

#### Diagnosing ASD and ADHD in parallel along the life line
How a doctor determines that a person has both ADHD and autism: because ADHD and ASD very often occur together, when she has suspicions and enough time she begins asking about ADHD symptoms in parallel with ASD. Sometimes, while going "по линии жизни" (along the life line, i.e. chronologically through the person's life), she asks about symptoms of both at once. The reason: the picture in РАСДВГ differs somewhat from pure ADHD or pure ASD, so it needs to be assessed as a combination [#612].

#### Recognising AuDHD (РАСДВГ) — how the combination distorts ASD criteria
Purpose: avoiding missed ASD in people with ADHD. Given the high comorbidity of ASD and ADHD, the combination is not rare, and it especially often makes it hard to recognise ASD in someone with ADHD [#365]. Afanasyeva describes three criteria that get distorted:
1. Order and systematisation. People with ASD love order, systematisation and fixed sequences; with ADHD added, the person loves all of this but lives in chaos. The person suffers from that, and it also strongly misleads specialists, who dismiss the criterion as not met [#365].
2. Novelty. People with ASD dislike the new and unpredictable; people with ADHD are thrilled by novelty. In РАСДВГ people often like trying new things, but only in areas where they feel more confident and safe — e.g. trying new food only in a particular context (only if they cooked it themselves, or only in a certain familiar café) [#365].
3. Criterion A, communication — the most misleading in her observation. Where people with ASD tend to have a fairly limited social circle, people with РАСДВГ often have a large one and may enjoy new acquaintances; but "дьявол кроется в деталях" (the devil is in the details): difficulty getting close and maintaining closeness, understanding the other person in relationships, acquaintances that are often rather superficial, rapid fatigue from socialising [#365].
Rule: do not hurry; question and examine carefully exactly where things initially seem obvious. ASD looks different in everyone, and РАСДВГ can combine completely individually — for some it gives relief and partial compensation of their ASD and ADHD, for others it makes the symptoms heavier [#365].

#### Criterion B rigid rules — what they look like in masked adults
Afanasyeva calls criterion B (rigid rules) one of the hardest criteria when diagnosing ASD in adults with support level 1, especially in women who mask [#288]. Mechanism: people build their routine so well, making many tiny rules, that they don't notice the rules are rigid — "Ну правильно же так, в чём проблема" (Well, that's the right way, what's the problem). It is very hard to recall these in the moment of an appointment, particularly if you don't live with people who notice these "oddities" [#288]. Examples she lists:
- dishes stored in a particular order (she herself is jarred if a big plate is put on a small one, or a glass is not in its place);
- getting dressed in a particular order;
- greeting every person you see;
- always reading to the end whatever you started;
- arranging objects this way and no other;
- drinking tea only from one mug and coffee only from another [#288].
There can be countless such small rules whose violation causes discomfort, without the person thinking about them. Key caveat: rigid means too strict — the rule holds regardless of context. Example: you are visiting someone and cannot drink coffee from the same cup you drank tea from the day before [#288]. Her own example: on a trip she noticed that cash must ALWAYS lie in ascending order of denomination, and if not she must rearrange it at once; and, to the point about rigidity, she recalled it had always been so, even as a child when she took money from her parents' wallets and played with it — showing that a rule's lifelong persistence is part of what makes it relevant [#288].

#### Four reasons adult autism is "invisible" in Russian state psychiatry
Afanasyeva describes (as of 2026) the situation in Russian state medical institutions as still difficult, despite some formal changes in recent years [#869]:
1. Historical inertia and the "Soviet school". For a long time domestic psychiatry held that autism is exclusively a childhood diagnosis; at 18 it was thought either to "pass" or turn into something else. As a result, until the mid-2010s, on transfer from the children's to the adult clinic, an ASD diagnosis was almost always changed to schizophrenia or schizotypal disorder. Old-school psychiatrists sincerely believe "аутистов-взрослых не бывает" (there are no adult autistics) and classify features of social interaction as symptoms of mental illness [#869]. An illustration from 2023: at a conference a colleague of hers was told by an acquaintance working at a large state centre: "ну не знаю конечно, а вы что из тех, кто верит во взрослое СДВГ? У нас на кафедре конечно на это не так смотрят, и либо вялотекущую ставят после 18 лет, либо просто диагноз снимают" (well, I don't know, are you one of those who believe in adult ADHD? At our department they don't see it that way — after 18 they either diagnose sluggish [schizophrenia] or simply remove the diagnosis). Afanasyeva notes that amid the popularisation of neurodivergence she had even become shy of telling psychologists in lectures that somewhere people with ASD still get schizophrenia after 18, presenting it rather as history — "А тут вон оно как" (and here's how it is) [#59].
2. Lack of specialised education. Training for "adult" psychiatry for decades did not include in-depth study of ASD, nor ADHD. Training happened only in child psychiatry and only for support levels 2–3. So PND doctors are trained to identify acute psychoses, depression or dementia but often don't know what autism looks like in an adult, especially one with high intelligence who has learned to "mask". ASD symptoms are often mistaken for OCD, social anxiety or personality disorders [#869]. (She also says no one is taught about ADHD in residency — "нам даже в детском отделении не особо рассказывали" (even in the children's department they didn't tell us much) [#124].)
3. Bureaucratic and legal barriers. Although the Ministry of Health issued a letter back in 2017 on the inadmissibility of unjustified change of an "autism" diagnosis at 18, in practice the system resists and points 1–2 continue. There are still no standards of care or adequate adopted clinical guidelines. For children with ASD there are established routes (special education, tutors), but for adults there are practically no systems of "supported living" or employment in state structures. Disability for autism is extremely hard for adults to obtain: MSE experts often require a concomitant intellectual impairment to confirm status — and again all of this is only for support levels 2–3 [#869].
4. Difficulty diagnosing "mild" forms. Adults who come to the PND themselves for a diagnosis (often people with conditional Asperger syndrome) find they are "слишком нормальные" (too normal) for state psychiatry. If a person can hold a dialogue and finished school, the PND doctor may consider complaints of sensory overload or communication difficulties "причудами" (quirks) or "дурным характером" (bad character) [#869].

#### Three benefits of early diagnosis in children
A parent sees ADHD and autistic traits in a 6-year-old and asks at what stage to see a psychiatrist. Afanasyeva says research shows that the earlier a child receives neurodivergence-related diagnoses, the [#842]:
1. less shame and non-acceptance of their own features will form;
2. easier it is to introduce the needed non-medication help and develop behavioural strategies;
3. lower the probability of developing co-occurring disorders in future, such as depression and anxiety disorders.
Her advice: don't postpone; show the child to a child psychiatrist [#842].

#### Three reasons ADHD belongs to psychiatry, not neurology
A reader asks why psychiatrists rather than neurologists diagnose neurodivergence if it concerns nervous-system development. Afanasyeva calls it a reasonable question: neurology and psychiatry overlap a lot, disputes arise over who handles which disorder, so areas of responsibility are divided by the symptoms being worked with. Example: epilepsy is basically handled by neurologists, but secondary mental states within epilepsy itself by psychiatrists [#844]. ADHD is handled by psychiatrists because:
1. we can only minimally influence its biological basis;
2. the main diagnostic process must take the form of psychiatric anamnesis collection, since analyses show nothing;
3. diagnosis requires careful differential diagnosis specifically with other psychiatric disorders [#844].

#### What to do when criteria seem not to be met but traits interfere with life
Case: a reader sees very many ASD (possibly РАСДВГ) traits but recalls no childhood behaviours that the diagnosis requires; the psychiatrist doesn't see it and diagnoses recurrent depression (several episodes, always amid burnout at study/work). Afanasyeva's recommendation [#851]:
1. Return to the question after mood stabilises — not during an affective episode.
2. Get one or two second opinions.
3. Remember that how the anamnesis is collected matters. We often feel we remember little, but the way questions are asked is crucial; sometimes information is collected "по крупицам" (bit by bit) by recalling how school lessons went, what one played (the source ends this list with "и т.д."), until the needed information is gathered.
So she recommends coming back and trying to figure it out after all [#851].

### Detailed insights

#### ICD codes: adult ADHD and autism are official diagnoses in Russia
Afanasyeva constantly meets the claim that one cannot get an ADHD diagnosis in Russia, "особенно раз МКБ-11 не приняли" (especially since ICD-11 wasn't adopted). She regrets ICD-11 not being adopted "до слёз" (to tears), but says this doesn't stop clinicians knowing it and using it outside of coding [#124]. ICD-10 does have a code for ADHD: F 90.0 "Нарушение активности и внимания" (Disturbance of activity and attention). It just doesn't look like "ADHD" and sits in the "children's" rubric, which confuses and scares everyone, doctors included. It is in that rubric because the rubric contains disorders that begin in childhood and persist throughout life; the same applies to autism, and there are often small-print footnotes and ICD annexes saying so. Hence, "вот вам и диагноз #СДВГ по МКБ-10. Официально" (there you have an ADHD diagnosis under ICD-10. Officially) [#124].
For autism in private clinics: first, in ICD-11 autism has no age binding at all — just a single rubric; second, even in ICD-10 the rubric with the forms of autism belongs to disorders BEGINNING in childhood and PERSISTING throughout life, so clinicians have every right to diagnose adults. What matters is meeting the criteria and onset in childhood [#869].

#### What laboratory and instrumental tests can and cannot show
- EEG. Although EEG is often prescribed for both ADHD and ASD, current research says EEG can reliably show only two processes: (1) epilepsy; (2) sleep phases and their disturbances (which is why EEG is part of polysomnography). So it cannot detect autism, in adults or otherwise — "ничего мы там не увидим" (we won't see anything there) [#598].
- MRI — see *Four components* above: only structural findings (cysts, tumours, cortical thinning) [#605].
- Genetic tests. Following a discussion about genes and Genotek, where she said one should expect nothing sensible from them beyond entertainment, she saw at the Gemotest lab an advertised panel. The photographed price list showed: panel "Hereditary epilepsies and seizure conditions" — NGS of 560 genes, 30 days, 34,900 ₽ (code GNP319); panel "Developmental delay and autism" — 286 genes linked with delayed psycho-speech development, intellectual disability and autism, claimed to help build a personal treatment and support plan for children, 30 days, 34,900 ₽ (code GNP321); panel "Cerebral palsy (motor development disorders)" — NGS of 945 genes, 30 days, 34,900 ₽ (GNP322); "Large neurological panel" — NGS of over 2,000 genes for hereditary nervous-system diseases, 30 days, 44,900 ₽ (GNP318) [#606]. Her verdict: it's "грустно" (sad); the test won't give much, and at such a price; moreover it treats autism exclusively together with developmental delay and intellectual disability. She reiterates that there is currently no evidence that such tests — or tests for antidepressant response and similar — give genuinely usable information [#606].

#### Why diagnose at all: against "don't put labels"
Afanasyeva often hears from psychologists, students and colleagues: why "label" the state at all, just help the client. She finds this approach understandable in psychology — until we are in the field of clinical disorders [#123]. Her supervision group's metaphor: "why treat the leg if the person limps — just help them not to limp". Her counter: diagnosis exists precisely to understand WHY the person limps, HOW LONG they have limped, and whether, because of the limp, they keep injuring the leg — falling into potholes and coming to the doctor with injuries beyond the limp itself [#123]. "Упрямый врач внутри меня" (the stubborn doctor inside me) holds that fear of diagnosing prevents understanding what's happening and building a competent treatment plan, and above all continues stigmatisation in mental health. Analogy: imagine a doctor told a coughing patient "don't put a label on it", just take bromhexine and do breathing exercises. In psychiatry/psychology too one must understand what works and why — to explain it to the client, write a case conceptualisation and separate which symptom comes from where [#123].
In 2025 she adds a further argument formulated in supervision: diagnosis of neurodivergence should be not only timely but often anticipatory, even before specific complaints (she expects accusations of overdiagnosis). When neurodivergence is not looked at first, or diagnosis is postponed while treating other conditions, the work — especially psychotherapy — is often ineffective or even worsens the state, since what works well for a neurotypical person may work poorly for a neurodivergent one. Personal example: in her first therapy, still at university, they long struggled with certain problems that would not resolve, and she despaired; once she understood what they were linked to, controlling them became much easier. Hence her rule: diagnosis of neurodivergence should come, if not first, then at least right after acute affective symptoms decrease and at the slightest hints of neurodivergence [#783].

#### Finding a competent psychiatrist and how to present your suspicion
- To find out whether you have ASD you must see a psychiatrist "100%", but not any psychiatrist: many are uninformed about adult ASD, especially at support level 1 and/or near the broader phenotype. She points to her lists of specialists (tag #специалисты): one crowd-sourced list where doctors added themselves, and one of graduates of her and Maria Novikova's Neurodive course whom they trained themselves [#603].
- On "where in Moscow do they diagnose ADHD best": she doesn't know of a "best" place; in fact you just need a psychiatrist who understands adult ADHD — herself or any psychiatrist specialising in it, e.g. from her specialist table [#697].
- Whether to say you suspect РАСДВГ when booking or at the visit, or not say it and just describe your features: as with any doctor, you may say at the start that you suspect a condition, or just list your complaints. But since not all psychiatrists know adult forms of neurodivergence well, choose a specialist who works with this [#746].
- A patient registered at a PND with bipolar disorder (БАР), who suspects ADHD and is ashamed to say so because doctors roll their eyes and treat patients as hypochondriacs: "Если врач не верит во взрослое СДВГ боюсь вам ничего не поможет" (If the doctor doesn't believe in adult ADHD, I'm afraid nothing will help you). But sharing your self-observations with doctors is normal — "никто не знает вас так хорошо, как вы" (no one knows you as well as you do). If the PND doctor reacts badly, go to another doctor who works with ADHD and can perform differential diagnosis with bipolar disorder [#57].

#### Suspected but absent: diagnosis checks alternatives
A frequent question: do people come for ASD diagnosis and turn out not to have it? Yes. At an appointment doctors look at the state and what the person reports and quickly run through what it could be linked to; since neurodivergence often resembles a hundred other conditions, diagnosis checks this. Sometimes it isn't ASD or ADHD at all; sometimes there is neurodivergence but the complaints are not caused by it [#545].

#### Late-appearing symptoms and single signs (Q&A from DocPsyFest)
- If two thirds of symptoms appeared in adolescence and the rest after 30: most likely after 30 compensatory abilities and the body's resources decreased, so what the body used to cope with and compensate began to show [#57].
- Women twirling hair on a finger while talking, reading or thinking — is it ADHD or ASD? Not necessarily: disorders are not diagnosed by one sign, though it can serve as stimming [#57].
- Do features of ADHD in women also apply to men? Overall ADHD manifests similarly; some signs simply look different and less noticeable in women [#57].
- Methods to calm and focus the brain and follow a plan: a skills training for people with ADHD contains practically everything; she also hoped her book with practical advice would come out by spring [#57].
- Tension-type headaches (ГБН) are a real "pain" of neurodivergent people; main advice: rest more and practise muscle relaxation, especially head massage [#57].
- Fitting into society without suppressing yourself: find a community that accepts you as you are — search via your special interests; there are now self-support groups for neurodivergent people [#57].
- Asked about the pronounced positives of women with ADHD and ASD, she answers jokingly: "Жизнь - боль. Положительные стороны - мы женщины и мы классные" (Life is pain. The positives — we're women and we're cool) [#57].

#### The СДВГ vs РДВГ naming debate
Some online voices (she links a psychologist's post) assert that СДВГ is a mistranslation and should be called РАССТРОЙСТВО (disorder) rather than СИНДРОМ (syndrome), so the abbreviation РДВГ may now appear online. After discussing with Maria Novikova whether to change it in their course, Afanasyeva — explicitly as her personal opinion, acknowledging others may differ — finds it irritating and suspects hype on a now "fashionable" diagnosis. Her arguments: (1) medicine beyond psychiatry has many "historical" names and eponyms/syndrome names that don't hinder treatment; (2) words matter, but first we should talk about normal diagnosis of ADHD and other neurodivergence, competent psychotherapy, needed medications with doses, and accounting for the female phenotype and cycle — and the official translation in ICD-11 remains СДВГ. She calls it "чистой воды демагогия" (pure demagogy), while reflecting that her autistic rigidity and attachment to the familiar term may play a role — her irritation comes from the idea that everything should be consistent and by the rules, and there was no discussion or centralisation. She says she wants to offend no one and invites opinions [#205].

#### Diagnosis abroad and confirming a Russian diagnosis
- Afanasyeva used to wear "rose-tinted glasses" about Europe being much better and easier for these diagnoses than the CIS; after several years of close work with patients from various countries it turned out less rosy. First, everything depends on the specific country or state. Second, on the field where you study or work: studying seems easier, since there is real help — study-process support, tutors, buddies. Work differs: many fear and do not confirm their ASD in Europe because it can really harm their career. So research in advance your specific field and country before moving. Access to more ADHD medications is an undoubted plus [#870].
- Confirming a diagnosis obtained in Russia/CIS is fairly simple, depending on the country and availability of a psychiatrist (especially one who works with neurodivergence — same as in Russia). If you have a detailed report in the right format from a Russian doctor, stating problems, diagnosis and recommendations, in some countries it has recently become possible even via a family doctor who simply prescribes the medication per the recommendations. A couple of years ago that was hard to imagine; she supposes the increased patient flow in Europe simplified the process. Previously, in some countries one waited 4–6 years for access to an ADHD psychiatrist. Summary: foreign doctors in most cases readily accept Russian reports [#870].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- Afanasyeva's positions are her clinical opinions; she hedges the РДВГ critique as personal and possibly coloured by her own rigidity [#205], and anticipates accusations of overdiagnosis for her "anticipatory diagnosis" stance [#783].
- (!) CONTRADICTION (between the system and practice): a 2017 Ministry of Health letter forbids unjustified changes of autism diagnoses at 18, yet she reports the practice and the belief "no adult autistics / no adult ADHD" persist in state centres [#869, #59].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the DIVA-5 questionnaire file and the stream recording are not transcribed (the audio has no text detected), so their content is not available [#184].

## Differential diagnosis: ADHD and autism vs bipolar, depression, BPD and OCPD

### What this covers

This topic gathers the author's clinical rules for separating neurodevelopmental conditions (ADHD, autism) from conditions that can look similar: bipolar II disorder with hypomania, depression, dysthymia, borderline personality disorder (BPD) and obsessive-compulsive personality disorder (OCPD). It also covers the author's position that the activist "neurodivergent umbrella" should not absorb acquired, treatable disorders such as OCD and bipolar disorder, plus what is known about the adult outcomes of childhood ODD and DMDD. According to the source this matters because misdiagnosis is common — many adults with previously undiagnosed ADHD receive an erroneous bipolar diagnosis [#11] — and because labelling a treatable disorder as a "neurodivergence" can lead to normalising it instead of treating it [#75].

### Core ideas

- ADHD energy cycles mimic bipolar II. ADHD produces "pseudo-bipolar" cycles of frantic over-engagement followed by exhaustion and even depressive episodes; the main discriminator is whether mood itself is persistently elevated, plus age of onset [#11]. Full method: *ADHD vs bipolar II differentiation*.
- What drives a mood swing tells you the diagnosis. Bipolar mood shifts are biological, persistent and context-insensitive; ADHD swings follow interest/novelty and above all energy; BPD swings follow interactions with other people [#371]. See *Mood-swing triggers*.
- Feeling good is not hypomania. People with dysthymia — common in neurodivergence, CPTSD and personality disorders — may not know what normal mood is and mistake recovery for hypomania; hypomania has specific duration, mood and sleep markers [#345].
- Neurodivergence = neurodevelopmental, lifelong. Neurodivergences belong to the neurodevelopmental rubric because they form mainly in utero and persist for life; OCD and bipolar disorder are predispositions that may or may not manifest and can be cured or put into long remission, so they should be treated, not normalised [#75, img #75].
- Impulsivity and overcontrol can coexist. ADHD (impulsivity) and OCPD (overcontrol) co-occur, often because OCPD traits develop secondarily as a coping strategy against lifelong criticism (forwarded post) [#311].
- Suspected autism changes the depression work-up. If autism is suspected, it is worth confirming, mainly for self-understanding and psychotherapy; and depression must be differentiated from autistic burnout, the key difference being the absence of persistently low mood for most of the day over more than 14 days [#604, #617].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### ADHD vs bipolar II differentiation (mood change + age of onset)
Purpose. Separating ADHD "pseudo-bipolar cycles" from bipolar II disorder (hypomania alternating with depressive episodes). The author calls this a real problem for differential diagnosis, made worse when a person has both conditions — which, she says, nobody is protected against — and the reason many adults with undiagnosed ADHD are wrongly diagnosed with bipolar disorder [#11].

How the pseudo-bipolar cycle looks (the author's own example). She describes returning from holiday with plenty of energy, and her ADHD saying: "давай возьмем еще парочку проектов" (let's take on a couple more projects), "надо срочно увидеться со ВСЕМИ" (I urgently need to see EVERYONE), "а еще надо сходить воооон на ту выставку и записаться на тот курс" (and I also need to go to that exhibition over there and sign up for that course). From the outside one sees a person rushing back and forth, grabbing "100500" tasks, getting distracted and leaving some tasks unfinished. Then the body's energy runs out and the person lies down and stays down — a week, two — and sometimes slides into "нормальный такой депрессивный эпизод" (a proper depressive episode). This sequence closely resembles bipolar II: hypomania → depressive episode → hypomania again [#11]. The attached meme captures the ADHD energy-phase mindset: "perhaps THE adhd mood: I will have seven first priorities." [#11]

Discriminating steps.
1. Check for a change in mood itself — the main anchor. The author's "главная наша опора" (our main anchor) is the change in mood: no amount of hyperactivity gives a persistently elevated mood of the kind seen in hypomanic phases. In ADHD the person is busy, scattered and over-committed, but not stably euphoric. She adds that a mood change is likewise present in the low phases of bipolar disorder (!) UNCLEAR: the sentence "так как в фазах гипомании, а так же есть и при фазах сниженного настроения" is grammatically garbled; it appears to mean that bipolar disorder shows genuine mood change in both hypomanic and low phases [#11].
2. Check the age of onset. ADHD symptoms most often appear in early childhood, from ages 3–5, whereas bipolar phases are found in adolescence and young adulthood. A lifelong pattern from early childhood points to ADHD; a picture that begins in the teens/youth points to bipolar disorder [#11].
3. If both are present — the author says this is "совсем другая история" (a completely different story) to be covered later. (!) INCOMPLETE: the source does not explain how to diagnose or handle combined ADHD + bipolar disorder [#11].

#### Mood-swing triggers: bipolar vs ADHD vs BPD
Purpose. Discussed at a training on executive dysfunction: how mood changes and emotional dysregulation differ across bipolar disorder, ADHD and BPD [#371].
1. Bipolar disorder vs ADHD — the "relatively simple" split. In bipolar disorder mood changes are biologically grounded and persistent: even elevated mood lasts several days and responds little to changes in context. This does not happen in ADHD, "даже если вы словили гиперфиксацию" (even if you've caught a hyperfixation) — an intense ADHD absorption does not produce days-long, context-independent mood elevation [#371].
2. BPD vs ADHD — the harder split. Both show emotional dysregulation and can be hard to tell apart. The author's "небольшой лайфхак" (little life hack): look at what the fast mood shift is tied to.
   - ADHD: rapid mood shifts are most often tied either to interest/novelty or — mostly — to energy. "Вот у вас есть силы - и всё классно и зашибись. Устали - привет почти депрессия." (You have energy — and everything is great and awesome. You're tired — hello, near-depression.)
   - BPD: what matters is interaction with other people — who looked at me how, how someone treated me [#371].

#### Normal mood vs hypomania criteria (dysthymia vs bipolar)
Purpose. For clients with stably lowered mood not reaching depression (dysthymia) who, when therapy starts working and their mood evens out and they begin to feel joy, get frightened and decide it is hypomania. The author says it is important to separate this from genuine bipolar hypomania [#345].

Why it happens. Dysthymia can be a frequent companion of neurodivergences, complex PTSD and personality disorders, so the person may simply not know what level of mood corresponds to the norm [#345].

What is normal (the author's examples): jumping in a puddle and enjoying it; laughing and running with your dog when you are over 30 and a serious person at work; looking at a sunset and feeling existential delight — "нормально!" (normal!) [#345].

What is not normal (hypomania markers), all together:
1. Lasting more than 4 days;
2. Rushing about, not finishing tasks and switching between them;
3. Feeling euphoria or irritability for the greater part of the day;
4. Sleeping only 3–4 hours and feeling fine about it.

Everything outside this is "вполне нормальное состояние человека. Даже с СДВГ 🙂, даже с РАС и со всем остальным" (a perfectly normal human state. Even with ADHD, even with ASD and everything else) [#345].

The problem. Online and among mental-health activists (психоактивисты) there is a lot of information about what belongs to the neurodivergence family; the author says some of it makes you scratch your head — e.g. claims that personality disorders are a neurodivergence, or OCD, "because the person thinks differently." She grants that it is great that destigmatisation is working, "но... НО!" (but... BUT!): scientists, doctors and other specialists try to understand causes and mechanisms of onset and development, which is why classifications and research exist [#75].

The author's classification logic.
1. Psychiatry currently divides, strictly speaking, into "приобретенные" (acquired) disorders and "врождённые и сохраняющиеся всю жизнь" (congenital and lifelong) conditions [#75].
2. Neurodivergences sit in the rubric "нарушения психического развития" (neurodevelopmental disorders) precisely because of their neuro-ontogenetic mechanism: they form mainly during intrauterine development and disappear "только вместе с гибелью особи" (only together with the death of the individual) [#75].
3. Other disorders involve a predisposition that may "fire" at some point in life — or may not. Moreover, some of them can be cured completely or brought into long remission; this applies to OCD and bipolar disorder [#75].
4. Conclusion: "не смешивать теплое с мягким" (*don't mix apples and oranges*, lit. "warm with soft") and treat what needs treating rather than normalising, for example, OCD thinking [#75].

#### Impulsivity vs overcontrol spectrum (ADHD + OCPD comorbidity)
*Forwarded from the channel Маша does therapy.* [#311]
- The model. Mental-health problems can be placed on an impulsivity–overcontrol spectrum: in some conditions the core is a lack of ability to control impulses; in others the core is excessive control [#311].
- Can opposite poles co-occur? Yes. ADHD lies on the impulsivity side and OCPD on the overcontrol side, yet ADHD–OCPD comorbidity is encountered. A study the post cites (linked on guilfordjournals.com) looked at links between ADHD and personality disorders and found OCPD among the four most frequent combinations. The author of the forwarded post says she sometimes meets this combination in clinical practice [#311].
- Mechanism (secondary development). Clients with ADHD typically face criticism from early childhood for their difficulties completing tasks, and are forced to develop coping strategies in the form of very rigid demands and standards for themselves. With great effort they do everything "правильно" (correctly) and control themselves heavily to avoid constant criticism — and so many OCPD traits may develop secondarily [#311].

#### Autistic burnout vs depression differentiation
- When to get assessed for autism. A reader in depression treatment was persuaded to take several autism tests, scored high, and was told people with autism have specific features in depression treatment. The author's view: if autism is suspected it is worth checking, especially with high test scores. It will have little effect on pharmacotherapy, but a very strong effect on understanding one's own features and on psychotherapeutic interventions [#604].
- Why it matters for depression. When autism is present or suspected, depression must be differentially diagnosed against autistic burnout — so it is better to clarify open questions [#604].
- What autistic burnout is. A condition not yet in disease classifications, but a definite symptom picture observed in autistic people. The author says that over the last 5 years about 40 studies on it have appeared (she checked PubMed the day before writing) [#617].
- The key differentiator. "Самое главное отличие от депрессии - это отсутствие стойко сниженного настроения большую часть дня в течение более 14 дней." (The main difference from depression is the absence of persistently lowered mood for most of the day over more than 14 days.) [#617]

Asked what ODD (oppositional defiant disorder) and DMDD (disruptive mood dysregulation disorder) potentially turn into in adulthood, the author says there is little research. From what exists:

## ADHD and autism in women: phenotype, masking and hormones

### What this covers

This topic gathers what the channel taught about why neurodivergence (ADHD and autism, Russian СДВГ and РАС) in women is "invisible": the myths clinicians repeat about overdiagnosis, the large DIVA-5 study on sex differences in adult ADHD, how masked autistic women hide rigid rules even from themselves, and how female hormones (estrogen across the cycle and after menopause) change ADHD symptoms and medication effects. According to the author, it matters because women with ADHD are under-assessed and under-estimated, and late diagnosis means years of misunderstanding, anxiety, burnout and guilt, while a diagnosis helps a person understand what is happening, reduce shame and accept their traits [#46, #618].

### Core ideas

- Rising diagnosis is not overdiagnosis. The author argues that given prevalence data and the lifelong nature of neurodevelopmental differences, many people remain undiagnosed, so the growth in diagnoses reflects need, not fashion [#46].
- Women are missed because their symptoms are quiet and masked. In childhood girls show less visible, socially acceptable symptoms; in adulthood women confirm as many or more symptoms than men but hide them behind lists, schedules and perfectionism, at the cost of burnout and low self-esteem [#618].
- Masking hides rigid rules even from the person. In adult women with masking, autism criterion B (rigid rules) is among the hardest to assess, because the rules feel simply "correct" [#288].
- Hormones modulate ADHD in women. Estrogen affects dopamine neurons, so ADHD symptoms and stimulant response fluctuate across the menstrual cycle and worsen after menopause [#118, #706].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Five main conclusions of the DIVA-5 sex-differences study
Source: a study (Frontiers in Global Women's Health, 2025) summarised in a post forwarded from the NO STIGMA channel, titled «Успешная, но истощённая: как женщины маскируют СДВГ» ("Successful but exhausted: how women mask ADHD"). It examined how adult men and women with ADHD describe their symptoms and difficulties using the DIVA-5 diagnostic interview. Sample: 2,257 adult patients in the Netherlands undergoing ADHD diagnosis in real clinical settings; 61% women. Method: statistical analysis of how often symptoms and the life examples offered in DIVA-5 were endorsed, focusing on sex differences. The authors did not just count "who has more symptoms"; they analysed in detail (a) which symptoms patients confirm in childhood and adulthood, (b) which life examples resonate with them (e.g. "I lose keys", "I interrupt the other person"), and (c) how ADHD interferes with life — work, family, leisure, self-esteem — with particular attention to male–female differences [#618].

1. Women endorsed more adult symptoms. Women confirmed adult ADHD symptoms more often than men, especially (inner) hyperactivity — "feels restless", "talks too much" — and inattention — "loses things", "easily distracted". The summary calls this unexpected, since such symptoms (hyperactivity and impulsivity) were traditionally thought to be stronger in men. Per the post, this refutes the myth of "milder ADHD in women" and shows women can have marked symptoms while using masking strategies [#618].
2. In childhood, symptoms were more often reported by men. Men more often confirmed childhood symptoms, especially those visible to teachers: "leaves seat", "interrupts", "disrupts order" — hyperactivity and conduct problems. Girls could have symptoms, but they were less visible and socially acceptable, so they were often ignored and diagnosis was missed in childhood [#618].
3. Women suffered more from self-esteem problems and perfectionism. Self-esteem problems: 89% of women vs 81% of men in adulthood; 77% of women vs 64% of men in childhood. Women report more inner distress, anxiety and a sense of "not measuring up"; the post links this to social expectations to "be perfect" / of the "ideal woman", which conflict with the nature of ADHD [#618].
4. Many women mask symptoms through compensation. Frequently endorsed female answers: "strictly uses lists", "cannot relax even on weekends", "tired of constant busyness". This reflects perfectionism and anxiety rather than overt impulsivity — women hide symptoms behind hyper-organisation (lists, clear schedules, perfectionism). These strategies conceal the real difficulties but often lead to burnout [#618].
5. DIVA-5 does not always reflect the female experience. Some examples (about fights, trouble with the law) do not fit women and are almost never endorsed by them. The authors recommend revising the instrument to account for female presentations of ADHD [#618].

#### Examples of hidden rigid rules in level-1 autism (criterion B)
Purpose: helps recognise rigid rules — part of DSM criterion B — when diagnosing autism (support level 1) in adults, especially masked women. The author considers this one of the hardest criteria: people build their routine so well, making themselves many super-small rules, that they don't notice the rules are rigid — «Ну правильно же так, в чём проблема» ("Well, that's the right way, what's the problem"). Such rules are very hard to recall on the spot in an interview, especially if you don't live with people who notice these "oddities" [#288].

The author's list of what such rules can look like:
1. Dishes lie in a specific order — e.g. the author is irked if a big plate is placed on a small one, or a glass is not in its place [#288].
2. Getting dressed in a specific order [#288].
3. Greeting every person you see [#288].
4. Always reading to the end whatever you started [#288].
5. Arranging objects this way and no other [#288].
6. Drinking tea only from one mug and coffee only from another (the source ends this item with «и т.д.», marking it as one pattern among many similar ones) [#288].
7. (Author's personal example from a trip) cash must ALWAYS be arranged in ascending order of denomination, and if it isn't, it must be rearranged immediately; she recalled it had always been so, even in childhood when she took money from her parents' wallets and played with it [#288].

Key qualification: there can be «миллиард миллионов» ("a billion million") such small rules whose violation causes discomfort, without the person thinking about them. What makes them *rigid* is that they are too strict — they persist regardless of context. Example: you are visiting someone and cannot drink coffee from the same cup you drank tea from the day before (and this bothers you) [#288]. The childhood recollection matters for the criterion because it shows the pattern has always been present [#288].

#### Why late diagnosis hurts

#### Estrogen, the menstrual cycle and ADHD
The author (post for 8 March) explains why female specifics must be considered in ADHD [#118]:
- Unlike men's, women's hormonal background changes over the month and over the life course, and research has begun on how this affects ADHD symptoms.
- Mechanism: it has long been known that estrogens participate in the development and plasticity of midbrain dopamine neurons. Since ADHD specifically involves the dopamine system, researchers began studying how monthly changes in estrogen concentration affect ADHD.
- Finding: compared with men of the same age and the same ADHD severity, women's symptoms fluctuated depending on the cycle phase. Moreover, amphetamine-class medications acted on them with different intensity depending on the phase.
- Menopause: after menopause estrogen levels drop sharply, and as a result — together with age-related changes — ADHD symptoms worsen.
- Hedge: there have not been many studies yet; the author hopes their number will grow so the female phenotype can be taken into account and women helped better.

A reader's Q&A [#706] adds a first-person case: the reader finds medication works much worse in the second phase of her cycle, so she gradually increases the dose and then lowers it again when the phase ends. The author confirms: yes, because of fluctuations of estrogens and other hormones the amount of dopamine changes, which also affects ADHD symptoms in women [#706]. (!) > "это ригидные, то есть слишком жёсткие правила, это значит, что вне зависимости от контекста - они будут сохраняться" (these are rigid, that is, overly strict rules, which means they persist regardless of context) — Anastasia Afanasyeva [#288]

> "эстрогены участвуют в развитии и пластичности дофаминовых нейронов среднего мозга" (estrogens participate in the development and plasticity of midbrain dopamine neurons) — Anastasia Afanasyeva [#118]

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- The hormone–ADHD research base is small; the author explicitly says there have not been many studies yet [#118].
- The cycle/stimulant findings are reported without naming the studies [#118].
- (!) UNCLEAR: tension between the comparison card (women "inattentive") and the study's conclusion that women endorsed more adult hyperactivity/impulsivity — see *Men vs women with ADHD comparison table* [#618, msg #618].
- (!) INCOMPLETE: the DocPsyFest talk's content, Lena Urdina's review, and the answer on orthostatic dysfunction are not in the source [#46, #619, #706].
- The study summary is a second-hand interpretation by the NO STIGMA channel, not the paper's own text [#618].
