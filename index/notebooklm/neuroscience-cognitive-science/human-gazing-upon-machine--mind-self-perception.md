## The self, consciousness and free will

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's posts on what the "I" is, whether we have free will, and how consciousness and the sense of self are measured in the lab. It has two strands: the author's own position that the "I" is a model the brain builds and that wording which separates "us" from "our neurons" is a mistake, and news write-ups of research on consciousness under anesthesia and on the brain rhythm behind body ownership. According to the source, this matters because the "I", though "only" a symbol, is still a key tool for planning and cooperating. Better measures of shifts in consciousness are also described as the key to treating insomnia and to bringing people back from coma. The body-ownership findings may help explain schizophrenia, where the sense of self is disrupted [#13, #14, #173, #690].

### Core ideas

- The "I" is a model the brain makes of itself. The self is a social-modelling routine that the organism turns on itself. It is a convenient symbol and not a thing in the head (see *Five-part definition of the "I"*) [#13, #14].
- Illusory but useful. At this stage of evolution the "I" is still an important and sometimes necessary tool, so the author says we should make the most of it (see *The "I" as a tool*) [#14].
- No little decider in the head. The author objects to IIT authors writing that "we, not our neurons" cause our actions. In the author's view this is leftover Cartesian-theatre thinking [#50].
- Sapolsky: no free will, and that makes the world more humane. In Sapolsky's view, giving up free will does not lead to anarchy or meaninglessness [#248].
- Measuring consciousness without disturbing it. A breath-squeeze task removes the sound stimuli that distort anesthesia studies, and it detects loss of responsiveness earlier [#173].
- Alpha frequency sets the timing resolution of the bodily self. A faster parietal alpha rhythm means sharper separation of "my body" from "not my body" [#690].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Five-part definition of the "I" (the self as a brain model)
The post opens with a joke-warning that it will be "hard, but good" and suggests forwarding it to Saved Messages. It then builds a definition of the "I" in five layers, each starting with «Я» — [#13]:
1. The first marker of separateness. "I" is the first word a human being says at about three years old. With it the child marks itself as something separate from the world and from other beings [#13].
2. The narrator of inner experience. The "I" is what the stream of thoughts in the head is described through, along with bodily sensations, labels for emotions, impulses and decisions to act [#13].
3. The anchor of memory and prediction. Through the "I", experience is stored in the memory of a specific individual. This lets the individual forecast how successful its activity will be [#13].
4. Social modelling turned inward. "«Я» — это результат социального моделирования организма, только применяемый к самому себе" (the "I" is the result of the organism's social modelling, only applied to itself). The same machinery we use to model other beings is pointed at ourselves [#13].
5. The keeper of continuity (Ship of Theseus). The "I" keeps up the subjective impression that a person's "Ship of Theseus" stays unchanged, even when all its parts have gradually been replaced [#13].

The post is illustrated with a picture: a painting signed "Magritte" of a man in a bowler hat and dark coat whose face is hidden by a green apple. It contains no text [#13].

The conclusion comes in part 2: "«Я» — это всего лишь удобный символ" (the "I" is merely a convenient symbol). In the current evolutionary branch of Homo sapiens, a "describer program" in the brain uses this symbol for two jobs. It reflects the dynamics of the organism's different systems, and it observes the forecasts of neighbouring programs. According to the author, the second becomes possible through special training of so-called "mindfulness" and "self-awareness" [#14].

#### Breath-squeeze method for tracking loss and recovery of responsiveness under anesthesia
Purpose. The method assesses when a person loses and regains consciousness under sedation without external stimuli from the experimenters. The source says it was first described in 2014 by sleep researchers and was used by the MIT/MGH team [#173].
1. Instruct once, then stay silent. Before the anesthetic was given, 14 volunteers were told to squeeze a special force sensor on every in-breath and release it on every out-breath. After this the scientists gave no more external stimulation [#173].
2. Sedate and watch the squeezing. The sedative (dexmedetomidine) was then given. When a subject stopped the "inhale-squeeze" pattern, they were counted as having lost responsiveness. When they started it again after the dose was lowered, they were counted as having recovered it [#173].
3. Record the brain throughout. The whole time, 64 scalp electrodes recorded brain rhythms. These showed the standard pattern of dexmedetomidine's effects, then the reverse as people woke. For example, as people lost responsiveness, the power of the occipital "alpha" rhythm (~10 Hz) fell and the power of slower "delta waves" rose [#173].

Why it was needed. The usual design has scientists use voice commands to find the moment a sedated volunteer stops responding, which means the anesthetic has taken effect. Guay argues that this sound can itself keep people awake longer and wake them earlier than usual. Guay also works on a second study, which tests whether closed-loop acoustic stimulation (CLAS) strengthens dexmedetomidine's effects. That study also needed a way to assess consciousness without sounds that skew the results [#173].

Results. (1) Under these conditions, the auditory-stimulation artifacts that had disrupted these EEG patterns in an earlier sound-based study disappeared. (2) Judging by drug concentration in the brain, the breath-squeeze method flagged loss of responsiveness at a lower amount of anesthetic than the sound method. The source reads this as greater sensitivity to change [#173].

#### Rubber hand illusion protocol
The source calls this a classic method for studying how sensations are perceived. The Karolinska study used it [#690].
1. One of the person's real hands is hidden from view. A realistic rubber hand is placed next to it, within the person's field of view [#690].
2. For some time the experimenter does the same thing to both hands at once, the hidden real one and the rubber one, for example stroking them [#690].
3. The person sees the rubber hand being touched while feeling the touch in their own hand. After a while, in many people these signals synchronise. When the artificial hand is touched they start to feel it as if in their own hand, and they may even flinch if the rubber copy is struck [#690].
4. Control: if the stimulation is asynchronous, the feeling soon fades, partly or completely [#690].

#### Four-method design of the Karolinska body-ownership study
The study had 106 participants and asked how the brain combines visual and tactile signals into one whole sensation. It combined four methods [#690]:
- Behavioural experiment. This was the rubber hand illusion, above [#690].
- EEG recording of the brain's electrical activity. This found that the frequency of alpha waves in the parietal cortex, where information about bodily sensations is processed, predicted how accurately participants told touches to their own hand from touches to the rubber one [#690].
- Brain stimulation. To test whether alpha frequency itself drives the effect, the researchers used non-invasive electrical stimulation to slightly speed up or slow down participants' alpha waves. This clearly changed the sense of body ownership. It also changed whether visual and tactile stimuli were perceived as simultaneous or asynchronous [#690].
- Computational modelling. The models showed that alpha frequency affects how precisely the brain estimates the timing of sensory signals. So these brain waves regulate the temporal precision of perception, and through that they help form the bodily "I" [#690].

### Detailed insights

#### The "I" as a tool, not just an illusion
After calling the "I" "merely a convenient symbol", the author adds that it is currently a very important and sometimes necessary tool. It helps a person to:
- understand their own capabilities;
- set goals for themselves;
- build a program of action;
- pass important information to other people;
- cooperate with others to expand the ability to adapt and develop.

The author's stance is pragmatic: «Если в данный исторический период мы устроены так, давайте брать от этого возможный максимум» (If in this historical period we are built this way, let's get the most we can out of it). The post says the practice in the next post aims to strengthen exactly this "somewhat unique" ability of our species [#14].

#### Critique of "we, not our neurons" wording in the IIT free-will paper
The authors of integrated information theory, Tononi, Koch and colleagues, published a paper detailing how fundamental free will follows directly from their theory. Its title is "Only what exists can cause: An intrinsic view of free will". The post quotes their claim in Russian translation: «…у нас есть истинные альтернативы, мы принимаем истинные решения, и мы — а не наши нейроны или атомы — являемся истинной причиной наших волевых действий и несем истинную ответственность за них» (…we have true alternatives, we make true decisions, and we — not our neurons or atoms — are the true cause of our willed actions and bear true responsibility for them) [#50].

The author objects to the split itself: why do «мы» (we) and «нейроны» (neurons) keep being separated? As a more grown-up alternative (the source says «по-взрослому»), the author offers this example wording: «Та часть наших мозговых программ, которая высчитывает, какое из решений наберёт наибольшее число аргументов за» (That part of our brain programs which calculates which of the decisions will gather the largest number of arguments in favour). The author calls this the minimum ("Ну хотя бы" — *at least that*). In other words, the decider should be described as a computing sub-process of the brain, not as a separate subject [#50].

The author's diagnosis is that the wording comes from the "Cartesian theatre" approach, a little decider-person in the head who supposedly *is* "us", and that such wording is still surprisingly hard to kill. The author asks readers why this is so and says they need peace on this subject. The author also mocks the title. It is translated as "Only what exists can compel", and the author asks who "compelled" the authors to think up such a title, since the paper argues for free will. The post signs off with «можно утащить моего человечка со сцены» (you can drag my little man off the stage), a joke that turns the Cartesian-theatre image back on the author [#50].

#### Sapolsky's "Determined": life without free will
The post frames the topic with everyday questions: Could I have acted otherwise? Am I to blame for having been in that state then? Did I really have a choice if I didn't see it at the time? According to the post, Sapolsky lays the free-will question out in terms of anthropology and ethics. The channel shares its own translation of the book's annotation, which makes these claims [#248]:
- The book synthesises what we know about how the mind works. It covers the tight link between "reason" and "emotions" and between stimulus and response, both in the moment and across a whole life [#248].
- Sapolsky goes through all the main arguments for free will and dismisses them, taking readers through quantum physics and the wildest shores of philosophy [#248].
- The history of medicine, and of brain science in particular, is in large part a history of realising that fewer and fewer things are anyone's "fault". For example, for centuries seizures were thought to be a sign of demonic possession [#248].
- Sapolsky admits it is very hard to give up our eagerness to judge others and ourselves. He applies his idea of understanding life without free will to some of the most important questions of human behaviour: punishment, morality and living together peacefully [#248].
- He concludes that living with the knowledge that there is no free will will be extremely hard day to day. It will not lead to anarchy, meaninglessness or existential malaise. On the contrary, it will make the world much more humane [#248].

#### Why studying consciousness is hard: the observer problem
Consciousness research runs into a general scientific problem: it is hard to study a system without affecting it. The MIT/MGH approach offers a way to assess consciousness without external stimulation, and it also shows that this way can be more accurate (see *Breath-squeeze method*). Co-author Emery N. Brown says the approach removes a major confound tied to the usual external stimulus. The team wants to use the technique to study other anesthetics. Guay notes that understanding the move from consciousness to unconsciousness could help treat insomnia better. Clarity about how waking happens could raise the chances of bringing people back from coma. Better ways to assess transitions of consciousness are key to both efforts [#173].

The channel's own note explains CLAS: clicks are played into headphones during sleep. This is believed to boost the slow electrical brain waves linked to sleep, and so to improve sleep quality and memory consolidation during that period (note signed HGUM/M. K.) [#173].

#### Alpha frequency and the boundary between self and world
The post opens with everyday examples of body ownership. What tells you that your body is really yours? That the gloves fit your hands, and that it was a passer-by who slipped, not you? Like many things we do easily every day, telling self from everything else is actually a complex brain process. The Karolinska study shows that we can tell our own body from the outside world thanks to a particular rhythmic brain activity in a specific part of the brain [#690].

The main finding compared two groups:
- Higher alpha frequency. These people were more sensitive to time differences between touches to self and to not-self. They noticed smaller differences than the other group, as if their brain worked at a higher temporal resolution [#690].
- Lower alpha frequency. These people had a wider "temporal binding window". Asynchronous touches were perceived more as simultaneous, and this loss of timing precision weakened the distinction between the body and the surrounding world [#690].

Implications stated by the researchers: lead author Mariano D'Angelo says the results may give new understanding of disorders such as schizophrenia, in which the sense of one's own "I" is disrupted. Co-author H. Henrik Ehrsson says they help explain how the brain combines signals from the body into a whole sense of self, and could help develop better prostheses as well as virtual reality that feels more real [#690].

## How perception works: from sensory fragments to meaning

### What this covers

This topic covers how the channel author explains perception: the brain's assembly of separate sensory fragments into a meaningful picture. It also covers why people in the same situation can end up with different pictures. Four posts make up the topic: (1) the visually evoked auditory response (vEAR) and the author's expectation-based explanation of it; (2) perception as "assembly", with the white-or-blue dress as the example; (3) simultaneous versus successive perception from Luria and Gibson, plus how this science turned into the "creative right brain / logical left brain" myth; (4) individual differences in colour perception and an online test of where a person draws the line between blue and green. According to the source, this matters because differences in perceptual "settings" show up everywhere, from optical illusions to a casually dropped phrase that sets off a heated argument (a "holy war") [#227].

### Core ideas

- Perception is assembly, and the assembly differs between people. We put fragments of sound, smell, colour and shape together into a meaningful picture. Different "settings" and "programs" of assembly give different results, even for people in the same circumstances [#227].
- Expectation shapes perception, possibly down to the body. The author thinks the most likely explanation of vEAR is that we perceive events through our expectations from past experience. She suggests that expecting a sound could even trigger a real physical effect in the ear (the acoustic reflex) [#199].
- The brain sees in two modes. It can see the whole at once (simultaneous) or build up details step by step (successive). Classic neuropsychology linked these modes to the right and left hemispheres, and damage studies still support that basic split [#366].
- The "creative right / logical left" brain is a popularised distortion. It is an appealing dualism that journalists and pop culture took from the real research and oversimplified [#366].
- Sensory hardware differs between people. Disputes like the dress and Laurel/Yanny show that people can register the same signals differently, depending on the number of different cones and hair cells they have [#474].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Simultaneous vs successive perception (Luria / Gibson)
Purpose: to explain how we can both recognise what we are looking at in an instant and then examine it piece by piece. The author calls this "zoom-in/zoom-out", seeing either in detail or as a whole. She introduces it as the background to an earlier post on perception in autism spectrum disorder (РАС), where she had used the two terms. She jokingly calls "симультанное" and "сукцессивное" "два матерных слова" (two swear words) because they sound so technical [#366].

The everyday illustration. When we see a picture we can quickly grasp "a goat". A second later we notice the parts: the cloven hooves, the white fur, the "hellish" slit-pupil eyes "that look straight into your soul". Both happen because the brain can perceive the world in two modes, sequentially and all at once [#366].

The two components:
1. Simultaneous perception (симультанное, "общее сразу", the whole at once). This is the direct, immediate perception of objects and events as a whole, "simultaneous" in English. In Luria's scheme it sits in the right hemisphere. When it fails, as after right-hemisphere damage, a person has trouble recognising the overall structure of an image and cannot "see the forest for the trees" ("за деревьями не видя леса") [#366].
2. Successive perception (сукцессивное, "по шагам", step by step). This is perception built up in steps, through details and through changes that happen over time, "successive" in English. In Luria's scheme it sits in the left hemisphere. When it fails, as after left-hemisphere damage, a person misses the small parts and perceives only the general features [#366].

The diagnostic example. Patients are asked to draw a large letter "M" made of small triangles. People with right-hemisphere damage draw only the separate triangles. People with left-hemisphere damage draw only the overall "M" [#366].

Origin and status of the construct, as the author tells it:
- In Soviet neuropsychology the split became a fundamental construct with its own terms. Alexander Luria wrote a great deal about it and created many probes (пробы, small diagnostic tasks) to diagnose these functions. He placed the "step-by-step" mode in the left hemisphere and the "holistic" mode in the right [#366].
- The author notes that neither Luria's works she found nor her university lectures explain *how* Luria arrived at these concepts. She asks readers to send her the answer if they find it [#366].
- At around the same time, the American scientist James Gibson was researching perception. In 1966 he published the book "Чувства как системы восприятия" ("The Senses Considered as Perceptual Systems"), which according to the author was the first to describe these processes. The book distinguished direct perception of objects and events (simultaneous) from perception of changes over time (successive). The author says this distinction reflects Gibson's broader argument: perception is not only the interpretation of sensory data from the sense organs, but also the way those data are organised in real time [#366].
- Some of Gibson's work has been criticised in light of new data, but the author says the foundation is still considered correct. Her evidence is the hemisphere-damage pattern and the "M" of triangles described above [#366].

#### Expectation-removal self-experiment for vEAR
Purpose: a test the author made up herself, offered to readers to check whether a phantom sound "heard" from a silent visual comes from expectation [#199].
1. Watch the silent visual that seems to "sound". In the original post this was a clip of a tower lifting off the ground and coming back down [#199].
2. Remove the expectations. Strip away your anticipation of what *could* happen during such an event, meaning the sound such an event would normally make [#199].
3. Say the facts out loud, several times. Describe only what is literally on screen, for example: «Вышка отрывается от земли и опускается обратно» ("The tower lifts off the ground and comes back down") [#199].
4. Check the result. Ask yourself whether the visual still "sounds", or produces any sensation other than a visual one [#199].
Why it should work, in the author's reasoning: if vEAR comes from expecting a sound, possibly through the acoustic reflex (see *What vEAR is and how it might work*), then focusing on bare facts should weaken the effect. The author poses this as an open question and does not report results [#199].

### Detailed insights

#### Perception as assembly of fragments
The author defines perception (восприятие) as how we gather separate pieces of something into an understandable picture. It is the meaning we extract from the flow of sounds, smells, colours, shapes and everything else that hits us, from outside and from inside. The picture we get depends on our assembly "settings" and "programs". Her examples: is the rustling in the bushes the wind or a tiger? Is the waterfall whispering our name? Both are perception at work [#227].

The author finds it most interesting when people in the same circumstances assemble the final picture differently. She says this shows up everywhere, from whether a dress is white or blue to how a casually dropped phrase can start a "holivar" (холивар, a heated online dispute) [#227].

#### What vEAR is and how it might work
The post opens with a reader poll from an earlier post: more than half of the readers said they heard a sound from a silent visual. In reply, the author introduces vEAR, the visually evoked auditory response, which she renders in Russian as СОВВ, «слуховой отклик, вызываемый визуально» [#199].

The discovery. According to the author, researchers at the University of London found it in 2017. People were shown a circle on a screen that flashed in the rhythm of Morse-code signals and were asked to find similar sequences. The people who did this best were those who, while watching, seemed to *hear* the flashes. They made up 22% of the participants [#199].

Explanation 1: synesthesia. The first hypothesis is that vEAR is a form of synesthesia. The author describes synesthesia as an unusual way the five senses can work, in which stimulating one sense also causes a response in another. Her examples are the number 1 feeling hot, the colour yellow causing a sour taste in the mouth, and the name «Володя» (Volodya) feeling smooth. The last is her own childhood memory [#199].

Explanation 2: anticipation (the author's preferred view). Some participants described the sensation as anticipation, a kind of readiness to perceive the sound that might go with the image. The author says this is in her view the most likely version. We perceive an event through a whole range of sensations, thoughts and emotions, and our perception is often closely tied to expectations from past experience [#199].

A possible physical mechanism: the acoustic reflex. The author calls the acoustic reflex well documented. Usually in response to a loud sound, though not always, the stapedius muscle (стременная мышца) in the ear contracts. This reduces the strength of the vibrations passed on to the cochlea (ушная «улитка») so that things get quieter. The contraction also slightly changes the shape of the inner ear, which can make us hear an extra noise. The author then asks whether expecting a sound could trigger the same effect, and answers «Похоже, что да» ("It seems so"). This is offered as her own reasoning, not as a tested finding [#199].

#### How the hemisphere science became the "creative vs logical brain" myth
The author explains the myth through a general human tendency. There are ideas we pick up easily and with pleasure, and as soon as something close to them appears on the horizon we rejoice: "this is it!" She promised to explain how this works in a repost the next day [#366].

Humanity has a very rich history of dualist ideas: soul and body, typically masculine and feminine, logic and ethics. "Rational versus creative" belongs in the same series. So when the idea appeared that the right hemisphere holds whole pictures and the "airy-general", while the left holds separate details and algorithms, it was irresistible. It was especially appealing to people who hunt for the juicy core of the news rather than the devil in the details. The author says the situation fits a well-known comic's phrase, «учёный изнасиловал журналиста» ("the scientist raped the journalist"), which inverts the usual "journalist mangled the science". After that, she says, the simplified version spread to the masses [#366].

The lesson is that the real finding concerns *modes of perception*, whole versus parts and simultaneous versus successive. The popular "creative right / logical left" personality typology is an oversimplification of it [#366].

#### Individual differences in colour and sound perception
The author recalls the heated disputes about whether the dress was blue or white, and whether the voice in the headphones says Laurel or Yanny. In her account, these showed that different people can register such signals differently at the level of the body, depending on the number of their different cones (колбочки, the colour receptors in the eye) and hair cells (волоски, in the ear). Her conclusion: «Тут наша сенсорика по-настоящему экстра-!» ("Here our sensory system is truly extra!") [#474].

She then describes a colour test, sent to her by Marco, that measures where a person places the boundary of "blue". She is careful about what it shows: she has not yet looked into how much it is about perception and how much it is about cultural norms for naming colours. Even so, it already gives interesting results. She and Marco turned out to be "beautiful freaks of nature" [#474].

Caveat for sensitive readers: if some colours can cause you sensory overload, the author advises skipping the test [#474].

## Music and the brain

### What this covers

This topic collects what the channel taught about why music moves people biologically: the author's notes from the Coursera course "Music as biology" (music as an outgrowth of speech intonation; how major/minor, rhythm and specific intervals carry mood), a long two-part review of the 2025 neural resonance theory paper by Eleonora E. Harding and co-authors (brain waves physically synchronizing with music, consonance/dissonance as frequency ratios, rhythm vs melody as different frequency bands, emotion as movement across a landscape of stability), Bobby McFerrin's live demonstration of universal note prediction, and the sonification of stars ("music of space"). [#157, #683, #684, #685, #720, #25]

The source's reason this matters: it answers why music produces a whole "sea of feelings" — not only joy but longing, awe, bright sadness, goosebumps and anger — and why people understand these emotional nuances despite cultural differences; the author's conclusion is that music is an ordered structure that lets people feel connected across eras and cultures, "literally on the same wave". [#683, #684]

### Core ideas

- Music is built on speech intonation. The author's course notes hold that music is inseparable from emotion, derives from the intonations of the human voice, and that the most common modes survived historically because they best match speech. [#157]
- Mood has two axes in music. Valence (+/-) is carried by major vs minor; energy by rhythm, speed and the sharpness of transitions between notes. [#157]
- The old consensus was prediction-reward; the new view is physical resonance. For years science explained musical pleasure as the reward of correctly predicting the next sounds; Harding et al. (2025) propose that perception does not depend on prediction or on a cultural "dictionary" — brain oscillations physically synchronize with the oscillations in the structure of music. [#683]
- Consonance and dissonance follow frequency ratios. Simple ratios feel stable, complex ones tense, the classical major/minor triads slightly shaky. [#684, #685]
- Rhythm and melody are different frequency bands processed in different brain systems (brainstem at 50–4000 Hz for melody; auditory and motor structures at ~1–8 Hz for rhythm). [#684]
- Emotion arises from moving across gradients of stability (valleys = attractors, ridges = dissonance), and this is said to be universal for all cultures within our species; culture only shapes preferences via Hebbian learning. [#684]
- People share an innate sense of harmony, as Bobby McFerrin showed by having an audience predict pentatonic notes from direction alone. [#720]
- Stars can be heard: their radiation can be converted into sound for research or for aesthetic pleasure. [#25]

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Music emotion code (from the "Music as biology" notes)
Purpose: explains how musical structure transmits mood, according to the author's notes from the Coursera course "Music as biology", which concerns the biological preconditions behind music becoming established in the human species. The author presents these as her "заметок-размышлений" (notes-reflections) and invited comments from musicians and other interested readers. [#157]

1. Origin in the voice. Music is inseparable from emotion. It was created on the basis of the intonations of the human voice, and the modes (лады) most frequently found in music became established historically because they correspond most fully to speech. [#157]
2. Valence via mode. The basic shades of mood — valence/charge (+/-) — are conveyed through major or minor. [#157]
3. Energy via rhythm and articulation. The degree of energy is conveyed through rhythm, speed (tempo) and the sharpness of transitions between notes. [#157]
4. The most expressive intervals. Thirds, sixths and sevenths are the brightest carriers of emotional mood: the minor versions are used more often in minor, the major versions in major. [#157]
5. Folk vs classical nuance. There are small differences between folk music (НМ, народная музыка) and classical music (КМ, классическая музыка): the minor sixth conveys a sad mood more often in classical music, the minor seventh — more often in folk music. [#157]
6. Hypothesis about composers' brains. The author writes that people who compose music "probably" must have a close connection between the limbic system and the temporal areas (the temple), especially in the right hemisphere, where the zones involved in the intonational side of speech are located. This is hedged as a probability, not a finding. [#157]

#### Neural resonance theory (Harding et al., 2025)
Purpose: a new explanation of music perception proposed in May 2025 by Eleonora E. Harding and co-authors (paper at https://www.nature.com/articles/s41583-025-00915-4). The author calls the section "Дирижёр нейронного оркестра" (Conductor of the neural orchestra). [#683]

- The problem it addresses. The prior consensus: we enjoy music thanks to a feeling of reward when we correctly predict the sounds following those already heard. But that does not explain why we feel not only joy but also longing, uplift, awe, bright sadness, goosebumps and anger, nor how we "understand" emotional nuances despite cultural differences. [#683]
- The claim. Our perception of music does not depend on predictions and does not even require a musical cultural "dictionary". Synchronization between the oscillations in the structure of music and the electrical activity of the brain happens physically. [#683]
- The theory. Neural resonance theory (ТНР) is a concept of dynamic coordination of rhythms — of the brain, the body and external stimuli. According to the approach, this coordination underlies our attention, perception and actions. [#683]
- Source limitation. Because the full text is copyrighted, the author shares only its most striking ideas, plus a selection of melodies and rhythms for readers to feel the ideas (that selection was posted as links in a comment and is not part of the source text). [#683]

The components of the theory, as the author lays them out, follow as separate frameworks below (neural entrainment, the two mechanisms, the ratio classes, the frequency bands, the stability landscape). [#684]

#### Neural entrainment — the brain synchronizes its own waves with musical ones
1. How brain waves arise. Neurons transmit chemical and electrical signals, causing excitation or inhibition of neighbouring neurons. This produces waves of electrical activity, part of which oscillate in sync and part out of sync. [#684]
2. What frequency means. Waves have a frequency — the number of rises and falls per second (Hz). From their pattern one can judge alertness, fatigue and cognitive load, as well as disturbances in neurological and mental illnesses (measured with EEG and MEG). [#684]
3. Entrainment. Waves in the auditory and related networks synchronize with the rhythm and tempo of music (neural entrainment): their phase and frequency reflect the structure of the sound. [#684]

#### Two synchronization mechanisms: how we hear notes and feel tension
1. Phase locking ("temporal coding") — happens in the hearing system; it is what lets us perceive a sound signal as a specific note. [#684]
2. Mode locking — a physics concept: oscillating systems synchronize their frequencies and phases. Through this we register a sound as harmonious (consonance) or as somehow wrong, unstable (dissonance). [#684]

#### Three stability classes of intervals by frequency ratio
How a sound is felt depends on the ratio of the frequencies involved: [#684]
1. Stable (consonance) — when the frequency ratio is simple: unison (one note, 1:1), octave (2:1), perfect fourth (4:3) and perfect fifth (3:2). "здесь живёт консонанс" (here lives consonance). [#684]
2. Tense (dissonance) — when the ratio is complex: all sevenths (the source gives 28:27 followed by "и др." without listing the rest), seconds (10:9 and 9:8), and tritones — "аккорды дьявола" (the devil's chords), 1.414:1, which split the octave exactly in half. [#684]
3. Slightly shaky — the triads of classical major (major third 5:4) and minor (minor third 6:5), and the pair of sixths (8:5, 5:3). [#684]

The accompanying images give the reference numbers. The first [#684] shows one octave of the C major scale with frequencies in Hz: C 130.8, D 146.8, E 164.8, F 174.6, G 196.0, A 220.0, B 246.9, C 261.6, labelled "musical intervals", with Root, 3rd, 5th and Octave marked (C, E, G and the upper C — the major triad plus octave). The second [#685], titled "Musical intervals and their frequency ratio", lists the major-scale intervals as ratios to the root: 1st (root or fundamental) 1.00; 2nd 1.12; 3rd 1.26; 4th 1.33; 5th 1.50; 6th 1.68; 7th 1.89; 8th (octave) 2.00. [#684, #685]

#### Two frequency bands of hearing: melody vs rhythm
Rhythm and melody feel different to us "не просто так" (not by chance): they are different frequency ranges. [#684]
1. Melody (notes, timbre, harmony) — continuing the mechanisms above, part of the waves are heard as melody; here the brainstem synchronizes phases with high-frequency sounds (50–4000 Hz). [#684]
2. Rhythm or pulse — when waves are low-frequency (delta/theta, ~1–8 Hz), processed in auditory and motor structures of the brain. This is exactly where we get the bodily sense of rhythmic movement and the desire to move in response. [#684]
3. The in-between zone — when frequencies lie somewhere between these ranges, we hear a buzzing of an unclear note and unclear rhythm. [#684]

#### Stability landscape of music: attraction and attractors
The author calls attraction the key to emotional impression ("привлекательность (а точнее, «аттракция»)" — attractiveness, or more precisely "attraction"). Attraction is the process by which a dynamic system tends towards certain states in which it gains stability; the point of attraction is the attractor. [#684]

Imagining music perception as a landscape: [#684]
1. Unison (the tonic) and major triads — very stable, like deep valleys with a strong attractor at the bottom. [#684]
2. Minor triads — still valleys, but shallower, moderately stable. [#684]
3. Dissonances — unstable, like ridges. [#684]

Our expectation, tension and emotional resolution arise as we move between these gradients of stability. This, per the source, is how music becomes emotionally expressive — and it is universal for all cultures within our species. [#684]

#### Two purposes of space-music sonification
Every star emits radiation in different ranges, and this radiation can be converted into sound. "Music of space" can be created for two purposes: [#25]
1. Research — to collect information and look for patterns. [#25]
2. Aesthetic pleasure. [#25]

### Detailed insights

#### Is music a cultural phenomenon at all?
The author's answer, drawing on the neural resonance review: culture shapes our preferences as a palette of rhythms and tonalities through our "сонастройки" (attunement) within a process of (Hebbian) learning. Styles differ, but our emotional reactions to basic structural patterns remain shared because the underlying neurodynamics are the same. Her conclusion: music is a "delightfully ordered structure" that can serve as a tool against the entropy of the world, and even without special knowledge it lets us feel connected with each other — erasing the boundaries of eras and cultures and becoming literally "on the same wave". [#684]

#### Why noise cannot be hummed
The author asked herself where white, pink and brown noise fit. Noise is not one frequency but almost all frequencies at once, irregular and differently shaped (by "colour"). That is why we cannot hum noise and why it falls into neither the category of rhythm nor even "roughness". Even if we hear "s-s", "sh-kh" or a "whistle", the sound would have to be filtered around a single frequency (for example 440 Hz) to become a pitch. [#684]

#### Even rhythms
The author recommends "starting with something sweet" before the long read: a video by Alexander Pushnoy which, in her words, very nicely shows one of the key ideas — why we like even rhythms. The video itself has no transcription in the source. [#683]

#### Universal note prediction: Bobby McFerrin's demonstration
About 17 years before the post, at the World Science Festival, musician Bobby McFerrin demonstrated in real time that people have an understanding of musical harmony independent of cultural experience. Through a live performance with the audience he showed that people can predict which note will come next in a melody if they are only shown the direction — "Сила пентатоник!" (The power of pentatonics!). The author links this to the "recent discovery of a universal biological basis" of musical perception (the neural resonance post). [#720]

#### Personal illustration of an early musical drive
The author recalls a childhood exchange: "Mommy, I'll go play." — "Play what?" — "The keys…" — offered as a light P.S. to her notes on music biology. [#157]
