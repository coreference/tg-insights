## Freedom, capitalism and contemporary culture

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel author's social and cultural essays: a critique of late capitalism ("Capitalism 3.0", which sells identity, taste and meaning, and the attention economy that leaves no room for recovery); a theory of freedom that links inner work and psychological maturation with resistance to power (drawing on Erich Fromm, Michel Foucault and James Scott); analyses of culture wars, post-individualism and status hierarchies; and cultural observations from Bali, Koh Phangan, the USA, Georgia and Moscow. The author frames these not as abstract philosophy but as "maximally applied" questions that run "like nerve endings" through concrete lives — he says he left Russia precisely because of the limits of its culture and the lack of realised freedom, and that "the main question of the 21st century is not how to be successful but how to be free" [#1284, #1332, #1601].

Two new posts extend the topic. The first (#1909, an excerpt from the author's forthcoming book) diagnoses a "crisis of relevance": after tradition and religion lost their unquestioned status and postmodernism deconstructed them, no shared language remained for telling reliable from unreliable, technology multiplied voices and bubbles, and people began to cling to metrics. The second (#1929) is a reflection from Kyoto on a woman playing the flute under a bridge, used to criticize a world saturated with the logic of market, power and achievement, and to point to purposeless play, celebration and contemplation (the Japanese "ma") as what has been lost. According to the author, this matters because it erodes the ability to know what is good "on the inside, not by KPI" and turns even rest and spirituality into production and self-presentation [#1909, #1929].

Relation to the existing knowledge file. These posts extend, not contradict, earlier material. #1909 gives a new intellectual genealogy (Taylor, Vervaeke, McGilchrist) for the "KPI life" and "material fundamentalism" critique from #1086, and its point about social media and media bubbles extends the attention-economy analysis (#1650). #1929 revisits the critique of tourism as consumption first made about Bali (#141), but now the author explicitly includes himself among the consumers ("И сам становлюсь одним из них"), echoing his earlier stance that he is an "accomplice" in culture's crimes (#1086). It also extends the attention-economy and Capitalism 3.0 critique by naming the loss of purposeless inaction as a central casualty [#1909, #1929].

### Core ideas

- Freedom is a product of inner work and maturation. All genuine inner work (psychoanalysis, therapy, mindfulness) is a liberating process — freedom from blind, conditioned, compelled repetition; freedom and psychological growing up are intertwined (full detail in *Freedom as the fruit of inner work*) [#1284].
- Two kinds of unfreedom. Authoritarianism controls through fear and propaganda; late capitalism governs through desire and attention. The free person today is not necessarily the one with more power, knowledge or money [#1601].
- Freedom is found inside systems, not by fleeing them. The path is "being in the system but not being the system", with awareness at three levels — personal, communal and systemic (see *Integrating path to freedom* and *Three levels of awareness*) [#1601].
- Fromm's fork. Modernity gives opportunity and uncertainty; one either grows into positive freedom through love and work or escapes into submission, domination or robotic conformism (see *Fromm's two paths*) [#1332].
- Capitalism 3.0 absorbs the alternatives. Corporations have learned to absorb the cultural patterns that used to let people stand partly outside the system — minimalism, slow living, honest formulas — and to sell identity and symbolic capital (see *Capitalism waves*) [#1648].
- The attention economy is late capitalism's key form of violence. It forbids nothing but leaves no space for recovery, fragments the wholeness of daily life and "proletarianizes" attention (see *Attention economy*) [#1650].
- Culture wars are about power, not about the stated issues. They are emotionally violent because shifting norms threaten people's sense of power and force painful reviews of their own histories (see *Culture wars*) [#1333].
- Systemic constraints, not laziness. The unconditional-income experiment is read as showing that people are limited by systemic factors such as poverty rather than by their nature [#1144].
- Culture is always becoming obsolete; every generation must build a new one. Culture always emerges from counterculture [#640, #557].

- Deconstruction was easier than construction. Postmodernism exposed traditional structures as constructs, often unjust ones, but no new supports arose in their place; everything became "a matter of point of view" (see *Crisis of relevance*) [#1909].
- Science cannot answer "how should I live?". It describes what is, not what ought to be; psychotherapy and neo-spiritual practices tried to fill the place religion left for inner work [#1909].
- Without discernment we worship numbers. Lacking a common basis for trust, we cling "as if in a religious fervour" to objective metrics, which hides causes, connections and systems behind symptoms and fragments (see *Crisis of relevance*) [#1909].
- We have become person-functions. Everything is done for a goal; market, power and achievement logic leaves no space for simplicity, and even rest and spirituality have become production and self-presentation [#1929].
- The living remains, but where nobody watches. The space for simplicity has not disappeared inside us, but it may need to be sought where there is nothing to buy and no one to sell to [#1929].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Fromm's two paths — positive freedom vs escape into authoritarianism and conformism
Context / when it applies. The author revisited Fromm's *Escape from Freedom* (classic social psychology he first studied in university) and presents it as "maximally applied philosophy or sociology — people really live like this", saying it is especially relevant to today's Russia (he declines to explain why, expecting it to become obvious when reading). Fromm's starting point as retold: after the traditional structure of medieval society collapsed, people from ordinary estates and strata gained the possibility of developing and creating a unique life. That life demands participation in competition and taking on responsibility, which places the individual in conditions of anxiety and loneliness. Modernity is therefore simultaneously positive (it grants many possibilities) and negative (it confronts a person with extreme uncertainty). In this reality Fromm distinguishes two fundamentally different paths [#1332].

1. The path of freedom: growth through independence. Fromm's quote: «Один путь ведёт человека к позитивной свободе: он может спонтанно связать себя с миром через любовь и труд, через подлинное проявление своих чувственных, интеллектуальных и эмоциональных способностей» ("One path leads a person to positive freedom: he can spontaneously connect himself with the world through love and work, through the authentic expression of his sensual, intellectual and emotional capacities"). Freedom here is not merely the absence of external coercion but the capacity to be oneself, to be aware of one's uniqueness and to build relationships with the world on the basis of love, creativity and work. It is an active, responsible existence in which a person does not lose individuality but unfolds it. This path requires courage, consciousness and inner maturity. It is a process of integration: the person accepts their separateness yet stays connected to other people — not through submission or fear but through meaningful interaction [#1332].
2. The path of authoritarianism and conformism: escape from oneself. The first path demands effort and facing one's own anxiety; for many people this encounter with inner freedom is too frightening, so a second path appears — flight into submission, authoritarianism and conformism. Fear of loneliness and uncertainty pushes people to look for external structures that give a feeling of stability; this can mean giving up personal autonomy to merge with a collective, a state, an ideology or a leader. Fromm's quote: «Механизм бегства от свободы состоит в тенденции отказаться от независимости своей личности, слить своё “я” с чем-нибудь внешним, чтобы таким образом обрести силу, недостающую самому индивиду» ("The mechanism of escape from freedom consists in the tendency to give up the independence of one's personality, to merge one's 'I' with something external in order to gain the strength the individual himself lacks"). It has two sub-forms:
   - Authoritarian tendencies: the person either submits to authority or strives to dominate others. In the extreme these are *masochistic* tendencies (voluntarily handing one's freedom to a strong leader) and *sadistic* tendencies (subjugating others through violence). Authoritarianism can unfold in a society all the way to fascism [#1332].
   - Conformism: life becomes *robotized* — we only act out the life and behaviour patterns accepted in the mainstream and are completely deprived of independence in the form of authenticity and spontaneity. The author calls this the form more familiar in modernity [#1332].

Why the second path fails. Any form of escape does not solve the problem of anxiety but only produces new forms of dependence. It is the path of *pseudo-freedom*: a person seemingly gains strength by joining a strong idea, leader or state, but in fact stops being himself, merges with collective patterns and loses individuality, refusing the path to genuine strength — individuation and growth. Summary: the path of freedom is difficult but allows a personality to be born; the path of authoritarianism and conformism looks tempting but leads to inner emptiness and loss of self [#1332].

The pseudo-self (from the book page shown in the post's image). Substituting genuine acts of thinking, feeling and wanting ultimately replaces the genuine personality with a pseudo-personality. The genuine self is the creator of its mental acts; the pseudo-self only plays a role prescribed from outside but does it in its own name. A person can play many roles and be subjectively sure each one is him, while actually playing each according to his idea of what others expect — "у многих людей, если не у большинства, подлинная личность полностью задушена псевдоличностью" ("in many people, if not most, the genuine personality is completely stifled by the pseudo-personality"). Sometimes in dreams, fantasies or intoxication part of the genuine self surfaces — feelings and thoughts that have not arisen for years; sometimes these are bad thoughts suppressed out of fear or shame, sometimes the best in a person, also suppressed for fear of ridicule or attack. Losing the self and replacing it with a pseudo-self leaves the individual extremely unstable: having become a reflection of others' expectations, he loses himself and his self-confidence, and to overcome the resulting panic he must conform further, obtaining a self from continuous recognition and approval of others — "Пусть он сам не знает, кто он, но хотя бы другие будут знать" ("Let him not know who he is, but at least others will know"). #### Integrating path to freedom ("in the system but not the system")
Purpose. The author's answer, written after a channel vote, to how to be free under both authoritarian and late-capitalist control, as an alternative to fighting or denying power [#1601].

Diagnosis first. Authoritarianism controls through fear and propaganda: in societies with archaic political structures freedom is still taken by brute force — it is forbidden to think, say and do anything that in any way threatens power. Late-modern capitalism governs through desire and attention: freedom is dissolved in endless activity, scrolling, simple and "mindful" consumption; we lose it in careerism and constant productivity. Outwardly we look free, but we are constantly plugged into a socket that is supposed to give us happiness but actually feeds on us. In one place we deal with direct and vile violence a person must turn away from every day "until they come for him"; elsewhere with likes, goods and an eternal need for ever more refined consumption and self-identity, for which we work without pause and try to gather all the world's resources into ourselves. "Успешность давно стала властным нарративом" ("Success has long become a narrative of power"); "Постоянно эффективное тело — это тело, тренированное под рынок" ("A constantly efficient body is a body trained for the market") [#1601].

Components of the integrating path:
1. See how power creates you (Foucault). The author says Foucault would say power not only oppresses but creates the subject: we are a result of power, not only its victims. Understanding the power structures that define us is the first step of resistance; resistance begins when we dare to see and study the invisible loops or shackles we live in [#1601].
2. Do not let systems' needs absorb you. Freedom is acquired by not allowing other people's and systems' needs to swallow you, govern you, or hold you in the vice of fear or desire. It is not simple denial of or flight from power, but the capacity to recognise how power shapes us and to choose who to be in this process, returning power to oneself [#1601].
3. Include, do not discard (integral approach). This integrating practice works at whatever stage a society is in — not utopian projects but life inside systems as a conscious subject. In the spirit of the integral approach: inclusion rather than rejection of the current level, its overcoming and development — "Не слияние, не отрицание" ("Not merging, not denial") [#1601].
4. Be in the system, not of it. To find yourself free you do not necessarily have to go to prison fighting lies, move to Bali, exit the system, stop earning money or stop going to the office. Each person has their own path, but it is more likely found "будучи в системе, но не будучи системой" ("being in the system, but not being the system") — in the skill of separating oneself from it and not abetting, from one's own side, the violence it causes. And understanding that it is completely impossible to avoid the influence of systems at all: we will always be included in them and be part of them [#1601].
5. Slowing down and attentiveness are no longer weakness but key means of liberation. Freedom is lost when we do not have time to feel what is happening and how events affect us, when we cannot make sense of life — and we start "marching in line, whether loyalist or successful". Multi-sided presence helps to create autonomy: not agreeing just like that, not believing propaganda, not broadcasting lies, not turning into a mechanism that thoughtlessly executes orders, remaining able to feel and think — "Иногда просто не стать тем, кем тебя хотят сделать" ("Sometimes simply not to become who they want to make you") [#1601]. #### Three levels of awareness required for freedom
Freedom is lost when we are absent from our own life, so the road to it requires awareness of oneself and presence in what is lived — but on three levels at once. Without all three, resistance turns either into an illusory isolated individual path or into activism without inner support [#1601].
1. Personal awareness. Guiding question: “Где мной управляют страх, желание или усталость?” ("Where am I governed by fear, desire or fatigue?"). Without it we become an automatic cog of any system [#1601].
2. Relational / communal awareness. Guiding question: “Как мы создаём поля доверия, где возможно быть живыми?” ("How do we create fields of trust where it is possible to be alive?"). Without it we get stuck in the illusion of solitary liberation that leads to no change [#1601].
3. Systemic / structural awareness. Guiding question: “Какие силы формируют поле, в котором мы живём?” ("What forces shape the field in which we live?"). Without it we risk becoming involved in reproducing oppression [#1601].

#### Capitalism waves — what each sells (Capitalism 3.0, part 1: intellectualized capitalism)
The author promised readers (after a vote) to write about modern capitalism from at least three sides; this is the first. The generalising feature of the new wave: corporations and power have learned to absorb the cultural and social patterns that previously allowed a person to be at least partly outside the system [#1648].
1. Industrial capitalism sold things [#1648].
2. Post-industrial capitalism sold emotions [#1648].
3. Intellectualized capitalism (Capitalism 3.0) sells the feeling of understanding life, cultural taste and identity. Minimalism, honest formulas, craftsmanship, slow living and the aesthetics of silence — once if not marginalised then certainly not mainstream — stopped being an alternative to the mass market and became fuel for a new cycle [#1648].

Case — Aesop. Historically a small Melbourne cosmetics lab: glass, monochrome, a philosophy of simplicity. It was bought by the Brazilian corporation Natura & Co, then in 2023 by L'Oréal for 2.5 billion dollars, turning into "cultural luxury" — not luxury in the classic sense but an aesthetic marker of a certain worldview, inside an ordinary corporate system. The author's reading: L'Oréal is not stupid — people buy Aesop because they do not want to buy big corporations' products; such brands are a refuge both from mass market and from showy luxury, "бренды для тех, кто считает, что не любит бренды" ("brands for those who think they don't like brands"). So L'Oréal keeps the lamps in the shops, the concrete and the eucalyptus smell, but behind the authentic brand now hides a corporation selling not soap or cream but personal refinement [#1648].

From possession to self-identification. In third-wave capitalism the product more than ever becomes a way to build a subtle identity. Buying "smart" things — membranes, fitness bracelets, supplements — a person writes himself into a narrative: not a consumer, but a conscious, trend-aware, feeling subject. The author invokes Bourdieu's *symbolic capital* — status that cannot be measured directly in money but is recognised by cultural signals; in today's urban class this symbolic capital has become the main currency: taste, knowledge, mindfulness and the right aesthetics practically determine a person's place in society [#1648].

Why the construction is so powerful. The striving to create identity and belonging to a circle through brands — which covers not only face cream but also a fashionable psychotherapist or a retreat — carries many important needs. Modern people have lost many points of support, and the meanings offered by the market have become a way to reassemble oneself. Aesop, Le Labo, Muji and Kinfolk sell not goods but ritual orderliness that becomes "микрорелигией нашего времени" ("the micro-religion of our time"): spirituality through aesthetics and meaning gathered not in church but in consumption. We really do want honest things, to not get wet in a breathable jacket, depth and taste — and modern capitalism has been able to give this, or more precisely to package and sell it neatly. It integrated the critique of both mass and luxury consumption and added to its shelf something different: minimalism, naturalness, mindfulness, honest formulas. In this paradigm meanings easily become the new luxury, and naturalness turns out to be expensive [#1648].

The problem of late capitalism. The problem is less the brands than the overall result and the quality of life the majority gets. To buy Aesop cream, do vipassana in beautiful hotels and stay dry on the way to a coffee shop in a technical jacket, today you need to earn like the top 15% of the population in a developed economy and the top 1–3% in Russia, which the author calls the country with the strongest inequality on the planet. The world of late capitalism is technologically and semantically abundant but economically increasingly exclusionary; the status of quality and taste turns into a tool of social segregation, "и это грустно" ("and that is sad") [#1648]. #### Attention economy and the proletarianization of attention (Capitalism 3.0, part 2)
Mechanism. Post-industrial systems create an environment in which attention becomes the key scarce resource. According to the author this happens not through a corporate conspiracy or ill intent but as a natural consequence of a new technological stage where profit is created, among other things, through attention. A person gains the ability to manage identity with unprecedented fineness — but along with it comes the problem of what state he is in to actually live that identity [#1650].

The great battle for attention. The author cites philosopher Jonathan Crary: sleep today is not just physiology but the last zone not fully captured by the market; the world of the struggle for attention strives for life to have no sleep and no pauses at all. The result is a space and culture of total usefulness, in which we always need to do more. Under constant overload a person must keep reassembling himself, as if not so much living as constantly administering his own state. Unlike the experience of ancestors over millennia, in the attention economy, where every "useless" moment must be filled with "use", the natural wholeness of being is crushed — "дробится естественная целостность бытия"; daily life is experienced as a sequence of fragments rather than a continuous process [#1650].

Working on ourselves but never keeping up. We increasingly treat ourselves as a project — maintaining focus, motivation and resources. But when the whole environment constantly claims our attention, any attempt to keep access to depth becomes an effort available only to a few. Most live in constant stimulation, meeting a "pandemic of fatigue". The author's thesis: this is one of the key forms of violence of late capitalism — "он не запрещает и не давит напрямую, но просто не оставляет пространства для восстановления" ("it does not forbid and does not press directly, but simply leaves no space for recovery") [#1650].

The central unresolved conflict. On one side, the demand for subtler forms of identity based on contact with oneself is growing: mindfulness, therapy and retreats have become necessary. What was marginal culture ten years ago will remain a key trend today and for the next ten years — an objective trend, not a fashion. On the other side, the environment multiplies the obstacles to real contact with oneself; these tools often become mere compensation for circumstances instead of development, and the person loses the familiar foundation. Bernard Stiegler called this the *proletarianization of attention*: attention economics and media algorithms do not just take our time — we lose centuries-old cultural capacities to be in depth: to think deeply, pass on experience, form a stable "I", contemplate and be bored [#1650].

How to resolve it (the author's position). The answer must be sought together. When Crary writes about the 24/7 world without sleep and silence, the author reads him as describing a cultural shift rather than criticising technology; it need not be seen as decline or degradation, but must be analysed as a significant transformation. The author personally sees no constructive answer in romanticising the past or in the romantic nationalism promoted in right-wing discourse — these lead to false closedness and devastating authoritarianism. Late capitalism does create new forms of load (he says this is confirmed by research, clinical data and cultural observation), but it also opens new capacities: flexibility, complex thinking, sensitivity to diversity, the ability to live in uncertainty and to work with identity. The constructive stance is neither to reject modernity nor to dissolve in it without remainder, but to learn to understand the present and on that basis build a healthier future [#1650].

#### The Post-Individual — three stages of society (Yancey Strickler)
Kickstarter co-founder Yancey Strickler presented a development model in which society evolves toward a post-individualism of the future, already emerging thanks to the spread of network technologies. Strickler has been experimenting with an alternative creators' community, Metalabel, where "творческие люди кооперируются, а не соревнуются" ("creative people cooperate rather than compete") [#1290].
1. Pre-individualism — traditional societies prioritising collective values and communal ties [#1290].
2. Individualism — modern societies emphasising personal freedoms, autonomy and individual achievement. Classical individualism is the life-defining search to establish who you are and to develop that personality throughout life [#1290].
3. Post-individualism — an emerging culture combining personal and collective interests, seeking balance between individual needs and the common good. Strickler argues it reflects our ancestors' experience of living in communities, but in a new, modern context: a move to a more integrated society where personal and collective interests complement each other. In a world where a search for individuality can be started and repeated simply by logging in or creating a new digital profile, the meaning of that search has changed. Key line: “Когда-то люди рождались в сообществах и должны были искать свою индивидуальность. Сегодня люди рождаются индивидуалистами и должны искать свои сообщества.” ("Once people were born into communities and had to search for their individuality. Today people are born individualists and must search for their communities."). The post-individual experience arises when a person accepts their individuality but for various reasons feels the need for deeper meaning and contact with others, and learns to develop individualism within communities [#1290].

Key features of post-individuality:
- The follower count is the key social metric of the individualist era; in the post-individual era what matters more is which groups you belong to [#1290].
- Post-individual dynamics spread group identities, many formed around small interests or differences between groups; micro-identities become a way to obtain belonging [#1290].
- Like a collective language inside groups, memes are the natural form of post-individual communication; mastering your group's language becomes the key initiation ritual proving belonging. These symbols show which "tribes" you belong to and how deeply immersed you are in their context [#1290].

#### Five points of a new culture (the author's draft mission for deep mind)
Context. Re-sharing reflections from 2019 when he looked for wording for deep mind's mission, the author argues that mainstream culture is always on the verge of ending: it was created by the past while we live in the present. Citing Peterson and Spiral Dynamics, culture constantly becomes obsolete in response to changing living conditions. "Задача каждого поколения - создать лучшую культуру, возможную для текущего уровня сознания" ("The task of every generation is to create the best culture possible for the current level of consciousness") — understanding its inevitable end due to natural, never-ending change. The company's big goal lived intuitively in the team's values, actions and lifestyle but was not yet expressed in a simple message; he feels it is about creating a new culture. His first drafts of what culture to create [#640]:
1. From atomisation to community. We are too atomised and disconnected; it is time to take a conscious step toward community and an integrated society [#640].
2. Out of the overwork wheel. We work too much; it is time to look at the causes and consequences of this way of life and find a way out of the wheel [#640].
3. Emotional work, kindness and love. People at all social levels feel insecurity and tension, which paralyses and creates suffering in society as a whole; we need to realise the importance of emotional processing, kindness and love, and of non-egoistic motives for action [#640].
4. Legitimise the search for meaning. We feel a lack of meaning in our activity; it is time to legitimise searching for one's own path and discovering life-filling meanings, plus flexibility toward different forms of living [#640].
5. New forms of joy. The author sometimes does not understand what makes us happy as a community — sport and politics do not; "Зачем жить вместе, если не радуешься?" ("Why live together if you don't rejoice?"). New forms of joy for society must be found [#640].

What unites the people he feels strongly connected to by values is the desire to find a better way of living and uncompromisingness toward unsatisfying social standards; this need generates a natural process of answering the question "how much good can one person bring?" He calls on readers to create new cultures where they are, calling it vitally necessary [#640].

#### Laloux's critique of the demand economy and four proposed solutions
Reported live from the IEC2020 conference. Frederic Laloux said that in China during the virus more asthmatics survived thanks to changes in the atmosphere than died of COVID — the virus not only kills but also saves lives (from humans themselves). The situation points to the damage constant human activity does to the world: 75% of insects disappeared over the last 20 years, and the same applies to fish, reefs, mangroves and forests [#207].

Diagnosis (three points):
- The system of constant demand creates a "Frankenstein economy" that requires constant pumping [#207].
- The marketing cycle of desire → purchase makes us unhappy and constantly instils a sense of inferiority [#207].
- The mindfulness movement of recent years has already produced a new class of people dissatisfied with the current system; before COVID they were too shy to start building a new one [#207].

Solutions Laloux puts up for dialogue (four directions):
1. What if we completely banned all forms of advertising [#207].
2. Obliging manufacturers to give multi-year warranties on all goods [#207].
3. Moving fully from the "extraction–processing–waste" system to a zero-waste cycle by raising taxes on everything extracted from the earth [#207].
4. Developing consciousness everywhere, for example through mindfulness practices in schools [#207].

The author's verdict: "! Crazily ambitious" [#207].

#### Rosling's destiny instinct
The author calls Hans Rosling's *Factfulness* one of the most useful recent books, one that "clears the brain" with a factual picture of the world, and quotes its passage on the destiny instinct, framed as how perception errors make us miss change and lag behind progress [#223]:
- Definition: «Инстинкт судьбы - это идея о том, что внутренне присущие характеристики определяют судьбы людей, стран, религий и культур.» ("The destiny instinct is the idea that innate characteristics determine the destinies of people, countries, religions and cultures"); things are as they are for inevitable reasons — they always were so and will never be otherwise. It makes us believe that false generalisations or dramatic gaps in numbers are not only true but fated [#223].
- Evolutionary origin: earlier, living conditions changed rarely; it was enough to learn once how things work and assume they would keep working that way, instead of constantly re-evaluating [#223].
- Social function: choosing a fixed destiny for a group helps unite it around an unchanging goal and fosters a sense of superiority over other groups — ideas that must have been important for the morale of clans, tribes, nations and empires [#223].
- The trap: the claim that a religion, culture or country will never change (or must not change) because of traditional or unshakeable "values" recurs again and again; it seems analytical, but on closer inspection "подобные заявления часто оказываются проявлением чувств под маской констатации фактов" ("such statements often turn out to be feelings masked as statements of fact"). Today this instinct prevents us from noticing revolutionary changes in the societies around us. «Общества и культуры не похожи на скалы. Они меняются и не стоят на месте» ("Societies and cultures are not like rocks. They change and do not stand still") [#223].
- Typical examples the author lists: "our special path"; ineradicable corruption; lazy employees; unlucky children; the conviction that the Islamic world is fundamentally different from the Christian one; the conviction of Africa's insurmountable backwardness [#223].

#### Crisis of relevance — the chain from lost tradition to the cult of metrics

Purpose: the author's explanatory sequence for why modern people cannot tell what matters and whom to trust, and why inner work itself is turning into an efficiency project. Each link causes or enables the next [#1909].

1. Life inside tradition. Following Charles Taylor's "A Secular Age" — described as a fundamental study that rethinks the West's transition from a society where faith was unquestioned to an era where it became just one option — for a long time people lived inside a religious, cultural and communal tradition. Tradition gave not only meaning but also structure. Religion also gave ethics: a shared understanding of good and evil that did not need proving because it was given [#1909].
2. Postmodern deconstruction. Postmodernism, the author says, did important work: it revealed that these structures were not universal truth but constructs, often unjust and causing much suffering. But deconstruction proved far easier than building: the supports collapsed and no new ones arose. The result is a world where everything "depends on the point of view" and therefore every point of view is equally valid [#1909].
3. Substitutes that cannot fully replace. Science took religion's place as the source of truth, but by definition it has no answer to "how should I live?", because it describes "how things are", not "how they ought to be". Psychotherapy and various neo-spiritual practices tried to take over the place for working with the inner world [#1909].
4. Chronic instability of foundations. Taylor describes the modern person as having no "naive" belonging to a moral tradition: one always knows one could have chosen otherwise. This gives freedom and at the same time creates chronic instability of foundations [#1909].
5. Technology arrives at the worst moment. Exactly when no common language remained for distinguishing reliable from unreliable, technologies arrived that gave everyone a platform and divided people into political media bubbles whose information literally does not penetrate one another. Social networks, and now AI, let anyone look like an expert without real expertise. As a result there is more information than ever and almost no way to understand whom and what to trust [#1909].
6. Crisis of relevance realization. John Vervaeke describes this deficit of discernment as a crisis of relevance realization: the destruction of the ability to feel what is really important and what is secondary, what is signal and what is noise. We are surrounded by endless interpretations and techniques but understand less and less which can be trusted and what exactly each is for [#1909].
7. The cult of objective metrics. When it is unclear what to rely on, we cling to "objective metrics" "as if in a religious fervour" and start believing only in numbers, indicators and proven facts. Behind this surface view it becomes harder to see causes rather than symptoms — connectedness, causes and effects, the system rather than pieces of reality. The author leaves open the questions: what is the reference point, what is the norm, what is good "на самом деле, а не по KPI" ("really, not by KPI") [#1909].
8. Hemispheric imbalance (McGilchrist). Psychiatrist and neuroscientist Iain McGilchrist, in his many years of research on the asymmetry of the brain hemispheres, reaches a non-obvious conclusion, as the author reports: modern culture systematically strengthens the fragmentary, analytical, measurable mode of perception at the expense of the holistic and contextual one. We have learned to classify and optimise excellently but hold the whole ever worse [#1909].
9. Inner work becomes efficiency. Consequently whatever does not fit into a metric starts to be perceived as non-existent or unreliable, and inner work gradually turns into yet another domain of efficiency: how many minutes of meditation, which protocol, what measurable result [#1909].

### Detailed insights

#### Freedom as the fruit of inner work
In a post called "Library of freedom", the author says that this season the topic of freedom matters to him more than mindfulness — prompted by an anniversary at his alma mater, Moscow State University, held with a portrait of Stalin. These "philosophical" questions run like nerve endings through concrete life "на реальной шкуре" ("on one's own hide"), so freedom is a maximally practical conversation. All genuine inner work — psychoanalysis, other therapy or mindfulness practice — is a liberating process: by bringing the light of consciousness into automatic habits or into the group thinking reproduced in oneself, we unexpectedly find ourselves in the territory of freedom from blind, conditioned and, in that sense, compelled repetition — a territory perhaps never available to our ancestors. Hence freedom is closely intertwined with the development of consciousness and psychological maturation, and vice versa [#1284].

His research is "integral" — not only from inside out but from outside in. Having spent 5 years in central Russian hierarchies and much longer in clan structures, he felt that culture, mentality and logic of life well. They are not Russia, and he will fight so that nobody ever equates them, but they strongly determine "the smell in the air". He says he left Russia precisely because of this culture's limits and the lack of realised freedom, showing in details that are not small — from portraits of the creators of totalitarianism on the walls of the leading university to many other symptoms. "Война — не причина, а лишь следствие этого мышления" ("War is not the cause but only the consequence of this thinking"), which still largely defines the society. Through inner practice and concrete outer struggle he has become less tolerant of abuse of power, and increasingly wants to live in places able to give an individual, regardless of status, the highest level of freedom — which requires maturity both of public consciousness and of institutions working toward that goal. Where those places are he leaves an open question [#1284].

Therapy that teaches submission. He meets a limitation in his research: one can truly talk about freedom only with truly free people; others can teach you to submit, calling it adaptation — sometimes even considering it genuine therapy. Real inner work does not merely soften pain and help a person adapt to violence but leads to "радикальной свободе быть человеком" ("the radical freedom to be human"); many psychological methods and schools instead contribute to normalising the system of violence by merely relieving its symptoms. Beyond systems of submission and control, human history and thought are full of examples of refusal to submit and achievements in proclaiming freedom, which he looks for in books as historical examples and role models [#1284].

#### Freedom-loving cultures: James Scott's hill peoples and the new nomads
A Financial Times journalist interviewed the author about the crypto boom on Koh Phangan and asked why, of all places in Thailand, Phangan attracts crypto-interested people. The author's answer: Phangan, Bali, Portugal and other rising points on the map accumulate people of a new format and culture, which is first of all about freedom and unwillingness to put up with the current imperfect world order. This culture demands openness to the new, so everything that was recently taboo is seething around it: research into using substances and practices for personal development, open or semi-open relationships, life outside career and other classic social norms. One could call it marginal, but "культура всегда возникает из контркультуры" ("culture always arises from counterculture"); today's marginality sounds ever more attractive to people tired of foolish governance and the endless race. Nation states offer citizens a path that becomes unattractive with even a small increase in awareness [#557].

History repeats: there have always been people unwilling to trade freedom for anything — "ведь именно такими мы рождаемся и такими умираем" ("for that is how we are born and how we die"). In his anthropological study of the hill peoples of Southeast Asia, James Scott described in detail groups who centuries ago rejected the lowland system of tribute and taxes; their whole way of life in the mountains was built around one idea — the art of not being governed. These groups flowed into the mountains from China, Siam, Vietnam and surrounding lands and for centuries stayed outside any neighbouring statehood; nation states could do nothing with them. The author asks whether decentralised technologies now create an economic basis for the freedom-loving people of the new age [#557]. In a later post he adds that he once lived in villages of the Karen, one such hill society, who arranged their life so as not to fall under the power of states, keeping an autonomous and free way of life [#1284].

#### Culture wars as a struggle for power
Thesis. Culture wars are not really about feminitives (feminine forms of nouns) or whether it is acceptable to hit on younger girls; consciously or not they are fought over power — a topic traumatic for most of us, which is why these internet battles carry so much attention, fear, pain and anger. Context: in the past month Russian-language internet was "like a front line" — some see sexualised violence, others do not; some want attention to trauma, others already hate the "new ethics" [#1333].

The absurdity of "new-ethics terror" in Russia. The author finds it strange in a context where a celebrity sex scandal leads to no real consequences, and where commenters do not yet live in a society in which an average IT company office (let alone a gas company) seriously discusses gender identity or tackles toxicity. Talking about the "terror of new ethics" where one can easily "get a mop" at a police station is, he says, absurd and sad at once. Most telling was the scene where investigators and victims spent weeks explaining how they re-checked facts a thousand times, why it is violence and why the victims are "not to blame themselves", while the accused simply ignored it. Rationally that should settle it, yet the battles continue to the death — even in the therapeutic community people threw so much mud at each other that face to face it would likely have come to blows [#1333].

Why it is emotional, not rational. The war would not exist if it were only rational; it is mostly emotional and traumatic. It touches the point where the "I" begins, the boundary between "old" and "new", "normal" and "imposed" [#1333].

Mainstream vs marginalised social location — the author's own example. When our viewpoint, which we often identify with ourselves, rests in the mainstream (roughly everyone around thinks as we do), we are privileged and not inclined to understand those who fall out of the norm. Living in Russia, the author did not think of himself as a member of the titular nation — "Это была просто вода, в которой я плавал" ("It was just the water I swam in") — and barely noticed the reality of people on construction sites or in taxis. In Georgia, when neighbours playing loud music at 3 a.m. turned his attempt to quiet them around by calling him a "Putinist" at the door, he suddenly felt what it is like not to have habitual power. In reality he had simply met the ordinary burden of his new social location, previously in his blind spot: the mainstream part of his social location abruptly became marginalised, and the amount of power he could dispose of changed with it [#1333].

Applied to new ethics. The same happens with new ethics: some people suddenly find that what seemed "the norm" stops being one — even a norm where parents may beat children, a boss may yell and men may harass. Under the pressure of new ethics such a "norm", slowly, is pushed from the mainstream into marginality. This is an objective process society demands, but also a threat — less to ideas than to the sense of power, or real power, people hold along with these "norms". To cope with the fear of losing power and with a painful revision of one's personal history — where "вдруг выясняется, что ты не круто тусовалась с мужчинами постарше, а тебя просто грязно юзали" ("it suddenly turns out you weren't cool hanging out with older men, you were just dirtily used") — it is easier to attack the object that triggered it: easier to wave one's arms than change one's views of self and world, easier to defend the old than accept a change of status [#1333]. #### Status, respect and invisible labour (Moscow after four years)
Sitting in a gym locker room on Chistye Prudy after training, the author noticed a wet mop silently approaching his feet; he realised that if he did not lift his feet the cleaner would simply skip the spot. He lifted them, the mop slid exactly where his feet had been, he put them back and said "thank you" — the cleaner clearly heard and saw him but said nothing and moved on, "as if used to being invisible and knowing his place". Returning to Moscow after 4 years, he says the city's changes or stagnation in visible details tell him little about how life develops; it is captured much more clearly in such contacts — "тут и происходит жизнь" ("this is where life happens") [#1380].

What he feels acutely is the special significance of social position, status and place in the hierarchy: everything feels a bit more black-and-white and fixed, as if a person must constantly know who he is relative to others, and if you do not recognise this immediately and accidentally cross a boundary, punishment may follow, sometimes severe. In this stiffness he feels like a "village fool" newly arrived in town — he understands perfectly but is not ready to play along everywhere, not because he became higher or purer, but because he belongs a bit less to this society and has seen too many other statuses and roles. It prompts a reflection (explicitly "not as a comparison") on caste societies, where your role is defined and you grow into it: you must understand it, accept it and freeze in it. He stresses New York and San Francisco also have hierarchies — "how many judgments and evaluations in New York" — but with more possibility to be different and with "уважением в пространстве между статусами" ("respect in the space between statuses") [#1380].

The Colorado trail image. One summer in Colorado he sat on a hilltop where a mountain trail starts right from town and watched people in sneakers and leggings stream into the mountains after work — some from a coffee shop, some from a Google office — united on the trail in their ability to enjoy nature, their country. He reflected on respect that changes space: a "здоровое, а не патриархальное" ("healthy, not patriarchal") respect in which one can be okay regardless of status. For Trump to get to work someone has to switch on the traffic light; that person needs a morning coffee someone brews — all of them create the country today, though very differently [#1380].

Without respect. Without respect come fear, division, fragments; "we" and "they" appear. The cleaner becomes not someone with whom "we created Russia this day" but someone who wipes the floor "for me" — and since I am the centre of this world, I may not notice him at all. Walking near Kitay-Gorod, giving way to a cyclist who thanked him, he called out "you're welcome" and felt it sounded unexpected. Whether from habit of creating the environment he wants to be in or because it feels needed here, he notices a growing wish to add respect: support good-neighbourliness, thank people and make invisible labour visible [#1380].

Honesty. At the same time he feels embarrassed to speak of such experiences, as if conversations about the present and future that require comparison and reflection have become inappropriate, with a boundary already prepared for his status: any words touching reality are either dangerous or will trigger insults. Yet "меня нет без честности" ("I do not exist without honesty"): to live as a human it is essential to create spaces where honesty is possible, where one can speak the truth without twisting words — not to isolate oneself in one's truth but to see. "Если перестаём быть честными, видеть и рефлексировать — то умираем" ("If we stop being honest, seeing and reflecting, we die") [#1380].

#### Material culture, the KPI life and the metaphysical
After visiting the mother of a friend who has died — both cried — the author notes how superficially and dismissively modern culture treats everything metaphysical: as if only the material exists, and subtle states, views and acts are not just denied but treated as stupid, although, he says, modern and traditional science have enough evidence of the stupidity of such crude materialism — perhaps "material fundamentalism". What replaces the subtle: all of life becomes a KPI — a project or problem, often in a successful wrapper, that must constantly be solved. We solve things until a *bang* hits us on the head; until then we focus on goals and keep trying to build something up, thinking about ourselves 99% of the time "as if infected with a mental virus of self-absorption", and one can live for years on the surface with no contact with something subtler and vaster than our separate story [#1086].

"Either you go to the monastery, or you wait until it comes to you": sooner or later something happens that no answer about external improvements and accumulation will help with, and we realise all our security is useless — "бесполезная фальшифка" [sic] ("a useless fake") on which we spent all our life or all of ourselves. The author stresses that when he criticises culture he always does it through himself: he can be far but never fully separate — "В ее преступлениях я соучастник" ("In its crimes I am an accomplice"); he too is stuck and forgotten in some race, perhaps together with readers, and it takes something extraordinary that touches the heart to open to a reality that obviously and mercilessly exceeds his KPIs. The saddest thing in human stories is that we kill each other without seeing common connectedness and the preciousness of life — a direct consequence of material culture, a crime against the nature of life that turns from an idea in the head into a real criminal act on earth [#1086].

From sadness to inner protest. Sadness prompts searching, and then protest against culture's crimes, which starts inside rather than outside: first notice them in yourself, decide that this common hypnosis is not useful to us as a species, look for its causes, and try to show a different view, live and act anew. Alan Wallace, as quoted by the author, says he understands young people who seek and expect that something material — things or reputation — will satisfy them with comfort, security and joy, but asks his peers over 70: «ну а вы то, что? Правда еще верите в такое?» ("and what about you? Do you really still believe in that?"). Returning to a more natural state or exploring the metaphysical is always available, but it requires admitting the limitedness, even abnormality, of the view in which many people around live — not starting yet another war with them, but at least removing oneself from all visible crimes; "unpupating" as much as possible from the culturally shared and endlessly personal anxiety and desires about one's own story; looking at what has always been in the space behind them; and stopping the search even for a second to see a space free from neurotic seeking [#1086]. (The post also mentions the author's mother may be seriously ill — a personal note without further content [#1086].)

#### Unconditional income: people are limited by systems, not by nature
The author describes a three-year unconditional income experiment by Sam Altman's organisation (OpenAI's head): 1,000 people received $1,000 per month on top of their earnings for 3 years, while a control group of 2,000 received $50 per month for filling in questionnaires. His reading: "Мы не никчемны по своей природе, но ограничены системными факторами" ("We are not worthless by nature but limited by systemic factors"); financial stability makes people healthier in every sense [#1144].

He studied the entrepreneurship section first — how attitudes and, more importantly, actions toward entrepreneurship change when a person has more money and stability. Key finding: receiving cash raises interest in entrepreneurship. Those receiving $1,000 showed more interest in entrepreneurship, were more inclined to take risks, more often said they had a business idea, and were more confident they would start a business within five years. This interest did not always lead to real action, but a significant increase in entrepreneurial activity was recorded. Against the stereotype that people, especially poor people, are lazy and unable to act on their own, the author concludes the problem is systemic constraints such as poverty: with financial support people become more confident in their abilities and start taking initiative; many stereotypes are wrong, people are able and willing to act given the right conditions [#1144].

Reduced stress from systemic factors improved the whole situation: participants used over-the-counter painkillers 53% less, men by as much as 80%, and men were 45% less often in dangerous situations. The study also analysed changes in finances, consumption, health and employment, which the post does not detail. #### Bali: tourism, self-alienation and information inequality
On a trip to Bali in February 2020 the author describes (ironically) that at Denpasar airport all Russian women get false eyelashes, and that he saw much of a culture he has long not encountered; the women and the men accompanying them disturbed him. They reminded him of growing information inequality in the world despite seemingly full information access. He had grown used to thinking that modern science and widely available development practices were washing away the culture of violence against oneself, and that waves of the embodiment movement, mindfulness, feminism and social democracy were exposing some cultural barbarities and freeing people from them [#141].

He asks what fragility of self-esteem lets someone "violate" their body with cosmetics in 40-degree heat, and what must be so eagerly sought from others outside that one can fully alienate one's own body. He suggests this inner alienation is what creates modern Bali — a beautiful place deeply wounded by tourism: unaware of himself, the tourist throws around tips and over decades spoils a national culture built over centuries. "Туризм - привилегия современных людей и боль планеты" ("Tourism is the privilege of modern people and the pain of the planet"). He notes his own privilege of avoiding tourist routes for years and that he is now growing out of his rose-tinted glasses [#141].

Through maturation and development, places like Bali can be restored to health. But not everyone has access to information about the new world, current data and scientific views on human growth; not everyone has begun healing self-esteem and depending less on the outside. Such people not only suffer more and violate themselves at a time when this is no longer necessary — they are increasingly unable to fit into a modernity ever less tolerant of violence. Mitigating this inequality and growing up are needed by the world; only an adult consciousness begins to see the world systemically and understand interconnections — then every plastic bottle thrown into the sea echoes as pain in your own body and stops being something far away and unimportant. He would like the privilege of seeing Bali without traffic jams, the ocean without garbage, and beautiful girls naturally beautiful [#141].

#### America through Spiral Dynamics and lived experience
The author spent almost the whole year in the US (New York, Boulder, San Francisco). First visiting at about 20 and then going to New York almost every year until 28, he says NYC laid a foundation of his worldview and gave breadth of thinking, though it was also where his best friend was killed and where he flew to recover after a serious loss [#1242].

America triggers people: it is loved and hated, dismissively simplified or needlessly complicated; behind projections and lack of real experience people simply do not understand it — "как и я сам" ("like me"). There is reason to be triggered even setting foreign policy aside. The US sets many trends, and as in European countries a substantial part of people at the leading levels of consciousness is concentrated there. In Spiral Dynamics terms: green groups more often develop in democratic, affluent societies with a strong focus on inclusivity — evident in California and Colorado; yellow groups concentrate where learning, interdisciplinarity and innovation are highly valued, e.g. San Francisco and Silicon Valley. And, strangely, the US today hosts a key centre of Tibetan Buddhism, if not of Buddhism generally [#1242].

American economic well-being remains a fact: the influx of talent from around the world, stable institutions and entrepreneurial culture mean California's GDP alone is twice Russia's, and the average salary in Texas is $5–7k. Yet the American self-assurance that everyone wants to move there looks funny to him, since the world has changed so much that many places are now more comfortable for everyday life. In midlife New York resonates less: after a round-the-world trip he does not want to live there, though there are reasons to visit — a world power centre offers many opportunities — but he no longer wants constant overload, and the community's interests, though multilayered, feel rather conformist. Building dialogues by tossing statuses at each other became so stifling that after 2 months he left for Colorado, with its gold-rush spirit and Jack London's "real men" [#1242].

In Colorado a "modern Tibet" has emerged — many monasteries and qualified teachers with views of snowy peaks in Crestone; very expensive ski resorts where a season stay costs more than flying halfway around the world to Courchevel; and Boulder, legendary in spiritual, triathlete and intellectual circles and roughly the size of Moscow's Chistye Prudy, where big tech, Buddhism, psychedelics and mountains mix. #### The flute under the bridge: purposeless life in a functional world
In Kyoto, going out after breakfast for matcha, the author passed a young woman sitting alone under a bridge by the river. On the way back, when rain had started, he heard a flute: she was playing in that secluded place, sheltering from the rain. He felt he had witnessed something alive that would have existed without him, and the scene captivated him "like a rare animal". The lesson he draws: such simple, unwatched activity has become rare enough to seem striking [#1929].

His diagnosis: today we are used to doing everything for a goal — running somewhere, solving, selling and buying. Everything is saturated with the logic of the market, power and achievement, leaving literally no space for such simplicity. "Мы живем в функциональном мире и сами все больше становимся людьми-функциями" ("We live in a functional world and are ourselves increasingly becoming person-functions"), to whom simple flute-playing seems unusual and perhaps even inappropriate. We have lost the capacity for inaction that is not directed at anything — play, celebration, contemplation; everything has turned into production and self-presentation, even rest and spirituality [#1929].

#### Temples consumed: tourism and self-presentation
Even entering majestic temples, where for centuries there was a place for ritual and for something greater than the bustle of material life, the author says he can feel nothing today. Instead he meets thousands of tourists trying to consume the place: one must put on a kimono and take beautiful photos. He does not exempt himself — he too goes to take photos to post on Instagram later. This continues the earlier Bali critique of tourism (#141) but with self-inclusion rather than observation of others [#1929].

#### Doing something not for oneself, with the body
Hearing the flute in the rain, the author felt a desperate wish to do something not for himself, and to do it less with the head and intellect than with the body, as she did — as if expressing and manifesting life in that body with one's whole being, not for someone and not for achievements [#1929].

#### Ma 間 — the living pause
Japanese aesthetics has the word "ma" 間: a pause, interval, emptiness that is not empty but alive. The author likens the flute under the bridge to such a ma in the middle of a functional city. Judging by how strongly the scene affected him, he concludes the inner space for simplicity and the living has not completely vanished; but it should perhaps be sought where nobody is watching us, where there is nothing to buy and no one to sell to [#1929].
