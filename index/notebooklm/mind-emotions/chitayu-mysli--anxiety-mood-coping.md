## Anxiety, phobias, panic and OCD: exposure and self-observation

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's material on working with fears, specific phobias and panic attacks through exposure, on why medication taken "just in case" undermines that work in panic disorder, and a forwarded therapist's post on self-observation for suspected OCD with an adapted Florida Y-BOCS scale. According to the author, exposure is the approach that currently shows the best long-term result, while other routes mainly relieve the state; the forwarded post argues OCD is under-recognised and that self-observation can help people suspect it and seek help [#370, #614, #699, #772].

### Core ideas

- Exposure is the most effective long-term method. Among the many ways people relieve fears and panic, the author holds that gradual exposure — facing triggers little by little and learning to live through anxiety — remains the most effective and the one showing a lasting result (see *Gradual exposure*) [#614, #370].
- Keep facing the fear even when some of it remains. A residual fear does not mean the work failed; what matters is continuing to meet the fear and continuing to act [#370].
- Medication "in reserve" is a safety behaviour in panic. Pills relied on as a rescue reinforce the belief that anxiety is dangerous and unbearable alone, so within safety-behaviour work they are removed (see *Medication as safety behaviour*) [#699].
- OCD is hard to recognise and benefits from structured self-observation. People rarely report OCD symptoms; a home self-observation scale (Florida Y-BOCS) helps reveal the pattern of obsessions and compulsions (see *OCD self-observation*) [#772].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Gradual exposure
Purpose / when to use: for fears that limit you, specific phobias and panic attacks. The author describes exposure as "по чуть-чуть сталкиваемся с триггерами и страхами, учимся переживать тревогу и условно дрессируем наш мозг на то, чтобы не реагировать такой паникой" (we face triggers and fears little by little, learn to live through anxiety and, so to speak, train our brain not to react with such panic) [#614].

Components, as the author teaches them through her swimming story and her answer:
1. Start small, in a controllable setting. Begin with a dose of the feared situation you can bear. In her case she went to a swimming pool, where she got used to depth gradually — a pool helped because "все видно" (everything is visible), unlike open water where she could not see or control the bottom, which was the core of her fear [#370].
2. Increase intensity step by step. She kept increasing the time she could swim; the general rule she gives is "начинайте по чуть-чуть сталкиваться со своей тревогой, постепенно наращивая её интенсивость [sic] и перенося её" (start facing your anxiety little by little, gradually increasing its intensity and bearing it) [#370].
3. Tolerate the anxiety instead of escaping it. The point is to bear (переносить) the anxiety and learn that it can be lived through, which is what retrains the brain's panic response [#370, #614].
4. Be consistent but not perfectionist. She went to the pool irregularly ("кое-как", haphazardly), which she links jokingly to her ADHD, but decided to "отстать от себя" (get off her own back) and went as she could while continuing to increase her time in the pool. Over several years this was enough: she now swims "Даже в море, даже до буйков" (even in the sea, even out to the buoys). Lesson: irregular but continued exposure still works over time [#370].
5. Continue even with residual fear. Some fear remained: swimming alone one evening in Turkey she thought "вот сейчас я утону, и никто не узнает" (now I'll drown and nobody will know). Her conclusion: "очень важно продолжать сталкиваться со своим страхом и продолжать действия" (it is very important to keep facing your fear and keep acting) — and, as she notes, she did not drown [#370].

The author's story as a worked example (including what did not help): As a child she was very afraid of swimming: she liked water, but not where she could not see or control the bottom, so she always swam with armbands. Once, deciding a spot was shallow, she swam off without them — and, wanting to check the bottom, went under, panicked and began to drown. After that she did not swim for a long time and was very afraid of depth. Later her husband taught her to swim with "суровыми уроками" (harsh lessons), pulling her under water by the leg — after which she again regularly avoided swimming for a while. The lesson implicit in the sequence: a frightening experience and forced, overwhelming exposure led to more avoidance, whereas gradual, self-paced exposure in a controllable environment worked [#370].

#### Medication as safety behaviour in panic disorder
Purpose / when to use: answers the question whether, with panic attacks, one should where possible give up strong medication such as antidepressants, tranquillisers and neuroleptics. The author answers "Да, все правильно" (Yes, that's right) [#699].
1. Mechanism — safety behaviour forms. With strong anxiety, and panic attacks in particular, the brain begins to form safety behaviour, which the author defines as essentially short-term avoidance of facing anxiety. The inner logic is: I have the pill; if I feel bad I'll take it and everything will be fine [#699].
2. Long-term cost — anxiety is reinforced. In the long run this reinforces the anxiety through the belief "я не могу самостоятельно выдержать тревогу, она опасна, мне нужны таблетки" (I can't withstand anxiety on my own, it is dangerous, I need pills) [#699].
3. Intervention — remove the medication. Therefore, "в рамках работы с охранительным поведением, мы убираем прием препаратов" (within the work on safety behaviour, we remove taking the medication) [#699].
(!) INCOMPLETE: the source does not describe how or under whose supervision medication is withdrawn (pace, involvement of a prescribing doctor); it only states that it is removed as part of safety-behaviour work [#699].

#### OCD self-observation with the Florida Y-BOCS
This framework comes from a post forwarded from the channel NO STIGMA, written by a therapist [#772].
1. Why self-observation is needed. The forwarded author calls OCD "один из самых сложных диагнозов" (one of the most difficult diagnoses) to diagnose and treat. People rarely seek help on their own and do not complain about the symptoms even to doctors; most often the disorder becomes known already in therapy, when the therapist notices a repeating structure of anxiety [#772].
2. What to watch for. In OCD that anxiety takes a specific form — obsessions and compulsions [#772].
3. Homework. At the slightest suspicion of OCD, the therapist often gives patients homework: observe their thoughts, rituals and reactions so that patient and therapist can see the patterns together [#772].
4. The scale. Sometimes she uses the FLORIDA YALE-BROWN OBSESSIVE COMPULSIVE SCALE (FLORIDA Y-BOCS), filled in by patients at home; it helps structure observations and understand exactly how the anxiety shows up. She spent 10 hours preparing an adapted table ("настоящий гиперфокус", a real hyperfocus), because there is no time in appointments to explain the scale in detail. The original scale often causes confusion ("а что сюда писать?" — what do I write here?, "а это считается?" — does this count?), so she adapted it to be understandable and comfortable for patients first of all, for self-completion. She recommends filling it in on a computer [#772].
5. When to seek a specialist. Use the table and be sure to see a specialist if while filling it in you catch yourself thinking "Вау, у меня тут много плюсиков" (Wow, I have a lot of pluses here) or "Да, это на меня похоже" (Yes, this sounds like me). The separate file is meant so that people can suspect OCD in themselves and, if needed, seek help [#772].

### Detailed insights

#### Alternatives to exposure and how the author ranks them
A subscriber asked whether there are ways to overcome specific phobias or panic attacks other than behavioural experiments (facing them directly and living through the emotions). The author lists the options people use, stressing she means it "без издевки" (without mockery) — all of these are ways people ease their state [#614]:
1. Ignore it and wait for it to pass by itself ("забить и ждать когда само пройдет").
2. Search for the reason why it arises.
3. Relieve only the attacks themselves, for example with anti-anxiety medication.
4. Even taking ayahuasca.
Her verdict: for now the most effective option, and the one showing long-term results, remains exposure [#614].

#### Why OCD deserves more attention
The forwarded author shares the scale because she wants OCD discussed more often. She calls it a very widespread and complex disorder — "в тысячу раз сложнее, чем генерализованная тревога или паническое расстройство" (a thousand times more complex than generalised anxiety or panic disorder) — an opinion of that author [#772].

## Coping with terror attacks and collective tragedy

### What this covers

A cluster of posts from late March 2024, right after the terror attack at Crocus City Hall near Moscow (22 March 2024). It contains a forwarded first-aid post on what to do when the news is making you feel bad, the channel author's own reflections after giving a media comment, and a forwarded six-card interview with clinical psychologist Anastasia Afanasyeva for the outlet «Черта» on the psychological consequences of the attack for individuals and society [#130, #134, #135]. The source treats it as important because attacks raise anxiety in society "several times over", and how people and society react decides how fast the shared trauma heals — fighting with each other slows it down and, in the author's words, plays into the aim of the attack itself [#134, #135, #137].

(!) UNCLEAR: in #134 the channel author says she gave a media comment that day on society's reactions to tragedies like Crocus; the forwarded interview (#135–#140) is with Anastasia Afanasyeva. The source does not say outright that the author and Afanasyeva are the same person. The interview text is therefore attributed to Afanasyeva below, and the #134 reflections to "the author".

### Core ideas

- Terror attacks create threatening unpredictability, and that drives anxiety. Like large natural or man-made disasters, an attack shows that ordinary actions can get you or your loved ones killed. This raises anxiety and the sense of being unsafe across society (see *Chain of societal reactions*) [#135, #136].
- People react in different, opposite ways, and the main harm starts when the groups collide. Some get angry and radicalise, others shut themselves in at home. When these groups meet in the same space, the result is conflict and fragmentation, and the shared trauma heals more slowly [#135].
- Calls for the death penalty, bans and xenophobia are defensive reactions: an illusion of control. They try to remove unbearable uncertainty, but Afanasyeva says they do not actually reduce crime [#136].
- Dividing people is the point of terror. The author: attacks are carried out "to frighten, to anger and above all to disunite", so turning our anxiety and anger on each other plays into that aim [#134].
- The pace of healing depends on authorities, society and media. Witness trauma heals over months to decades. How fast it heals depends on how the authorities and society respond and how the event is presented. Loud spreaders of anxiety and demands for restrictions slow normalisation [#137, #138].
- Limit the information flow; doomscrolling and online fights don't help. Both the forwarded first-aid post and Afanasyeva say this, and the first-aid post gives concrete steps [#130, #139].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### First-aid steps when news of a tragedy overwhelms you
Source: a post the author forwarded from the channel Psycho_pharmacologist on the day of the attack. Purpose: what to do right now if reading the news is making you feel bad. Its starting point is that "думскролинг может скорее навредить, чем помочь" (doomscrolling may harm rather than help) [#130].
1. Stop reading. "Если вы сейчас читаете новости и вам от этого плохо - остановитесь 🛑" (If you are reading the news now and it makes you feel bad — stop). Cutting off the stream comes first [#130].
2. Use cold water. Wash your face with cold water. If it is very hard, fill a basin with water and dip your face into it ("ныряйте в него, пока не станет легче", *dive into it until it gets easier*). Repeat until you feel some relief [#130].
3. Switch to everyday tasks. Distract yourself with household chores. The post adds a key reframe: "это не избегание ситуации, а сохранение своего ментального здоровья" (this is not avoiding the situation but preserving your mental health). Stepping back from the news is presented as a legitimate act of self-care, not a failure to face reality [#130].
4. Connect and comfort yourself through your senses. Hug your loved ones, play with your dog or cat, put on music [#130].
5. Overall stance: support yourself as much as you can. "Все, что сейчас происходит, очень страшно и нам важно поддержать себя насколько это возможно" (Everything happening now is very frightening and it is important for us to support ourselves as much as possible) [#130].

#### Chain of societal reactions to a terror attack
Purpose: Afanasyeva's explanation of what happens to a community psychologically right after an attack, and why society splits [#135, #136, #138].
1. Threatening unpredictability. Terror attacks, like major natural or man-made catastrophes, create "ощущение угрожающей непредсказуемости" (a sense of threatening unpredictability). Unpredictability "всегда вызывает очень сильное чувство тревоги и небезопасности" (always causes a very strong feeling of anxiety and being unsafe). The forwarding text says anxiety in society rises "в несколько раз" (several times over) [#135].
2. Very high uncertainty in daily life. It turns out that "самые обычные бытовые действия могут привести к тому, что ты или твои близкие могут умереть" (the most ordinary everyday actions can lead to you or your loved ones dying). That is a very, very "высокая степень неопределенности" (high degree of uncertainty), and people want to get rid of it somehow [#136].
3. Divergent reactions, depending on how each person lives through the tragedy. Some get angry. Some start to generalise, look for enemies and radicalise; Afanasyeva notes that talk of bringing back the death penalty started right away. Others do the opposite: they don't go out and stay at home [#135].
4. Collision of the groups → conflict and fragmentation. These different "layers" of society start to collide in the same space, which produces the next reaction: conflicts and "разрозненность" (fragmentation, disunity). According to the forwarding text, this collision is "главная проблема" (the main problem), because the shared trauma then heals even more slowly [#135].
5. Amplifiers. Some people are always more prone to anxiety, and they start to spread it "очень громким и высоким голосом" (in a very loud, high-pitched voice), online or in public. Afanasyeva says research found that this is what most often sets other people off too. (!) INCOMPLETE: she does not name or describe the studies [#135].
6. Effect on the rest of society. Fear and anxiety reach some part of society anyway. The question is how much that part acts on the rest. If it actively spreads anxiety and demands bans or restrictions, this slows normalisation and deepens traumatisation, "Потому что на самом деле чем больше ограничений, тем больше тревоги" (because in reality the more restrictions, the more anxiety) [#138].

#### Timeline of witness trauma after a terror attack
Purpose: Afanasyeva's answer to how witness trauma settles in and lives on over the long term [#137, #138].
1. Time periods, loosely defined. As with any trauma there are phases, but they are "довольно размытые" (rather blurred): a month, then several months, a year, decades. The wound slowly scars over. How fast depends on how the authorities and society respond to the tragedy and how it is presented [#137].
2. First reaction: a heightened sense of being unsafe, and attempts to cope with it. Her example: after the series of Chechnya-related attacks "15 лет назад" (15 years ago), people who saw a woman with her head covered in the metro simply got up and left the carriage, because they were afraid. This phase can last from several months to a year [#137].
3. Delayed and prolonged reactions in anxious people. People who already feel very unsafe have a longer phase, and for them it can lead to mental disorders: depressive episodes, anxiety, avoidance [#137].
4. Long term for direct survivors: PTSD. People who went through the attack directly may develop PTSD (ПТСР) [#138].
5. Sensitisation even without PTSD. When they meet a similar situation later, their reaction will be sharper and stronger [#138].
6. Re-activation of past attacks. A new attack immediately pulls up similar memories and adds to the sense of being unsafe. Her personal example: when this attack happened, the first thing she and a friend remembered was past attacks. Neither of them had been caught in one, "но это было все равно очень близко" (but it was still very close) [#138]. The channel author describes the same effect in #134: after the interview she remembered every attack of recent years that she had read about or that happened in her lifetime [#134].

### Detailed insights

#### Radical proposals as an illusion of control
Asked whether talk of the death penalty and xenophobia is also a defensive reaction, Afanasyeva answers "Ну, конечно" (Well, of course). Because the situation is so frightening and unpredictable, people want to remove the uncertainty. When they propose radical actions — banning something, punishing someone, bringing in the death penalty — "это просто такая иллюзия того, что такие ситуации можно контролировать в дальнейшем и снизить их количество" (it is simply an illusion that such situations can be controlled in the future and their number reduced). She adds that "в реальности это не помогает снизить количество преступлений" (in reality this does not help reduce the number of crimes). It is only an attempt to protect oneself and to feel in control of what happens next [#136].

#### Why this attack (Crocus) was especially shocking
Afanasyeva says this attack differed from earlier ones: it was not an explosion but a cold-blooded shooting of unarmed people. Many people, especially the older generation who grew up with the attacks of the 1990s and 2000s, had a ready script: "если ты видишь людей в автоматах, скорее всего, тебя возьмут в заложники" (if you see people with assault rifles, you'll most likely be taken hostage). Most people tried to react according to that script, but the scenario was completely different and unpredictable. This made it a big surprise and raised stress in the moment even further. On top of that, defenceless people were being killed in front of you, and you could not understand what was happening [#140].
Her view on preparation: "Я, конечно, считаю, что к этому невозможно подготовиться" (I of course believe it is impossible to prepare for this). Even so, people carry "зашитая информация" (built-in, hard-wired information): what to do in a panic, during an attack, if taken hostage. Here, "все перебираешь, и все не подходит" (you go through everything, and none of it fits). So stored scripts can fail when the scenario is new [#140].

#### Media, news flow and online fights
- The media and the news shape reactions. Asked whether people get "разгоняется" (wound up) by media, especially by TV talk of the death penalty, Afanasyeva says "Конечно" (Of course). That is why "ограничение этого невероятного потока информации очень-очень важно" (limiting this incredible flow of information is very, very important) [#139].
- The amount of information is far greater than 15 years ago. At the time of the earlier attacks there was not so much information. Now TV, Telegram, YouTube, TikTok — "куда ни зайди, ты получишь огромную порцию информации" (wherever you go, you'll get a huge portion of information) [#139].
- Flame wars under unrelated posts. She noticed people starting "адские срачи" (hellish flame wars) under completely innocent posts that had nothing to do with the attack [#139]. The author noticed the same thing: in places the internet turns into a "портал ад" (hell portal), for example under ASMR videos [#134].
- Venting online does not really help. People on social media try to vent ("вентилировать") and somehow live through these emotions, "Но это не очень помогает" (but this doesn't help much). There are "более рабочие и экологичные навыки регуляции эмоций" (more effective and healthier emotion-regulation skills). (!) INCOMPLETE: the interview does not say which skills [#139].
- Fights are contagious. Flame wars strongly wind other people up too, because at some point you naturally want to join in and dump your own anger, anxiety and resentment [#139].

#### The author's reflections after giving a media comment
The channel author gave a media comment on society's reactions to tragedies like Crocus. She says the interview stirred up many emotions and memories, and lists four of them [#134]:
1. Sadness at how routine such interviews have become. In recent years interviews about what to do in emergencies and how to cope with emotions and anxiety have become too frequent; "Я бы предпочла отказаться от этой части своей жизни" (I would prefer to give up this part of my life) [#134].
2. Recall of past attacks. She naturally remembered all the attacks of recent years that she had read about or that happened in her memory. This is the re-activation effect described in the trauma timeline [#134].
3. Tragedies as a lever on public opinion. While explaining the theory of how such tragedies act on us as a community, she began to wonder how far such events can really be used to steer public opinion and lead society in a direction someone wants. She raises this as a question, not a conclusion [#134].
4. Displacing stress onto others. Because of how we react to this stress and try to cope with it, we often pour out anxiety, anger and resentment on other people: loved ones, friends, strangers on the internet. She feels torn: on one hand she wants to hug and comfort everyone, on the other "именно для этого же и совершаются теракты. Чтобы напугать, разозлить и главное разобщить" (this is exactly what terror attacks are carried out for. To frighten, to anger and above all to disunite) [#134].
Her closing point, which she explicitly says is not an "enlightened" conclusion: we are all people with our own reactions, but "ругаться и обвинять друг друга не самый помогающий способ справиться с таким стрессом" (quarrelling and blaming each other is not the most helpful way to cope with such stress) [#134].

## Behavioral activation, movement and mindfulness for mood

### What this covers

This topic gathers the author's posts on non-drug, everyday ways to support mood: behavioral activation and walks (particularly for people with ADHD and other mental-health conditions), a personal anti-slump plan for November, the three "conditions for neuroplasticity" she presented at a talk (sleep, movement, safety), mindfulness practised in very short, enjoyable formats suited to ADHD, and research on how much of our happiness is inherited [#41, #166, #239, #358, #787]. The author, a psychotherapist working in CBT and ACT, presents these as things she recommends to clients and also struggles to do herself; her recurring message is that small, regular activity helps even when motivation is absent, and that people's achievable happiness differs, so comparison is pointless [#166, #41].

### Core ideas

- Move even when you don't want to. Behavioral activation and physical activity, walks above all, are "extremely important" for people with ADHD and for people with mental-health conditions in general; the author's own bad-day walk in the rain lifted her mood. Fully explained under *Behavioral activation through walks* [#166].
- Any regular activity counts. Sport, even just stretching, a walk, or sitting on a bench by the house all improve quality of life when done regularly [#166].
- Seasonal slumps call for a concrete plan. For November, the author lists vitamin D testing and supplementation, a plan of pleasant activities and returning to sport [#358].
- Neuroplasticity needs sleep, movement and safety. Sleep integrates experience, movement drives neuron growth (BDNF), and low stress means high learning capacity [#787].
- Mindfulness for ADHD starts super short. It is not about long meditations or retreats; it is about finding an enjoyable activity that keeps you in the present moment, for the author, photography [#239].
- Happiness is partly heritable. Studies she cites estimate wellbeing and life satisfaction as 30–40% heritable, with a genetic basis for one's general happiness level, so different people can reach different levels of happiness and comparing yourself with others makes no sense [#41].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Three-step plan for the November slump
Purpose: a self-help plan the author wrote for herself against the seasonal low she experiences every November in Moscow, and which she recommends to readers with the same problem ("Чего кстати и вам советую, если у кого-то такие же проблемы" — "Which, by the way, I advise you too, if anyone has the same problems") [#358].

Context: she describes November as a month when all she can do is sleep and work ("и то, потому что куда от этого денешься" — "and only because there's no getting away from it"). Posts don't get written, her book doesn't get written, she doesn't watch series or even read books, and "Только депрессия подглядывает из-за двери" ("Only depression peeks out from behind the door"). She notes this happens even though she generally likes "conditionally bad weather"; she calls November in Moscow "какой-то портал в ад" ("some kind of portal to hell") [#358].

The steps, in her order:
1. Get vitamin D tested and start taking it again ("Сходить сдать витамин D и снова начать его пить"). The word "again" shows this is something she has done before. She first gets the blood test and then resumes supplementation; the post gives no dose [#358].
2. Draw up a plan of "pleasant" activities ("Составить план "приятной" активности"). This is the behavioral-activation tool: deliberately scheduling enjoyable activities instead of waiting for the mood to produce them. The post does not list example activities [#358].
3. Return to sport more actively ("Активнее вернуться к спорту"). This links to the author's broader point that regular movement improves mood (see *Behavioral activation through walks*) [#358].

Honest caveat from the author: "Осталось как это начать делать 😵‍💫" ("All that's left is to start actually doing it"). Making the plan and acting on it are separate, and starting is the hard part [#358].

#### Conditions for neuroplasticity (sleep, movement, safety)
Purpose: what the brain needs for neurons to form new connections and for new habits to form, and how to take this into account in psychotherapy. The author presented it at a talk at Mosfilm (the slide bears the event's wellness slam logo) [#787, img #787].

In the post she sums it up as: she had to explain "как важны сон, еда и движение, чтобы нейроны создавали новые связи и у нас формировались новые привычки" ("how important sleep, food and movement are for neurons to create new connections and for us to form new habits") [#787]. The slide photographed in the post shows these numbered items:

4. Sleep: integration of experience ("Сон - интеграция опыта"). Sleep is where what was experienced and learned gets integrated [#787].
5. Movement: growth of neurons (BDNF) ("Движение - рост нейронов", followed on the slide by "(BDNF)"). Physical movement is linked to neuron growth through BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) [#787].
6. Safety: low stress = high learnability ("Безопасность - низкий стресс = высокая обучаемость"). Feeling safe keeps stress low, and low stress is the state in which the brain learns best [#787].

The post text also names food as a condition, which does not appear among items 4–6 and may be one of the missing items. The talk itself is not transcribed [#787, img #787].

The author's personal aside shows the gap between knowledge and practice: "Где этот нормальный сон и еда то 😁" ("Where is this normal sleep and food, anyway"). She also says that the day after the talk she had "нулевая пластичность, только мигрень и восстановление от рабочей недели и конференции" ("zero plasticity, only a migraine and recovering from the work week and the conference") [#787].

#### Short, activity-anchored mindfulness for ADHD
Purpose: building mindfulness skills in neurodivergent people, for whom long formal meditation is a poor entry point [#239].

1. Understand why mindfulness comes first. The author defines mindfulness as "группа навыков" ("a group of skills") that neurodivergent people especially need. Neurotypical people also benefit, but when "ты плохо чувствуешь свое тело/эмоции и вообще понимаешь себя, а ещё и куча разных коморбидностей" ("you poorly sense your body/emotions and understand yourself in general, plus a heap of different comorbidities"), mindfulness skills are often one of the first things therapy starts with [#239].
2. Drop the myth of long meditation. A common misconception is that mindfulness means meditation, ideally long sessions of an hour or two, or even going away on a retreat. Her answer: "Спойлер - нет" ("Spoiler: no"). People with ADHD in particular should start with something "супер короткого" ("super short") [#239].
3. Expect it to be hard, and train it constantly. The author says these skills never come easily to her either, and they need to be practised all the time [#239].
4. Anchor presence in an activity you enjoy. "Важно найти что-то, что вам нравится как занятие, и что будет помогать вам задерживаться в текущем моменте" ("It's important to find something you like doing that will help you linger in the present moment") [#239].
   - Her example is photography, which she found in early adolescence when she got her first film camera. Photography lets her return to the "here and now" and stretch out the moment. While you search for a beautiful frame and think about it, "мир преображается, пока ты смотришь на него через призму красоты" ("the world is transformed while you look at it through the prism of beauty") [#239].
   - She mentions that she "hyperfixated" on photography so strongly that she worked as a photographer for several years. She presents this as an ADHD-style intense interest and does not tell the story here [#239].

### Detailed insights

#### Behavioral activation through walks (ADHD and mental-health conditions)
The author's case: a month before the post, on the day after the Crocus attack, she was in an awful mood and the weather was dreadful, with everything melting unattractively and drizzle falling. Even so, she put her dog Shirik in his vest and took him to the park for a walk with a friend, and her mood "очень даже улучшилось" ("improved quite a lot"). That was in the rain, in "ugly" weather (she adds, "хотя как погода может быть некрасивой?" — "though how can weather be ugly?"), and in a bad state. She found this note when finishing an old entry in her journal [#166].

The lesson she draws: behavioral activation, and specifically physical activity in the form of walks, is "крайне важна" ("extremely important") for people with ADHD and for people with mental-health conditions in general. Done regularly, any sport (even just stretching), a walk, or simply sitting on a bench near the house improves quality of life. Her conclusion: "даже, если не хочется, надо двигаться" ("even if you don't feel like it, you need to move") [#166].

Dogs as a built-in activation device. The author says dogs help her a lot. Rain, hail or snow, you go out of the house for at least 10 minutes whether you want to or not. That gets you fresh air, you notice something beautiful in nature, and your blood gets moving. The obligation to walk the dog removes the need for motivation [#166].

Journaling, honestly. She keeps a journal for mindfulness and reflection skills and usually recommends this to others, yet admits that however hard she tries, she cannot fill it in regularly. In this case she found an entry started a month earlier and finished it. The post shows that even an irregular journal can surface useful evidence, here that a walk improved her mood [#166].

#### Heritability of happiness and comparison
The author first came across the idea that our happiness level may be "programmed" a few years earlier, while studying CBT and ACT intensively. She was very upset and needed "некоторое радикальное принятие" ("some radical acceptance") of the fact that she will never experience as much joy as some lucky people (joking that she will remain "унылой какашкой", a "glum little poop"). She now looks at this fact with acceptance, as she does at the general unfairness of the world. She notes that happiness and positive psychology are one of the large-scale projects of modern psychotherapy [#41].

Research she cites:
- Wellbeing and life satisfaction, which are closely linked to happiness, are 30–40% heritable (Røysamb et al., 2018). The rest depends on our lifestyle and our environment. She quotes the same paper: "«генетические факторы, по-видимому, играют важную роль в большинстве характеристик человека»" ("genetic factors apparently play an important role in most human characteristics"), p. 2 [#41].
- Not all genetic studies use twins. Some compare the genome, meaning all the genetic information a person needs to grow and develop, across unrelated people and groups (Larsen et al., 2017) [#41].
- A 2022 study comparing individual genomes found a genetic basis for the "общего уровня счастья" ("general level of happiness"), as opposed to momentary changes caused by temporary circumstances. This basis also affects brain structures that stay unchanged throughout life (Ward et al., 2022) [#41].

Her conclusion: different people can reach different levels of happiness, so "ни в коем случае нельзя сравнивать свое "счастье" с другими" ("under no circumstances should you compare your 'happiness' with others'"). Everyone's is different, and she adds that for some it is glum [#41].
