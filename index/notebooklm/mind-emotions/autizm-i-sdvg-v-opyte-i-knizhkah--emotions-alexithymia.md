## Alexithymia and interoception in autism

### What this covers

This topic covers the channel's posts on how autistic people relate to their own emotions and body signals: what alexithymia is and why it is a separate trait from autism, the alexithymia hypothesis of autism, the research showing that interoception differences are largely explained by alexithymia (with negative appraisal of body signals as the one difference that remains), and a practical timed spoken exercise for clarifying what you are feeling and why. It also covers the channel author's behaviorist (Skinnerian) explanation of why autism so often comes with alexithymia: emotion words are learned from other people in context, so difficulty decoding communication makes emotions hard to recognize in oneself. According to the source, the distinction matters for practical reasons: it stops others from prejudging autistic people, reframes the "no empathy" stereotype, points to which tools actually help, and supports self-understanding [#261, #281, #39, #176].

### Core ideas

- Alexithymia is not autism. It is an independent trait — difficulty recognizing, describing and distinguishing emotions from body sensations — that co-occurs with autism in roughly half of autistic people; the other half do not have it at all (see *What alexithymia is*) [#261].
- Many "emotional deficits of autism" are really alexithymia. The alexithymia hypothesis (Bird and Cook, 2013) holds that emotion-recognition difficulties, reduced empathy and flatter emotional response are better explained by co-occurring alexithymia than by autism itself [#261].
- "Autistic people don't feel their bodies" is largely a myth. A 2025 systematic review of 31 studies found no systematic interoception difference between autistic and non-autistic people; where differences appear they look like alexithymia effects [#261].
- The one difference that survives is negative appraisal. A 2026 Oxford study found that after controlling for alexithymia, anxiety and co-occurring conditions, autistic people differed from controls only in negatively appraising body signals — and this may be a learned response [#281].
- A behaviorist account: emotion words are learned socially. In her longread the author argues, following Skinner, that no body sensation uniquely marks an emotion, that saying an emotion word is a behavior learned from the verbal community, and therefore that communication difficulties in autism make it hard to attach emotion words to one's own states — while hedging that brain specifics most likely play a role too (see *A behaviorist explanation of alexithymia in autism*) [#176].
- Naming feelings and their causes helps. Naming emotions works as emotion regulation, knowing the cause allows acting on it, and understanding brings a sense of control; a three-minute spoken exercise helps get there [#39].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Three-minute spoken timer exercise
Purpose / when to use. Lena Foer presents this as a way to understand what is happening to you "за пару минут" (in a couple of minutes) when you do not understand your state. She notes that people with ASD (РАС) can find it hard to work out their emotions and what caused them. It is her favorite method for raising emotional awareness; she stresses it does not come from psychology textbooks but, "внезапно, из сабреддита аутистов" (surprisingly, from a subreddit of autistic people), where she picked it up a year before writing, and has used it since — "работает волшебно" (it works like magic) [#39].

Why understanding emotions and causes matters (the author's three reasons):
1. When you name feelings, this already works as emotional regulation and reduces the intensity of suffering [#39].
2. When you understand the cause, you can try to solve it and thereby change your state [#39].
3. When it is clear what is happening, there is a greater sense of control and a greater possibility to influence the situation [#39].

What you need: (1) you, not understanding what is going on with you; (2) a phone with a timer open [#39].

Steps:
1. First minute — say it out loud. Set a timer for one minute and, during that minute, speak out loud, with your voice ("вслух, голосом(!)" — out loud, with your voice(!)), about whatever you think might be bothering you right now. When the minute ends — stop. While speaking you are also listening to how it all sounds: which words get some kind of response inside, and which produce a feeling of "не, точно не то" (no, definitely not that). The point of speaking aloud is that you hear yourself and can notice resonance or its absence [#39].
2. Second minute — refined version. Set the timer for one minute again and repeat the whole thing, but now say a refined version, building on what resonated in round one [#39].
3. Third minute — corrected version. Do the same a third time. By the end of the third minute you will most likely have a refined and corrected version of what is happening to you and why you feel the way you do [#39].

Worked example (from the post):
- *Minute 1:* somewhat tired, tension, probably lack of sleep and hence procrastination, keep going to Facebook, worked little, won't get anything done, worried that I can't pull myself together and stop [#39].
- *Minute 2:* Facebook takes a lot of my time and attention, can't tear myself away, there are many posts about the war today, I read them and worry about my acquaintances, how they are [#39].
- *Minute 3:* "меня очень пугает война, я переживаю за друзей, поэтому постоянно чувствую напряжение и тревогу и лезу проверить, все ли с ними в порядке" (the war frightens me a lot, I worry about my friends, so I constantly feel tension and anxiety and keep checking whether they are okay) — and whether anything else has happened in the meantime [#39].

Lesson of the example: the first round names surface symptoms and a plausible but wrong explanation (sleep deprivation, procrastination, self-blame); by the third round the real driver — fear about the war and worry for friends — emerges, and the Facebook checking turns out to be an anxiety-driven attempt to check on them [#39].

Expected result and limit: in just a couple of iterations you can refine the model of your state — "если не до полного понимания, то хотя бы до появления идей “куда смотреть”" (if not to full understanding, then at least to having ideas about "where to look") [#39].

#### Alexithymia hypothesis (Bird and Cook, 2013)
In 2013 Geoffrey Bird and Richard Cook proposed the so-called alexithymia hypothesis. Its idea: a large share of what was traditionally attributed to the "emotional deficits of autism" — (1) difficulty recognizing emotions, (2) reduced empathy, (3) a flatter emotional response — is much better explained by co-occurring alexithymia. Roughly speaking, the issue is not autism itself but whether the person additionally has alexithymia [#261].

#### Three dimensions of interoception (the radio metaphor)
Interoception is the processing of signals from inside the body: hunger, thirst, heartbeat, the urge to use the toilet, pain, breathing. Researchers now divide it into three dimensions, explained via a radio receiver [#281]:
1. Accuracy (точность) — how cleanly the radio catches the station, i.e., how precisely body signals are perceived [#281].
2. Attention (внимание) — how often the radio is switched on at all and how loudly it plays, i.e., how much and how often you attend to body signals [#281].
3. Appraisal (оценка) — what you think about what you heard, i.e., how you interpret the signals. Appraisal began to be studied last of the three, and still the least is known about it [#281].

#### Why distinguishing autism from alexithymia matters (four reasons)
The channel lists four practical reasons [#261]:
1. Don't decide for the person in advance. Knowing the real picture is a reminder not to presume from the outset that an autistic conversation partner does not understand their emotions, because half of autistic people have no difficulty with this [#261].
2. It changes the empathy story. The famous "аутисты не способны на эмпатию" (autistic people are incapable of empathy) largely turns out to be the same alexithymia effect — an emotion (both one's own and another's) is hard to identify — and not that the person does not care [#261].
3. It determines what helps. If the root is alexithymia, quite specific things work: learning to notice and name your states, and gradually linking bodily sensations with emotions [#261].
4. It aids self-understanding. When you fail to catch "все, я на взводе" (that's it, I'm wound up) in time, tension accumulates unnoticed; knowing about your own alexithymia helps catch these signals a little earlier [#261].

Conclusion of the post: autism and alexithymia are definitely not the same, and distinguishing them is useful for a practical reason — it makes it much easier to understand yourself and not to write off as "ну такой уж у меня аутизм" (well, that's just my autism) something that actually has its own name and its own working tools [#261].

#### A behaviorist explanation of alexithymia in autism ("this endless garland")
Origin and purpose. In post #176 the channel author (Lena Foer) writes that she "только что поняла что-то важное про природу алекситимии" (just realized something important about the nature of alexithymia) — i.e., difficulties understanding one's own emotions — in autism, and that she realized it while reading a book about behaviorism; she finds it "ужасно интересно" (terribly interesting). The full argument is laid out in her Telegraph longread «Аутизм, алекситимия и радикальный бихевиоризм: эта бесконечная гирлянда» (Autism, alexithymia and radical behaviorism: this endless garland). The longread builds a chain of seven numbered steps ending in an explanation of why a neurotype with communication difficulties also tends to come with alexithymia [#176]. The title of the behaviorism book is not named [#176].

Step 1 — No body sensation uniquely identifies an emotion. The author cites a huge study — an analysis of four meta-analyses with tens of thousands of participants — which, she says, convincingly shows that there is not a single bodily sensation that would unambiguously and consistently indicate which emotion a person is feeling. Why it matters for the argument: if emotions cannot be read off the body in a fixed way, then "recognizing an emotion in oneself" cannot simply be a matter of detecting a specific physical signal [#176].

Step 2 — Psychologists still disagree on what an emotion even is. Psychologists are still arguing about what we call an emotion at all and how we understand what distinguishes each emotion from the others. Her popular example of this unresolved question: what is the difference between guilt and shame («чем вина и стыд отличаются?») [#176].

Step 3 — Defining emotion like a physical object is doomed. Attempts to define "what an emotion is" the same way we define iron, an atom or light are, in the author's words, "обречены на провал" (doomed to fail), because access to emotion exists only through the subjective experience of individual people, and that experience differs between people (as steps 1–2 show) [#176].

Step 4 — Skinner's alternative: speech is behavior. It was precisely out of this experience of failure that the American psychologist Skinner's idea of going a completely different way arose. The core of Skinner's idea as she presents it: our speech is behavior. When we say the word "emotion" (or any other word — dog, mom, apple, sunset), we perform a behavior. Behavior is led to by certain stimuli, and it has consequences.
- *Worked example (pizza and langos).* If she is in Russia and wants pizza, she will say in a café "маргариту, пожалуйста" (a margherita, please) and with some probability get what she wants. The Hungarian analogue of pizza is called lángos; coming into a local café in Budapest with this knowledge, she will most likely ask for lángos, because she wants pizza and knows that in a local café it will be the closest analogue. So her behavior "ask for lángos" is produced by a combination of factors: (a) her feeling of hunger — which is itself a combination of different stimuli, such as blood-sugar level and information from the stomach walls; (b) her knowledge that she is in Budapest; (c) her visual observation "this café looks like a place with Hungarian cuisine".
- *Consequences.* If she gets the lángos and is satisfied, her behavior is reinforced and the probability of it repeating rises.
- *Application to emotions.* Coming back from lángos to emotions, Skinner proposed to drop the reasoning about "what an emotion really is" and to focus instead on which stimuli and contexts lead a person to say the word "emotion" — or to suddenly decide that they are sad [#176].

Step 5 — The verbal community teaches us names (manzana, cringe, the blind men and the elephant).
- *Easy case — the apple.* You are on a trip to Spain, go into a shop and want apples. You point at an apple, the seller says "manzana!", and you understand that his behavior is somehow connected to your pointing. After a couple of repetitions you will most likely remember the Spanish word and start saying "manzana" to sellers yourself when you want apples. The lesson: this is about the role of the verbal community in our ability to name objects and phenomena — we orient to how others name the same thing and start building connections in our head [#176].
- *Hard case — feelings, e.g. "кринж" (cringe).* Sometimes it is simple, as with apples; sometimes much harder, as with feelings. When we, together with other people, try to define what cringe is, it looks like blind people feeling an elephant to understand what an elephant looks like — in the sense that everyone's cringe is a little bit their own and different. So each particular person's understanding of cringe is "скорее облако тегов, чем четкие характеристики, общие для всех" (more a tag cloud than clear characteristics common to everyone). These characteristics are absorbed from other people's speech, meme culture and Reddit posts [#176].
- *How the word gets said.* When you partly hit this tag cloud — you feel uncomfortable at a colleague's behavior, others also look at him askance, you want both to turn away and to record him on video — you say "это кринж" (that's cringe). Thus you perform a behavior as a result of the influence on you of linguistic culture, the context of the colleague's behavior and other variables — and, she adds, there are always some other variables [#176].

Step 6 — Understanding your own emotions is a function of understanding communication. It follows, the author argues, that our understanding of our own emotions is, by and large, a function of how we understand communication and decode signals from other people. If we cannot decode something, we do not understand it.
- *Analogy (35 kinds of snow).* We then feel like a person from the temperate middle zone («из средней полосы») who has landed in a community of people who distinguish 35 varieties of snow: they have named something, and we just see — well, white, snow, what else is there to say. The same happens with emotions.
- *Mechanism.* If, because of communication difficulties, we do not understand why and in which contexts another person says "мне грустно" (I'm sad), we will not be able to match "sad" with what is happening inside us and learn to "recognize sadness" — she notes it is hard, after all of the above, not to put those last words in quotation marks [#176].

Step 7 — Conclusion: why autism comes with alexithymia. This is how a neurotype associated with communication problems turns out to also be associated with alexithymia. To feel emotions in yourself, you need to understand well the contexts in which others talk about what they feel. If perceiving contexts and nuances in communication is difficult, and if understanding metaphors and abstractions is hard, then it is hard to catch the meaning of emotions, applying it to oneself works poorly, and finding something analogous in oneself is difficult. "Итого, алекситимия при аутизме" (Hence, alexithymia in autism) [#176].

The author's own hedge. She adds that possibly — "и скорее всего" (and most likely) — alexithymia is driven by more than this, and that there are specifics of brain organisation that also play a role. "Но, как и Скиннеру, мне сложно не связать с поведением то, что можно связать с поведением" (But, like Skinner, I find it hard not to link to behavior what can be linked to behavior) [#176].

### Detailed insights

#### What alexithymia is
The common story is that autistic people seem cut off from their own emotions: they poorly understand what they feel and struggle to name it. This sounds logical and the literature described it roughly this way for decades, but on closer look most of this story is not about autism at all but about a separate trait called alexithymia [#261].

Alexithymia is difficulty recognizing and describing one's emotions and distinguishing them from bodily sensations. Crucially, it does not mean feelings are absent: feelings exist, but they can be hard to catch and name, so instead of a clear "я злюсь" (I'm angry) one has a vague "мне как-то не по себе" (I feel somehow uneasy) circling inside. It is an independent trait, not another name for autism, though they often co-occur: by various estimates about half of autistic people have alexithymia (versus roughly 10% in the general population). The author repeats the point deliberately because it matters: the other half of autistic people have no alexithymia at all [#261].

#### Interoception: the "insensitivity to the body" myth
Interoception — the ability to sense body signals, from heartbeat to hunger and tension — has traditionally also been attributed to autism. But a 2025 systematic review covering 31 studies indicates no systematic difference in interoception between autistic and non-autistic people. Where a difference does occasionally show up, it looks more like an effect of alexithymia, and it occurs more in children than in adults. So the familiar "аутисты не чувствуют свое тело" (autistic people don't feel their body) turns out to be largely a myth; the truth is a more boring formulation: some people have alexithymia, and then interoception can indeed be harder [#261].

#### The 2026 Oxford study on interoception and autism
The channel introduces this study as work that at least explains why interoception in autistic people has long been debated [#281].

Design. A team from Oxford published it in the journal *Autism* in 2026. 519 participants, of whom 232 had a confirmed autism diagnosis and 143 were in the control group (people with high autistic traits were deliberately not included in the control group). Each of the three interoception dimensions was measured with a separate scale; in addition, alexithymia (TAS-20), anxiety, autistic traits and cognitive measures were collected so they could be entered into the analysis as adjustments [#281].

Findings:
1. Looking at autistic traits across the whole sample: the higher the traits, the higher the negative appraisal of signals, the lower the accuracy of perceiving them, and the higher the attention to them [#281].
2. Comparing the diagnosed group with the non-diagnosed group, adjusted for alexithymia, anxiety and co-occurring conditions, only one of the three dimensions held up — negative appraisal: 70.8 points vs 60.1 [#281].
3. For accuracy and attention, the difference between the groups disappeared [#281].
4. To make the accuracy and attention differences non-significant, it was enough to add alexithymia alone to the analysis [#281].

Interpretation (channel's). Linking back to its earlier post on alexithymia, the channel concludes that a substantial part of what was for years attributed to "autistic features of body perception" actually rests on alexithymia, which is common in autistic people but exists separately. Negative appraisal, by contrast, survives this adjustment and stays linked specifically to the diagnosis [#281].

#### What negative appraisal looks like in practice
A signal arrives — the heart starts pounding, the stomach twists, the ears ring — and it is immediately read as alarming and foreboding. Instead of "кажется, я проголодалась" (I think I'm hungry), what sounds inside is "что-то не так" (something is wrong). The body is perceived as a source of bad news by default [#281].

#### Hyper- and hypo-sensitivity cancelling out
The authors expected autistic participants to show many very high and very low scores (by analogy with sensory processing, where hyper- and hypo-sensitivity coexist). The distributions did not show this, but the explanation may be simple: hyper and hypo live in the same person in different domains. Heartbeat during anxiety is heard deafeningly, while the urge to go to the toilet does not get through at all — and in the total score they cancel each other out. The channel calls this an elegant observation by the authors [#281].

#### Negative appraisal as a learned response
The channel offers one more reading of the results: negative appraisal may well be learned. If for years the body brought overload, pain, nausea and unpleasant surprises, and doctors brushed off complaints, then reading a body signal as a threat is a reasonably learned reaction. In that case it is more useful to work with one's attitude toward the signals [#281].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- Limitations of the Oxford study (as listed by the channel): the effect is small — group membership explains about 2% of the variance; all measures are self-report in an online survey; 70% of participants were women; data were collected at a single time point, so the direction of the relationship is unknown; the negative-appraisal scale is brand new and has not yet been validated on autistic samples; and the authors themselves warn about variation within groups — a group average does not describe a specific person [#281].
- The alexithymia prevalence numbers are hedged as "по разным оценкам" (by various estimates): about half of autistic people versus around 10% of the general population [#261].
- The 2025 review notes that where interoception differences do appear, they occur more in children than in adults [#261].
- The "learned negative appraisal" reading is presented by the channel as one possible way to read the results, not as a finding [#281].
- The timer exercise is the author's personal favorite from a subreddit, not a textbook method; she says it may lead only to ideas about "where to look" rather than full understanding [#39].
- The behaviorist explanation is the author's own reasoning, not a research finding; she hedges that alexithymia is possibly "и скорее всего" (and most likely) driven by more than this, including brain specifics [#176].
- The "analysis of four meta-analyses" in the longread is cited without title, authors or year [#176].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the longread's behaviorist account links alexithymia to communication difficulties, while post #261 stresses that about half of autistic people have no alexithymia at all; the source does not reconcile why communication difficulties would leave that half unaffected [#176, #261].
