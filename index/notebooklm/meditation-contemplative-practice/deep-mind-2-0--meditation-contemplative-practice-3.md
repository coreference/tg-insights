## Metta, compassion and the four immeasurables

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel author's teaching on "heart practices" (сердечные практики): why Metta (loving-kindness) and self-compassion matter, the benefits and timelines the author cites, how to adapt classical Metta wording and structure for Westerners (based on Kristin Neff's MSC approach), the four immeasurables as a systemic alternative to gratitude practices, and first-person accounts of using compassion with acute and chronic pain, hatred and political polarization. According to the author, Metta is "the most important part of our development in meditation and life", but because of its strange form it is the practice that most often fails to land in the West, so he explains its deep benefit and how to fix the technique to overcome the cultural barrier [#358, #366].

Part 2 adds two posts. The first (#1891) is a personal essay by the channel author on how, in restless and frightening times, his formal practice collapses — and how loving-kindness nonetheless resurfaces spontaneously, which he reads as evidence that practice is a reminder of something innate rather than an invented framework. The second (#1978) presents a recorded Loving Kindness practice led by maechee Brigitte Schrottenbacher and gives a compact description of the classical Metta structure, its real target (intention, not pleasant feeling) and its role alongside vipassana [#1891, #1978].

Relation to the existing knowledge file: #1978 adds the classical five-recipient order (self → close ones → neutral → difficult → all beings), which the existing file did not spell out; the existing *Adapting Metta — part 2* offers a modified order (beloved being → "you and I" → self) as an alternative to it. #1978 reinforces the existing point from #366 that one seeks the inner echo of good intention rather than pleasant feelings, and extends the existing *Two reasons mindfulness needs compassion* (#359) with a new formulation: clear seeing without kindness becomes dry and harsh, including toward oneself. #1891 extends the existing first-person compassion accounts (#1580, #1805) with a new situation — not acute pain but the loss of practice itself — and adds the self-before-others principle and the minimal-dose range of 15–20 down to 1 minute [#1891, #1978].

### Core ideas

- Metta develops unconditional love for oneself and others and changes people fast. The author claims, citing research, that Metta practitioners notice visible changes in well-being and behaviour faster than in any other mindfulness practice; 2 weeks of 30 minutes daily is presented as enough to feel results [#358, #556].
- Unconditional love is a core psychological need that most people lack, and self-criticism is a harmful motivation habit — these are the author's two reasons why Metta is "for all of us" (see *Two reasons to practice Metta*) [#358].
- Mindfulness without compassion is unnatural and shallow. The author is "extremely sceptical" of secular mindfulness schools that neglect heart practices (see *Two reasons mindfulness needs compassion*) [#359].
- The classical form can be adapted. Phrases can be personal and plain, and the order of recipients can start with a loved being instead of oneself (see *Adapting Metta*) [#366].
- Gratitude helps but does not transform the structure of mind; the four immeasurables do, and they should be developed together because they balance each other [#1647].
- Hatred harms the hater first and is a virus to build immunity against; compassion and openness reduce one's own suffering [#1185, #1807].
- Compassion is a way into pain, not away from it: widening the circle to all who suffer, or wishing happiness to the pain itself, creates a small gap and reduces resistance even when the pain stays [#1580, #1805].

- Metta trains intention, not feeling. The point is the attitude toward oneself and toward other people; warmth follows as a consequence, and not always immediately [#1978].
- Metta balances vipassana. Clear seeing without kindness easily turns dry and harsh, including toward oneself [#1978].
- A living example transmits more than an instruction. The same words land differently from someone for whom kindness has long been a way of being with people; your own mind starts to attune to that state [#1978].
- Practice is a reminder, not an invented concept. According to the author, kindness is the innate nature of a living being; when formal practice lies "in ruins", the wish for beings' happiness still breaks through by itself [#1891].
- Kindness to the world cannot be fully realized while skipping kindness to oneself [#1891].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### What Metta is and the minimal entry dose
The author introduces Metta as "классическую практику развития развития [sic] сострадания и безусловной любви" (the classical practice of developing compassion and unconditional love). To do it, it is enough to sit down with headphones for 15 minutes and follow an audio guide (the channel's own guide, "Self care and empathy mindfulness practice", was offered as a Christmas gift) [#93].

#### Two reasons to practice Metta
Purpose: to convince sceptical readers ("если сразу подумаете что розовые сопли про безусловную любовь не для вас" — *if you immediately think that pink snot about unconditional love is not for you*) that heart practice is for everyone [#358].

1. The feeling of unconditional love is a key psychological need of a person. Most of us did not get enough of it, and Metta can help. Unconditional love is called a necessary foundation for health and self-realization: we want to be loved as we are, and until this need is satisfied, all of our interaction with the world is only an indirect search for that love in different forms — in relationships, in achievements at work, in grievances against relatives and colleagues, in passionate pursuit of hedonic pleasures. Without deep unconditional acceptance, sympathy and wishing happiness to ourselves, it is hard to live from fullness and basic sufficiency — "Поэтому так часто мы видим таких бедных богатых" (That is why we so often see such poor rich people). Parents should have laid this foundation in childhood but, for various reasons, did not do so for most of us; so today it is our own task, through psychotherapy and Metta practice, to fill these gaps in health [#358].
2. Metta helps change a habitual and truly harmful way of motivating ourselves: self-criticism. We may believe that being kind and receptive toward ourselves will make us lazy and stop us reaching goals. The author calls this a thinking error most of us acquired in childhood, because parents believed a child must be frightened or he/she will not study diligently and will "end up under a fence". Receiving such motivation, we gradually grow our own inner critic. The inner critic is described as an evolutionary part of the threat-protection system passed from generation to generation — useful in the past, ineffective today. On some level it warns about danger and tries to force us to change for survival; at the same time it creates inner conflict and stress and does physiological harm. We end up in a constant "цикле насилия" (cycle of violence) that exhausts us and makes us act from deficit again rather than from the fullness of life. The author states that many studies have proven that self-compassionate, self-accepting people are more self-confident, fear failure less, are more likely to try again after a failure and persist toward goals, are more productive and more satisfied with life, less prone to depression, and able to build strong relationships. This should motivate us to replace the archaic system of harsh self-flagellation with a healthier and more effective system of self-compassion — which can be done through regular Metta practice [#358].

#### Two reasons mindfulness needs compassion
The author states he cannot imagine developing mindfulness without substantial attention to heart practices — developing acceptance, unconditional love, friendliness and compassion. Secular schools that ignore them represent a "продуктовый или макмайндфулнесс подход" (product or McMindfulness approach) "который выкидывает то, что сложно объяснять достигаторам предпринимателям" (which throws out what is hard to explain to achiever entrepreneurs) [#359].

1. It is highly unnatural to develop mindfulness without developing compassion. They are different practices and cultivate different qualities, but on close inspection the boundary is quite conditional. Mindfulness is part of compassion toward oneself and others: to relate to your own discomfort with acceptance and friendliness, you first need to notice it. Conversely, without directing attention to the suffering of others, many practitioners of "dry" approaches quickly become attached to the pleasant sensations of deep concentration and "collapse" in their progress; without Metta they develop detachment from the world rather than the full and truly needed engagement with it [#359].
2. Huge numbers of people can only go even a little deeper in mindfulness if they learn to treat themselves with compassion. Because of self-criticism (see reason 2 above) and the prevalence of psychological trauma — the author cites 7 out of 10 people in Russia and the USA — practice can be too painful and unfamiliar. Compassion and sympathy toward oneself are therefore a natural and necessary support for meeting one's own baggage of traumatic experience and pain. Hence, for many people the self-development track is best started precisely with compassion or self-directed Metta. The author notes this contradicts some mindfulness teachers but is his current position, shared also by Trish Magyari, a mindfulness teacher at the Johns Hopkins public health department [#359].

Disclaimer from the author: heart practices require special caution; it is better to do them with the professional support of a trauma-informed mindfulness teacher or a psychotherapist with a mindfulness lens [#359].

#### Adapting Metta — part 1: the phrases (MSC-based tips)
Purpose: if the classical form feels strange and archaic and does not work for you, change it. Based on Kristin Neff's MSC approach, the author gives two main tips for modern practitioners; the first concerns the words [#366].
1. Choose your own words. They do not have to be exactly what the audio guide or teacher gives. Pick phrases that work for you, conveying kindness, sympathy and love; they should be simple, understandable and genuine ("простыми, понятными и настоящими") [#366].
2. Understand what the words do. By saying them we consciously cultivate a sense of kindness and sympathy, not appeal to any external forces; the words are only pointers to qualities that are already inside us [#366].
3. Seek an inner response of good intention, not pleasant feelings. Through the words we look for an inner echo of our good intentions toward others rather than trying to feel pleasant sensations — although pleasant sensations are an inevitable by-product of such intentions [#366].
4. Replace the odd wish form if needed. "Пускай я буду" (may I be) can seem very strange; it is just a form of wishing and can be replaced with "хорошо бы" (it would be good if) [#366].
5. Formulate phrases in general terms, for example "пускай я буду здоров" (may I be healthy) [#366].
6. Say them slowly and tenderly, trying to put maximum sincerity into them and to feel their meaning [#366].

#### Adapting Metta — part 2: the order of recipients
Why: for Westerners, because of their upbringing, wishing oneself well can be hard. So the author proposes changing the structure of the practice and trying this sequence [#366]:
1. Recall a being that evokes especially pleasant feelings of unity, care and love — a person, an animal, any being. Notice what pleasant feelings arise from holding this image in mind [#366].
2. Reflect on how this being wishes to be happy and well, in its own understanding of that happiness [#366].
3. Wish them happiness through phrases: "Пускай ты..." (May you...) [#366].
4. Move to wishes that also include you: "Пускай ты и я..." (May you and I...) [#366].
5. Thank that being, then shift attention fully to yourself and do Metta for yourself: "Пускай я..." (May I...) [#366].

#### The four immeasurables
Context: gratitude practices have spread widely in neo-New-Age and pop psychology. The author calls them pleasant, nice and effective for shifting attention to the constructive and nourishing instead of the evolutionarily familiar but often painful focus on difficulties. But: (a) they are extremely easily commercialized — convenient to insert into marathons and sales warm-ups, and writing down 3 things you are grateful for is easier than developing "some strange equanimity"; (b) they sometimes become the basis of spiritual bypassing, when instead of facing pain and psychological tasks we sweep them under the rug of gratitude; (c) they do not transform the structure of the mind, so they cannot substantially and systemically reduce suffering and increase well-being. Respected traditions, available today also in secular forms, develop the mind systemically and go far beyond gratitude — for example, the four immeasurables, qualities of mind that bring it into a constructive, stable and balanced state [#1647].

1. Loving-kindness (любящая доброта) — a good-hearted attitude toward others striving toward unconditionality; the intention that others be happy and that all be well with them [#1647].
2. Compassion (сострадание) — has nothing to do with pity and does not imply that we must suffer together with the one we have compassion for; it is the intention wishing others freedom from suffering [#1647].
3. Sympathetic joy (сорадование) — the ability to respond to the good that happens to others, to rejoice in their achievements, luck and worthy deeds; instead of envy or unconstructive emotions, we join in others' well-being [#1647].
4. Equanimity (равностность) — the ability to keep clarity and calm toward things that provoke attachment and desire or, conversely, aversion and rejection. It has nothing to do with indifference or irresponsibility: "это мудрая включённость в жизнь, без липкости" (it is wise engagement in life, without stickiness) [#1647].

How to work with them: each quality has its own practices, both in the traditions and in the modern secular contemplative approach. Done with understanding of their essence and the necessary discipline, they can seriously transform the mind, reducing suffering and making us more skilful in relationships. Such work needs qualified mentors who deeply understand these qualities and their methods, but even small independent steps can bring powerful relief from destructive states and emotions we are not used to working with. In the tradition the qualities are considered to balance each other mutually, so it is important to develop them as a complex; yet if you have never cultivated them, even one small step toward any one of them can show the power of the path, give a tangible shift in state of mind and be a positive start to deeper inner work [#1647].

#### Sympathetic joy as an antidote to envy
Everyone sometimes feels envy, and the practice of sympathetic joy targets it; it can be done each time envy arises. Steps: (1) notice that someone has what we do not have and that envy is arising; (2) consciously try to evoke sympathetic joy — joining the other person's joy, rejoicing for the good happening in their life; (3) by doing so we change our own state of mind [#1647].

#### Two choices: clinging vs not clinging
From a day with Dzigar Kongtrul Rinpoche, the author frames reactions to change as two choices [#1185]:
1. Not clinging — seeing that everything is in motion, everything changes, nothing is permanent [#1185].
2. Clinging — wanting firmly and angrily for things to stay unchanged; rejecting change and seeing it as a threat, as if something outside is harming you. In reality it is you who cannot let go of a strong attachment to your picture of the world. That attachment and its emotions of fear, anger and hatred make for a heavy existence [#1185].

#### Compassion in acute pain: widening to all beings
Context (author's self-report): after falling asleep on a beach he got an inflammation of the chest muscles, with 3-hour attacks of pulsing nerve pain radiating to teeth and head. Exhausted, he found himself fused with the pain — "я и есть моя боль" (I am my pain) — unable to look at the experience from outside. Previously, years of regular practice let him almost always separate from experience while keeping contact with it; "ровно вот этот небольшой гэп между наблюдателем и опытом делает любую ситуацию не такой плохой" (precisely this small gap between the observer and experience makes any situation not so bad) — but this position was unavailable, because his and Dasha's practice had slipped due to constant moves and long high workload [#1580].
1. He suffered for about an hour, fused with the pain [#1580].
2. He then thought of how many other people and animals suffer the same or worse right now — a countless number. Immediately some space formed: he could see the pain not only as his own and climb a little out of the compression into a narrow "I-perspective", where you are so bound to an unpleasant experience that you "stew in a cauldron" [#1580].
3. He wished himself and all others to be immediately freed from all suffering, and it became a little easier still [#1580].
4. Result: neither their pain nor his went away, but a small gap appeared in the mind — "словно к котлу подставили лесенку или огонь перевели на умеренный, как после закипания пельменей" (as if a little ladder was put against the cauldron or the fire was turned down to medium, like after dumplings come to a boil) [#1580].

Lessons the author draws: the episode genuinely frightened him and raised the possibility of worse pain for years; usually we brush off such risks by thinking "this won't happen to me", which he calls "абсолютно детский способ" (an absolutely childish way) of dealing with the truth of life, in which anything can happen and the probability of having to endure terrible physical or emotional pain is surely higher than the opposite. He hopes the episode helps him see what is truly important to focus on so as not to end up in "real hell". A side observation: after their conversation on the drive back about how their practice had slipped, Dasha took up practice and did it 4 days without missing, despite a heavier load; neither knows why, but the conversation may have influenced it [#1580].

#### Wishing happiness to the pain itself
Context (author's self-report): after years of chronic stress overload (an "abusive homeland", the volatility of entrepreneurship, family misunderstanding and dysfunction, emigration — and earlier crises since moving to Moscow at 17), he feels "like a beggar": the depth of presence in life has radically decreased, which he calls worse than losing all money. He has many tools and understanding for stress, but compares it to dismantling Chernobyl — whatever equipment you have, you will probably get radiation and suffer. He has years of sleep problems, cognitive decline, wakes early from morning cortisol, goes to therapy (individual and couple), but has no stable "45+45 minutes" of practice anymore and fears he lost contact with the deeper part of himself that was system-forming and enabled his counter-intuitive, hard choices — "нет ничего хуже, чем менять правду на комфорт" (there is nothing worse than trading truth for comfort) [#1805].

The sequence he describes one morning [#1805]:
1. Woke from morning cortisol with aching pain all over the body and physical dullness [#1805].
2. Used breathing with breath-holds and progressive relaxation; still unable to sleep [#1805].
3. In a hopeless, drowsy state, something inside began wishing happiness to the pain itself — not trying to get rid of it or improve the situation, just having compassion for it, sincerely wishing it to be free from suffering and the causes of suffering. The pain did not leave, but resistance to it decreased, and from inner split he felt as if fused into unity [#1805].
4. The intention of kindness then widened by itself: he stopped looping on his own suffering, and the wishes came to include all others — even those who did, do or will hurt him, including all dictators, rapists and murderers [#1805].
5. Outcome: "Страдание становится точкой входа, а не тюрьмой" (Suffering becomes an entry point, not a prison), and there is so much love there that no Chernobyl is frightening [#1805].

Related attitude: by allowing life to be hard — not because someone is to blame, but letting suffering simply be — he learns to see not with "children's eyes" that seek the right, flawless path, scolding oneself and blaming others and life for not matching expectations. The childish view is sweet in its simplicity but chained to fantasies, not reality. Looking back he also feels "healthy self-compassion" toward himself and joy at having built healthy supports, left many toxic relationships and made a leap in worldview, experience, people and skills — "вопреки, а не благодаря" (despite, not thanks to). He notes from experience that few people are ready to hear such things unless they have psychotherapeutic training [#1805].

#### Immunity to hatred
Purpose: a stance for staying healthy amid polarization and propaganda [#1807].
1. See hatred as a social mechanism, not personal depravity. The author's examples: residents in the provinces whose house was hit by falling debris reacted not by wanting to stop "the machine of malice" that makes such events possible but with another round of dehumanization and hatred toward those who launched it — the system converts fear and pain into hatred. And an entrepreneur whose business grew after February (weaker global competition) said his circle now pays taxes openly "because the state needs help"; the author physically felt how this disease works — a social mechanism where violence becomes background, profit looks like patriotism and adaptation looks like wisdom [#1807].
2. Don't condemn, but don't accept as normal. He does not want to judge, as there is already too much condemnation and division, but he wants to develop immunity [#1807].
3. Locate health deeper than politics. Immunity is not in correct slogans or belonging to the right camp, but in the ability to keep human perception where we are pulled again and again into simple explanations, collective anger and seeing living people as enemies [#1807].
4. Use hatred itself as the diagnostic. We may not understand politics or who is right, but we can always notice that hatred has arisen in us or around us — and if so, "то это лживый нарратив — это вирус" (then it is a false narrative — it is a virus) [#1807].
5. Take personal responsibility. When billions are invested in hatred, the future becomes possible again if we take responsibility for our immunity; healing the communities we are connected to is possible only through ourselves, because those who seek and seize power always shift questions about problems onto someone else — that is how new enemies keep arising [#1807].
6. Make kindness a practice, not a mood. A significant number of people (the author includes himself and readers) will have to make kindness and humanity "не настроением, а практикой. Не сентиментальностью, а дисциплиной восприятия" (not a mood but a practice; not sentimentality but a discipline of perception). As soon as we let hatred in and begin to wish another person pain, at that moment we catch "the virus of evil" ourselves and become a weapon in someone else's hands [#1807].

#### Classical Metta sequence (five recipients)
Purpose: the traditional practice for developing the quality of loving-kindness (любящая доброта). The post calls it "классическая" (classical): if you meditate, you have almost certainly met it on a retreat, in a book or in an app. Its structure is simple — "мы последовательно желаем счастья, покоя и свободы от страданий себе, затем близким, затем тем, к кому равнодушны, затем тем, с кем трудно, и в конце всем живым существам" (we successively wish happiness, peace and freedom from suffering to ourselves, then to those close to us, then to those to whom we are indifferent, then to those with whom it is difficult, and finally to all living beings). The three wishes in each stage are: happiness, peace, freedom from suffering [#1978].

1. Oneself — the wishes are first directed to yourself [#1978].
2. Close ones (близкие) — people you love and are near to [#1978].
3. Those you are indifferent to (те, к кому равнодушны) — neutral people [#1978].
4. Those with whom it is difficult (те, с кем трудно) — difficult people [#1978].
5. All living beings (все живые существа) — the final, unlimited circle [#1978].

How to hold it: the essence is not to evoke a pleasant feeling in yourself. "Мы тренируем намерение, то есть само отношение к себе и другим, а тепло появляется как следствие этой работы и не всегда сразу" (We train intention, that is, the very attitude toward ourselves and others, and warmth appears as a consequence of this work and not always right away). So the absence of warmth during a session does not mean the practice is failing [#1978].

Why it pairs with vipassana: "ясное видение без доброжелательности легко становится сухим и жестким, в том числе по отношению к себе" (clear seeing without kindness easily becomes dry and harsh, including toward oneself). Metta therefore "хорошо дополняет випассану" (complements vipassana well) [#1978].

Note relative to the existing file: the existing *Adapting Metta — part 2* (#366) proposes, for Westerners who find self-directed wishes hard, starting with a beloved being and arriving at oneself last; this classical sequence instead starts with oneself. The two are not presented as contradicting each other — the adaptation was explicitly offered as a modification of the classical structure [#1978].

#### Informal Metta in a moment of lost practice (self then widening circles)
This is not a prescribed technique but the author's description of what happened to him; it carries a sequence and a lesson [#1891].

1. Notice the collapse without forcing it. In periods of constant planning of moves, many projects, or "подпрыгивать на диване от хлопков в центре Москвы" (jumping on the sofa from bangs in the centre of Moscow), he loses practice completely: stops sending check-ins to chats with like-minded practitioners and even stops thinking that it would be good to return to a daily rhythm. When he notices this he gets badly frightened — "Меня до холода продирает мысль, что связь действительно потеряна" (The thought that the connection is really lost chills me to the bone) [#1891].
2. Let the worry go. Then he stops worrying, because in the moment he seems unable to change the situation, and releases it [#1891].
3. Wish yourself happiness wherever you are. At some moment — having already lost both the practice and the disappointment about not being able to return to it — not in a formal meditation posture but simply settled into a doctor's chair for an IV drip, or seated in a plane, he begins to wish himself happiness and freedom from suffering [#1891].
4. Widen the circles. Then he goes on to wider circles and wishes the same to all living sentient beings [#1891].
5. Recognize it as what remains. This "просто прорывается откуда-то изнутри, словно приходит само" (simply breaks through from somewhere inside, as if it comes by itself) and, in a sense, is what stays even when he is in "сне обыденности" (the sleep of ordinariness) and practice "лежит в руинах забвения" (lies in the ruins of oblivion) [#1891].

Why self comes first: he values these minutes of kind attitude toward the world, which "не получается полноценно реализовать, если пропустить такое же отношение к самому себе" (cannot be fully realized if you skip the same attitude toward yourself). They feel good, "но не только в этом дело" (but that is not the only point) [#1891].

### Detailed insights

#### Metta as a complement to psychotherapy
Metta is described as the most important Buddhist meditation practice, which according to research gives the fastest and most tangible results among meditative practices: after only 2 weeks of 30 minutes a day, positive changes appear in subjects' brains that they also feel in life. It develops an essential quality of a healthy psyche — "чувство безусловного принятия, любви и заботы о себе и других" (a feeling of unconditional acceptance, love and care for oneself and others). Since most of us did not receive enough of this feeling in upbringing, Metta can be an extremely important addition to psychotherapy and, in combination, can help heal one's neurosis faster. Over time the practice teaches us to fill independently what we lacked in childhood and to be a loving adult for ourselves and others — which the author calls one of the key tasks of therapy. Expected difficulty: many people find the practice hard at first — a feeling that nothing is happening, or painful thoughts that wishing oneself love and happiness is wrong. The author invited readers to take 1–2 practices for at least 2 weeks and check how it works for them [#556].

#### Why hatred hurts the hater first (Dzigar Kongtrul Rinpoche day)
Context: a viral video of Elon Musk "firing" an employee who corrected his misgendering turned out to be a fake cut from podcasts and dubbed into Russian; it collected thousands of likes, joy toward Musk and hatred toward the non-binary employee — "Фейк для игры в ненависть" (a fake for playing at hatred). The author felt uneasy at the flow of aggression people seem to feed on; he asked GPT why, and it answered with conservative values and fear of change, perceiving non-standard gender identities as a threat to established norms. He then spent a day with Dzigar Kongtrul Rinpoche, who spoke for an hour about this closedness "with great compassion", reconnecting the author into a whole with the politicians and commenters he wanted to hit; the author realised he too must open compassion in himself, or he would lose the joy and lightness they had already lost [#1185].

Teachings recorded:
- When we feel hatred, who suffers first? We do. Physical pain and discomfort permeate our existence; these emotions become our world, fully defining our focus and sensations and separating us from contact with others [#1185].
- The more we close, the more we suffer and the more we see only the world of our anger — that everything outside is going "wrong". This is a deep mental habit that prevents you from seeing how much suffering you create yourself. Watching people get stuck in such clinging is heartbreaking — so much aggression, pride, envy and violence they create [#1185].
- In hating, we do what someone else wants from us; we are reactive and deeply unfree. While we stay closed, there are no conditions to leave this vicious circle; therefore openness and softening of mind and heart are what is needed to become freer and reduce our own suffering [#1185].
- War can be reduced to one person or group responsible for it, but there would be no war in a society saturated with respect and love; to wage war you must hate your neighbour, the West, non-binary people, and see threat everywhere. "Любящее сердце не ненавидит и не боится" (A loving heart neither hates nor fears). So the most important task now is to stop anger in oneself and to have compassion when a brother is in trouble, however different he/she/they may be — "Пожалуй, начну" (I suppose I'll begin) [#1185].

#### Practice in times that seem incompatible with it
The author writes that he seems to live in times not at all compatible with meditation — so restless and busy that a practice of calming the mind feels "абсолютно контр-логичной" (absolutely counter-logical), although, if one follows logic, this is exactly where it is needed [#1891].

What he says is lost when practice lapses: the daily rhythm of formal presence, of developing kindness, and of equanimity toward pleasant and unpleasant experiences — "Вернуть контейнер для утойчивости [sic] внимания" (To return the container for stability of attention). His ideal: start the day with such practice and devote at least 15–20 minutes to it — "Ну может 10 или даже одну" (Well, maybe 10 or even one) [#1891].

What he considers the real loss: not the formal minutes "сидения в странной позе" (of sitting in a strange posture), but losing contact with "бескоайней [sic] просторностью ума, полного доброты и сострадания по своей природе" (the boundless spaciousness of a mind that is by its nature full of kindness and compassion); amid all the madness in the world, this seems to him the saddest of all possible events. He describes having become so absorbed in external objects and lost in vain worries and anxieties that he forgot, turned away and lost the thread leading to the most valuable thing one can find — something he had already discovered and cultivated with all his strength [#1891].

#### Practice as a reminder of innate kindness (the author's view)
The author argues that practice "это не концепт, не чья-то искусственная идея, изобретение Будды, Иисуса или Кабат-Зинна" (is not a concept, not someone's artificial idea, an invention of Buddha, Jesus or Kabat-Zinn), nor a conceptual framework of the kind "какие шаги надо сделать для создания продукта или успешной стратегии" (what steps to take to create a product or a successful strategy). "Практика — это всего лишь напоминание о том, что всегда лежит в глубине нашей души" (Practice is only a reminder of what always lies in the depth of our soul), "if one uses this unclear word" [#1891].

The spontaneous wish to create an "островок доброжелательности" (little island of kindness) is for him a reflection of the naturalness that is the inherent nature of a living being — the foundation of reality called unconditional love, which permeates the very essence of all sentient consciousness. He says he may indeed live in times of decline — external, internal, or both — but no circumstances can cancel these "законов живой физики" (laws of living physics): even behind the blackest clouds of anger, fear and the feeling of separateness, the sun of clarity, kindness and compassion always shines brightly. This is presented as the author's conviction, not as a demonstrated claim [#1891].

#### Garchen Rinpoche: compassion for tormentors
The author recalls the "already famous" words of Garchen Rinpoche, a Tibetan master who spent 20 years in a Chinese concentration camp in terrible conditions. About that period he said: "Самое ужасное, что могло случится — это если бы я потерял сострадание к своим мучителям" (The most terrible thing that could have happened would be if I had lost compassion for my tormentors). The author invites the reader to "Прочувствуйте сознание, которое так видит мир" (Feel into a consciousness that sees the world this way) and says that living without glimpses of it is truly frightening. He closes with a Metta wish to himself and readers: that all be happy, free from suffering and never lose a kind attitude. This complements the existing *Immunity to hatred* material (#1185, #1807) with a model case of compassion toward those who caused harm [#1891].

#### Why learn Metta from a long-term practitioner
The post argues that Metta is worth doing at least once with a person who has cultivated for decades the qualities the practice points to: then you have not only an instruction but a living example of where it leads. The same words sound different from someone who has learned the instruction and from someone for whom kindness has long been a way of being with people — in the second case you hear not only the text but the state from which it is spoken, and your own mind begins to attune to it [#1978].

The teacher in question: maechee Brigitte Schrottenbacher has taught vipassana at the Wat Prayong centre in Thailand since the 1990s in the Mahasi Sayadaw method, and received the Thai royal family's "Outstanding Woman in Buddhism" award. The image card accompanying the post shows only the title LOVING KINDNESS and the teacher's name in capitals [#1978].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- Safety: the author warns that heart practices require special caution and are better done with a trauma-informed mindfulness teacher or a mindfulness-informed psychotherapist [#359]; the four immeasurables need qualified mentors, though small independent steps are encouraged [#1647].
- Disagreement among teachers: starting the path with self-compassion/Metta contradicts some mindfulness teachers' views; the author presents it as his current position [#359].
- Expected resistance: early practice often feels like nothing happens, or brings painful thoughts that wishing oneself love is wrong [#556].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the research behind "fastest results" and "2 weeks × 30 min" is cited only via links to book pages (Altered Traits; Neff and Germer); no specific studies, samples or measures are named. The 7-in-10 trauma prevalence for Russia and the USA is given without a source [#358, #359, #556].
- (!) CONTRADICTION (minor): #358 says changes are felt in well-being and behaviour after 2 weeks; #556 says the changes appear "in the brain" of subjects; both cite 2 weeks at 30 minutes per day [#358, #556].
- The GPT answer about why people celebrated the firing is presented as GPT's view, not a verified finding [#1185].
- The pain episodes are personal, single-case accounts; in both, the pain itself did not go away — only resistance and the sense of being trapped lessened [#1580, #1805].
- In the four-immeasurables post the author notes his own vantage point: he was writing from a retreat with the quoted teacher [#1647].

- (!) CONTRADICTION (soft): the classical order in #1978 starts with oneself, while the existing file's adaptation (#366) recommends starting with a beloved being for Westerners who find self-wishes hard. The source does not reconcile them in these posts; #366 frames its order as an optional modification [#1978].
- The claims that kindness is the innate nature of sentient beings and that unconditional love is "the foundation of reality" are the author's personal, explicitly hedged convictions ("если использовать это непонятное слово" — *if one uses this unclear word*; "Кажется" — *It seems*) [#1891].
- Warmth may not appear right away in Metta; the post treats this as normal rather than a sign of failure [#1978].
