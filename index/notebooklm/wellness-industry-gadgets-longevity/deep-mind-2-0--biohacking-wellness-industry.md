## Supplements and recovery protocols

### What this covers

How the channel author restores and protects mental clarity and physical state under demanding conditions: brain fog (after covid or simply from intense city life and work), frequent flights, and periods of high workload, stress and uncertainty. The topic spans an anti-inflammatory diet, healthy fats (MCT and ghee) taken as "bulletproof" coffee, a brain-support supplement shortlist, a seven-supplement stress list, a flight-recovery protocol, an ergonomic laptop setup, and a functional-medicine clinic's model of mental clarity. It matters, per the author, because a clouded mind instantly shows up in the quality of his work and his leadership of the company, so he switches on the "full arsenal" of recovery methods as soon as he notices it [#329]. Every supplement post carries the same caveat: the team has no medical or nutrition expertise, so consult a doctor and check your blood and contraindications [#329, #966, #1160].

### Core ideas

- Brain fog is environmental, not only post-covid. Many covid patients report a dull or clouded mind that does not pass right after the illness, but the same state is easy to get just by living and working actively in Moscow [#329]. The clinic speakers add the mechanism: city life and intense business keep cortisol chronically elevated (which physically affects the brain) and maintain chronic inflammation, so the brain "literally does not manage to fully clean itself" [#1079].
- Clarity is multifactorial. Both the author's seven-point protocol and the clinic's model treat clarity as the result of sleep, nutrition, movement, light/information hygiene, supplements and assessment together, not one fix [#329, #1079].
- Inflammation is the common enemy. Diet, supplements and movement are all framed as ways to lower overall inflammation, which "also negatively affects the ability to think clearly" [#329, #1079].
- Mental clarity can be trained. The author's addition to medicine: clarity is a natural property of consciousness, and meditation grows neural pathways supporting attention and memory — "the most direct way" [#1079, #329].
- Introduce supplements one at a time. Check contraindications, add one, observe several days, then add the next — this is how he caught a bad reaction to NAC [#1160].
- Small "game changers" give large returns. Simple, easy-to-adopt things like a laptop stand or a daily healthy-fat drink make a big contribution to one's state [#950, #966].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Seven-point protocol against brain fog
Purpose: what the author considers especially important to do when he notices fog in his head (written while he himself was ill with covid). He says he immediately switches on the full set for fast recovery [#329].

1. Quality sleep. During sleep the brain undergoes both physical cleansing and psychological integration of information. (The author refers to an earlier sleep guide of his; its content is not part of this source.) [#329]
2. Anti-inflammatory nutrition with cognitive support. Three moves:
   - *Remove* all allergens and inflammation-producing foods. First in line: all sugars and sweeteners, even natural ones; casein; gluten; non-organic red meat; any other foods containing toxins.
   - *Add* healthy fats. First tier: MCT (refined coconut oil) and organic ghee from grass-fed cows — the author drinks coffee with them every morning (see *Bulletproof coffee with MCT and ghee*). Second tier: unrefined organic olive, sesame, flaxseed and coconut oils.
   - *Increase* fresh, unprocessed, untreated organic foods: greens, vegetables, nuts, olives, fruits and berries.
   For a mitochondrial-support protocol he points to another channel's post and to Dave Asprey's Bulletproof diet (the linked protocol's content is not reproduced in the source). [#329]
3. Supplements. Supplements let you support brain neurofunction, mitochondrial work and lower general inflammation in a targeted way. His shortlist, which he usually takes through autumn and winter regardless of covid: (a) Fisetin; (b) True Focus (a NOW capsule product); (c) MethylFolate (Jarrow, 400 mcg capsules); (d) EPA/DHA Balance (Jarrow softgels; written "EPH/DHA" in the post); (e) High Absorption Curcumin (Doctor's Best, 500 mg). He describes all of them as substances supporting quality brain and nervous-system work, and adds that you should check your blood before and during taking any supplements and do it together with an adequate doctor. (!) INCOMPLETE: no doses, timing or per-item rationale are given. [#329]
4. Information and digital detox. Multitasking and mixed types of information "defragment" the brain and overload memory and attention systems. A day without the phone will certainly help; better still is permanently reducing screen time. [#329]
5. Daily meditation. It could be first on the list, since it is the direct way to work on clarity, but when physiology suffers badly, meditating is harder and people tend to drop it — hence physical basics come first. Choose whatever meditation works for you right now and that you can do regularly; concentration-building meditations take priority in this case. [#329]
6. Daily movement. Even while ill with covid, the author does exercises and moves whenever he can. Movement lowers inflammation, completes the stress cycle and supports brain function; he says so much evidence exists that movement cannot be ignored when discussing wellbeing or effectiveness. Minimum norm: 30 minutes of brisk activity per day. [#329]
7. Light therapy. Light is very important for mitochondrial health and for balancing circadian rhythms. With no sun, buy light-therapy lamps. For 2–3 hours before sleep, exclude screens and any blue or bright white light. [#329]

#### Functional-medicine multifactor model of mental clarity
Source: a session at an integrative functional medicine clinic during NYC Tech Week, summarized by the author. He likes the integrative functional approach because the doctor does not view a disease as isolated from the rest of the body and environment but looks at the whole system, focusing on optimizing health, supporting function and the person's real daily life. The clinic therefore treats brain fog through a multifactor model ("as it should be"), and the author notes it matches the base recommendations in deep mind's own stress-management program. The goal is to "stop looking at life through a fogged-up glass" [#1079].

1. Assessment of brain health and general markers. Blood sugar, inflammation, nutrients, various profiles (lipid, hormonal, with the source itself ending the list with "и т.д.", i.e. the list is open-ended); presence of post-covid, chronic infections, cognitive dysfunctions. [#1079]
2. Nutrition. It must lower inflammation rather than raise it and supply enough nutrients. Big emphasis on reducing sugar and processed foods, and on supplements including pre- and probiotics. Fasting is recommended as a way to work with inflammation and detoxify the brain. [#1079]
3. Sleep. Called the most important foundation of health, which many high performers neglect without realizing they are giving up cognitive quality. Major brain detoxification happens in deep sleep, so do everything to sleep enough and well. [#1079]
4. Exercise. The best thing you can do for brain health and for balancing the hormonal system, which also strongly affects the brain; movement also lowers inflammation and helps clear cortisol from the body. [#1079]
5. (Author's addition) Meditation. What the doctors "literally forgot": mental clarity can be trained and is a natural property of consciousness. Through meditation we can grow neural pathways that support cognitive function — "the most direct way" to build attention and memory. He remarks, with irony, that this knowledge has apparently not yet seeped even into advanced medicine. [#1079]

#### Flight recovery protocol
Purpose: reduce the stress load on the body before and after each flight. The author built it as someone who flies a lot (three flights in the previous week, which he calls normal for him). He admits parts are obvious, but stresses that the point is to actually *do* obvious practices [#867].

Before and during the flight
1. Lower stress around travel day. Plan and prepare departure and arrival, pack on time, cancel any other tasks and calls the day before. The fewer situations where he has to gut his luggage to get something out or arrives at the wrong airport, the better. [#867]
2. Hydrate, sleep well and take supplements. Drink "living" water that still contains minerals — always good, and especially before a dry environment: mineral water, good spring water, electrolytes. Coffee and alcohol can be replaced with matcha and hojicha, which he says help retain water in cells, countering dehydration. Sleep boosts immune capacity and avoids unnecessary stress in flight. The day before, adaptogens can be added, e.g. cordyceps or ashwagandha. Some studies, he says, show antioxidants such as vitamins C and E may help with the negative effects of long flights. [#867]
3. Move and stretch. Possibly the most important point: do not stay still on long flights. He has tested many times that standing up hourly, warming up and stretching changes how he feels "many times over", even on transatlantic flights — much less fatigue on arrival. Loose, comfortable clothing that doesn't press anywhere belongs here too. [#867]
4. Practice concentration. Airports scatter attention with many small actions and information. Calming attention for at least 10 minutes at any stage of the journey helps reintegrate the body's disintegrated needs and do yourself less harm during the flight. [#867]
5. Don't eat airline food. With rare exceptions it is bad. When possible he brings fresh plant food on board, especially berries rich in antioxidants and enzymes; this helps him feel fine. [#867]
6. Reduce harmful information noise. A cap, a hood and yellow glasses that shield skin and eyes from the harmful blue light abundant in airports and planes; earplugs against constant noise. [#867]

After the flight
1. Ground yourself as fast as possible. Do yoga on the ground without a mat, swim in the sea or a lake, or just stand on a natural floor. The author claims this is "proven" to help normalize the body's electrical charge, which changes during flight. [#867]
2. Keep hydrating. He drinks several liters of different good-quality water, takes electrolytes, applies a skin mask and moistens his eyes with Japanese vitamin eye drops. [#867]
3. Fast or eat light fresh food. Many top coaches who constantly fly recommend not eating on the first day after a flight, to help the body clear damaged cells through fasting. If that is very stressful for you, at least don't overeat, avoid heavy and carb-rich food, and add large portions of fresh vegetables and greens. [#867]
4. Sleep enough and return to the natural chronorhythm. He says this seems to be the one case where melatonin really can help sleep, by helping adjust the circadian rhythm to the new location. [#867]
5. Get a massage. As soon as possible he goes for a 2-hour massage to move lymph and carefully knead the whole body. [#867]

#### Bulletproof coffee with MCT and ghee
Purpose: daily intake of "super healthy fats" that nourish body and brain — the author's "Game changer 2", which he says noticeably changes his life [#966].

- Ingredients. (1) Organic MCT oil — pure medium-chain triglycerides, described as the most rapidly absorbed fats available today; a source of saturated healthy fats said to harmonize internal systems and first of all raise endurance and give an instant energy boost. (2) Organic ghee from grass-fed cows — made by heating milk or butter above 100 °C, which evaporates moisture and harmful chemical pesticides; used for millennia in Ayurveda as a therapeutic remedy, believed to promote longevity, improve digestion and memory, and strengthen the nervous system. [#966]
- Preparation and timing. Blend black coffee with the oils in a shaker and drink it first thing in the morning before breakfast, then go meditate — the practice goes better. He has used decaf for more than a year (he wrote elsewhere about who that suits). The combination seems strange at first, then you get used to it and enjoy both taste and effect. Alternatives: add the oils to salads or drinks, or just take them by the spoon. [#966]
- Dose. Standard: 1 teaspoon of each oil per day; if there are no problems, gradually increase to a tablespoon. Ghee is not recommended above 1–2 tablespoons per day. [#966]
- Contraindications. MCT: the team found none except individual intolerance. Ghee: liver and biliary-tract diseases, the acute phase of ulcer and gastritis, pancreatitis, neoplasms, exacerbation of chronic diseases. [#966]
- Effects the author reports on himself. (1) His head clears quickly and strongly; (2) an even, "organic" surge of energy; (3) mood swings become softer; (4) less craving for junk food during the day — sweets and questionable snacks. [#966]

#### One-supplement-at-a-time introduction scheme
The author's personal routine for starting anything new: (1) check contraindications (and consult a doctor, since the team lacks medical expertise); (2) add only one supplement at a time; (3) watch your reaction for several days; (4) if all is fine, add the second. Lesson from his own case: he once had an awful state from taking NAC, and this scheme is what allowed him to pinpoint the cause. Pair with the advice to check blood before and during supplementation [#1160, #329].

#### Ergonomic laptop work setup
"Game changer 1": a laptop stand, which he finds obvious yet still unused by most people who work at computers; he constantly sees people in cafes and offices bending their heads down and creating unnecessary stress and fatigue in the body [#950].
- Why. The stand completely rid him of neck pain, gave more clarity in his head and improved his overall sense of comfort. He cites research that posture can strongly influence psychological state: an upright position (easy to hold with a stand, "impossible" without) creates confidence and good mood and makes it easier to cope with stressors and fatigue; the reverse also holds — hunching produces bodily discomfort that turns life grey and lets stress accumulate faster. His own addition: healthy posture aids mental clarity because blood moves naturally through the body and nourishes the brain. [#950]
- Choosing a stand. He tried three and settled on one that rotates around its axis, so you can easily change body position and adapt the laptop to it. Second criterion: it must fold easily and fit in a backpack so you always carry it. The accompanying photo shows his setup: a silver folding aluminium stand raising the laptop, a separate white wireless keyboard and a grey wireless mouse on a wooden desk [#950].
- Pitfall. Some friends bought stands but could not use them because typing was uncomfortable. It is a whole setup: the stand needs an external keyboard, a mouse and a carrying bag. [#950]
- Rule. Don't postpone or skimp on a proper work setup — "it changes everything." Working standing is another option through which many people gained energy and health via correct posture. [#950]

Investment in mental health and telemedicine hit records in 2020; the author predicted explosive growth of prevention, diagnostics and psychological-help services and suggested mental health may become "the new biotech" for investors (detail in *Healthtech and mental-health investment*) [#196, #263].
- Wellness trends start in luxury and trickle down. Meditation gardens, Wim Hof ice baths, spiritual concierges and shamans in luxury residences reflect a broadened notion of wellbeing that includes emotional and spiritual health; such trends later become mass lifestyle [#839].
- Technology can do part of the work, not replace practice. Neurofeedback devices and recovery tech support state management, but do not replace meditation and only make sense once the "base" (sleep, food, movement, mental support) is closed [#901, #1335].
- Wearables: trends over single days, inner sense over the device. Trackers have documented benefits and harms; the key value is in trends, and one must never give the device the right to know one's state better than one's own feeling [#1637].
- Critique of reductionism. Huberman-style biohacking isolates one marker or study and builds a protocol around it, and rests on the "myth of isolated health"; wellbeing is relational and systemic [#1371, #1492].
- Wellness as performance is spiritual materialism. When meditation, therapy and procedures serve productivity, wellness loses its main achievement — restoring connection with oneself and reality — and feeds the "burnout society" [#1492, #1493, #1494].
- 2026: from biohacking to biohealing. The author sees wellness culture moving from performance to healing: nervous-system sovereignty, gut-soul connection, sleep as sacred space, spiritual biohacking and nature as teacher — as a symptom of fatigue from constant acceleration [#1654, #1655, #1656, #1657, #1658, #1659, #1660, #1661, #1662].

- Recovery has moved from reward to metric. Founders now manage rest, diagnostics and body data the way they manage business indicators [#1955].
- The bar is being replaced by the wellness club. Business socialising migrates from cocktail bars and meeting rooms to adaptogen bars and infrared saunas [#1955].
- Scheduled recovery is management hygiene — but recovery-as-KPI breeds a new pressure. The author holds both views at once (detailed below) [#1955].
- The underlying logic is a dubious "culture of happiness". It reflects the functional materialist logic of modern business, which stops seeing people in employees and now in ourselves [#1955].

#### Three functions of a home neurofeedback headband
Context: in September 2023 the author was practising home neurofeedback with a headband (обруч) from a Russia-based startup, Neiry Mind Tracker, which he also consults "a little" (conflict of interest he discloses himself). He says the headband does three key things [#901]:

1. Gives feedback on your general state. If he is tired, after a measurement in the app he gets information about it and can understand his state a bit better — useful especially "if I can't do it myself" (i.e. for people who cannot yet read their own state) [#901].
2. Trains you to enter the focused-relaxed state. The author calls «состояние сфокусированности-расслабленности» (the state of focus-relaxation) "the basic goal of meditation"; in it "we are most effective, creative and generally satisfied with life". Neurofeedback helps find this state through sound: if birdsong in the program grows louder and extraneous sounds quieter, it is a signal that you are approaching the target state. The lesson: "так мы получаем внешнюю опору для понимания себя" (this way we get an external support for understanding ourselves) [#901].
3. Shows the level of cognitive load. Knowing it is generally useful for self-understanding; and if the band is worn like a fitness bracelet, one can calibrate one's schedule, placing high-cognitive-load tasks into periods of maximum productivity [#901].

The author's reasoning and verdict: despite the "mindfulness revolution", few people are really able to practise awareness and see results. «Технологии не заменять медитацию, но способно сделать часть работы» (Technology does not replace meditation, but can do part of the work [sic — grammar errors in original]) and support us on the way to greater resilience and clarity of mind. No "cosmic breakthrough" in mental-health tech has happened yet, but projects like this may bring it closer. Verdict on the product: "in places rather raw, but close [to him] and actively developing". His personal results: he uses it out of professional interest and as additional support for the balance of engagement and relaxation; he falls asleep slightly better immediately after a neurofeedback session and looks with curiosity at the analytics of his state. He promised a fuller user report after a couple of months once results consolidate [#901].

#### Recovery-studio procedure set with course schedules
Context (March 2025): massage and bathhouse no longer surprise anyone; worldwide more wellness and recovery studios open, using technologies previously available only in clinics or sanatoriums. The author has visited such studios in New York and goes to one in Bali, "but I don't exaggerate their importance". During a cycle of recovery from flights and load he visits a centre a few minutes from home a couple of times a week; the routine "helps return clarity of mind, reduce fatigue and generally feel better". The five procedures, each with what it is, the claimed effects as the author states them, and the course [#1335]:

1. Drips (IV drips, капельницы). Nothing new except that they are now done not in hospital when we are very ill, but preventively for recovery — e.g. drips with a vitamin complex, an antioxidant such as glutathione, and saline restoring salt-water balance. Claimed effects: restores the body after stress at the cellular level, reduces inflammatory load, raises immunity, improves skin, liver and brain function. Course under prolonged stress: 1–2 times a week for 4–6 weeks [#1335].
2. Cryochamber (криокамера). 2–3 minutes in a chamber at minus 140 degrees. Claimed effects: reduces inflammation, speeds recovery of the nervous system, lowers cortisol, improves sleep. Course: 2–3 times a week for 3–4 weeks [#1335].
3. HBOT / oxygen chamber. Inside the chamber the pressure changes while oxygen is fed into the nose, so that within an hour "we are substantially saturated with oxygen". Claimed effects: accelerates cellular regeneration, improves cognitive functions, restores the nervous system. Course: 1–2 times a week, 6 to 10 sessions [#1335].
4. Vibration platform with red light. Gently shakes the whole body and slightly warms it with infrared light. Claimed effects: improves microcirculation, reduces muscle tension, calms the nervous system, promotes deep relaxation. Course: 2–3 times a week for 4–6 weeks [#1335].
5. Pressotherapy (прессотерапия). The whole body or parts are squeezed gently but noticeably by a special inflating suit ("mask" in the source). Claimed effects: stimulates lymph drainage, improves sleep, reduces stress and anxiety. Course: 1–2 times a week for 4–6 weeks [#1335].

The accompanying photo shows the studio's wall sign "This is where the magic happens" and "Oxygen Health Systems" branding on a chamber [#1335].

#### Rules for recovery and biohacking procedures (closing the base first)
What the author says is important to know about such wellness and state-support methods [#1335]:

1. Discuss them with specialists, because they have contraindications [#1335].
2. Combine procedures and do them as a course, alternating days of load and recovery. Preferably run a course of at least a month to consolidate the cumulative effect [#1335].
3. Support yourself psychologically during the course — with psychotherapy and self-regulation practices — because "Подобные процедуры могут способствовать возникновению триггерящий мыслей и другого материала" (Such procedures can contribute to triggering thoughts and other material arising) [#1335].
4. The main rule: close the base first. All such biohacking makes sense "not by itself" but when the base is closed: conditions for normal sleep, nutrition and movement are created, we don't sustain harmful overwork patterns, and we have mental support. If the base is not closed, procedures can give relief and be useful, but the effect fades fast because the cause of the problem is not eliminated [#1335].

The same "first level" principle is voiced by a Palo Alto entrepreneur the author interviewed (see *Critique of capitalist reductionism*) [#1492].

#### Benefits and harms of wearable health trackers (research summary)
Context (November 2025): in entrepreneurial and IT circles it has become almost shameful ("зашкварно") not to wear Whoop or similar state-measuring gadgets, while many wellness bloggers have started criticising such devices because of emerging studies with negative data. The author says deep mind analysed this question specifically for its corporate state-management programme (which ran in several Yandex businesses and other companies, including a section on wearables). Method claimed: an integral view — ranking and analysing reliable studies, observing and creating practice, applying common sense [#1637].

Principle: technologies are multifaceted; you cannot take one study out of context and declare "gadgets are harmful! gadgets are useful!" — by that logic cars (people die in crashes) and social networks (many became addicted) would be banned [#1637].

What is known from research, according to the author:

Benefits:
1. Increased awareness of one's state and of specific areas and strategies (e.g. nutrition) [#1637].
2. Motivation through external feedback [#1637].
3. Forming healthy habits [#1637].
4. Reduced overtraining, sugar spikes and inflammation [#1637].

Harms:
1. Orthosomnia, eating disorders (РПП) and other psychological difficulties tied to fixation on numbers — e.g. we learn from the metrics that we slept badly, start to worry about it and sleep even worse [#1637].
2. False interpretations — both external (from the gadget) and internal (our own wrong interpretations of the data) [#1637].
3. Reduced awareness and inner intuition — the ability to know and understand one's states subjectively [#1637].

What it means: a given day's data can diverge strongly from our subjective interpretation, but the key value of data is not the local assessment but trends, which we tend to notice and analyse poorly without external support. It is crucial not to give external tools the right to understand our state better than we feel it from inside — both for an adequate picture of the world and for building inner skills, without which it is very hard to progress in state management. Data and metrics are not another game of perfectionism; if we focus on them too much, reduce immersion and take breaks [#1637].

#### Six rules for using trackers (Трекеры - итого)
The summary card of the wearables post gives six numbered rules [#1637]:

1. Look at trends rather than at data in the moment — single-day numbers may contradict how you feel; patterns over time are where the value is [#1637].
2. Prioritise subjective perception and learn to assess it before receiving external data — first ask yourself how you are, then look at the device; this protects inner intuition [#1637].
3. Do not try to optimise everything to perfection — metrics are not a perfectionism game [#1637].
4. If dependence on or anxiety from the data arises, reduce the focus on numbers and keep minimal monitoring [#1637].
5. Take breaks from receiving data [#1637].
6. With strong deviations, consult a doctor [#1637].

#### Wellbeing 2.0 — relational model of health versus the myth of isolated health
Purpose: a lens for looking at wellbeing beyond biohacking protocols (May 2025) [#1371].

1. Diagnosis of wellbeing 1.0. Modern wellbeing "[naturally 1.0]" is often built on a one-sided model "ака Хуберман" and all kinds of biohacking, where we reductively focus on one marker, hormone or aspect and build a protocol and routine around it. Worse, it rests entirely «на мифе изолированного здоровья» (on the myth of isolated health), presenting wellbeing as a personal project. If wellbeing is a personal project, it can be achieved by willpower: building a regime, keeping a diet, collecting steps in an app, meditating on schedule [#1371].
2. The metaphor of the tree. «дерево не может быть живым, если у него зелёные листья, но стоит оно в отравленной почве» (a tree cannot be alive if it has green leaves but stands in poisoned soil). Looking closely at real experience — one's own, clients', collective — it becomes clearer that health does not arise in isolation, does not belong only to the body and does not exist outside context [#1371].
3. Why habits are not enough. We can know everything about biohacking and still remain exhausted, anxious and disconnected from ourselves; physically fine but inwardly feeling that everything slips away. Because health and wellbeing are «не сумма отдельных привычек, а наша согласованность с самим собой и реальностью вокруг» (not a sum of separate habits but our coherence with ourselves and the reality around us) [#1371].
4. Next-order wellbeing: relationships instead of control. It arises from ever finer attunement and coherence between interdependent parts of a large system. It brings not so much the idea of control and achievement as the idea of relationships: with body, mind, others and environment. It includes not only *what we do* but *in what reality we do it* — four questions the author lists: at what pace we live; what language we speak; whether we can stay in a pause; whether there are people nearby with whom we can be authentic and without masks [#1371].
5. Wellbeing as an ethical act. Through this lens health stops being an isolated effort and becomes an ethical act aimed at healing the whole system. By healing relationships at different levels we become a resource not only for ourselves but for others, bringing more clarity, warmth and stability into the field we touch — "not just feeling good, but the quality of contact we create in this world" [#1371].
6. Why this lens now. In an atomised, fragmented reality this is "perhaps the best lens" for wellbeing: not merely collecting healthy habits but seeing that we are connected and influence each other, paying attention to rhythms, connections and depth of response — «Не для продуктивности и результата, а потому что так мы снова становимся живыми» (Not for productivity and results, but because this is how we become alive again) [#1371].

#### Five wellness trends for 2026 (from biohacking to biohealing)
Thesis (December 2025): we are entering a phase where health is no longer built around productivity at any cost and endless pursuit of external achievements; focus shifts to deep presence, inner capacity and genuine bodily clarity. Wellness culture moves from performance mode toward healing and recovery: away from Huberman's functional and superficial optimisation protocols; Maté, Van der Kolk and Levine become mainstream; understanding the fundamental reality of the nervous system and rethinking spirituality become truly fashionable [#1654]. The cover card frames it as "Wellness 2026: when the body becomes a compass again": wellness stops being only a way to relax or "fix oneself" and becomes «фундаментальный язык для ориентации в сложной реальности» (a fundamental language for orientation in a complex reality) [#1654].

1. Nervous-system sovereignty (Суверенитет нервной системы). "This really is the sovereignty worth fighting for." Nervous-system regulation stops being an emergency measure against burnout and becomes an integral part of everyday life and an important area of self-knowledge. People want not just to calm down but to deeply understand their own reactions, sensitivity thresholds, neurodivergences and stress patterns in real time [#1655]. Working with this sovereignty «мы возвращаем телу агентность» (we return agency to the body). It shows up in polyvagal rituals and "somatic tracking thinking", where attention goes not only to thoughts but to how they manifest in the body. Metrics like HRV serve not as a goal but as feedback — returning to body-mind the genuine capacity for self-regulation [#1656].
2. Gut-Soul Connection: gut and state. Returning bodily logic to where "only the head" ruled for too long. The gut is no longer seen only as a physiological organ but re-understood as a deep archive — a store of intuition, lived traumas and basic instincts. Hence interest in: (a) psychobiotics for mood regulation; (b) writing as a form of emotional "digestion"; (c) gentle somatic work with the belly, breathing and the lower body [#1657].
3. Sleep as sacred space. Sleep stops being merely a pause between tasks and regains the status of a dimension for healing and deep recovery. Preparing for sleep is perceived as a sacred ritual including deep tuning of the environment, protection of the sensory system and conscious slowing down [#1658].
4. Spiritual biohacking. A drive to combine ancient rituals with modern neurobiological knowledge. The author calls it «рискованная, но интересная зона» (a risky but interesting zone), balancing between genuine depth and superficial consumption; but the vector itself shows that "we are seeking contact with something greater" [#1659].
5. Nature as teacher, not resort. 2026 will sound like a time of eco-somatic wisdom: nature is perceived not as a place for escape and rest but as a source of knowledge — of silence, spontaneity and deep understanding of cyclicity: accumulation, decline, winter and renewal. The forest becomes a metaphor of resilience, mountains a school of boundaries, and seasons remind us that linear growth is only an illusion [#1660].

Interpretation ("Not fashion but a symptom of fatigue"): the author sees in these shifts not a passing fashion but a deep symptom of fatigue from artificial continuity and constant acceleration. We begin again to address the body with fundamental questions: "Where am I?", "How am I made?", "What is truly important and possible now?" [#1661].

Conclusion ("The future of wellness: a new literacy"): the future of wellness lies not in inventing new methods but in developing «новой грамотности - способности читать сигналы своего тела и мира» (a new literacy — the ability to read the signals of one's body and of the world). We learn to understand our nervous system much better and begin to look for something beyond success and consumption [#1662].

He describes changes that have already happened in the lives of wealthy people in first-world countries, which, "as usual", later spread wherever the trend can be copied more cheaply [#839].

- In just the last five years, new-wave wellness attributes became absolutely habitual in luxury hotels and homes: meditation gardens, Wim Hof-style ice plunge pools, in-house spas with traditional Eastern procedures, breathing-practice classes [#839].
- To stand out, more buildings raise the bar by offering a spiritual concierge, whose job is to connect residents or guests with local experts in bodily healing, mental health and various spiritual practices. Example: the concierge of the Maverick Chelsea hotel in Manhattan will come to a guest and recommend the best Reiki healers, meditation and yoga teachers, and advise where to fly for a weekend ayahuasca ceremony [#839].
- Further still: at the luxury residential complex One&Only Mandarina Private Homes in Mexico a spiritual assistant works for residents — a shaman performs a traditional Mexican staff ceremony for buyers to bless their new homes [#839].

#### Silicon Valley wellness as the operating system of body and mind
From the author's guidebook article for the outdoor project *Дикие* (August 2025), based on qualitative analysis of sources, his own feel for social processes from inside the location, and interviews with about 10 Valley entrepreneurs. Its cover quote: «В будущем выживут не те, кто больше работает, а те, кто умеет восстанавливаться и быть в контакте с собой» (In the future, it is not those who work more who will survive, but those who know how to recover and be in contact with themselves) — Max Rodin [#1487].

- The Valley as infrastructure. The author spent much time in San Francisco and the Valley talking with entrepreneurs, engineers and big-tech employees. Silicon Valley is not a fairy-tale place where success follows an idea, but an ecosystem and dense reality built to maximise chances of a breakthrough. You can draw a lucky ticket there, but less and less is it luck and increasingly how embedded you are in the system; non-random people embedded in the infrastructure win. Each year the share of prestigious US university graduates among Y Combinator participants grows; it is harder to compete with someone who has trained to do business since childhood and who feels better — "this is an important point" [#1488].
- Wellness as career trajectory. Infrastructure is increasingly built not only around pitches and presentations but around state, physical and mental. Wellness thus becomes part of the career trajectory: if you want to launch a product, move in high stakes, not burn out and not lose creativity — learn to recover. In this sense wellness is not a spa but «операционная система тела» (the operating system of body [and mind]) [#1488].
- Health and energy as part of the work process. In the Valley wellness is not a bonus or a pleasant addition to an office with falafel and a massage chair, but part of strategy. You won't hear "didn't sleep — have a coffee"; you'll be advised to measure heart-rate variability, build a recovery cycle after Zoom, and pick a breathing protocol for the evening. Wearable trackers, special morning light, ice dousing and breathing practices are no longer just fashion but increasingly basic hygiene — because when you must make complex decisions under uncertainty, «ресурс организма становится главным активом» (the body's resource becomes the main asset) [#1489].
- Body, technology and therapy merge. Boundaries between body, psyche and technology blur; more people use neurofeedback, breathing trackers and VR meditations to learn to manage their state (the $10k chair is the example given above). Another shift: AI is increasingly used as an assistant in self-regulation and therapy — people record audio notes, discuss their state with bots, and get psychologist-style support 24/7, especially where classic therapy is expensive and time-consuming. In the US, not using AI in personal inner work already looks "a bit archaic"; according to HBR data cited by the author, coaching and therapy are one of the main real-life AI use cases in the States [#1490].
- The Huberman catechism. Globally, Valley wellness discourse forms around a materialist neurophysiological model promoted by figures such as Andrew Huberman — Stanford professor, neuroscientist and in effect the flagship of a new philosophy of managing state through bodily markers. His recommendations — morning light, cold shower, breathing, proteins, creatine, giving up cheap dopamine — «стали своеобразным катехизисом местной эффективности» (have become a peculiar catechism of local efficiency) [#1490, #1491].

#### Critique of capitalist reductionism of self-work
Continuation of the guidebook article — where the "space of critique" begins [#1492, #1493, #1494]:

- Reductionism. Biohacking or neurophysiological models are often not systemic or holistic but reductionist. Example: we pull out one study on melatonin levels, build a routine around it and start living by protocol, but lose a holistic view of health as a system in which not only the cold mattress matters equally but also attachment, stable connections, trauma-informedness and access to resources [#1492].
- "First level" first — the Palo Alto entrepreneur. Many sense this. Asked about trends, a Palo Alto entrepreneur told the author: «Я, честно говоря,» (honestly) he doesn't know what Thiel and Huberman are doing there, but as long as you don't have normal sleep, food and regular training, everything else is meaningless Most of his friends should sort out this first level rather than run to cryochambers and transfuse plasma [#1492].
- The flip side of prioritising state. The author considers the trend of putting one's state first — and not treating it as weakness — rather positive, but notes its reverse side: even inner work (therapy, meditation, wellness procedures) increasingly becomes part of the same logic of productivity and upgrade that turns a wondrous human life into a machine for creating value. This is very capitalist: as philosopher Byung-Chul Han described, the person increasingly internalises the external demands of success and becomes «надсмотрщиком в постоянном движении» (an overseer in constant movement) of himself, moving constantly forward [#1492].
- Spiritual materialism. A person runs in an endless circle of needing to be productive, positive and competitive: we meditate not to hear ourselves but to concentrate better; we work with emotions not to live through them but so they "don't get in the way" of grinding ("фигачить"). Chögyam Trungpa, a famous spiritual teacher who lived many years in the US, called this spiritual materialism: using transformation tools not to overcome the limits of a self-centred view of the world but to make it more successful. In this we lose the very essence of the path to deep wellbeing and happiness, because real inner work can not just improve one's state but pull one onto entirely different levels of existence [#1493].
- The alternative stance. For that, we don't run from our state wanting to fix it with psychotherapy, meditation or a cryochamber, but learn to coexist and interact with it — thereby as if transcending the strategies and capacities of an ordinary person [#1493].
- Wellness as performance zone. «Когда мы делаем из велнеса ещё одну зону перформанса, мы рискуем потерять его главное достижение» (When we turn wellness into yet another performance zone, we risk losing its main achievement) — the ability to restore connection with oneself, with reality, with something greater than our material goals [#1493, #1494].
- Burnout society. Byung-Chul Han calls a society where such a capitalist approach has spread not a society of prosperity and success but a society of fatigue: behind this "free" choice to keep constantly moving forward stands deep exhaustion, which results in the main diseases of modernity — burnout, depression, cancer [#1493].

#### Live data as a long-term trend
The author calls live data an objective and big trend for years ahead. Besides wearables, the programme covers glucometers and sweat and saliva sensors. Data and analysis become more accurate every year; though they cannot replace medical tests, many doctors make the mistake of ignoring new data. He states it is proven that Whoop shows near-laboratory accuracy in measuring HRV while being available 24/7 — "these are no longer the devices we saw just a couple of years ago". Live data are becoming an important factor of predictive personalised medicine: «Чем раньше и чем больше мы будем знать, тем лучше и дольше проживем» (The earlier and the more we know, the better and longer we will live). But technologies require reflection and a conscious attitude; one must analyse not only studies and theses but develop recommendations for handling data skilfully [#1637].

Author's personal verdict: he keeps updating and wearing Whoop "without attaching too much importance to it", and does not drop it impulsively because of yet another study that superficial blogger reviews do not consider systemically [#1637].

#### From bars to social wellness clubs
Studies the author cites show that only 54% of adult Americans drink alcohol at all — the lowest in almost 90 years of observation; two years earlier it was 62%. The bar is therefore no longer the place where business happens. Asking "if not the bar, then where?", the author says the US market answers: in social wellness clubs. Business swaps the cocktail bar for an adaptogen bar and the meeting room for an infrared sauna [#1955].

Examples given [#1955]:
- Love.Life (Los Angeles) — opened by John Mackey, founder of Whole Foods: functional medicine, more than 25 diagnostic tests, hyperbaric chambers (барокамеры), courts. Membership ranges from $400 a month to $28,500 a year.
- Santa Monica Proper hotel — built a club directly into the hotel. For $6,500 a year one can lie in a $160,000 recovery capsule, the same model used in the Boston Red Sox training facilities and at Wimbledon's recovery centre. The author's ironic aside: now one can lie down in it between a meeting and dinner, on an ordinary Tuesday.

Market size: the Global Wellness Institute, as cited, values wellness real estate at $876 billion, forecast to reach $1.8 trillion by 2030. The author stresses that what is interesting here is not the luxury but the shift in culture and logic [#1955]. This updates the existing file's 2023 thesis on luxury wellness (spiritual concierge, trends starting in luxury and becoming mass) with 2026 figures and a new emphasis: the driver is now performance logic rather than spiritual wellbeing.

#### The author's two reactions
1. For: recovery as managerial hygiene. For many years the author has observed that a leader in an over-revved ("разогнанном") state makes bad decisions, because a nervous system in survival mode narrows thinking. So recovery in the calendar is not only self-care but «важная управленческая гигиена» (important managerial hygiene) [#1955]. This echoes the Silicon Valley thesis in the existing file that the body's resource becomes the main asset under uncertainty.
2. Against: rest becomes another optimisation target. When rest becomes a KPI, it too starts being optimised: chasing a Recovery score above 90%, getting angry at a sagging HRV, adding to work pressure the further pressure to be recovered. An increasing number of people now go through this: «просыпаешься и первым делом смотришь в цифру, а не в себя» (you wake up and first look at the number, not into yourself) [#1955]. This extends the existing wearables harms (orthosomnia, fixation on numbers, loss of inner intuition) and rule 2 of the six tracker rules (assess subjective state before looking at data) to the social level of founder culture.

#### Verdict: a dubious culture of happiness
The author calls all this «весьма сомнительная культура счастья» (a highly dubious culture of happiness) — a reflection of the functional materialist logic that has saturated modern business, in which we long ago stopped seeing people in employees and are now ceasing to see them in ourselves [#1955]. This continues the existing critique of capitalist reductionism (wellness as a performance zone, Byung-Chul Han's self-overseer, spiritual materialism) with a fresh 2026 case; it does not contradict it.
