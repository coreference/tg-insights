## Supplements: benefits and harms

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's evidence reviews and cautionary tales about dietary supplements: Katya Strelnikova's 48-day NMN self-experiment with sleep-tracker screenshots, Marina Sidorich's reviews of vitamin D and collagen, a forwarded story of a stroke linked to high-dose omega-3, a chat answer debunking the fullerene C60 hype, and Katya's three-part series "Как навредить себе витаминами. И почему не надо слушать специалистов в интернетах" (How to harm yourself with vitamins, and why you should not listen to specialists on the internet). According to the source, it matters because supplements are active substances, not "just vitamins": they can overdose, interact, and be pushed by incompetent experts, by deepfakes of competent doctors, and by AI chatbots manipulated by manufacturers [#617, #635, #650].

Every supplement post carries the channel's disclaimer: "Посты в этом канале не являются рекомендацией к использованию добавок" (Posts in this channel are not a recommendation to use supplements) and a doctor must be consulted [#31, #34].

### Core ideas

- Supplements are active substances that can harm. Fat-soluble vitamins accumulate, some water-soluble ones and minerals cause damage at long high doses, and supplements interact with each other and with drugs (full list in *Vitamins and minerals that can harm*) [#617].
- "Practically no side effects" claims can be wrong. Katya's NMN experiment gave real energy but also insomnia, a rash and a depressive episode, which resolved after stopping [#31, #32, #33].
- Fashionable supplements often lack human evidence. NMN/NAD+, berberine, adaptogens and C60 are cited as hype with thin or animal-only data [#617, #637].
- "Pills feel like medicine, supplements feel like self-care" is a dangerous illusion. Anything that really changes the body can change it in the wrong direction too (omega-3 story) [#636].
- Vitamin D is the one supplement the channel's co-author endorses — at the minimal recommended dose or an individual dose after a doctor and a test — while warning of overdose [#34, #617].
- Collagen's benefit is not proven by independent research, and it will not work without a healthy lifestyle [#99].
- Online advice is unreliable at three levels: incompetent experts, deepfaked competent doctors, and AI chatbots that can be steered by hidden manufacturer instructions [#635, #650].
- Lifestyle first, supplements last. "Костыли и примочки систему не заменят" (crutches and gimmicks will not replace the system) [#650].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Diary timeline method for linking a supplement to side effects
Purpose: to find out whether scattered symptoms are caused by a new supplement, rather than dismissing them as coincidences. Katya did not connect her symptoms to each other or to NMN at first; the connection became obvious only when she looked at her diary and drew a timeline: "когда посмотрела в дневник и нарисовала таймлайн, все встало на места" (when I looked at my diary and drew a timeline, everything fell into place) [#31].
1. Keep a diary while taking anything new — she recorded the dates when sleep problems and the rash started [#31].
2. Lay the events on a timeline. Her timeline: 19 November — started NMN; 28 November — first noted sleep problems; 28 November — also first noted the rash on her legs; 5 January — stopped NMN; 9 January — for the first time in 1.5 months the Sleep++ app showed a sleep score of 100 [#31].
3. Compare the symptoms with the known side-effect profile of the substance's class. She stopped trusting the claims that the product "практически нет побочных эффектов" (has practically no side effects) and that it is not directly related to B vitamins although it is a B3 derivative, and checked the side effects of B vitamins: sleep disturbance, heightened excitability, skin lesions — exactly her three symptoms. "Круг замкнулся" (the circle closed) [#31].
4. Stop the substance and check whether the symptoms go away, using objective data where possible (sleep tracker, see *Evidence and numbers*) [#31, #32, #33].

#### Safe self-testing rule
When to use: "Если уж очень хочется попробовать что-то самостоятельно" (if you really want to try something on your own) [#617].
1. Only one new product at a time — so that you can tell what affected you [#617].
2. Observe yourself for at least a week — how you sleep, how you feel, whether there are any unusual reactions [#617].
Caveat from the author: this helps understand what affected you, but some consequences, such as vessel calcification from vitamin D overdose, accumulate silently and without symptoms. "Поэтому лучший наблюдатель здесь все равно врач и анализы, а не самочувствие" (so the best observer here is still the doctor and lab tests, not how you feel) [#617].

#### Decision path before taking supplements ("Как действовать")
Katya's three-question chain at the end of the series [#650]:
1. Is something bothering me? Examples: fatigue, hair loss, poor sleep, low energy [#650].
2. Do I understand the causes? She stresses that fatigue, hair loss, poor sleep and low energy are symptoms, not causes ("симптомы, а не причины") — a supplement aimed at a symptom skips finding the cause [#650].
3. How long ago was I at the doctor and/or did I take lab tests? The path ends in a real specialist and tests, not in self-prescription [#650].
Overarching rule: first sort out lifestyle — sleep, food, movement, stress. "Добавки — это именно «добавки». К тому, что уже работает." (Supplements are exactly "additions". To what already works.) [#650].

#### Three takeaways of the "How to harm yourself with vitamins" series
Katya's own summary of all three parts [#650]:
1. Vitamins can harm. At best they turn into "дорогостоящую мочу" (expensive urine). Detail in *Vitamins and minerals that can harm* [#650, #617].
2. Doctors who recommend supplements to everyone indiscriminately raise doubts about their competence. Worse, competent doctors may not know they are promoting something — deepfakes can no longer be told apart [#650, #635].
3. Consulting AI about health is possible, but with nuances. Even if you phrase the request correctly and interpret the chatbot's advice correctly, you may get unregulated advertising of drugs and supplements (see *Indirect prompt injection*) [#650].
Conclusion: do not listen to people from the internet and chatbots; observe yourself, use your head, and involve real specialists (doctors) [#650].

#### How a supplement hype is born (C60 pattern)
Katya calls fullerene C60 "классическая история, как рождается хайп вокруг добавки" (a classic story of how hype around a supplement is born) [#637]:
1. One study, not in humans — in rats, and only 6 rats [#637].
2. An accidental result — it showed a "doubling of life", although the scientists were not studying that at all [#637].
3. An article comes out, biohackers pick it up, then marketers [#637].
4. Mass adoption — "И вот уже мамы пьют масло с молекулой из космической химии" (and now moms are drinking oil with a molecule from space chemistry) [#637].
5. What is missing: no clinical trials in humans, no long-term safety data [#637].
Her verdict: the molecule itself is interesting, but far more research is needed before deciding to take it ("сейчас ничего пока непонятно" — right now nothing is clear) — unless you want to be that very test subject experimenting on yourself. Her general opinion on supplements: "не стоит в себя ничего пихать личшнего" [sic] (you shouldn't stuff anything extra into yourself) [#637].

#### How indirect prompt injection makes AI recommend a product
Definition: a supplement manufacturer embeds invisible instructions in its website — text a human does not see but AI does [#650]. Mechanism, step by step:
1. You ask a chatbot a question about a supplement, and the bot goes to search the internet [#650].
2. It makes a search query, gets a list of sites, reads their content and forms an answer [#650].
3. If the manufacturer's site is in the results — which can be ensured through ordinary SEO promotion, i.e. literally bought — the AI reads the page together with the hidden instructions [#650].
4. The AI starts recommending that particular product; "одному богу известно, насколько это полезно именно вам" (God only knows how useful it is for you specifically) [#650].
Evidence: a 2025 JAMA Network Open study the author cites simulated hundreds of AI dialogues, some with hidden manufacturer instructions; in 94% of cases the AI changed its recommendations, and sometimes recommended harmful drugs (for example, recommending to pregnant women drugs that are prohibited in pregnancy). The scariest part, per Katya, is that this is neither regulated nor labelled in any way [#650].

### Detailed insights

#### NMN: what it is and why people take it
NMN (nicotinamide mononucleotide) belongs to the vitamin B3 group, but strictly it is a "derivative" of the vitamin rather than the vitamin itself — a point often made when side effects are discussed, as if it were "не одно и тоже" (not the same thing). The molecule is a direct precursor of NAD+, which plays an important role in energy production. From age 30 the amount of NAD+ in cells steadily declines, so taking NMN is meant to raise NAD+ levels again [#31].

Katya describes herself as "slightly crazy" about finding energy and a lover of self-experiments. Having already introduced basic energy practices, she noticed NMN because Andrew Huberman ("всеми любимый Хуберман", everyone's favourite Huberman) and many respected doctors regularly promote and take it. Her superficial internet research found nothing alarming — even that the supplement has practically no side effects — so she bought it [#31].

#### Katya's NMN protocol
- Duration: 48 days, 19 November to 5 January [#31].
- Dose: followed the package recommendations ("usually quite safe"): first 10 days 2 × 0.5 g, then 1 × 0.5 g per day (she reduced it because of the side effects described below) [#31].
- Form and timing: powder under the tongue, after waking and a morning glass of water, then sitting down to meditate [#31].
- Product choice: there are many fakes and low-quality products; she chose one with good reviews, plus "дядя Андрей Хуберман" (uncle Andrew Huberman) said it was fine [#31].

#### NMN effects: energy, yes
Short answer to "did NMN give energy": yes. She felt it immediately — the sensation was like the film shot of a dilating pupil; later she found Reddit comparisons with amphetamine. During meditation she felt her brain and body come alive. She adds that energetic people do not jump out of bed either — she is still sleepy at that point [#31].

Through the day she felt increased energy, more strength and higher productivity — though in hindsight she would rather call it "возбужденностью" (agitation/over-arousal). She seemed to need less time to sleep: she fell asleep later (or did not sleep at all) and still woke without an alarm as usual [#31].

#### NMN side effects that "don't exist"
Three changes occurred while she was taking it, which she did not immediately link to each other or to NMN [#31]:
1. Sleep problems and general over-excitability of the nervous system.
2. A depressive episode.
3. Pimples / a rash on her legs that she had never had before (her husband affectionately called it "пузырями пошла", "broke out in bubbles").
They matched the B-vitamin side-effect list (sleep disturbance, increased excitability, skin lesions), and her timeline showed sleep problems and rash both starting nine days after she began; she stopped NMN. "Совпадение? Не думаю." (Coincidence? I don't think so.) [#31].

Will she take it again? She is not sure she needs it permanently at this stage of life; she thinks she may return to the idea after 60, since judging by reviews it is effective then — but she would first have to find her own dosage [#31]. Later, in the vitamins series, she reframes NMN as a fashionable supplement without an evidence base (see *Fashionable supplements*) and recalls that it "broke" her sleep [#617].

#### Vitamin D: the case for (Marina Sidorich)
Vitamin D is the only supplement recommended in the official dietary guidelines of many countries. Nearly 1 billion people on Earth are deficient — by Marina's estimate, every 8th subscriber [#34].

What is known: paediatricians recommend it against rickets; it has long been known to help absorb calcium and phosphorus needed for bone formation. Its action is broader: anti-inflammatory, antioxidant and neuroprotective properties that support immunity, muscle function and brain activity. Its role in increasing insulin sensitivity and preventing cancer cell growth is being researched [#34].

Who is deficient: deficiency is very common among people in northern latitudes, but not only — SPF cosmetics and clothing also block vitamin D production ("block the penetration of vitamin D", as written), and some liver, kidney and intestinal diseases can be predictors of deficiency [#34].

Atypical symptoms of deficiency: fatigue, muscle weakness, pain or cramps, depression [#34].

Doses: the most common daily recommendations are 400 IU for children under 12 months, 600 IU for ages 1–70, 800 IU for people over 70. Not all experts agree; many organisations recommend up to 2000 IU for adults and 1000 IU for children. A doctor can recommend the dose based on individual tests [#34].

Overdose risk: there is a risk of "передознуться" (overdosing), so consult a doctor and ideally test your vitamin D level before taking it. Excess and high doses can lead to calcium stones [#34].

Marina's personal experience: although she spent a year under the sun of Spain and Thailand, her test showed a severe deficiency — so chronic that it had already affected her TSH (ТТГ) hormone production. At the time of writing she had been taking a supplement for 4 months, awaiting the next test; she clearly had more energy and better mood, but she notes everything works as a complex because she also eats well, sleeps well and does various sports [#34].

Verdict: "Конечно, стоит" (of course it is worth it) — at the minimal recommended dose or an individual dose after consulting your doctor [#34].

#### Vitamin D: the harm side (Katya)
Katya calls vitamin D the most popular and advertised supplement; it is indeed often lacking (though, she notes, hers is fine). Overdose causes hypercalcemia: calcium is washed out of the bones and deposits in the kidneys and blood vessels. She personally knows a woman who was treated for kidney stones after high doses of vitamin D prescribed by a nutritionist. Vessel calcification from overdose accumulates silently, without symptoms, which is why tests and a doctor beat self-observation [#617].

#### Collagen: magic or myth (Marina Sidorich)
Collagen is a vital component of skin, bones, muscles and tendons; without enough of it these lose elasticity and firmness. With age, collagen production naturally falls by about 1% per year on average, and with menopause a woman loses up to 30% of skin collagen within 5 years [#99].

Evidence: the benefit of taking collagen is not yet proven by independent research. Some randomised controlled trials showed improved skin elasticity, better mobility or less joint pain, but most if not all collagen studies are fully or partly funded by industries that benefit from positive results — so it is hard to say how effective supplemental collagen really is [#99].

When collagen supplements will not work: collagen is destroyed by excessive alcohol, smoking, lack of sleep, physical inactivity and nutrient deficiency (protein, vitamin C, copper, zinc). Under these conditions there will probably be no skin effect from supplements [#99].

Habits that protect natural collagen, besides a healthy balanced diet [#99]:
- wear sunscreen or limit time in direct sun;
- do not smoke and avoid second-hand smoke;
- work with stress — constantly high cortisol can reduce collagen production;
- move and exercise to stimulate circulation and strengthen tissues.
Marina's rule: "ни одна добавка без ЗОЖ не работает" (no supplement works without a healthy lifestyle) [#99].

#### Vitamins and minerals that can harm (series part 1)
Katya opens by saying she is enraged by content like "best pharmacy products for summer", "vitamins every woman must take", "top supplements for 100 roubles that will change your life" — from nutritionists, and especially from doctors who took the Hippocratic oath — and argues it is worth scrolling past [#617]. Her list:
1. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) accumulate in the body — roughly, you can overdose on them [#617].
2. Vitamin D — overdose causes hypercalcemia (see *Vitamin D: the harm side*) [#617].
3. Vitamin A accumulates in the liver and can damage it; especially dangerous during pregnancy [#617].
4. Vitamin E in large doses reduces blood clotting and can lead to bleeding, including haemorrhagic stroke [#617].
5. Iron — "perhaps the most underestimated supplement by risk". People like to take it "for energy" without ferritin tests; a separate story is deciding on our own to raise ferritin within the normal range because bloggers said it should be higher. Excess accumulates in tissues and can lead to liver cirrhosis, diabetes, cardiomyopathy [#617].
6. Vitamin B6 — an unexpected guest: water-soluble, so supposedly excreted, but long-term high doses cause peripheral neuropathy: numbness, tingling, impaired coordination [#617].
7. Zinc — long-term high doses can cause copper deficiency, and hence anaemia, lowered immunity, neurological symptoms [#617].
She stresses: "И это прям далеко не все" (and this is far from everything) [#617].

#### Fashionable supplements without an evidence base
- NMN and NAD+ — one of the loudest anti-ageing and wellness trends. Impressive in mice; in humans only isolated, unfinished studies. Katya states the FDA banned selling NMN as a supplement in the US because it needs to be tested and certified [#617].
- Berberine, called "natural Ozempic" — per Katya, it is therapy, not prevention [#617].
- Adaptogens, from ashwagandha to chaga — studied very unevenly. She personally knows people who "broke themselves" with these supplements [#617].
- Fullerene C60 — see *How a supplement hype is born*. Bryan Johnson, who spends millions on his longevity, does not take C60, which Katya finds telling: for all his obsession with optimisation he filters fairly strictly by evidence [#637].

#### Interactions we don't think about
Katya: we often do not consider how supplements interact with each other, and it matters [#617]:
- Omega-3 and vitamin E thin the blood — combined with anticoagulants this is a risk of bleeding from a minor injury.
- St John's wort (зверобой), the popular "natural antidepressant", reduces the effectiveness of oral contraceptives and some antidepressants. Her husband, while on antidepressants, accidentally drank tea with St John's wort and was badly frightened: he experienced an altered state of consciousness, a racing heart, "и вот это все" (and all that).
- Magnesium interferes with absorption of some antibiotics.
Lesson: when prescribing yourself vitamins from the internet, you can miss very important factors [#617].

#### Omega-3 and the stroke story (forwarded post by Marina Demidova)
Marina Demidova had lunch with Olya Shemyatovskaya (leader of the neurologist and psychiatrist team at DocMed) and discussed, in an evidence-based way, supplements and "капельницы молодости" (youth IV drips). Olya told her horror stories "you won't read in articles"; the one that struck Marina most was a woman of about 30 who took high doses of omega-3 supplements and, as a result, had a stroke [#636].

Mechanism given: omega-3 usually lives in our heads as something useful (for heart, vessels, brain, skin). But high doses of omega-3 show an unpleasant signal in large studies — they may raise the risk of atrial fibrillation. Atrial fibrillation is not just "the heart beating strangely"; it is an arrhythmia in which a clot can form, and the clot can travel to the brain and cause an ischaemic stroke [#636].

The psychological lesson: people fear "pills" but calmly take a handful of supplements, because a pill feels like medicine while a supplement feels like self-care with only good effects. "Но если вещество реально что-то меняет в организме, оно может менять не только в ту сторону, в которую обещает реклама" (but if a substance really changes something in the body, it can change it not only in the direction the advertising promises) — nor only in the direction promised by a person in a white coat whose recommendations follow pharma promoters. Conclusion: "осторожнее с БАДами" (be more careful with supplements) [#636].

#### Deepfake doctors (series part 2)
Katya's point: online doctors are a risk not only because they may be incompetent, but because competent doctors may not even know they are recommending something. She learned this from Olya Titova's lecture on AI and health in the Biosisters community: deepfake doctors are already impossible to tell from real ones, and real doctors — even excellent specialists — simply do not know their image is used in ads. She stresses it is not conspiracy theory: The Guardian wrote about it in December 2025 [#635].

Case: British paediatrician David Taylor-Robinson found himself in 14 TikTok videos advertising menopause supplements with turmeric and Himalayan shilajit. In one, his deepfake seriously described a non-existent menopause symptom called "thermometer leg" (when you get hot at night and need to stick a leg out from under the blanket). The professor has nothing to do with women's health. TikTok at first refused to remove the videos as not violating its rules. "Это новая индустрия" (this is a new industry), not an isolated case [#635].

What frightens her even more than deepfakes: total trust in AI for health questions and recommendations, where there is "полнейший Дикий Запад" (a complete Wild West) and no regulation at all [#635].

#### AI as a health adviser (series part 3)
Increasingly, people go for advice not to a doctor or even social media but to Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini and the like — Katya admits she does too: it is convenient, fast, free, no waiting for an appointment, no discomfort of talking to another person. In that month alone she twice heard from clients about being "deceived by AI" on health; one client got so offended she did not open it for three days (the question involved 3,500 euros) [#650].

Her assessment: the reliability of AI medical advice, especially in women's health, is "пока не очень" (not great yet), even given how fast models improve. And it is not only the AI: the human factor plays a huge role — how we phrase the question, how accurately we interpret the answer, and whether we apply it correctly to our situation. On top of that comes indirect prompt injection (see the framework above) [#650].

### Caveats, disagreements and open questions

- (!) CONTRADICTION (tone): Marina's vitamin D post concludes "Конечно, стоит" (of course it is worth taking), while Katya's later series treats vitamin D primarily as an overdose risk and says her own level is fine; both agree on a doctor and testing first [#34, #617].
- Katya's NMN conclusion rests on a single self-experiment and her diary/tracker; she herself frames it as personal experience and says she may return to NMN after 60, having first found her dosage — while later calling NMN a trend without human evidence [#31, #617].
- Katya's NMN causal reasoning maps NMN onto general B-vitamin side effects, although NMN is a B3 derivative rather than the vitamin itself; she explicitly rejects the "not the same thing" argument [#31].
- (!) UNCLEAR: the screenshots do not label what the two percentages on each card refer to; HRV shows no data; Jan 10 has no score; Dec 31 appears twice with different values [#32, img #33].
- Marina's vitamin D benefit is hedged by herself: more energy and mood "but everything works as a complex" (good food, sleep, sport) [#34].
- Collagen: positive RCTs exist but are mostly industry-funded — a conflict of interest noted by Marina [#99].
- The omega-3 stroke case is second-hand (Olya Shemyatovskaya → Marina Demidova → forwarded by Katya); the "large studies" behind the atrial fibrillation signal are not named [#636].
- The claim that the FDA banned NMN as a supplement is Katya's statement; no further detail is given [#617].
- Katya admits she uses AI chatbots for health questions herself [#650].

## Gadgets and trackers

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's views on wellness technology: a structured review of the Lumen metabolism tracker, a wish-list of wellness gadgets, Katya Strelnikova's personal experience of the "nocebo effect" from wearing an Apple Watch, her rules for when trackers are justified, a forwarded 2x2 scheme matching tracker use to a person's interoception and anxiety, and a list of nine free apps she uses for self-observation [#21, #26, #100, #101, #587, #661]. According to the source it matters because trackers can both help (building a routine, learning body signals) and harm (anxiety, neurosis, a false sense of control, losing contact with the body), and because the wellness market sells devices that cannot replace basic habits [#26, #101, #597, #652].

Part 2 adds two posts. Both apply the channel's tracker skepticism to new devices: the "biological age" metric in Whoop and Oura, and body-composition readings from home smart scales (Xiaomi) [#670, #739]. The existing file already covers the nocebo effect, the 5 situations when trackers are needed, the Six-month rule, the interoception x anxiety scheme and the "trackers as a tool, not an oracle" post. These two posts extend that material in three ways:
- They give a specific mechanism for why the numbers are unreliable. Biological age is calculated from indirect proxies, and body fat is extrapolated from the legs only.
- They show again how such numbers feed anxiety.
- They show again Katya's stance of not letting gadgets decide whether she is "normal".

Why it matters, according to the source: these "diagnoses" are delivered "таким уверенным голосом, с графиками и цветными шкалами" (in such a confident voice, with graphs and coloured scales). For anxious people they become "новая ось, вокруг которой закручивается тревога и недостаточность" (a new axis around which anxiety and a sense of inadequacy spin) [#670, #739].

### Core ideas

- No device "hacks" metabolism. Gadgets can fine-tune a routine for advanced users, but sport, walking, good sleep and healthy food remain the actual work (see *Lumen gadget evaluation*) [#26].
- Trackers can cause a nocebo effect. Negative readings make a healthy person feel unwell: "see stress in the app — feel stress from what I saw — I'm stressed" (see *The Apple Watch nocebo story*) [#100].
- Long tracking without a purpose leads to neurosis. More than six months of tracking without a current change to measure is, per Katya, a direct road to neurosis (see *Six-month rule*) [#101].
- A tracker is only a tool with a specific purpose. It is needed in particular life phases (see *5 situations when trackers are needed*), and must not be given the authority of an oracle [#101, #652].
- Who benefits depends on interoception and anxiety. People who sense body signals poorly and are calm get the most benefit; anxious people risk dependence or self-doubt (see *Interoception x anxiety scheme*) [#661].
- The most effective tools are free. Katya's stance on biohacking and fashionable gadgets: the basics (sleep, nutrition, movement, recovery) and free apps are enough [#587, #597].

- Biological age in Whoop and Oura is marketing wrapped in science. The number comes from indirect indicators, not from clinical methods. See *Biological age in Whoop and Oura* [#670].
- For anxious people a biological-age number causes neurosis, not motivation. Only people who are fine with anxiety and with contact with their body can use it as a normal stimulus to change something [#670].
- Scales know for certain only your weight; everything else is a guess. Fat, muscle, bone and water come from a closed manufacturer formula applied to a measurement of the legs. See *How smart scales estimate body composition* [#739].
- Smart-scale fat readings are systematically fragile. The formula is built for an "average" woman, and fat distribution, age, time of day, coffee, training, cycle phase and foot condition all shift the reading. See *4 reasons a smart-scale reading goes wrong* [#739].
- Do not let a gadget decide whether you are normal. Katya's conclusion from both posts: "Я больше не разрешаю гаджетам решать, нормальная ли я." (I no longer allow gadgets to decide whether I am normal.) [#739].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Lumen gadget evaluation (Marina Sidorich's review)
Purpose: a review done at subscribers' request (they asked for it in comments under the wish-list post) of Lumen, a popular wellness gadget whose marketing promises to "hack" metabolism, increase fat burning, help lose weight and raise energy. Marina notes the marketing team did its job — after viewing the website you already want to order it [#26]. The review is structured in seven parts:
1. Essence of the device. Everyone wants to know how to burn calories more efficiently, build muscle, sleep better and be energetic. The tracker's key task is to assess the body's individual ability to switch between two kinds of fuel — fats and carbohydrates. This is called metabolic flexibility ("метаболическая гибкость"). The review states that the more effective it is, the higher insulin sensitivity, the better the state of the mitochondria, and therefore overall health markers [#26].
2. How it works. Lumen determines whether you are burning fats or carbohydrates at the moment by measuring the CO2 level in your breath. Based on 2 weeks of data collection, it gives recommendations on what can be adjusted in nutrition and training to make metabolism more efficient. Previously such tests could only be done in a clinic; now there is a smart gadget [#26].
3. Who it is for. Marina's conclusion after studying the question: definitely for advanced healthy-lifestyle users who have already implemented general principles of nutrition and sleep and are ready to observe their metabolism in more detail. "Прибор вас «не похудеет»" (the device won't "make you lose weight"), but it can help find individual insights. It may be very useful for marathon runners and athletes, to replenish carbohydrate stores in time and recover faster [#26].
4. Contraindications (as listed on the manufacturer's site): diabetes; asthma; COPD (ХОБЛ); pregnancy; thyroid diseases; kidney disease; cancer [#26].
5. Pros mentioned in online reviews:
   - the ability to observe and analyse your routine — how nutrition, sleep and training affect your individual metabolism;
   - a treasure trove of information and advice on how to increase energy levels;
   - everyone mentions a great and extensive collection of recipes [#26].
6. Cons:
   - there are no independent clinical studies;
   - it can lead to disordered eating behaviour, fixation on food, or misinterpretation of results [#26].
7. Personal verdict. No device will "hack" metabolism or make us lose weight — only our conscious behaviour will. With or without Lumen you still need regular exercise, walks, good sleep and healthy food. But it is nice to have the option to fine-tune your routine and training to yourself even more precisely [#26].
Side note: the Lumen website has a free metabolism test; Marina took it, it "praised her mitochondria", and gave the main reminder that to burn carbohydrates efficiently you need good sleep — 7–9 hours of real sleep [#26].

#### 5 situations when trackers are needed
Purpose: Katya's checklist for deciding whether wearing a tracker is justified, written as the follow-up to her nocebo post [#101]:
1. When we are restructuring our routine ("Когда перестраиваем режим") — e.g. in her own case, changing her daily schedule [#100, #101].
2. When we are instilling a habit of movement — tracking the amount of movement until it becomes habitual (she now knows she walks no less than 15,000 steps without checking) [#100, #101].
3. When we are learning to recognise the body's signals of fatigue [#101].
4. Or the sensations in different cardio zones at the initial stage of cardio training — she used it while learning to control cardio zones; now she knows the sensations [#100, #101].
5. On the recommendation of a specialist or doctor [#101].
Katya's own past reasons that fit these: surfing (tracking waves), changing her routine, learning to control cardio zones, monitoring movement. Once these were no longer relevant — she moved away from the waves, wakes up without an alarm, knows her step count and cardio-zone sensations, feels when she has slept enough and understands at body level when she needs to replenish energy — she concluded "Теперь можно не трекать, а жить." (Now you can stop tracking and just live.) [#100].

#### Six-month rule
Katya's rule: "Трекинг показателей больше шести месяцев и без наличия текущих изменений, которые нужно замерять, — прямая дорога к неврозу." (Tracking metrics for more than six months, without any current changes that need measuring, is a direct road to neurosis.) — and therefore to a worsening of one's state [#101]. Two further mechanisms she gives:
- Measurements interfere with real contact with the body, however tempting it is to rationalise sensations through apps and the "knowledge" of what you feel [#101].
- They give a fictional sense of control — hence the despair when something goes "not according to plan". "(Wake up, Neo. У тела нет никаких планов)" (Wake up, Neo. The body has no plans.) [#101].

#### Interoception x anxiety 2x2 scheme (forwarded from Daily Reminder)
Purpose: a self-described "(псевднонаучная)" [sic] (pseudo-scientific) scheme that grew out of several weeks of posts about trackers, to show for whom trackers help and for whom they harm [#661].
Axis 1 — interoception: people perceive body signals differently — hunger, fatigue, even heart rate. One interoception test asks you to compare an external rhythm with your heartbeat: how synchronous are they? The hypothesis: perhaps for people who sense their bodies poorly, trackers are an ideal support for interoception [#661].
Axis 2 — neuroticism (anxiety): some people are simply more anxious than others, and sensor data can strongly reinforce that anxiety. Doctors, the post says, advise anxious people who complain about sleep to take off their sleep trackers, to remove an extra reason for worry [#661].
The four quadrants from the image (read from the image itself; the OCR text is scrambled) [#661]:
1. High anxiety + poorly sense body signals: *Трекер подменяет ощущения; риск зависимости* (the tracker replaces sensations; risk of dependence).
2. High anxiety + sense body signals well: *Начинают сомневаться в собственных ощущениях* (they start doubting their own sensations).
3. Low anxiety + poorly sense body signals: *Получают максимум пользы* (they get the maximum benefit).
4. Low anxiety + sense body signals well: *Быстро понимают, что не узнают ничего нового* (they quickly realise they won't learn anything new).

#### How smart (bioimpedance) scales estimate body composition
Purpose: this is Katya's explanation of why "Весы точно знают только наш вес. Обо всем остальном они гадают." (Scales know for certain only our weight. About everything else they are guessing.) The scales make three steps, and only the first is an actual measurement [#739].
1. They send a current through the legs. When we stand on the scales barefoot, they pass a weak current from one foot to the other. Muscles contain a lot of water and conduct current well, while fat conducts almost none. The harder it is for the current to pass, the more fat the scales suspect [#739].
2. They see only the legs. The current goes from one leg to the other through the pelvic area. The scales do not see the belly, chest or arms at all. Katya calls this the catch: "Прикол в том, что весы оценивают только ноги." (The catch is that the scales assess only the legs.) [#739].
3. They extrapolate with a closed formula, and fat is found by subtraction. The manufacturer's closed formula takes the leg data plus our height, weight, age and sex, and extrapolates how much muscle, bone and water we have. Fat is then simple subtraction: our weight minus what the formula calculated. Katya frames this step as a magic trick ("И дальше начинается магия, следите за руками" — and then the magic begins, watch the hands) [#739].
What follows from this mechanism: any error in the estimate of muscle, bone or water ends up in the fat figure automatically [#739].

#### 4 reasons a smart-scale body-fat reading goes wrong
Katya's list "Где еще что-то может пойти не так" (where else something can go wrong) [#739]:
1. The formula is built for an "average" woman. If we have more muscle than average, especially in the upper body that the scales cannot see, the scales will undercount the muscle and record everything undercounted as fat [#739].
2. Fat distribution differs between people. If someone naturally stores fat mostly on the hips, scales that look only at the legs may overestimate fat for the whole body [#739].
3. Age by itself adds fat in the formula. Part of the percentage is drawn simply because "нам не 25" (we are not 25) [#739].
4. Conditions at the moment of weighing. Morning, coffee, a workout, the phase of the menstrual cycle, or dry or cold feet can each lead the scales to decide there is more fat [#739].
A related point: the "ideal weight" in the app is calculated from BMI. BMI knows nothing about muscles or bones, or about how we feel ("я — супер", I feel great) [#739].

#### Two-scales consistency check (Katya's self-experiment)
Katya calls this "деталь, которая окончательно убеждает в газлайтинге" (the detail that finally proves the gaslighting) [#739].
- A year earlier, different scales in the USA showed her 5 kg less fat at the same body weight [#739].
- If she had really gained 5 kg of fat in a year, that would be about 5.5 litres of fat. In her words, that is five and a half cartons of milk spread around her body, and it would be impossible not to notice [#739].
- Her body is the same. Only the scales and their formula changed [#739].
- The lesson: when two devices disagree this much while the body visibly has not changed, the disagreement shows that the formula is the problem, not the body [#739].

### Detailed insights

#### The Apple Watch nocebo story
Katya put on an Apple Watch a couple of years before the post, attracted by listening to podcasts without a phone and tracking waves (surfing). Gradually she became a person whose day starts with the Sleep++ app and continues with Welltory — "how else would I know whether I slept well and how I feel?" [#100].
One morning she saw a "bad" sleep score, then energy and health readings "at zero", and fell into despair and rage at her powerlessness: "Какого хрена я столько делаю, а получаю это?!" (What the hell, I do so much and get this?!). After calming down she realised she felt worse than before the measurements — and this was not the first time [#100]. Her formula: "Вижу стресс в приложении — испытываю стресс от увиденного — я в стрессе." (I see stress in the app — I feel stress from what I saw — I'm stressed.) [#100].
She found she was not alone: everyone has heard of the placebo effect (what we believe in helps), and with the global fitness-tracker market forecast to reach $139 billion by 2028, a conversation about the nocebo effect of gadgets has started. Definition (also on the glossary card): the nocebo effect is when a person feels bad not because they are unwell but because they believe this should make them feel bad; in this context, negative tracker readings cause feeling unwell despite a healthy state [#100, img #100].
Result: two weeks without the Apple Watch — "Полет нормальный." (Flight normal.) [#100].

#### Two years later: trackers as a tool, not an oracle
Following a post by Marina, Katya reminded readers of the nocebo post and said she has for some time worn an Apple Watch again and glances at it, but without her former fanaticism [#652].
She observes a mass abandonment of trackers — it seems "soooooo last season", trend-setters have already given them up; the pendulum has swung the other way, and she predicts Whoop will soon stop being fashionable and we will increasingly hear of people giving up trackers. On one hand this is an example of how trends and marketing work; on the other, a tracker is just a tool. At certain moments in life it is certainly needed, useful, and can help return attention to oneself and one's state. The key is, when putting it on a wrist or finger, to understand what goal we are pursuing — and not to give it the powers of a Pythia (oracle) who knows better than us what we need [#652].
She is surprised how easily we hand over to someone outside the right to decide how we feel and what we need: first scales, then cycle calendars, then steps, then sleep, then HRV. Each new instrument promises we will finally learn the truth about ourselves — as if we did not already have a built-in, ancient, perfectly working sensor inside [#652].
Why is it so hard to trust our own body without an intermediary? A number on a screen seems more reliable than a sensation, although the sensation came first — the body knew about lack of movement, lack of sleep, fatigue, hunger and anxiety before anyone told us. Her conclusion: "Мое будущее не за развитием технологий, а за развитием чувствования и познания себя." (My future lies not in developing technology but in developing sensing and self-knowledge.) [#652].

Katya laughed out loud on a run listening to an episode of the podcast «Никакого правильно» about gadgets for women — "it would be funny if it weren't so sad", because someone still produces these gadgets and someone buys them. The topic of unnecessary technologies is broader; she often writes about wellness independence and gadgets [#597].

She cites an RBC study on quality of life: the trend is toward everyday self-care practices, basic habits (sleep, nutrition, movement, recovery), preventive actions and skepticism toward fashionable technologies (respondents are skeptical of coaches too). She was especially pleased by the finding that people are tired of achievements and self-improvement [#597].

#### The Xiaomi scales story: from self-punishment to laughing out loud
Katya weighed herself on Xiaomi smart scales for the first time in a year. She again received two "(pseudo)discoveries" about herself [#739]:
1. "I am obese." In fact her weight is normal (71–73 kg) and her fat is within the norm. She knows this from the mirror ("ожирением там не пахнет", there is no hint of obesity there) and from a recent bioimpedance test on a professional device. That device showed 24.5% fat, while the scales showed 33%. It also noted that BMI does not reflect the real picture in her case, because she has more muscle than the norm [#739].
2. "I need to lose 10.5 kg." Yet the last time she weighed 61 kg, people cautiously asked whether she was all right [#739].

She calls the scales a "third voice" in a running argument about her body. She had recently written about why she will never be thin. She had also written that people consider her thin while she does not. The scales are the third voice, and in their view she is not just fat but obese [#739].

Katya describes the emotional effect of such numbers and how it has changed for her [#739]:
- In the past, these "diagnoses" would have thrown her off balance for weeks. Her mood would sour instantly. She might immediately go on a diet, and she would walk around for several days feeling something was wrong with her. The scales could plunge her into horror, self-hatred ("самоненависничество" [sic]) and self-torment.
- She explains why: the scales deliver their verdict "таким уверенным голосом, с графиками и цветными шкалами" (in such a confident voice, with graphs and coloured scales).
- Now she simply reacts with astonishment out loud.

This is the nocebo pattern from part 1, applied to scales instead of a sleep score. Katya's lesson is a stance she states directly: "Мое тело нельзя свести к формуле, написанной для женщины, которой не существует." (My body cannot be reduced to a formula written for a woman who does not exist.) [#739].

The post closes by asking readers to share it if scales have ever gaslit them and to send it to a friend who is constantly upset by "вердикта неодушевленного предмета" (the verdict of an inanimate object). It also asks readers what their relationship with scales is like [#739].
