## Psychotherapy: methods, choosing and getting results

### What this covers

This part collects what the deep mind channel (author Max Rodin and guest authors) taught about how psychotherapy and related inner-work practices actually produce change, and how a client can choose, sustain and boost the process. It covers realistic timelines and the "Valley of disappointment", a critique of the evidence-based-therapy hype, concrete ways to make personal therapy more effective, the Hoffman Process intensive, Internal Family Systems (IFS), Nancy McWilliams' personality types, the psychedelic-therapy renaissance with its risks, the support therapists themselves need, and why AI is expected to make live therapy a luxury rather than replace it. According to the source, the stakes are high: people who misjudge timelines or cannot trust a method "continue to stew in their problem", and people without tools of inner work push their pain outward onto others [#256, #792].

This part covers five posts by the channel author (deep mind 2.0): an argument for choosing a therapist who meditates, built on a three-level model of therapeutic depth; a practical guide to Focusing (Eugene Gendlin's felt sense) as a client skill that predicts therapy success; and three critical responses to public attacks on psychotherapy — the book *Bad Therapy*, a viral article claiming IFS is dangerous, and the thesis that therapy culture infantilizes society. According to the author, what matters is the depth at which therapy works, the client's active participation and alliance, and the ethics of how a method is applied — not ideological labels or narrow evidence hierarchies. [#1323, #1343, #1485, #1616, #1674]

Three new posts (July–September 2026) from the author of deep mind 2.0. The first argues that no inner work can rid a person of trauma, explains why the catharsis fantasy is so appealing through three lenses, and offers integration and the contemplative "gaze at flowers" as the alternative. The second is a first-hand, critical review of a family-constellations session: what was done well, why the "field" mechanism is doubtful, what the evidence and harm data say, and which methods give the same benefits more safely. The third describes the author and his partner starting couples therapy: how they found a fitting therapist, what qualities mattered, and why couples therapy is worth it (Gottman's numbers). [#1862, #1907, #1921]

How this extends the existing topic file: it adds the author's position on trauma and catharsis (new), a full method critique of constellations (new; it applies the same evidence-and-ethics lens the earlier file used in the "IFS is dangerous" and evidence-based-thinking sections, but this time the author is the critic), and the first material on couples therapy. ### Core ideas

- Change is cumulative and slow; linear expectations make people quit. Ethical development practices work only through accumulation; expecting results after three sessions throws people into the "Valley of disappointment". Therapy needs roughly 4–6 months at once or twice a week to start changing life, and deep work usually years (see *Valley of disappointment* and *Three ways to boost personal psychotherapy*) [#256, #835].
- No method is proven best for a particular person. The biggest factor of effectiveness is the quality of contact with the therapist, not the method; on average all therapies are about equally effective; CBT's spread in the USA is driven by cost-cutting (see *Seven reasons*) [#492].
- Therapy is a collaboration, not a purchase. Results depend first of all on what the client brings to and takes from sessions, and on work done between them [#835].
- Intensives complement long-term therapy, not replace it. The Hoffman Process compresses a whole personal therapy into 8 days and is valuable on top of analysis, or as the only accessible option for people who will never go into long therapy [#546].
- Ethics is the central criterion when choosing any group or practice that intervenes in psyche and body [#546].
- Sensitivity is our natural state; burdens hide it. Drawing on IFS, the author argues that behind burdened parts there is always sensitivity identical to love and compassion, and that violence at every scale comes from burdens of past trauma [#813].
- Personality typology with depth (McWilliams) beats superficial typologies such as Human Design or MBTI-like systems, because it looks at underlying dynamics, motivations and defences [#1029].
- Psychedelic-assisted therapy is a coming revolution in psychiatry — but only safe in reliable settings with qualified, trauma-informed facilitators [#666, #906, #1239].
- Healing happens through co-regulation with a live person, which AI cannot give; AI will take over basic algorithmic inner work, making live therapy a luxury [#1297].

- A meditating therapist can help more. The author holds that meditation is potentially the deepest method of development, and that its value is hard to grasp until the therapist is a serious practitioner; a therapist who can truly be present can point the way to deep liberation — provided they know it themselves. See *Three levels of depth in therapy*. [#1323]
- Neurosis is avoidance of being. Anxiety, narcissism and control are often attempts to avoid meeting the fundamental openness of life; therapy that only works on content may relieve symptoms but preserve the split between a small defensive identity and being itself. [#1323]
- The client's capacity for felt-sense contact predicts success more than the method. Per research the author cites on Focusing, clients who can contact and unfold their felt sense progress much faster; the skill is not innate and can be trained. [#1343]
- Real inner work is a dojo for facing what frightens us most, going beyond "being resourced". [#1343]
- Critiques of therapy often mix valid questions with ideology and contempt. The author treats *Bad Therapy* and its popularizers as presenting contested claims as facts. [#1485]
- Any method is risky without proper ethics, and the risks grow as a method is popularized; IFS is "neither evil nor a magic pill". [#1616]
- Evidence-based thinking can turn into a dangerous religion when it refuses to consider practitioners' and clients' experience. [#1616]
- Therapy is on average effective, but the mass market sells relief instead of change; infantilization begins not in the therapist's office but in society's loss of forms of growing up. [#1674]

- Therapy does not remove trauma; it integrates it. Removal is not part of the design — "not a bug but a feature". Healing means bringing the exiled part home into one's story. [#1862]
- The wish to purge trauma has cultural, religious and psychological roots, and the psychological one — the wish repeats the splitting logic of trauma itself — explains why harmful cathartic methods stay popular. [#1862]
- Constellations combine a valid tool (spatial metaphor) with an unverified one (the "field"). The author keeps the first and rejects the second; the evidence is very low quality and harm rates in one RCT were high. [#1907]
- Couples therapy reduces helplessness and returns agency; good relationships are not conflict-free — most conflicts are perpetual — and couples tend to wait far too long before seeking help. [#1921]

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Valley of disappointment (Долина разочарований) and realistic expectations
Purpose. To explain why people abandon therapy, mindfulness or body work halfway and how to avoid it. The post's headline thesis: "Этичные практики развития работают через накопительный эффект" (Ethical development practices work through a cumulative effect). Only gradually can one reach truly stable changes and breakthroughs that are not traumatising or useless [#256].

The mechanism, step by step (the author's typical scenario):
1. A person has a problem — overload, strong stress or burnout [#256].
2. They see scientific studies and hear about other people's positive experience, and come to therapy, mindfulness practices or body work [#256].
3. They try once, twice. It seems a bit better, but not much. On top of that additional pain appears, and it is expensive [#256].
4. They expected that after 3 sessions of mindfulness or therapy visits they would have more energy, better sleep, and that the number of tasks would somehow shrink by itself [#256].
5. Instead of real change they fall into the Valley of disappointment — a place where they conclude that something is wrong with them since the methods do not work for them; or that the methods are bad; or that the world is simply a place where one has to endure pain and survive, with no way out [#256].
6. They quit with a feeling of anger — or, more often, the new initiative simply "dissolves", falls away [#256].

The author's diagnosis: "С человеком все нормально, с методом тоже. Просто немного не хватило." (The person is fine, and so is the method. It just fell a little short.) One needs to walk the road a bit longer and expect a bit less from it; at the end the problem gets solved. Otherwise the person goes on living in suffering and dissatisfaction [#256].

Remedy 1 — explain timelines, technique and approximate results up front (what deep mind says it pays great attention to):
- Therapy: plan to go for 4–6 months, once or twice a week, for changes to start happening in your life. You most likely won't connect the changes with the therapist, because you do everything yourself and the changes seem to happen "by themselves". That is how the method works, and you need to know this going in. Understanding *why* is possible if you are willing to absorb a big chunk of new humanities knowledge [#256].
- Mindfulness practices: set up a real daily practice (practically without skipping) and bring sitting time to at least 20–30 minutes. Then within 2–3 weeks you will see something fundamental starting to change — at the level of perception, bodily sensations and habits. At some point you will realise your basic unawareness and understand the value of these "small" changes. If not, talk to the teacher and find out where the mistake is [#256].

Why information alone often fails — two blockers. Even full information frequently does not work, because trust in the method does not form and the person gets stuck in doubt, voiced as: "Сработало с другими, но я особенный / Метод ваш - какая-то шляпа" (It worked for others, but I'm special / Your method is some kind of nonsense) [#256]:
1. The pain has not yet "ripened" enough for the person to be ready to really work and change, i.e. to use the tool fully [#256].
2. Problems with the capacity to trust, and therefore with actually applying methods; the person always approaches the process with half-measures [#256].
In both cases the person keeps stewing in the problem, repeatedly running into pain without being able to do anything about it [#256].

Remedy 2 — the general rule for change. One must find one's own ways of choosing the right helper (expert), trust them at certain points and work with discipline. The author compares it to any other field: to change tyres or get a dental filling you have to go through the same path [#256].

#### Seven reasons there is no scientific proof of which therapy suits whom (Ruslan Zaydullin)
Context. The channel frames the wish to measure everything quickly through the scientific method as the driver of the hype around "scientifically proven" psychotherapy seen in recent years — a hype that, it says, is gradually starting to be questioned. The critique is by guest author Ruslan Zaydullin, lead of the psychotherapy direction at Profi.ru (which includes the Meta service). His question: why is there still no scientific evidence of which therapy method suits a specific person in a specific situation best, or which therapy is more effective than others? [#492]

1. People are not robots. Research reduces a person to a single-phrase problem (for example "low self-esteem"), which ignores a million details of context and personal history that influence solving the problem [#492].
2. The biggest factor of effectiveness is the quality of contact with the therapist, not the method. Nobody has yet managed to formalise and study this contact — it is unclear which therapist will "click" with whom [#492].
3. Psychotherapy is phenomenological. A person can subjectively undergo a hundred thousand changes while not moving at all on a clinical anxiety scale — yet subjectively their life, they themselves and "everything" changed. Example: a study asked group participants, the group leader and the group's supervisors to rate the effectiveness of group therapy; their ratings systematically did not match and together made no sense [#492].
4. Other factors cannot be isolated in a long-term study — the whole surrounding environment affects one's state [#492].
5. Comparative studies are very expensive, especially longitudinal ones, so there are almost none, and the existing ones are small [#492].
6. Funding bias. Research is sponsored mainly by American pharma and insurance companies interested in fast, short-term solutions, which can make studies biased and focused on particular solutions [#492].
7. The average hides the individual. All existing studies, "statistically verified and objectively", show that on average all types of therapy are almost equally effective for almost any problem. Looking deeper, inside each study some people got worse, some better, some no change — and nobody cares why exactly these subgroups responded as they did [#492].

Conclusions drawn by Zaydullin. Hence the wild growth of CBT in the USA: cost reduction, targeted therapy in about a dozen sessions with a high risk of the result not lasting. He stresses that the effectiveness of CBT and other evidence-based methods *is* proven and he does not dispute it; what he dislikes is that evidence-based approaches are often positioned as the only ones that really work or as more effective than others. "Статистический редукционизм — не классно." (Statistical reductionism is not cool.) Therapy is a highly personalised multifactor process; to calculate it statistically with precision and detail would need multi-year (2–5 years) studies with hundreds of thousands of participants, tracking hundreds of life and personality-dynamics parameters for the whole period. Such studies are unlikely to appear soon, and it is not certain they are needed — although he would like them [#492].

#### Three ways to boost personal psychotherapy
Preface (author's caveat). Not everything in life needs boosting and making more efficient — especially personal development, which requires a lot of resource for courage, vulnerability and going into the "dark halls" of our history. Nevertheless asking about the effectiveness of therapy is very important: without the question many of us get stuck exactly where we are trying hard to move from [#835].

1. Work in sessions and between them. In the consumer era we tend to bring consumer habits where they do not belong. "Терапевт не должен улучшить нашу жизнь потому, что мы его купили." (The therapist is not obliged to improve our life because we bought them.) Therapy is rather a collaboration where the result depends first of all on what we manage to bring to the session and what we take away. How to do it:
   - between sessions, explore yourself;
   - in sessions, try to be brave and bring what *really* bothers you;
   - notice how what all parties say in the session affects you;
   - share this as honestly as you can;
   - be flexible toward your own worldview: try on the therapist's interpretations, keeping the option neither to take them nor to reject them immediately.
   Caveat: the author admits this is an ideal picture — because of neurosis and trauma these are exactly the problems we bring to therapy. Still, in this space it is especially important to strive to be more honest with oneself, attentive, brave and responsible for one's progress, while remembering compassion and kindness [#835].
2. Change the modality or complement it with other tools. Examples given:
   - after several years of talk therapy, add body-oriented therapy, EMDR or process-oriented work, and see what changes;
   - if you do CBT, gestalt or symboldrama, try depth approaches — humanistic, psychoanalysis or Jungian therapy;
   - if you only do cognitive work, add embodiment practices or meditation, with their access to raw immediate experience not distorted by thinking.
   Why: multimodality usually gives a synergistic effect, and every method has its blind spots and limitations which we sometimes hit hard. Pitfall: this does not apply if you have a pattern of constantly jumping from method to method without ever progressing. The point also includes changing therapy patterns: e.g. start attending a group if you only do individual work, or take a break from therapy if you have done it regularly for a long time — "При всей моей любви ко внутренней работе — она не является само целью и от нее надо отдыхать." (For all my love of inner work, it is not an end in itself and you need rest from it.) [#835]
3. Go for a long time, and bring the difficulties of being in therapy into the therapy itself. Benchmarks: the classical guideline for successful therapy in analysis is 6 years. The tempting promises of third-wave CBT or EMDR to solve a problem in 10 sessions in practice still mean 2–4 years of therapy for most of us. The author has not yet seen a single transformational case where someone resolved deep anxiety or healed a core neurosis in 1–2 years; except for very targeted requests such as fear of flying, therapy is long work. If you chose carefully, it is very important not to bounce off the therapist at impasses — when there is nothing to talk about, therapy does not help, or you dislike the therapist. Such impasses are often exactly what gets resolved in this relationship, so that we stop repeating them elsewhere [#835].

#### Hoffman Process — "therapy bootcamp"
What it is. An intensive week-long therapeutic program founded in the USA by Bob Hoffman; popular in Europe and the UK since the 1980s and run in Russia with translation for 8 years (as of the post). It combines a whole set of techniques and approaches: psychoanalysis, gestalt, group therapy, a large amount of embodiment practice, and meditation with various visualisations. The author deliberately does not describe the techniques in detail, conveying only the essence [#546].

Core idea. "На процессе мы проживаем всю личную психотерапию в миниатюре." (In the Process we live through the whole of personal psychotherapy in miniature.) The conventional stages or themes one must pass in therapy are worked through intensively in 8 days together with a group [#546].

Underlying principle — awareness and choice (shared by all deep mind projects). First, various attention tools increase our understanding of what is happening; only after that do we gain the freedom to choose and change ingrained behaviour patterns:
1. Become aware of what we do.
2. Choose behaviour not in automatic mode [#546].

Ethics as the selection criterion. The author says the Process is ethical and professional in implementing this principle, and that for him ethics is the most important criterion for choosing any groups and practices, because "этика лежит в центре успеха и провала любой интервенции в психику и тело" (ethics lies at the centre of the success and failure of any intervention into psyche and body). Especially in intense experiences what matters is the facilitator's motivation, qualification, boundaries of competence, and understanding of what must *not* be done with a person. In his view, due to the immaturity of the Russian market this is more often bad than good there [#546].

Author's experience and verdict. The Process was an excellent complement to his seven-year analysis: it let him look at his whole story anew and touch traumas and emotions in a new form. A month afterwards he rated it as extremely useful therapeutic work and felt that in that month he finished off a whole layer of fundamental themes he had worked on in therapy for years. Rule: "Терапия Процессом не заменяется, а скорее дополняется. И наборот." (Therapy is not replaced by the Process but rather complemented — and vice versa.) In some cases the Process may be the only accessible form of therapeutic work: some people will never go into long-term therapy, but the intensive format suits them. His recommendation: put the Hoffman Process into your development plan, since it helps deal with the very foundation of your personality. Anecdote offered as colour: Justin Bieber left on day three, Katy Perry completed it; the author also completed it [#546].

#### IFS (Internal Family Systems) view of parts and burdens
Basic claim (from the description of Richard Schwartz's book, Russian title Мои разные я). Sometimes we hear inner voices that do not match our ideal self-image, and then we feel fear or shame. Schwartz's research challenges the "mono-mind" theory: in each of us there are several subpersonalities, and only by acknowledging them can we get rid of inner contradictions [#629].

Burdens (as quoted by the author from Schwartz):
- In childhood it is impossible to assess whether the emotions that arise are justified [#813].
- We are not developed enough to protect ourselves from trauma and hurt [#813].
- These are easily dragged along for a whole life, creating a burden [#813].
- By blending ("сливаясь") with burdened parts we lose the sense of interconnection; it is replaced by alienation from each other and from spirit — complete loneliness [#813].
- When freed of burdens, parts show their true positive qualities — joy, sensitivity, empathy, sexuality — and resources: the ability to concentrate, insight, problem-solving and the wish to help, which enrich our life [#813].

Author's extension. Therefore when our sensitivity is devalued for years, in adulthood we have to do long work and lift "the curse of other people's limitations", returning to a natural state where forgotten resources are available. Behind burdened and traumatised parts there is always sensitivity identical to love and compassion; conversely, all domestic violence, corporate toxicity and state wars come from burdens of past trauma and violence, identical to the loss of love and sensitivity. Hence the therapeutic stories of different approaches have for decades confirmed that sensitivity, or love of life, is "не идеи фанатиков и не отклонение, а наше естественное состояние" (not the ideas of fanatics and not a deviation, but our natural state). Seeing the world otherwise is simply a consequence of one's own burden and deserves compassion and support [#813].

Author's illustrative case. At 14 he felt he experienced life far more intensely than people around him (he is not sure this was true) in a provincial Russian yard culture that was "not about feelings": a talented girl-poet in his class was bullied until graduation, boys broke each other's noses for fun; he says sensitivity could compete for the most discriminated minority in that society. Contact with this intensity felt like knowing that he exists, is connected to life and can voice himself; he spent much time alone, wrote poems, then prose and a first book at 15. Despite support from some friends and teachers, the pressure and disapproval stayed inside and killed his writing; later, at university, a second wave did the same to photography, which earned big money for a student but irritated his family because it did not serve a career in his field (state administration) — each year he had less energy for it. After leaving corporate work in 2019 he does almost purely creative work and never fully lost contact with his sensitivity, though past years "rolled over it"; now he has music again, writes, and carries a small new camera. Lesson: devalued sensitivity becomes a burden that takes long adult work to lift [#813].

Social application. Writing in April 2022, the author closed channel comments because some voices were so charged with anger that he found them hard to bear and wanted to protect others from them. He says that for the health of society we will have to find a way to integrate these hurt, lost and frightened voices — maybe not him and not now, since it is too painful to stay in a healing meta-position and adequately perceive the panic fear that leads to aggression and wars and fixes a person in their position so strongly that it leads everyone around into trauma. He suggests IFS may help all of us integrate such different voices in society [#629].

#### McWilliams personality types
Why this typology. Nancy McWilliams is presented as one of the leading voices in personality studies whose works have strongly influenced psychotherapy practice and theory. Diving into it is not always easy but is a reliable investment, unlike popular but often superficial or outright unscientific typings such as Human Design. Unlike MBTI and similar systems, her approach focuses on personality dynamics and the mental processes underlying outward behaviour, aiming to understand a person's deep motivations and defences [#1029].

The types (from the channel's cards):
1. Narcissistic type. "Нарциссизм - не порок, а окно в душу" (Narcissism is not a vice but a window into the soul) through which one sees deep insecurity masked by a façade of grandiosity. Typical: excessive self-love, need for admiration, lack of empathy; may behave arrogantly and exploit others for their own interests. Example: someone actively posts about achievements, uniqueness and special status on social media seeking approval and admiration, while deep down seeking confirmation of their significance [#1029].
2. Schizoid type. Finds refuge in inner worlds to hide from the fear of being misunderstood or rejected; may seem detached or indifferent to social interaction and often prefers activities that do not require personal contact. Example: someone who prefers spending the evening alone with a favourite hobby rather than meeting friends, rarely shares thoughts and feelings, and finds true satisfaction in solitude where they can fully immerse in their inner world [#1030].
3. Obsessive-compulsive type. Order becomes a shield against chaos (the card's OCR reads "хасса", (!) UNCLEAR, apparently "хаоса"), and striving for control becomes an art; behind a manic pursuit of perfection lies a deep need for stability and predictability. Example: someone who plans every aspect of the day, keeps perfect order in everything, expects the same from others, and feels strong anxiety and dissatisfaction when someone disrupts their plans or order [#1031].
4. Hysteroid (histrionic) type. Lives in a world where every day is a stage and emotions are costumes; seeks attention and approval, can be emotionally expressive and sexually provocative; behaviour is often aimed at being the centre of attention, and they may struggle with deep emotional bonds. Example: a person who loves being the centre of attention and creates drama to attract others' attention [#1032].
5. Depressive type. Lives in the shadow of inner doubts and fears, often facing inner pressure and a sense of loss; life is a struggle with an inner voice that undermines self-esteem and treats every failure as an irreparable mistake. Example: someone who views their achievements through self-criticism, never feeling good enough; despite outer success, an inner sense of emptiness and despair makes them doubt their significance, generating an endless cycle of self-condemnation and sadness [#1033].
6. Neurotic type. Lives at the crossroads of desires and duties, often captive to their own expectations; functions socially and professionally quite well, but is prone to anxiety and excessive concern about others' opinions, and may have difficulty expressing anger and other strong emotions. Example: someone who strives for ideals in everything from career to relationships and is constantly anxious for fear of making the wrong choice or not meeting their own high standards [#1034].
7. Masochistic type. People with masochistic traits tend to self-sacrifice and may choose situations or relationships in which they suffer; they often feel guilty satisfying their own needs and may unconsciously seek punishment. Example: someone who often gives up their own plans to help others and feels important only when making sacrifices for others, even at the expense of their own wellbeing [#1035].
8. Paranoid type. "Eternal guards of their borders", living in expectation of betrayal; life is filled with searching for hidden threats and reading between the lines. Example: someone who sees ill-wishers in every shadow and spends great effort defending against possible, often imaginary, threats, which leads to isolation and loss of trust in loved ones [#1036].
9. Psychopathic type. "Masters of disguise" whose charisma hides a lack of empathy and remorse; life is a game without rules where the end justifies the means. Example: someone who easily wins others' trust with charm but uses it for their own goals without thinking about consequences for others, leaving a trail of broken relationships and conflicts [#1037].

Author's use and limits. McWilliams "literally built the foundation" of his worldview; since student years he and Dasha studied the typology and examined the described cases in real life — theory used in practice "comes alive" and becomes a fascinating journey through human diversity. Because the types are not evenly distributed, he mostly meets the hysteroid, narcissistic and neurotic types, though by now he has interacted with all of them. Limits: the typology does not answer all questions and does not account for states or stages of vertical development; but its depth gives a huge advantage both in self-knowledge and in interacting with others. Recommendation: McWilliams' works for a thoughtful, systematic psychological knowledge base [#1029].

#### Co-regulation: why live psychotherapy will become a luxury in the AI era
Authors: Darya Shmeleva (psychotherapist, embodiment facilitator) and Max Rodin [#1297].

Starting observation. Six months to a year earlier, coaching or therapy with GPT was a parody of real inner work; now AI systems are surprisingly powerful, and according to fresh research (a PLOS Mental Health paper the authors cite) people cannot distinguish chat responses from those of a highly qualified therapist. Yet testing current chatbots made the authors even more confident that good therapists will not be left without work. Against techno-optimists who often reduce complex real processes, they hold that AI cannot replace the essence of deep therapeutic or coaching work — but it will be able to do basic, algorithmic work [#1297].

Forecast. Simple, necessary inner work may become available to literally everyone, "like fast food", while the live process becomes a luxury for connoisseurs able to pay for it [#1297].

The argument, step by step:
1. Interpersonal neurobiology is named the leading edge of brain and mind science; from it, the human nervous system, brain and mind are not isolated mechanisms that can be "fixed" alone by executing the right algorithm of actions [#1297].
2. Capacity for recovery, regulation skills and serious change is built into one simple but rare process: attunement with other living people [#1297].
3. Physiological synchronisation. When two people meet in a space of trust, their nervous systems literally "connect": rhythms of heart, breathing and even neuron activity in the prefrontal cortex begin to synchronise — described as the fundamental mechanism of healing [#1297].
4. Co-regulation in stress. Under stress or anxiety the nervous system looks for anchors. When a living person nearby holds your emotions, does not devalue them, does not give ready-made solutions but simply stays, is present, breathes at the same pace, your state changes too — you literally "absorb" their capacity to be resourced. This is attunement, scientifically called co-regulation [#1297].
5. AI cannot be present and provide co-regulation. It has no body (one may appear soon but is unlikely to become truly alive quickly) and no consciousness (which probably will never arise). AI will choose the right words and suggest breathing techniques but will not create a space where the nervous system can absorb safety or live the experience of being heard. Safety is not words but gaze, voice timbre, facial micro-movements, smell in the air — and above all "the vibration of presence", the main force of therapeutic healing and transformation [#1297].
6. What actually heals. Healing and transformation happen not when knowledge of what to do appears (which GPT can write perfectly) but when a new experience is lived in warm contact — that one can be alive and vulnerable and not be rejected but held. This produces intuitive, deep bodily knowing: "я могу быть собой, и это безопасно" (I can be myself, and it is safe) [#1297].
7. Caveat and conclusion. This does not cancel the large benefit of AI formats, especially for people for whom today's therapy is inaccessible. But for substantial change — to stop merely "coping" and start living differently — you need someone who will hold your process by being nearby; in an era of virtualisation and loneliness this becomes the most valuable resource [#1297].

#### Three levels of depth in therapy (content, process, presence)
Purpose: the author distinguishes three levels of depth in therapy, and says depth correlates with the ability to create increasingly fundamental change. He uses it to explain why a meditating therapist is preferable. [#1323]

1. Content (Контент). Most methods and therapists, especially beginners, work with the contents of the mind — the thoughts, emotions and stories the client brings to the session. This lets the client order chaos, take a step back and stop fusing with emotions and reacting to them automatically. John Welwood called such changes a "горизонтальным сдвигом" (horizontal shift): they ease symptoms but barely touch the foundation of the psyche. CBT and similar behavioural approaches most often work here. [#1323]
2. Processes (Процессы). Depth methods attend less to content than to the processual nature of experience. The therapist helps the client tolerate the intensity of experience and follow the experiencing, revealing its dynamic nature, which often transforms from one state into another. IFS, EMDR, the process approach and analysis most often work here. [#1323]
3. Presence (Присутствие). A person is not only mental content and processes. Deeper still lies the foundation of the being, or its pure nature — the very level of being, consciousness, the sense of existing. Therapists who have cleared the obstacles to their own sincere presence work here. [#1323]

Why presence matters (the author's reasoning): when we lose contact with the dimension of presence, reality becomes flat, stifling and frightening. We try to overcome that fear and discomfort but never fully establish ourselves in complete safety — it always slips away. The fear of the shakiness and emptiness of being shows grotesquely in modern life, where we are catastrophically uncomfortable being alone with our experience and not looking at a screen at every moment of waiting. This fear drives us to endlessly structure, analyse and try to stabilize experience in search of illusory comfort. Hence anxiety, narcissism and control are often attempts to avoid meeting life's fundamental openness. Citing Bruce Tift, the author says such habits of controlling and improving experience create even more tension: "Невроз — это привычная неосознанная попытка избежать бытия, которая всегда оставляет страдания на месте" (Neurosis is a habitual unconscious attempt to avoid being, which always leaves the suffering in place). [#1323]

Pitfalls the author names:
- If therapy ignores this state of affairs and the level at which it can be resolved, it can relieve symptoms but sustains the fundamental split between the small defending identity and the nature of being — "Из-за которого и возникают все проблемы!" (Which is what all the problems arise from!). [#1323]
- Even depth psychotherapy can become a trap if it strengthens the client's drive to "understand", "work through" or "fix" their experience instead of helping them stop fighting it. [#1323]
- The good therapist's task is not to enter into "невротический сговор" (neurotic collusion) with the client — helping them settle more comfortably into an identity fragmented by the loss of an important part of reality — but to foster a return of genuine contact with life, where ever-growing openness and presence in experience arise. [#1323]
- Restoring a person's access to presence — possible not only in therapy but also, for example, in a group process — is, in the author's words, "сущностный дар устойчивого благополучия" (the essential gift of sustainable well-being). [#1323]

Context: the author recounts that for years his psychoanalyst interpreted his interest in contemplative practices as avoidance and kept asking what he was running away from into meditation. He grants that spiritual bypassing is a real problem — when practice becomes a way of escaping important psychological tasks — but says the issue is sometimes the therapist's own worldview. Therapy has advanced in understanding the importance of mindfulness, yet still often misses the essence of meditation and ignores important discoveries of the contemplative approach. The reverse is also true: a practising therapist can help a person more than others. [#1323]

#### Using Focusing as a client in a session (3 steps)
Purpose: to use the felt-sense skill — which, per the research the author cites, best predicts therapy success — to move further in one's own therapy. [#1343]

1. At the start of the session, slow down and ask yourself: what is truly important for me to talk about today? Do not answer immediately — wait for what resonates in the body. [#1343]
2. When an important topic comes up, stop and ask yourself: what am I feeling in my body right now as I say this? [#1343]
3. If there is no answer, say so out loud. "«Я что-то чувствую, но не могу сказать, что именно» — это уже начало фокусинга" ("I feel something but can't say what exactly" — this is already the beginning of Focusing). [#1343]

#### Developing the Focusing skill outside therapy (3 practices)
The author says we are not born with the ability to focus and are not taught it anywhere, so the skill can and should be developed. [#1343]

1. Pause during the day and listen: what is felt in the body right now? [#1343]
2. Notice which bodily sensations accompany your actions, and learn not to rush into reaction. [#1343]
3. Write down unformed bodily sensations, even if they seem meaningless. Do not analyse them — simply be with them. [#1343]

Why mindfulness helps: Focusing has much in common with the contemplative tradition — presence, observation, non-attachment, healthy patience. Mindfulness practice naturally develops bodily intelligence and the ability to feel the body from inside, so it can move us forward both in Focusing and in therapy. [#1343]

#### Three reasons popular methods trigger attacks
The author explains why a fact-poor article from a contested outlet about IFS spread so widely. IFS is a very popular method that in the US has become a huge business machine generating millions of dollars; training is expensive, and for the Russian-language training one sometimes waits in a queue for several years. Such phenomena carry an added risk of triggering: [#1616]

1. Envy — we would like to train in IFS but cannot because of various barriers. [#1616]
2. Irritation at popularity itself — popular phenomena bother us in general; if something is too hyped, it seems something must be wrong with it. [#1616]
3. Wanting to gain popularity by attacking — we want to attack something popular to acquire some of that popularity ourselves. [#1616]

All of this is fed by the fact that when a method becomes a brand, a commercial product, real grounds for doubt appear — and then the desire to attack becomes logical and partly justified. [#1616]

#### Four conditions under which the therapy community wins (IFS conclusion)
The author's takeaway: IFS is "не зло и не волшебная таблетка" (neither evil nor a magic pill) — a powerful tool that works and has risks, especially if used incorrectly. The community wins when: [#1616]

1. We check the training of those who offer IFS and other practices. [#1616]
2. We distinguish the client's level of readiness and the complexity of the clinical picture. [#1616]
3. We do not give in to emotional reactions such as "все бросились, значит там что-то не чисто" (everyone rushed to it, so something is fishy) or "все ходят, значит надо идти" (everyone goes, so I should go). [#1616]
4. We build a culture in which a method is not a brand but a tool requiring knowledge, practice and responsibility — of both the specialist and the client. [#1616]

#### Therapeutic vs contemplative approach to healing (Bruce Tift, as presented by the author)
Context: the author has assisted at intensives of the PAT method dozens of times ("счет таких кейсов у меня пошел уже на десятки" — the count of such cases is already in the dozens) and is very glad of these processes. In 9 cases out of 10, deep trauma work unfolds: situations or whole series of situations surface, and the therapist helps the person regain agency (субъектность), strength and safety inside those situations. Simplifying a lot, he says most therapy is built this way — as labour applied to a problem in our history, labour that often requires "sweat, blood and tears". [#1862]

Bruce Tift distinguishes two approaches to healing:
1. Therapeutic approach — active, directed work on the problem in our story (the PAT example above is this mode). [#1862]
2. Contemplative approach — we stop fixing the story as a problem and simply stay with experiences, whatever they are. Sometimes therapy looks completely different from labour: like a gaze that delights in living flowers, or hearing that suddenly notices the beauty of a melody. Paradoxically, this quiet presence, which requires doing nothing with reality, can turn out to contain more healing than active directed work with problems. [#1862]

Whichever path one takes, the author says, the shared truth that is often missed in the flood of ideas about therapy and trauma is that no inner work can rid us of trauma (next framework). [#1862]

#### Three lenses behind the wish to get rid of trauma
Purpose: to understand why clients hope for catharsis, why culture makes therapeutic work harder, and why people seek dubious methods. The author's thesis: "никакая внутренняя работа не способна избавить нас от травмы. Избавление здесь вообще не предусмотрено — и это тоже не баг, а фича" (no inner work can rid us of trauma. Riddance is not provided for here at all — and this too is not a bug but a feature). At the end of trauma work the trauma is integrated into our history, not expelled from it. [#1862]

Background from Judith Herman's classic *Trauma and Recovery*: the hope for catharsis — liberation and purification from trauma through vivid re-living — is socially constructed and was long supported by early psychological theories. Clients often think it is enough to pour out their soul and plunge into the most painful experiences and this will solve everything. Under this belief therapy starts to look like, in Herman's words quoted by the author, "садомазохистической оргии, в которой пациент будет кричать, плакать, истекать кровью, умрет и возродится очищенным от последствий травмы" (a sadomasochistic orgy in which the patient will scream, cry, bleed, die and be reborn purified of the consequences of trauma). [#1862]

The three lenses that condition this wish:
1. Cultural. We are used to treating ourselves as a product that has a bug: find the fault, delete it, return to factory settings. The repair logic that works perfectly with phones is quietly transferred to the psyche — and creates demand for fast, magical healing, ideally within a single intensive. [#1862]
2. Religious (named by Herman herself). Behind the catharsis fantasy stands the mythic-magical metaphor of exorcism, an ancient view of how reality works. Trauma is imagined as a demon that has possessed us and can be driven out by ritual. Many modern practices unconsciously reproduce exactly this drama of expulsion. [#1862]
3. Psychological: the striving to get rid of trauma repeats the logic of trauma itself. The author finds this the deepest lens. At the core of trauma lie rejection and splitting — the moment when part of our experience became unbearable and was sent into exile. Trying now to tear it out by the root, we act from that same logic of splitting rather than wholeness, continuing the same war with a part of ourselves, "только под флагом исцеления" (only under the flag of healing). [#1862]

Why it matters: the third lens is one reason cathartic methods are so popular despite their obvious harmfulness — they resonate with the inner logic of splitting and the internalized sense that something is wrong with our parts. But they do not help integrate trauma; they only exploit its presence. [#1862]

What healing looks like instead: returning the exiled part home, building it into one's story with acceptance and a right to a voice. Herman therefore opposes the idea of quick catharsis with another image — recovery as a marathon requiring preparation and constant practice. The author adds a caveat: part of that marathon is run not by effort but by a gaze falling on flowers, one that can love everything perfect and imperfect already present in life. [#1862]

#### Two things that get conflated in constellations
The author argues that in discussions of family constellations (расстановки) two different things keep sticking together and should be separated. [#1907]
1. Working with spatial metaphor. Taking the inner outside and looking at it from aside is a working mechanism about a hundred years old: Moreno's psychodrama, Satir's family sculpture, the empty chair in Gestalt, externalization in IFS, body figures in SPT. "Тут вопросов нет" (No questions here). [#1907]
2. The "field" as a source of knowledge about what a person did not know about themselves — plus access to this "field" through unprepared people and through the interpretations of a facilitator who is in a position of power. Something unclear, checked by no one, proven in no way, used by other people of unknown motivation and training. Here the author has many questions, the main one: what is the profit of using such a doubtful method when there are plenty of non-doubtful ones? [#1907]

#### Four missing criteria of effective inner work (constellations critique)
Setting aside the fixation on evidence (which, he concedes, is often excessive), the author saw in the session the absence of several criteria of effective inner work at once:
1. Trauma-informedness (травмоинформированность) — missing. [#1907]
2. Operational language (операциональный язык) — missing. [#1907]
3. Ethics of touch (этика прикосновений) — missing. [#1907]
4. High agency (субъектность) of both the client and the other group participants — missing. [#1907]

He stresses this is not an aesthete's nitpicking: the method "возможно кому-то помог, но кому-то точно навредил" (possibly helped someone, but certainly harmed someone), and he would not want to be that person. "Людей на группе открывают, а закрывать их обратно не факт, что умеют" (People are opened up in the group, but it is not certain they know how to close them back). [#1907]

#### Qualities of a good therapist (from the author's couples-therapy search)
The author and Dasha found a suitable couples therapist only on the third attempt, trying again and again with many doubts and much uncertainty because the process was new and they lacked reference points. They kept searching until they found, in this relationship with a third person, enough safety and their own agency. The qualities that made the fit:
1. Acceptance without judgment — able to accept any oddities and choices without condemning them. [#1921]
2. Support within a non-invasive but clear setting — a firm frame for exploring questions full of emotion where easy solutions simply do not exist. [#1921]
3. Not pushing one's own ideas into the clients' life and being able to separate oneself from cultural, gender and other constructs that may influence a person — important in therapy generally and in couples therapy especially. [#1921]
4. Naming one's own influences out loud. Their therapist often says "на меня сейчас влияет идея" (an idea is influencing me right now) before sharing a judgment, drawing attention to the fact that we are all human and shaped by the systems we grow up and live in. The author likes this because the freedom sought in therapy is largely the freedom to choose what works for us as individuals and as couples (or other relationship configurations) — the possibility of stepping outside imposed ideas. [#1921]

Persistence is part of the method: switching specialists until the fit is right was necessary. [#1921]

#### Mapping inner-work goals to specialists (the author's own case)
The author notes that leaders write openly about external goals and changes ("we're introducing agile, moving to the USA, sorting out team communication"), but there is little structured information about what goals people set for their *inner* development. As an example he lists what he was working on "inside" at 30 while building a startup. One big question — focus and calm — broke down into several tasks, each handled by a different specialist or practice:
- sleep problems and anxiety — a process therapist;
- prioritising tasks, a way of making choices, values and competencies — a coach;
- authenticity and presence in public speaking — two speaking trainers;
- adequate wellbeing at the end of the year under very high load — mindfulness and movement.
He planned to integrate the lived experience at year-end and possibly change his inner-work goals. Lesson: a broad inner goal can be decomposed into concrete requests matched to the right type of helper, with periodic integration and revision [#9].

#### Why clients should sustain their therapists (2023)
- Having tools is a privilege. The author has tools to change his inner state: when difficult emotions or negative thoughts take over, he can meditate and create a buffer — space between emotions and actions — or go to his therapist for support and mirroring. Most people do not have such an exit: in moments of pain they either push it outward onto others or muffle it with addiction. Without self-knowledge and inner-work tools, people push their pain outward, get angry at others and see enemies everywhere — which directly affects our shared situation [#792].
- Value of those who carry the tools. People who become conduits of inner-transformation tools first do this socially important work within themselves; traditionally a village supports yogis meditating in a nearby cave, and thereby each villager joins something bigger and creates an opportunity for their own healing and growth [#792].
- Penetration figures (as stated by the author): worldwide, psychotherapy has not reached even 10% of the population; in Russia, despite the recent therapy boom, only 1% of the population has a therapist. Therapy is mainstream in some circles, but not for the broad public [#792].
- Therapists burned out in 2022 under a huge emotional load — "they are people too"; the author heard of a session where the therapist cried. Thousands of practitioners left Russia, coping with emigration while helping clients with the same; some will move to non-Russian-speaking markets, others in a shrinking economy will return to better-paying professions. His forecast: in the Russian-speaking future, access to therapy for broad layers will shrink again, not expand [#792].
- Professional principles limit therapists' material stability: psychotherapists do not befriend clients; do not accept gifts; have no year-end bonuses. In some psychoanalytic schools clients pay for sessions even while the therapist is on vacation — the client also carries part of responsibility for the stability of the process [#792].
- Call to action. Take personal responsibility and support those who support you: a famous restaurateur will not close restaurants if you skip a couple of dinners, and Nike will survive without your sneaker purchase — but attend somatic practices, do not skip therapy, buy consulting for teams, meditate with genuine teachers, to preserve future access to these tools and the people who transmit them. Therapist-matching services could also explain more about how helping professions work from the inside [#792].

#### Marginalised experience and identity (applying to Naropa)
Helping his wife apply to a year-long psychedelic-assisted psychotherapy program for psychologists at Naropa Institute (Boulder), the author saw how hard it is to rely on experience and build it into identity when society considers it countercultural. The application questions explored applicants' personal development, including contemplative and transpersonal tracks:
- What is your experience of transpersonal / altered states of consciousness?
- How does mindfulness help in psychotherapy?
- How do you work on understanding and integrating your own privileges and oppressions?
A few hours of answers produced such a powerful self-narrative that both were surprised how transparent and unnoticed it had been: tons of hours invested in deep self-exploration that practically "does not count" in the Russian-speaking context. The author calls this the essence of the context: meditation only recently became okay, altered states are still taboo, even psychotherapy took off only a few years ago and has sceptics. So a significant baggage of contemplative path and subtle psychological work feels like "nothing special" — wealth becomes, if not poverty, then something ordinary. He suspects most people have such marginalised experience and untapped wealth; if we pursue what the mainstream does not approve and what does not fall into the "basket of success", we also face conscious or unconscious devaluation of that path and its achievements. Becoming aware of this oppression is useful because its nature is purely social; with that awareness one can, as an adult, appropriate much of it and build life more authentically. His conclusion: it is right to do the right things on time even if they are still marginalised in the mainstream, which always lags behind [#990].

#### Inner work as a dojo; why Focusing
The author frames this post as a constructive turn after repeatedly criticizing the downside of modern development culture (consumerist attitude, "Bali avoidance", wrong approaches to inner work). If you approach inner work seriously — genuinely motivated to sort something out and improve your life — sooner or later it comes down to readiness to see any practice, from therapy to meditation and tantra, as a dojo: a place where we meet what is truly important and truly frightening. On this dojo, which gradually extends to all of life, you have to go into what scares you most. [#1343]

He quotes Bruce Tift: "Наша жизнь во многом организована вокруг избегания самых глубоких страхов. Мы прячемся от них, строим образы, компенсируем, отвлекаемся — но, по сути, всё это только усиливает фрагментацию." (Our life is largely organized around avoiding our deepest fears. We hide from them, build images, compensate, distract ourselves — but in essence all this only intensifies fragmentation.) Therefore inner work in which we can progress and transform may include, but goes beyond, tasks like "being resourced": the task is to go into the darkest and most distant corners of the soul and stay there with friendly attention — not out of masochism, but to meet what we have been running from all our lives. Where fears, shame and tension live, real transformation lives too. [#1343]

John Welwood (another favourite therapist) puts it in terms of therapy: "Суть терапии в конечном счёте состоит не в диагностике, протоколах или курсах лечения, а в формировании новых живых отношений со своим переживанием." (The essence of therapy ultimately lies not in diagnosis, protocols or courses of treatment, but in forming a new living relationship with one's experience.) This is exactly where Focusing leads. [#1343]

#### What Focusing is and where it came from
Focusing was developed by Welwood's teacher Eugene Gendlin. It is the practice of attentive, live contact with "inner" bodily experience that is not yet formed into words but already shows up as some important meaning. Gendlin was a professor of philosophy at the University of Chicago, where he worked with Carl Rogers and did the key research that became the basis of Focusing. He noticed that clients who, during a session, paused, hesitated, searched for exact words and listened to bodily sensations achieved results. [#1343]

Key idea: clients able to establish contact with their *felt sense* — not an emotion, not a thought, but a non-verbalized yet intuitively accessible bodily sense of meaning — and to unfold it in therapy progress much faster than others. Today Focusing is a therapeutic classic and, per the author, one of the most researched approaches in therapy; research he cites showed that this ability, rather than the therapy method, best predicts success in therapy. [#1343]

#### Bad Therapy and the ideologization of therapy critique
The author compares this case to an earlier critique of "new ethics" he analysed, which he found weak in content and whose author discredited himself by answering commenters in an aggressive, insulting way — yet it still went viral and its theses stuck. Here he wants to look less at the theses than at the broader process behind them; the target this time is the culture of psychotherapy and psychotherapy itself. [#1485]

*Bad Therapy: Why the Kids Aren't Growing Up* (Abigail Shrier, 2024; the cover notes she is the bestselling author of *Irreversible Damage*) raises important questions about therapeutic practices in modern society. For example, the author of the book sees an alarming paradox: despite the growth in therapy consultations, anxiety and depression among teenagers are not decreasing. The channel author says there is something to think about, but the book is ideologized and embedded in conservative discourse, where psychotherapy is presented as part of a "left project" to weaken traditional structures — family, discipline, growing up — reinforcing the narrative that the therapeutic field creates weakness. He says it is therefore actively supported by bloggers who have become emotional relays of the right and even MAGA agenda. [#1485, img #1485]

Example: blogger Liliya Kim wrote "Хорошая книга на тему - как современная терапевтическая практика начала приносить больше вреда, чем пользы" (A good book on how modern therapeutic practice began to do more harm than good). The author says that from a blogger's position of power such a phrase sounds like fact — that therapy now does more harm than good. He asks whether it is proven, and answers no: Psychology Today criticizes the book's author for sharp generalizations about causes and effects without an evidence base; a review even on the conservative platform The Gospel Coalition says Shrier confidently links therapeutic culture to the fragmentation of growing up, amplifying traditional cultural critiques, while relying only on empirical observations and interviews. He adds that there are many studies confirming the opposite — the effectiveness of psychotherapy. A seemingly neutral post thus reproduces the book's rhetoric as facts, removing doubts and context and turning contested theses into ready conclusions. [#1485]

What troubles him is not the call for dialogue but the positional delivery and a sense of *contempt* — and the ease of generalizing and erasing context that follows, a readiness to speculate on popular topics as a blind continuation of large ideologies, and to throw ideas to an audience as facts without awareness of one's own power as a public author. Such ideas can gratify a feeling of being right but will not help a "right" person move forward or heal polarization in society. Critics also note the quality of contempt in the book's tone. What does therapy say about contempt? It is a manifestation of a person's own inner allergy, which prevents them from seeing reality objectively. [#1485]

The author notes that despite all the cries about cancelling new ethics, he practically does not observe its death, because it is not just an ideology but an objective process for which there is demand in society: new ethics is not just an ideology imposed by a "liberal gang" but a powerful tool for improving the human environment and skills; psychotherapy is not merely a liberal project but also helps people who need help. He prefers to view therapy not as the truth of the left or right but as a tool of human maturation that should be discussed without contempt and ideologization. [#1485]

#### The "IFS is dangerous" article
The author discloses his position: his team actively uses Internal Family Systems in various processes; it works especially well in psychedelic-assisted therapy, where EMDR, for example, works poorly. From live cases they know the method works well, though it has limitations like any other. This gives him an interest in a certain position, and on the other hand experience to rely on. [#1616]

A clickbait-titled article claiming IFS is dangerous spread in the Russian-speaking environment. Its two main premises: (1) there were cases where IFS led to problems; (2) the method lacks a sufficient evidence base. Many commenters missed an important point the author stresses: even with these claims, the article does not say IFS does not work. [#1616]

His comment: the article matters not in itself; the community seems mature enough to quickly take apart superficial theses, and in therapeutic circles he sees a consensus that IFS cannot be called a harmful method. But the episode again draws attention to what he writes about regularly: "Любой метод может быть рискованным, если применяется без должной этики" (Any method can be risky if applied without proper ethics) — meaning without preparation, with unsuitable clients, and without understanding the limits of one's competence. These problems tend to sharpen when a method is popularized: the IFS Institute offers trainings of various levels, sometimes coach versions, and in recent years a huge number of people have trained in IFS, often without any clear psychological or psychotherapeutic base. [#1616]

#### Problems of evidence-based thinking in therapy
The author is saddened that the evidence-based community readily and gladly declares anything a dangerous pseudoscience if meta-studies "verified on a meta-computer" have not been done. In that case, he argues, evidence-based thinking discredits itself and turns into a dangerous religion: it seems unable to rely on the community's view — the real experience of therapists and clients, an understanding of the logic of inner work. It is as if we trust ourselves so little that we see only a very narrow square containing the limited field of current therapy research, at a completely embryonic stage, and are afraid to see what lies outside it even if confirmed by logic and experience. "Так и живем в этом квадрате веры в КПТ+, после которой тоже регулярно встречаются клиенты с плохими последствиями. Зато исследования доказали!" (So we live in this square of faith in CBT+, after which clients with bad consequences also regularly turn up. But research proved it!) [#1616]

#### Does therapy infantilize society?
As psychotherapy and inner-work practices became mainstream, a backlash began. The author increasingly sees the thesis that the culture of therapy, mindfulness and trauma language makes people infantile and fragile: adults get stuck in grievances, avoid responsibility, and use psychology as a convenient language for blaming parents, society and the past. This thesis is no longer marginal; it has become part of everyday discourse, especially in intergenerational conflicts where the very idea of adulthood has become contested. He says it is important not to dismiss the reaction: it arises from concrete experience — contact with what psychotherapy has become on the mass market. [#1674]

Mass therapy and the market of relief. When psychotherapy became ubiquitous, it inevitably entered product logic, without fully being a product in essence. The market amplifies what sells more easily, and in therapy and practices that is often not change but relief: validation, explanation, reduction of inner tension, the feeling that the problem is not in you. "Рынок превратил терапию в сервис, а не в путь. А вместо взросления предложил человеку комфорт." (The market turned therapy into a service, not a path. And instead of growing up, it offered a person comfort.) [#1674]

Is therapy therefore empty? No. The author says large meta-studies show therapy is on average effective in terms of maturation, but the result depends heavily on the quality of the alliance and the client's active participation. Therapy does not do the work for the person — it creates the conditions in which that work is possible at all. At the same time, negative effects have long been discussed in the literature: researchers like Scott Lilienfeld point out that deterioration, increased dependence or regressive patterns are not a rare anomaly but a statistically recorded reality. But, as in the recent IFS case, this is most often linked not to the idea of therapy itself but to its superficial or simply wrong application; systemically it reflects the market and its laws operating on the territory of "therapy+", not a fundamental property. It is exactly here that experiences arise which are then generalized into an accusation against the whole culture. [#1674]

Loss of forms of growing up, and the Russian context. It is a mistake, the author says, to think infantilization (if such proud words are used at all) begins in the psychologist's office. It begins when society stops reproducing forms of growing up that were adequate *for the past*. Religion, community support and initiations have much less influence on life trajectories in post-industrial society. There is more freedom and fewer containers for it. Psychotherapy arises not as fashion but as a response to a structural rupture — an attempt to learn to live where identity is no longer given but assembled. In Russia these distortions are even stronger: "therapy" today covers almost everything, from real clinical work to the outright dangerous "German New Medicine". Educational platforms and aggregators created a harmful (but profitable for them) illusion of quick professionalism — "3 месяца обучения — и можно работать с чужой психикой" (3 months of training — and you can work with someone else's psyche). So inner work starts to look like a set of universal formulas, which often strengthen narcissism and turn pain into identity. Critics most often encounter this caricature version of therapy. [#1674]

Where ideology begins. Ironically, the anti-psychologizing discourse is itself deeply psychological: it expresses anxiety about losing old hierarchies and familiar forms of authority, about a world where gender, age and status no longer guarantee power. The simple solution — returning to so-called "normality" — is typical of populists, who often substitute ideological myth for analysis. Psychologization is declared a left agenda, a victim culture and a refusal of responsibility. Labelling rather than analysis does not help understand or solve the problem; instead of an adult encounter with reality it offers an emotional retreat into a past that cannot be returned. "И это и правда инфантильно, но очень по классике — проецируем на других то, что сложно принимать в себе." (And that really is infantile, but very classically so — we project onto others what is hard to accept in ourselves.) [#1674]

#### The author's constellations session: what was good
The author describes trying, "for fun", things in inner work he usually avoids (or reading Veselago), admits he is biased against constellations, and says he sometimes tests that bias. Invited to a closed process, he went with a beginner's mind. Like any inner-work method, constellations can be done very differently, and he thinks he took part in a fairly high-quality example. Good signs: he liked the facilitator and was not particularly triggered during the process. He also appreciated the depersonalization of the case: only the most general basics of the situation the main participant brought were stated, the figures had only numeric labels, nobody was told "you are the father" or "the investor", and he himself did not know whom he represented on the "board". He considers this a good way to reduce projections, dramatization and play. [#1907]

Yet sympathy for the facilitator and an anonymized case are not, in his view, enough for the work to be truly clean, and his conclusion stood: "метод ой какой сомнительный" (the method is oh so doubtful). [#1907]

#### Why the representative mechanism is doubtful
The author does believe people feel something, and that we have an inner system whose parts' interactions strongly influence our view of life, behavior and decisions. What he does not believe is that representatives (заместители) — people who in effect map someone's inner parts into physical space, without serious introspection skills or trained attention — can separate their own impulses, projections and material from what belongs to another person's inner system. Even with such skills, it is "a task with an asterisk" (very hard). [#1907]

The mechanism has a name — vicarious/representative perception (заместительское восприятие). Whether the representative's experience actually matches that of the person they represent in the case-giver's life, how often and under what conditions, has not been empirically studied. There is only a large body of anecdotal evidence, while the phenomenon is taken as a given both in the professional constellation community and in the specialist literature. It has no scientific basis and is explained through the "knowing field" or Sheldrake's morphic resonance. The author's argument: when a method explains itself through a "field" or morphic resonance, it explains one unknown through another unknown — "Ощущение понятности возникает, знания не прибавляется" (A feeling of understanding arises, but knowledge does not increase). [#1907]

Nuance: sometimes a method works simply because it works — he cites EMDR therapy as having been in that position for some time. But here reliance on the "field" seems very doubtful to him because it passes itself off as science. And he felt the group space that arose was heavily clouded by personal content and projections — unreliable as a place for real work. [#1907]

#### Why people may still feel better after constellations
The author grants the appeal: sorting out one's case in 2 hours instead of two years of therapy is very tempting, and one leaves with a story that has a beginning, climax and resolution. Therapy cannot do that; it is much more boring and requires much more active work. Plus 30 people look at your pain and take it seriously — for many this is the only such experience in years, and that heals in itself, without any "field". So he allows that people really do feel better afterwards; the open question is why this benefit comes bundled with metaphysics that can be neither verified nor falsified, and whether it has to be so or can be done differently. [#1907]

#### Safer alternatives the author recommends
- An IFS-type approach — "much more reliable", in his taste, for this kind of work. [#1907]
- Theory U practices for those drawn to placing figures in space: moving in SPT (social presencing theater), "but ecologically", or 3D Mapping, in which one sculpts from plasticine all the forces involved in one's situation, looks at them from the side and sees where they want to move — without cluttering one's space with other people's projections, hallucinations and power. [#1907]

He closes by marking all this as his subjective experience and opinion, and acknowledges that the figures in the session were not figures of another's inner world but real living people; some of them received insights and benefit, which he supports. [#1907]

#### Why couples therapy is worth it
- Context. This year the author's meditation has been difficult, but inner work and development continued: one of his most stable sport periods (training almost daily when possible, feeling much stronger and more energetic), a new individual therapist, and couples therapy with Dasha. Their therapy spending is at an all-time maximum — 3 therapeutic processes for the couple, "and that is a lot" — but, aware of the privilege of access to therapy and to great specialists, he is fully satisfied with it. [#1921]
- Sessions became a resource. Fairly quickly (though they had to get there) the hour-and-a-half sessions stopped being another calendar task and became one more "window of resource" — not consuming the therapist as a service but, he supposes, a mutual process of exploration and enrichment. [#1921]
- Why close relationships deserve this investment. The closer the relationship, the more influence it has on life, well-being, understanding of self and others, and movement toward goals. If tension and misunderstanding accumulate in such a relationship — and all of us have a "bucket, or a pail" of negative thoughts about our partner — it drains a great deal of resource and can become fatal. [#1921]
- Zones of helplessness. Relationships often contain areas where one feels powerless. The author's examples: someone waits for years or decades for a partner who is always late and suffers over it; someone wants a non-drinking husband who drinks and cannot be influenced; a couple has different sexual rhythms or temperaments, producing chronic mismatch that feels irreparable. We can be tired and angry about these situations to our very depths; couples therapy helps lower the intensity of this helplessness, returning strength and agency. [#1921]
- Good relationships are not conflict-free. Relationships where conflicts are all resolved or never arise are "the lot of dead or lifeless relationships". What makes a relationship good despite unresolved conflicts is trust, each side's authorship, and the ability to hear not only each other but oneself. "Отношения — это месс, а не идиллия" (Relationships are a mess, not an idyll); the closer we are, the more likely we hurt each other, more than once. The author's takeaway from couples therapy: "мы не одни в наших бессилиях" (we are not alone in our powerlessness). [#1921]

### Practical playbook

- When choosing a therapist, the author recommends one who meditates seriously and can be genuinely present, since presence-level work requires the therapist to know that level personally. [#1323]
- Be wary of therapy (even depth therapy) that only intensifies your urge to "understand", "work through" or "fix" experience; the aim the author proposes is to stop fighting it. [#1323]
- Before trusting a viral claim about therapy (that it does more harm than good, or that a method is dangerous), check whether it is proven and whether it presents contested theses as facts; notice contempt as a signal of bias. [#1485, #1616]

### Evidence and numbers

- Gendlin's research at the University of Chicago (with Carl Rogers): clients who paused, hesitated, searched for exact words and listened to bodily sensations achieved results; research the author cites says felt-sense ability, not method, best predicts therapy success. [#1343]
- *Bad Therapy* (2024) paradox: rising therapy consultations, yet adolescent anxiety and depression are not falling (the book's claim). Psychology Today and The Gospel Coalition reviews, as reported by the author, fault it for generalizations without evidence, relying on observations and interviews. [#1485]
- Large meta-studies (per the author): therapy is on average effective; outcome depends heavily on alliance quality and client's active participation. Researchers like Scott Lilienfeld: deterioration, dependence and regression are statistically recorded, not rare anomalies. [#1674]
- Russian-language IFS training: waiting lists sometimes of several years; "3 months of training" as the illusion of quick professionalism sold by platforms. [#1616, #1674]

- PAT intensives: in 9 out of 10 cases the author has assisted (dozens in total), deep trauma work unfolds — the author's own observation. [#1862]
- Constellations systematic review (April 2026, medRxiv preprint, as cited by the author): it included only RCTs; across the whole history of the method only 5 were found, all at high risk of bias, with the certainty of evidence rated very low. The author's honest summary: we do not know whether it works; science has no grounds yet to think it does. [#1907]
- Hungarian randomized study of constellations (registered on clinicaltrials.gov, as cited by the author): one month after a one-day seminar, 36% of participants reported negative changes they did not rule out as related to the process — worsened mood, exacerbated family conflicts, uncontrollable crying, intensified eating-disorder symptoms and others. After six months, 25% still reported possible deterioration. The author's verdict: "Это слишком много!" (That is too much!) [#1907]
- Gottman — delay before seeking help: by psychologist John Gottman's estimate, couples on average wait about six years from the appearance of serious problems before seeking help — "Шесть лет, за которые ведро успевает наполниться" (Six years, in which the bucket has time to fill). [#1921]
- Gottman — perpetual conflicts: about 69% of a couple's conflicts are "perpetual"; stable couples never resolve them, often because there are no normal solutions. The author was surprised by this finding. [#1921]
