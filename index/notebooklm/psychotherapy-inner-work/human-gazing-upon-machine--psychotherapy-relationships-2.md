## Relationships, communication and repairing connection

### What this covers

This topic gathers the channel's posts on how people relate and communicate: why refusing a request hurts and how to frame a refusal in nonviolent communication; why familiar partners (even unhealthy ones) feel right; how the author thinks through a crisis in a close relationship (facets of contact, mutual influence, desynchronised change, and a three-part review); the evidence against Gary Chapman's five love languages; Grice's conversational maxims illustrated with a comic "Dad, how do you make pancakes?" dialogue; and how to support someone who has been hurt or traumatised — validation first, attachment as an antidote to trauma, and restorative justice instead of escalation [#3, #190, #229, #280, #281, #286, #395, #498, #499].

According to the author, these matter because humans are a social species for whom refusals threaten connection, because we acquire all our skills through contact with others, and because a hurt person left alone with their pain is the very thing that turns a painful event into trauma [#3, #281, #498].

### Core ideas

- Every refusal is a choice in favour of something else that is more important to us at that moment; refusals hurt because they are perceived as a threat to cooperation and to helping, by both sides. The author's four-step algorithm (pick a moderately charged case, map the needs you sacrifice and the needs you choose, phrase a brief / motivated one-sided / motivated interactive refusal, practise aloud) turns a regretted refusal into a conscious choice. Full treatment in *Refusal as a choice for something else* and *Refusal algorithm — four steps* [#3, article #3].
- Familiarity feels good because the brain's prediction system confirms we were right — and the same mechanism draws us to "toxic" partners who resemble significant people from our past: "both a bug and a feature" [#229].
- A relationship is a mutual exchange sustained by expectation of reward; when we lose the belief in reward, meaning and motivation go and we let the connection go [#281].
- We only know a person in the facets where we interact, so losing a connection may mean losing only some facets; whether to continue depends on whether something valuable remains [#281, #286].
- Contact is two-way and partly steerable (Bateson's second-order cybernetics), and relationship crises arise when partners' tastes, needs and values change out of sync [#286].
- Chapman's love languages did not survive testing: people value all five, there are more than five, and matching languages does not raise satisfaction — expressing love in any form does [#395].
- Grice's maxims are baseline expectations for conversation that we can also break creatively (exaggeration, irony) [#190].
- When supporting a hurt person, first be with them and validate their pain; steering them early toward understanding the offender leaves them more alone, unheard and defenceless. Reparative measures (restorative justice) work better than aggression answering aggression — but only after the victim's safety is restored [#498, #499].

### Frameworks, models and step-by-step methods

#### Refusal as a choice for something else (NVC refusal exercise)
Thesis. The post's headline: «Каждый наш отказ от чего-то — это выбор в пользу чего-то другого, в это время более важного для нас» (Each of our refusals of something is a choice in favour of something else, more important to us at that time) [#3].

Why refusals are hard. In communication refusals go hand in hand with requests. A request is defined as «запрос на сотрудничество, поддержку или какие-то ресурсы, которыми обладает другой» (a bid for cooperation, support or some resources that the other person has). Therefore, in socially oriented species — "for example, you and me" — a refusal of a request can be perceived in two ways [#3]:
1. As a refusal of cooperation as a whole — which brings the threat of a break in the social bond and of isolation [#3].
2. As a refusal of help to someone who otherwise will not cope — which means possibly causing harm and pain, and also failing to be a mentor for someone in need, a blow to one's sense of contribution and competence [#3].

Both sides feel it. This perception can exist not only in the listener but also in the person refusing. So when we say "No", we too may feel unpleasant feelings from the frustration of an important part of our needs. Nevertheless, we have reasons for refusing [#3].

The exercise. It consists of two parts, which can be practised in life together or separately [#3]:
1. Understanding your motivation to refuse — how to understand what, and why, you are saying "yes" to at this moment (the refusal reframed as a "yes" to something more important) [#3].
2. Communicating it — how to tell the other person about it in the language of nonviolent communication (ненасильственное общение) [#3].

#### Refusal algorithm — four steps (from the linked longread)
The full algorithm is in the author's Telegraph longread «Отказы: от сожаления к пониманию мотивации и осознанному выбору» (Refusals: from regret to understanding motivation and conscious choice), which she presents as the algorithm accompanying the post and a practice for training these abilities. The title itself states the aim: to move from regretting a refusal to understanding one's motivation and choosing consciously [#3].

Step 1. Choose a case. Take a situation in which it is important for you, or you want, or it is hard for you to refuse another person [#3].

Safety rule («Техника безопасности», *safety precautions*). If any of these holds [#3]:
- you are doing this for the first time;
- you are feeling emotionally vulnerable right now;
- you currently have no tools in reserve for quickly getting out of a heavy emotional state;

then it is important to pick a situation emotionally charged at 4–6 out of 10, on a scale where 1 = «я маленькое белое пёрышко, мирно наблюдающее за происходящим» (I am a small white feather peacefully watching what is happening) and 10 = «хочется бегать по потолку и орать, разрушая всё вокруг» (I want to run on the ceiling and scream, destroying everything around). The point is to train on a moderately charged case, not on the most painful one [#3].

Step 2. Understand your motivation. Make two columns (three, if you do the starred task *️⃣) and write into the corresponding column [#3]:
1. «Отказывая сейчас, какие нужды я не удовлетворяю» (By refusing now, which needs am I not meeting) — the needs the refusal frustrates (these feed the first half of the "motivated interactive" refusal in Step 3) [#3].
2. «Отказывая сейчас, какие нужды я выбираю удовлетворить» (By refusing now, which needs do I choose to meet) — what the refusal is actually a "yes" to [#3].
3. *️⃣ (optional, starred) «При удовлетворении каких нужд я сказал(а) бы: «Да»» (If which needs were met would I say "Yes") — the conditions under which you would agree [#3].

As an orientation, support and inspiration you can use a list of needs (the article links to one; its contents are not part of the source) [#3].

Step 3. Formulate the message. Once we have become aware of our needs, we can speak about them. Depending on how close the interlocutor is, how important it is to keep contact with them, and the context of the situation, different kinds of refusal may suit us. The author distinguishes three main types [#3]:
1. Brief (Краткий): «Нет, я сейчас не готов(а)/не могу» (No, I'm not ready / can't right now) [#3].
2. Motivated one-sided (Мотивированный односторонний) — clarifies the reasons for the refusal to the other person through your own needs: «Нет, потому что мне сейчас важно… [нужды из второго столбика]» (No, because right now it is important to me… [needs from the second column]) [#3].
3. Motivated interactive (Мотивированный взаимодействующий) — names both what you value in the other/the relationship and what you need now: «Мне важно сохранить с тобой…/мне ценно… [нужды из первого столбика] при этом сейчас мне необходимо… [нужды из второго столбика]» (It is important to me to keep with you… / I value… [needs from the first column], at the same time right now I need… [needs from the second column]) [#3].

Additional support phrases (📜 «Дополнительные фразы-опоры»), usable separately or combined [#3]:
- «К сожалению, сейчас не могу.» (Unfortunately, I can't right now.)
- «Можем в другой раз/попозже/после того, как я...?» (Can we another time / later / after I…?)
- «Мне бы хотелось, в первую очередь,…» (I would like, first of all, …)
- «Мне необходимо…/мне сейчас *эмоция*…, поэтому прямо сейчас у меня нет возможности…» (I need… / right now I feel *emotion*…, so right now I don't have the possibility…)
- «Спасибо, что обращаешься ко мне — мне ценно, что ты… /твоё… При этом мне сейчас нужно…» (Thank you for turning to me — I value that you… / your… At the same time right now I need…)
- «Я понимаю, что тебе хочется… при этом мне комфортнее делать…/мне больше подходит.../я бы предпочёл/предпочла здесь…» (I understand that you want… at the same time it is more comfortable for me to do… / suits me better… / I would prefer here…)

If you did the starred task, you can voice the needs from the third column with any of the three refusal types [#3]:
- «Я готов(а) это сделать, если/когда мне будет хватать...» (I'm ready to do it if/when I have enough…)
- «Можешь тогда ты, пожалуйста, сделать для меня вот это — это мне нужно, чтобы…» (Could you then please do this for me — I need it in order to…)

Author's example (starred variant — a conditional yes). "Could you please cook something to eat? I'm wildly hungry." — "Mm… okay, then please get the dishes ready for it. Or give me a little massage, please, while it cooks :-)" The request is accepted, but on the condition that the asker meets a need of the person agreeing [#3].

Step 4. Practice. Train saying aloud what you formulated in the previous step [#3]:
- a) choose a person with whom you would feel comfortable trying it, or
- b) record yourself on video/audio addressing your interlocutor, then rewatch/relisten to the message as if it were addressed to you.

Afterwards it can be useful to note which feelings arose in you while listening to the message, or to ask your live interlocutor how it was for them [#3].

Closing rule. «чем чаще мы практикуемся, тем легче нам удаётся это делать» (the more often we practise, the easier it becomes), and «мы всегда можем сделать по-другому в следующий раз» (we can always do it differently next time) [#3].

#### Contact of facets — three things to understand when a relationship cracks
Context: the author wrote this text on 15.07.2021 and republished it in March 2024 as "Контакт граней. Как быть, когда в отношениях — трещина" (Contact of facets. What to do when there is a crack in a relationship), in two parts [#281, #286].

Premise — relationships as exchange. The author states that a human, unlike many other animals, is born without instincts: all skills, including survival, we acquire through contact with others — caring elders, mentors, peers, partners. These relationships are about mutual exchange: each decides for what and how much they are ready to invest, and what they expect in return. In the same way we decide with whom we "won't cook porridge" (can't get along), won't go on reconnaissance, won't sit at one table [#281].

We have many biochemical, cultural and social mechanisms to maintain this system of cooperation — from the release of oxytocin during hugs to «unicuique secundum opera eius», «да воздастся каждому по делам его» (to each according to his deeds). In the author's opinion, our belief in justice (including a just world) is rooted here too — in our conviction that there must be balance between "taking" and "giving" [#281].

Why we stay and why we let go. As long as we expect and believe in reward, retribution or encouragement in some form, we say "Yes!" to contact with a person — because dopamine is released most strongly not at the moment of achieving something but at the moment of anticipation. When and if we lose this belief or expectation, we unclench our hands and let go: meaning slips from under our feet, and with it the motivation to continue. We are ready to lose the connection — with a person, a place, even with God [#281].

The author then lists what she sees as important to understand:

1. We know a person only in the planes where we interact, and our idea of them is an image we constructed ourselves. This image can be more detailed and change flexibly over time — for example if you are an inveterate Bayesian and strive to learn as much about the person as possible. Or, conversely, it can be more rigid, "as if cut from a template", more resembling ourselves and people from our past — if we rely more on already-built models ("friend", "partner" and the like) [#281]. Consequence: our loss of connection with someone may in fact be an interruption of contact only with those facets — of them and of our relationship — where we used to touch. This does not mean we should write off everything: continuation can make sense if something we value remains. Through this same value, the author believes, works the maintenance of connection with distant relatives, with the dead (through their image in our memory), and with people we re-enter a relationship with after a breakup. She adds that this mechanism also has a dark side ("if you just thought of that, high five") without elaborating it [#286].
2. Mutual influence (second-order cybernetics). Gregory Bateson wrote that human interaction works by the laws of second-order cybernetics: our influence on each other is mutual. By doing certain actions we get a certain reaction and respond to it in a certain way, and a change in any of the links will produce changes in the others. Hence our contact is a two-way process that can to some extent be steered. The question is where you want to steer it and what is needed for that [#286].
3. Crisis as desynchronised change. Over time our tastes, needs and even values can change, quite radically. Doing so in sync with close people is "a task with an asterisk" (an extra-hard task). The fact that our old ways of interacting with reality stopped working while new ones have not yet formed is exactly what is called a crisis [#286].

Bottom line. What remains is to understand how valuable this contact is now, for the sake of what, and how much to invest in preserving what has cracked [#286]. The decision procedure is the *Three-part relationship review mosaic* below.

#### Three-part relationship review mosaic
The author describes her way of deciding as «бесконечную мозаику, что закручивается в восходящую спираль» (an endless mosaic that twists into an ascending spiral), with three parts oriented to past, present and near future [#286]:
1. Past — remember. Recalling how vivid the colours of life became when you entered this relationship lets you understand that it was not lived in vain [#286].
2. Present — attend and grieve. Listening, paying attention to each other against the background of the changes that have occurred, showing vulnerability and pain, grieving together and separately — "perhaps the best thing that can be done in the present" [#286].
3. Near future — sort. What can be preserved, what can be put on pause with the possibility of returning, and what new can be added in place of what was lost — these are the questions for the near future [#286].

Outcome: this is how the author makes her choice, and how there is born in her a sincere «Привет, дорогой мой человек» (Hello, my dear person) or «Похоже, наше завтра откроется на другой странице…» (It seems our tomorrow will open on a different page…) — i.e. the review can lead either to renewed closeness or to a parting [#286].

#### Relationship premortem
The author tells that in recent days she has been sleeping badly — waking at 4 a.m. fully awake, trying breathing, mental arithmetic like 24*17, "turning it into sheep", all to no avail; dozing from 9 until noon, then working until night — because a crisis has ripened in one of her closest trusting relationships. She explains the mechanism of her rumination: being a problem-solving person, valuing lasting relationships, loving a high intellectual load and multiplying all this by systems thinking yields "a process of reassembly 24/7" [#280].

The method. Several years earlier she had, for close relationships, strategically drawn up premortems "on the shore" (at the start): a premortem is «штука, когда прикидываешь, что может пойти не так, и где «подстелить соломки» — заранее и в кризисе» (a thing where you estimate what could go wrong and where to "lay down straw" — in advance and in a crisis). Rereading it now, she is helping her present self. She offers it to readers going through a hard time in a relationship that matters to them; the *Contact of facets* text is that premortem material [#280, #281].

#### Grice's conversational maxims (pancake dialogue)
Purpose. How to rely on something to successfully convey your inner world; "how to be the soul of the company and not its bore" (душнила). The author recommends a SciShow video (from 3:20 for just the part on Herbert Paul Grice's rules for understanding each other in conversation) and gives her own summary, illustrated with an invented conversation «Пап, как готовить блинчики?» (Dad, how do you make pancakes?); the examples and verses are her own [#190].

1. Quantity — a sufficient amount of information.
   1. *Too little.* "Dad, how do you make pancakes?" — "Very carefully." [#190]
   2. *Too much.* Dad launches into choosing coarse-ground pancake flour, the price-quality ratio at a particular supermarket ("take the medium price and navigate within that category"), then village eggs of category C0 with an expiry date no later than two weeks from purchase… "are you writing this down?" [#190]
2. Quality — sufficient quality of information.
   1. *Do not report knowingly false information.* "Dad, how do you make pancakes?" — "Devil knows…" — "Dad, then what do you think that round thing is you're spinning in the pan right now?" [#190]
   2. *Do not report things whose reliability we are unsure of.* "Your grandmother said that for proper pancakes all ingredients must be mixed strictly clockwise 12 times, and their edges cut off before serving." [#190]
3. Relevance — staying on topic. Simple: strive to speak to the point. Don't jump by association, don't give random facts, don't give a report on the benefits of cucumber procedures at a medical symposium (a jab at TV host Malakhov). Example: "Pancakes… the pancake in Rus' symbolised the sun… And the sun is a yellow dwarf located in the Milky Way galaxy… By the way, about dwarfs…" [#190]
4. Manner.
   1. *Avoid vague words and long-winded phrasing.* The task of communication is to clearly convey the intended meaning to the interlocutor, not «всю совокупность персонального вокабуляра до реципиента релевантной информации» (the entire totality of one's personal vocabulary to the recipient of relevant information). It is important to consider context and the vocabulary shared by the participants. Example: Dad answers in a pompous verse about the art of crêpe-making open only to the chosen — "DAD!" [#190]
   2. *Sweep away unnecessary ambiguity.* Example: Dad hears "pancakes" (блинчики, also gym slang for weight plates) and answers: "if you want to gain muscle mass, approach pancakes gradually — start with just the empty bar, heh!" [#190]
   3. *Strive for brevity* (may overlap with excessive information under Quantity). «Краткость — талант. Контекст — ключ.» (Brevity is talent. Context is key.) Don't make a three-hour excursion into the history of the question without consent, need and request; completeness can be elegant in information too. Example: "To make really good pancakes, you need to understand that in Rus' they symbolised the sun…" [#190]
   4. *Be orderly (systematic).* The main thing is to model the interlocutor's knowledge: when talking on the topic, leave out only what, in our view, they definitely know; give the rest step by step and, if needed, supplement it through questions from both sides. Example: "Very simple: pour the batter into the pan…" — "Batter?" — "Batter." — awkward silence, awkward silence — "And… where do I get batter?" — "Ah! Well, look…" [#190]

Caveat — the maxims can be broken creatively. Grice collected basic expectations with which we come into contact even without knowing any systems. These are guidelines that we can creatively violate, for example by exaggerating or through irony — and violating them this way is "great", because language is a way to convey the most varied shades of our interaction with each other. "Experiment" [#190].

#### Debunking of Chapman's five love languages
The theory. Gary Chapman wrote his book after ten years of counselling couples as a Baptist pastor in North Carolina, having concluded that problems arise because people speak different love languages. "The Five Love Languages…" came out in 1992 and has sold 20+ million copies. Chapman holds that the key to a happy relationship is understanding the partner's love language, and that different people's "love" can fit into five boxes, of which only one is primary for each person [#395]:
1. Words of affirmation, compliments [#395].
2. Gifts [#395].
3. Acts of service, help (around the house or in other ways) [#395].
4. Quality time with full attention (active listening, empathy; phone switched off, and the like) [#395].
5. Physical touch (hugs, kisses, sex and the like) [#395].

The test. Until a study in January of "this year" (2024) the idea had never been tested. Emily Impett — psychologist and director of the Relationships and Well-Being Lab at the University of Toronto — and colleagues examined experiments in which a broad audience could state their love preferences in their own words and say whether five categories were enough for them. Result: despite wide recognition, Chapman's assumptions underlying the theory — and the whole book — were not confirmed; love languages turned out to be not a static picture but «калейдоскоп, где больше цветов и сложный, динамичный рисунок» (a kaleidoscope with more colours and a complex, dynamic pattern) [#395].

Claim by claim:
1. Chapman's idea 1: each person has one primary love language, «И мы не можем быть счастливы в отношениях, если с нами не говорят именно на нём» (And we cannot be happy in a relationship if we are not spoken to in exactly that one). Result: people rate all five love languages as very significant, both for expressing love and for feeling loved [#395].
2. Chapman's idea 2: there are only five distinct love languages. Result: Chapman's personal love-language profile measurement forces a strict choice of one option over another, which by itself ignores nuances of personal preference. Among the signals real participants named as important expressions of love were others as well [#395]:
   - supporting the partner in their personal growth and autonomy;
   - thinking through conflict-management strategies;
   - including the partner in one's wider social network.
3. Chapman's idea 3: couples are more satisfied when they speak the same love language. Result: the experiments found no confirmation that relationship satisfaction is higher when partners match in their preferred language. Moreover, expressions of love in any form were closely linked with increased relationship satisfaction regardless of matching — «Главное — проявлять» (The main thing is to express it) [#395].

Overall conclusions of the study's authors. In love we need the same variety and flexible tuning as in a diet, because people can have different needs at different times. Encouraging partners to talk with each other, responding mutually to these deep desires, helps better than "a thousand drops on one spot" [#395].

Other experts quoted. Helen Fisher (biological anthropologist, Kinsey Institute, known for research on three brain areas important for long, alive and passionate relationships) adds: «Языков любви — сотни» (There are hundreds of love languages) — simply being polite to your mother-in-law, arriving at the opera on time, creating interests together, learning something together, doing something new together; "this is a bit different from just spending time together". Evidence-based family therapist John Gottman believes the attention to love languages leads away from another important, open question: «Что я могу сделать, чтобы ты почувствовал(а) себя более любимым (/-ой) сейчас? Помоги мне, пожалуйста, понять» (What can I do to make you feel more loved right now? Please help me understand) [#395].

#### Supporting a hurt person — what to do first and what to avoid
Source. The author attended a webinar on attachment in adult relationships and how it can serve as an antidote to past trauma, based on an article by L. Mikaelyan, run by the Balkan centre "Balentein", "wholehearted supporters of EFT, emotionally focused therapy". She took away two ideas she considers very valuable [#498, #499].

Trauma model from the webinar. The psyche was compared to biological tissue, with trauma as a tear in this tissue; from Greek "trauma" literally translates as "wound". This wound destroys a person's basic beliefs about justice, personal effectiveness and the meaning of life, colours relationships with distrust, alienation and isolation, and self-perception with shame and self-deprecation (Johnson, 2002). Trauma usually arises where, in vulnerability, a person needed help (most often closeness and protection) but received indifference, blame or sharp refusal. Signature line: «Травмирующее событие становится травмой, если ты — один» (A traumatising event becomes trauma if you are alone). That is why, for healing, supporting the person through warm, secure attachment is so important [#498].

Resentment. Toward the end of the lecture part it was said that a traumatised person has a very strong emotion of powerless grievance, "ressentiment" (apparently from English "resentment"): a very strong feeling as if you got punched in the gut, can do nothing, and there is nowhere to expect help and protection from [#498].

The key idea (the anti-pattern). If, when talking with a hurt person, you first try to reach an understanding of the motives and state of the "offender" — i.e. essentially switch on empathy toward the one whose actions cause the person's suffering — then even if this is done without sympathy for the "inflicter", the hurt person will feel even more lonely, unheard and defenceless. "Switching on empathy" means, for example, asking them to imagine that the person they suffer from behaves this way because they themselves once suffered, that they have such a history, that they don't know how to act otherwise, and the like [#498].

Thought 1 — why it backfires and what to do instead. The author supposes that all these heavy feelings intensify because the person gets the impression that they have no right to their pain, that it is not quite justified, and that for some reason they still have to "understand-forgive-let go" «когда у него, именно у него ещё кровоточит» (when it is he, precisely he, who is still bleeding) [#499].

Therefore, first (from the webinar):
1. Show that you are together with the suffering person in this right now and ready to listen [#499].
2. Look at the situation through their eyes and let them know that you see why it hurts them and how much [#499].
3. Empathise with them [#499].

The author adds from herself — it is important to:
4. Refuse the urge to dismiss and to question the significance of what is happening with phrases like «оно того не стоит» (it isn't worth it) and «зачем из-за такой мелочи портить себе жизнь» (why ruin your life over such a trifle) — this is usually called «обесценивать» (devaluing) [#499].
5. Not rush to sort out the situation in terms of what was «правда» (true) and what was «надумано» (made up), and, following from that, not to hold forth from the position of someone who from the outside sees better all these human mistakes and «недопонимания» (misunderstandings); and not to strive — without real immersion in and understanding of the situation — to reach some neutral position like «каждый по-своему был неправ» (each was wrong in their own way) and «все свидетельства одинаковы в пользу обеих сторон» (all evidence weighs equally for both sides). For more on this she recommends Yudkowsky's essay "Pretend wisdom" [#499].

A commenter on the webinar added that there are imagination techniques in which the victim punishes the "offender", presented as one of the ecological (healthy) ways of getting rid of resentment. This is reported as a participant's claim, not tested by the author [#499].

#### Restorative justice vs. the cycle of aggression
Thought 2. The author thinks that if aggression begets aggression and violence begets violence, this is a closed circle, possibly with growing escalation. She has heard that reparative measures work better — for example when the person from whose actions someone suffered repents before them and may even offer some compensation; this approach is called «восстановительным правосудием» (restorative justice). But before that it is important to "stroke" — to restore, in the moment, the victim's violated safety. She notes that restorative justice is described decently on Consultant Plus (one of the largest legal-support sites in Russia) and that there is a separate programme (she writes "UNESCO"; the link is to UNICEF) on working with young people in Belarus [#499].

### Detailed insights

#### Why familiar people and places feel good — and why that can trap us
Humans love the familiar; we feel a vague joy meeting the habitual and recognisable. Why: at that moment, in the brain, the prediction system in cooperation with the error-control system gives us a signal: «Ты прав(а)! Всё ровно так, как ты думаешь! Ты всё верно понимаешь!» (You're right! Everything is exactly as you think! You understand everything correctly!). This is competence, power, the safety of predictability; it can bring calm or the motivation to build "faster, higher, stronger", because we just received confirmation that we know how the world works [#229].

The same mechanism, however, operates when we choose friends and partners with whom it is "toxic", hard and unbalanced, because they remind us of those with whom we earlier spent a large or significant part of our time. We understand what to do with them and what to expect, and in that lies the very pleasant familiarity. Not necessarily parents — first love and exes count too. Hence, «и баг, и фича» (both a bug and a feature) [#229].

Author's illustration (energy from the familiar). She wrote the post "in one breath", having barely persuaded herself to sketch a draft, because after a long break she flew to Moscow and came to her favourite café, where she used to spend days and evenings at the same time, ordering roughly the same food, doing science and talking with loved people, with a weekly schedule and a vector of action for the year ahead. Only two familiar faces remained, but she was warmly welcomed, could order "the usual", look at the familiar view; the music and smells were all as then. And it seems that right now anything can be done and will work out — "I try, I want, I act! While it lasts." The lesson she draws is that the familiar can be a source of energy; she asks readers where they find theirs [#229].
